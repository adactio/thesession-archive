When is a Session not a Session?

I know that sometimes you need to seed a session by getting some strong musicians to take up centre stage or, as in festivals, hire some names to atract the punters. However, many of these situations end up as performances or gigs where the main musicians play sets from their latest CD or other obscure tunes from their personal repertoires - remember it’s the easiest thing in the world to play tunes no one else knows! To me the real session is where a group of musicians (including overseas visitors) come together to play as many tunes as they can and no one feels excluded. Having had experience of both types at this weekend’s Ennis Trad Festival, the latter type of session is the only one that generates the sort of electricity that makes attending these type of events worthwhile. I don’t know if many other players agree with me on this.

Re: When is a Session not a Session?

Festival sessions are a whole different creature than a regular session, IMO. They’re often more of a free-for-all, and go out of their way to be inclusive, especially if they have a paid host, whose job it is to make everybody feel at home.

But I have a bit of a problem with trying to make a regular session “inclusive”, per se. If you talk about including everyone, then you invariably get some unwanted elements (djembes and dobros come to mind). If you do as cathycook says and only include people who “normally play ITM”, then you have the issue of where to draw the line… Do you welcome bluegrass or Scottish players that play some Irish tunes (but play them with a completely different feel, and drag the session down)?

I like to think of a session as being friendly and open, but also aware of what it is trying to be. If someone is ruining it for other people, for whatever reason, then they are made aware of the fact, and asked to either change their behavior or leave… This allows for a lot of “inclusiveness” without compromising the integrity of the session.

Fortunately, issues like this don’t arise *too* often, and pretty much everyone has fun at the sessions I go to…

Well, that’s a good illustration of the difference between festival sessions and regular sessions, jig.

Re: When is a Session not a Session?

I also have an issue with the “Irish” v. “Scottish” fiddler thing. Bluegrass is an entirely different story and I would not categorize that with Scottish fiddling. The best metaphor I have heard was that this music is like a language. Some people have American accents and others have English accents (or assorted English accents) and others have Irish accents, and they have some different words for things and slightly different ways of putting sentences together. But someone from England, Ireland, Scotland, and the US could all sit together in a pub, have a nice conversation, and more or less understand one another. So long as everyone is polite, they can all get on. However, if someone showed up and tried to join the conversation by speaking Portuguese, it wouldn’t work very well. I see Scottish fiddling (of various styles, as there is no one thing you can call “Scottish fiddling”) and Irish fiddling as different dialects of the same language. Bluegrass, on the other hand, is a different language.

Where do you start drawing lines between who should or shouldn’t be in your session? I know lots of Scottish fiddlers who all sound quite different from another, and of course, do we keep Donegal fiddlers out of “Irish” sessions as well, since Donegal fiddling has more similarities to Scottish fiddling than has to how I think most people understand West Clare fiddling. And last time I looked, Donegal was still in Ireland.

So long as the player was capable of politely joining in on your conversation in a reasonable way, I don’t care what “accent” they have. And some of the things specific to certain “dialects,” strathpeys for instance, are bloody cool.

Nice post, JNW. Session metaphysics. :)

Sessions are completely self-referential things. I’d say that no session “exists” before a group of musicians sit down and start playing together and refer to themselves as a session. So here we are, debating what a session is or isn’t, but at the end of the day, a session is what people call a session, which could well be a more gig-like entity at a festival. It could be lead, it could be total democracy or anarchy. It could let anything go, or it could limit itself to one style of playing. There is no a priori definition of the thing. I suppose the problem we are framing here is that quite a few people think that if something is a session, it therefore must be inclusive. But I think most of us agree that inclusivity is not a necessary component of a session.
