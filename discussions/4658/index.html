I mangled my left pinky in an ice skating accident at the tender age of 10, never got it set or even x-rayed, and it clicks at the outer-most joint, audibly and visibly, when I bend it. It took me a long time to develop enough stretch and strength in that finger to have it work properly on the strings, so now that it does, I’m not against using it.

I’ve never understood the argument that trad Irish fiddlers shouldn’t use the pinky--we use it all the time for cuts and rolls and triplets on the ring finger notes, and there are plenty of places where it makes sense to stay on the same string rather than crossing to the open one only to come back to the string below. In tunes where that sort of thing happens, I notice that I use both approaches, depending on what my bow is doing and where the tune in my head is leading me.

Of course, sometimes the tune really calls out for that open, ringing string, and using the pinky *would* sound strange. So.

The trick that Fi mentioned (playing two Ds, As or Es at once) is de rigueur as far as I’m concerned, and any fiddler that isn’t doing it ought to get practising it right now. Fantastic way to put some more welly into notes, esp. (but not limited to) the final note of a tune that ends on an open string. Re: the pinky

For what it’s worth, here’s some perspective from a guitarist with a little bit of anatomy background: The music comes first. If the music is best served by using the pinky, use it when you have to. In fact, try to become very adept at using the pinky, because it is relatively handicapped compared to the other fingers. Then, use it as little as possible.

The pinky is the 5 foot basketball player in the NBA. It has fewer muscles, so tires easier. Over-usage can lead to hand pain quicker than with the index or middle fingers. There is much less brain-space (the motor & sensory humunculi, to be specific) devoted to the coordination of the pinky. As an analogy, it is possible to learn to walk on your hands, but most people walk on their feet most of the time, because it is easier and more natural. On stringed instruments, why not use the fingers with more range of motion, longer bones, more muscles, and more brain cells?

A good friend who is a classically trained violinist told me that one of the “rules” of violin technique (keeping in mind that probably all technique “rules” may need to be broken at times) is to shift positions using the index or middle finger as a preferred guide finger, whether shifting up the neck or down the neck. I am curious whether fiddlers / violinists here have heard any advice on this topic. (?)

Re: the pinky

I never had any interest whatsoever in learning to use vibrato - generally speaking when I hear it used in playing, I think, oh, classical player playing trad isn’t that cute.

That is until I listened to Martin Byrnes for the first time.

All I have to say is that if you are staunchly against it in fiddle playing, go listen to his recording of the Blackbird, and tell me his slidy vibrating high A the first time through the B part the second time through the tune is not the coolest thing you’ve ever heard.

All gushing aside, the more you listen to a guy like that the more you realize how it can be used in a more subtle fashion - most of the time he’s not laying it on thick - using it rather to bend his notes this way and that. I think there’s no arguing that he’s a fiddle player’s fiddle player up and down.

On the flip side, my other favorite fiddle player, Mick Conneelly, doesn’t use it at all, and his fiddle playing provides me with the same sort of thrill as Byrnes’ playing. Go figure.

On the matter of using the pinky - I find it gets me out of some otherwise awkward bowing patterns sometimes - yes making the overall effect *more* swingy and less disjointed.

For the same reasons I have even opened myself up to learning to play in other positions. (On my head for example!) The more I learn about the instrument the more I realize that being able to do something only provides you with more options - options that can make it easier to play the thing for starters, and options to make it sound cooler (which may or may not lead some nice looking box player or rich patron to buy me more drinks). We all have our motivations I suppose.

Letting the open strings ring out is well and good, but what about those times when you want to de-emphasize a heavy downbeat?

***subtlety??? shock horror ***

Sometimes I play them with the pinky on the A string because they can overpower the tune. I suppose if you are dead set against using your pinky, you could always upbow them to de-emphasize as well.
