Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

This is probably going to be controversial but I believe that if you want to play fast, you need to practice EVEN FASTER than your target speed, so that when you are playing at your target speed, you still have some reserve of additional speed available.

I believe that a lot of players are using techniques that simply are never going to be physically workable for playing fast.

One easy test -- try laying your bow on the strings, without any concern for left hand fingering, and just quickly shake the bow in such a way that you get some fast notes. However fast you can do this basic motion is going to indicate the current upper limit of your speed capabilities.

It is interesting to do this in a sort of experimental mindset and to bring your awareness to whatever is keeping you from going faster. It is going to be some kind of physical constraint or obstacle -- muscles getting tense and/or sore somewhere most likely. Over time, you can work at removing these obstacles.

An even simpler exercise is to hold a pen (or pencil) with your right hand, make a small circle with the index finger and thumb of your left hand, stick the pen into the circle, and see how quickly you can shake the pen so
that it gently thwacks against the edge of the circle formed by your fingers. Try for the minimum effort necessary. If you can not move a pen in this way at the speed of a fast tune rhythm, then you need to figure out why, in terms of basic physical causes, or else you are never going to manage the more complex task of managing the movement of a longer, heavier object (the bow) while also noting with the left hand.

Note that I am NOT recommending this as the only way you should practice. But as a way to discover and deal with impediments to faster playing, I think it is very worthwhile.

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

I find using a metronome to be very helpful when practicing. It can kind of give the same feeling as playing with a group--the tempo just pushes right over all the little pauses and corrections that I unconsciously put it. It’s about playing phrases instead of notes.

Another suggestion I’ve heard for working on speed is to cut notes out of the music; instead of playing all the eighth notes, play only the downbeats. This is also good way to learn tunes by ear. You can always fill in more later.

I’ve also heard that at the Julliard School, they teach students to practice with a really exaggerated swing, so half the notes are really fast. Then reverse the swing (short-long instead of long-short) so that the other half of the notes get practiced fast. Then you can put it back together to get it all up to tempo. I assume this method is only for getting something you already know up to tempo, and not for learning a piece from scratch. Also, I was told about this method at least three or four steps away from someone who actually studied at Julliard (and now everyone can say they learned on the internet). I may have the details wrong, and I’ve never really tried it in earnest.

Learn the tunes by playing them over and over slowly, until you can play every note perfectly without having to think about it. It is important to play the tunes without mistakes, no matter how slow you have to play them to do it. If you are unsure of yourself at slow speed, you will definitely lose the thread of the tune at high speed.

And then, over time, the speed will take care of itself.

Chad - that method that you mention as practiced at the Julliard: it works, but only if you want the eventual result to be fast notes in a passage that are to be played really evenly with all swing ironed out. It’s practiced everywhere that classical music is practiced, on every instrument (AFAIK). However, I wouldn’t recommend it for trad, because you don’t want it to come out sounding all ironed out and even.

Two years is a very short time to have been learning. If you want to be able to play eventually in a way that’s going to sound reasonably acceptable, I strongly suggest NOT trying to play fast. Just don’t attempt it. Keep practicing very slowly and don’t even think about trying to speed up. One day - probably years down the line - you’ll find that you’re able to play with others at session speed.

BTW, the last thing I’d do would be to practice faster than you want, as suggested above. It is very likely, IMO, to wreck your playing.

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

I believe the inability to play quickly is mental, not physical. There are certain situations where rapid muscle movement is needed, to be sure; but mostly your muscles work within normal expectations. Keeping track of what they are doing is the problem; which is why you practise slowly -- to build up a clear picture in your brain of what is happening. If you are playing fast and, say, get the bow direction wrong, it all goes to pot. Play as slowly as you think suitable while ‘overseeing’ all the necessary components -- finger position, bowing, rhythm, timing etc., (it is quite a long list) until you actually want to speed up. Increase by a small margin and repeat.
I do agree with a couple of the comments above -- play with swing, then reverse the swing. Try it from time to time -- if you think about it, you are playing half of the tune more quickly while giving yourself time to keep control of the whole. Another point was playing ‘too’ fast: do it from time to time to give yourself an idea of what to expect. One of the problems with playing slowly is that you can ‘fill in’ with ornaments and gracenotes that may not be possible or desirable at a higher speed. Playing very quickly can also reveal errant bowing patterns that might otherwise go unnoticed. For example you might play a certain passage the same way every time it occurs in a tune except for one occurrence -- which can ‘feel’ wrong even if you don’t know why.

Instead of concentrating on every note, emphasise the beat until you know exactly on which note it falls (not always obvious, especially if the beat is in a triplet or a slur) and can pick them all out right through the tune.
Lastly -- two years is hardly any time at all. Slow down, relax, and enjoy the music for what it is. Your ‘ceiling’ speed will increase over the years.

↳ Posted by jeff_lindqvist on December 27th, 2013.

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

Lets’ assume you are playing your tunes (let’s take reels for the moment) well enough at a slow speed, with good timing and intonation.

Here’s what generally should happen as you speed up : your left-hand fingers and action ‘tighten up’ (not tense up), and there will be only the minimum movement required - just enough pressure on the string to sound the note cleanly, and the fingers will only come off the strings enough to let the string vibrate freely - ie not flapping about all over the place. Economy of movement, to avoid fatigue.

As tune speed increases, the bow travel will decrease proportionally, so that the each note has a shorter duration (the actual bow speed won’t change much). So, as tune speed increases, bow travel decreases. If you speed up your bowing without reducing the travel, it probably won’t work.

I think it’s worth trying some simple exercises (eg just start with scales), and use a metronome or drum track, just to get the mechanics right to begin with.

↳ Posted by Jim Dorans, JustAFiddler on December 27th, 2013.

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

There is an old school of thought …. ‘Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast’.
It has a lot of truth to it, and it is why you should spend most of your time really learning the tune at a slow or moderate pace, until it flows very naturally. Once it flows, you will find you can play it faster without losing the musicality. I suspect this is really the answer you are looking for.

But to play very fast, you need to practice the tunes fast. There really is no substitute. At very high speed things do change -- mentally and physically. There are plenty of tricks to learn to play at high speed, including the swing method described earlier. Another approach is to attack it a phrase at a time. Pushing the speed up on one phrase until you have it flowing easily before moving to the next phrase. You might find bowing changes are appropriate at higher speed (such as using more slurs).

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

A large part of the secret of good fast playing is relaxation in the left hand hold and in the bow hold, both of which have a close connection with good posture. Any stiffness in these areas will mean that muscle groups will be opposing each other when they shouldn’t, effectively putting a low top limit on what you should eventually be capable of. “Eventually”, as others have pointed out, is measured in this case in years - at least 5 I’d reckon - and that with the guidance of good face-to-face teaching. Incidentally, I don’t think age is a barrier.

Here is an example of quick accurate playing of some 18th c music: http://213.188.106.66/04b07.wma
It is the final presto, in 2/4, from a Divertimento in D for strings composed by Mozart when he was 16. It’s a little faster than what you’d normally come across in ITM, but is a good example of what is achievable by an ensemble playing a separate bow to each note.

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

I think that a player of a reasonable standard should be able to play fast if they like, but also choose not to. Control is key. It’s great fun to have one or two sets in the night where you’re flat-out. If most tunes are like that, it’s a bit relentless and horrible. One of the things I worked on quite consciously for while was the ability to control speed and swing of a tune. You meet people who have been playing for 7328942 years and they cannot play faster or slower or more swung than the way they play. You don’t want to be them.

I didn’t learn to play fast by practicing scales, either. I did so by playing tunes dead slow, slower than I would normally want to play, a speed where keeping control of the timing and phrasing was very easy, and then over the course of playing the tune over and over again, gradually and deliberately increasing the speed but never to the point where the rhythm and phrasing got ragged (to be honest, I was using training a horse in dressage as a metaphor). I’d get the tune up to a speed where I still had control of the timing but if it went any faster, I wouldn’t. It was just on that edge of control. If you’re playing fast but the timing is wonky as a result, you’ll get into the habit of playing fast with lousy timing. As I got better, the point where the tune started to become ragged was at increasingly quicker speeds, and now I can play at a pretty good clip if I want to.

The shorter version is that if Robbie Hannan, who I took classes with at Willie Week one year (eeep! name drop alert!), says the way to play fast is to play slow, then it must be true.

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

Lillymcgill, I’m curious how fast you can type. If you watch the great players that are in your area, and watch their hands specifically for speed (and try to ignore how fast the music is coming out), I think you’ll find that they are not moving their hands very fast, and it often looks pretty effortless. It’s just that their hands know where to go to make the notes (kind of like how your fingers know where to go when you type, I’m assuming). It really comes from familiarity with your instrument, and two years isn’t a long time to develop that.

I’m not a fiddler, so what worked for me is not necessarily what would work for you. But there was a combination things that helped me learn to play fast:

1. Play Slow. Just like people have mentioned above, part of it is being able to concentrate on the minute details. Work on getting good tone and shape of the notes. Get your phrasing and articulation to be crisp. And this helps train your hands to be able to do what they need to do.

2. Play Fast. Like a few other people have mentioned, the only way to learn to play fast is by playing fast. Push yourself. Push yourself past your ability to keep it together (being aware that you don’t want to practice bad habits), and then when you slow it back down to a controllable tempo it is likely to be a faster tempo than it was before.

3. Play with people that are better than you. If all you can find is great players who aren’t willing to work with you, and just leave you in the dust, that doesn’t help. But playing with people that can play better and faster than you is important. And it can be fun, because you can let them take care of some of the proverbial balls that you’re trying to juggle. They can keep the the tune going even if you mess up, and they can provide the basic tempo control and rhythm, leaving you to concentrate on what you’re doing a bit more.

4. Relax. Our natural instincts are to tense up to play faster. When you want to run faster, you run harder. But when you want to play faster, you need to play more relaxed. Your hands need to learn a certain economy of motion. But if you’re tensing your muscles up, trying to push faster, you’re not going to have that economy of motion.

5. Be aware that playing fast is not exactly like speeding up slow playing, especially when it comes to ornamentation. When you play a roll at a slow tempo, the roll is not just a fast roll slowed down, it still needs a crisp rhythmic feel. You should be aware of this, especially if you’re using something like the Amazing Slow Downer application to slow down recordings. If you take a fast recording and slow it down, the rolls stretch out too much, and can lose some of their rhythmic impact.

Re: Trying to play fiddle faster

I’m far from an expert or anything, but i’m kind of in the same boat as you, and can tell you what has been working really well for me. I suppose with all advice, everyone is slightly different so different things will work better for different people.
I’ve only been playing for 2 yrs too, through a mixture of lessons, trying to observe other players and trying to teach myself.
When i want to go faster, i just take it a bar at a time. Play the 1st bar slowly and perfectly a few times. Then start to speed it up slowly but surely. Keep going on those 4 or 8 notes or whatever until you get them at the speed you want. You’ll find yourself automatically adjusting things to enable you to do this. I usually find my hand position on the neck changing slightly, bowing, the way i hold my bow, even find myself sometimes repositioning my shoulder rest! I do whatever it takes to be able to play that bar perfectly but fast. Then move on to the next one, once thats the way i want it join the 2 together, and so on. It can take a bit of time, but hey who cares, it definitely works really well for me. You’ll notice yourself adjusting automatically and then you’ll instinctively know what you need to be doing. Worth a try anyway. Good luck! :)
