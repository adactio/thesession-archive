‘Trimming the fat’ from a CD

So i’m going to bite the bullet *deep breath*

Been recording tunes for a CD over lockdown (solo whistle) as a gift for a friend I haven’t seen in 8 years. I’ve sweated blood over the thing, every day for literally months. Got 21 sets ~80 mins which i can bear to listen to; of which i’m chuffed with about 6-8, the rest markedly less so. Want to get rid of 4-10 of the weaker sets, so it fits on a CD. I want as little ‘filler’ as possible. Rather have it ‘lean but good’.

https://choonz-irish-music.bandcamp.com/album/thrush-in-the-storm

At this point, need a fresh pair(s) of ears. i’m too familiar with listening to them. i know where all the mistakes are, so its hard for me to pick between them objectively.

Excuses, excuses…

Anyway, I’m running out of time before i give it to my friend. I might have chance to try and re-record a few sets before then, but not all.

Re: reducing the breath sounds in a recording.

You could try using a pop filter, these will deal with the worst ones. Are you using a condenser mic? While they do capture the most high frequency detail, this will also exaggerate things like background noise and breath sounds. If you also have a dynamic mic, try this as well. It will capture less “air” sound.

To be honest though, I personally don’t think your recordings are too breathy.

Christy said:

“Richard when I said ‘solo whistle’ I really meant SOLO! The Mary Bergin vid above with Ringo McDonagh and the late Alec Finn I could happily listen to all day.”

Point taken, that the famous Mary Bergin album does have accompaniment.

But for me it’s about internal consistency, and in my case accompaniment is by no means the sine qua non for an album to keep my interest beginning to end. Many of my favourite albums are unaccompanied, and I think it’s because accompaniment often detracts more than it adds.

For examples my favourite Baroque albums are unaccompanied, mostly solo, a few duet. (Which brings up the fact that the words “solo” and “unaccompanied” aren’t synonymous: soloists are usually backed by an orchestra, keyboard, or other accompaniment and there are unaccompanied duets and trios as well as unaccompanied soloists.)

And many of my favourite Irish albums are unaccompanied, many solo, some duet (fiddle & pipes, concertina & pipes, flute & fiddle, etc.)

Most Highland pipe albums are unaccompanied soloists.

Re: ‘Trimming the fat’ from a CD

I think there is some confusion as to what constitutes a soloist. A soloist is a performer who principally plays alone or with a person or ensemble the performer is not normally contractually associated with. This is a broad definition, but I think for general purposes, it covers most situations where there is a question regarding use of the term.
