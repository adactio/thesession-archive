@Jim - yes I made it. it’s solid, with a 10-mm ‘tunnel’ drilled side-to-side under the bridge, so that it is sitting on about 2 mm thickness. The drone is just a keyboard with a sustain pedal wired the wrong way (so the notes stay on until you press the pedal).
I remember Cathedral guitar strings from when I was a kid. They were fantastic for about the first day, then abruptly went supermammary.
A friend of mine does wire-winding (or whatever it’s called) as part of his trade, and he made me aware of some of the pitfalls that await the uninitiated. Another chap whose name I can never remember was similarly employed, but changed to making guitar and, I believe, mandolin strings. One of the main differences between his and the usual makers‘ is the core, which is normally octagonal or hexagonal in cross-section to facilitate winding. The problem is the ’pockets’ created between the winding and the flats. This other chap uses round cores, which makes winding more difficult, but the result is (I am reliably informed) much better sounding. His strings, while slightly more expensive, are still nowhere near the price of fiddle strings, which could well be made of spun unicorn tails, going by the price.

Re: Alice Strings

Gam,

I think your unhappiness with the price of fiddles stems from a misunderstanding of the amount of work that goes into one: “they are no more difficult to make than guitars, yet the prices are consistently higher.”

A typical handbuilt guitar takes about 150 hours, and sells for £3-4,000.
A typical handbuilt fiddle take 250 - 300 hours and sells for £5-7,000

In both cases, knock of materials costs and various overheads and you find the craftsman is working for about £15-20 per hour, which seems reasonable enough to me (he could earn a lot more as a plumber!) In both cases

The reality is that both fiddle and guitar are extremely competitive markets, and makers have their prices stripped to the bone, with the exception of a few ‘big name’ makers in both fields - £35,000 for a Greenfield guitar? Makes even the most expensive new fiddle look cheap!

Re: Alice Strings

Sorry Gam, I still can’t see it. Market forces push prices DOWN, not up. Apart from the esoteric world of collector violins ( i.e. antiques with sufficient provenance to tie them to a specific named maker) the fiddle trade is like any other marketplace, it is competitive. And the only way anyone succeeds is to beat the competition on both price and quality.

A dealer buys a German trade fiddle for £100, spends 20 - 30 hours getting it playable and pretty enough that someone might buy it, then sells it for £600. He’s making about £12 per hour, is that really so extortionate?

As for strings, look at the number of different makes out there, it is a very competitive market. Do you not think that if anyone could make a decent string any cheaper they wouldn’t be doing it?

The reality is that fiddle strings are a completely different beast to guitar strings. As you say, anyone with a lathe and a few accessories can make a guitar string. But take a look at the windings on a fiddle string - its not round wire, its not even flat tape, it is a very specially shaped section, that allows the windings to overlap in such a way that you can’t feel the join, even when the string stretches. Drawing the aluminium or silver to that cross section alone probably costs more than a complete guitar string.

There is quite an interesting (though not particularly informative) article here: http://www.violinist.com/blog/laurie/20118/12542/

Personally, I know that string makers do everything they can to keep the price down and their market share up, so if they make a good string I’ll pay the asking price, and I certainly wouldn’t feel ripped off.
