Re: The curse of little hands

It’s strange but arguably the 2 most influential guitarists of the 20th century had “unsuitable” fingers for guitar. Segovia’s were not that large but fat and of course Django only had 2 fingers that worked. If you ensure that your fretting hand is facing the fretboard at a right angle you will maximize the area your small fingers can reach. I’d also strongly recommend a music teacher who can show you the various technical ins and outs of music. You might try some of guitars with smaller fretboards. My fingers are not that large and I know that I always had difficulty in playing some of the older model Martins that had thick necks, although I now play a 2003HD28 which has a neck of suitable size for me. Obviously mandoline sounds ideal for your hand size if you can’t find a suitable guitar.

Re: The curse of little hands

It’s all a matter of technique. Even if your hands are abnormally small (and everyone who’s ever told me about their hands being too small to play guitar has been within normal range) playing the guitar or any other instrument is still going to be a matter of getting the technique to be able to do what you want.
My feeling is that you’ll be able to play the guitar if that’s what you want to do, or really any other instrument you’re interested in. Mandolin is a fine thing to play, but you should pick it from a preference, not from a perceived necessity.

Probably the tenor banjo would be the hardest of the instruments that have been suggested so far, since it will require the biggest stretches on tiunes that are active up at the high b.

Re: The curse of little hands

If you really want to play guitar, then play guitar.

Not much in Irish music requires a big stretch on guitar. Especially backing. There are always workarounds. Quinn Bachand became a fine guitar player well before he was fully grown, and the poster above had it right: Django Reinhardt became one one of the great virtuosos of all time, despite having a left hand mangled in a fire as a youngster.

Also, the late Emily Remler became a great jazz guitarist - her hands were fairly small as well. And Liona Boyd became a world class classical player with feminine hands. Classical is even more demanding on left hand technique than -- well, than anything else I can think of on guitar.

Don’t get intimidated. Get some lessons early on so you learn with good technique, and I promise you - your small hands won’t be much of an obstacle to your guitar playing.

Re: The curse of little hands

Why not just quit smoking if you’d like to play the wind instruments? It seems like a silly, self imposed limitation which restricts your lifespan and makes your clothes smell bad. It’s hardly cool or glamorous, unless you consider accidentally burning down your house with an errant butt or drowning in your own fluids to be glamorous. It makes a good prop for people who need props.

And small hands aren’t a limitation on a guitar. You don’t need large hands to play chords near the nut, and if you capo farther up the neck then the scale changes and smaller fingers work to your advantage. I started playing when I was 10 and it was full size instrument. It takes practice to build up hand strength at first, so you might be attributing aches to stretches, but that’s true for any instrument. Moreover, guitar necks are different from company to company. Go to a large music store and try them all out until you find the scale and neck profile that’s most comfortable for you.

I would advise against the mandolin as a compromise for having small hands, again citing that small hands aren’t a limitation for playing 99% of the instruments most commonly used in trad.

Re: The curse of little hands

I have short fingers, which kind of gives similar issues to small hands.

Mandolin is great for small hands; so is tin whistle. BTW, string changes on thin whistle are much cheaper.

Fiddle is ok, but some people find reaching over to the low G string a bit awkward, and the high B is a pain in the arse on any instrument! On the whole, though, with a bit of practice fiddle is very well within the realms of possibility.

I can’t support the idea of tenor banjo as an ideal choice. I play a short-scale 17 fretter and that is a stretch. Same applies to octave mandolin, standard-scale tenor and ’zouk - all a bit long.

Another blowy option is harmonica. Small hands don’t stop you blowing a harmonica and they are quite versatile for many genres of music.

So, mandolin, whistle, fiddle, harmonica are all ideal for someone with small hands, IMHO, and they are all great fun. Also, they are all quite easy to learn the basics of - except possibly fiddle, which tales a bit longer as it’s weird. :P

Emphasise BASICS. Competence and mastery don’t come easy on any instrument - or so I’m told by people who have achieved them…

Re: The curse of little hands

Recently I’ve had several people say to me that their hands are too small for guitar, yet theirs are the same size as mine. In each case they’ve grabbed the neck of the instrument in what seems the natural way to them - with the thumb wrapped right it. Get the ball of the thumb behind the neck and suddenly your fingers are twice as long.

If mandolin is what is attracting you go for it! Playing melody is no problem for small hands; some chords take a stretch but many are easier with thinner fingers. It will take time to toughen the finger tips up but being tuned in fifths makes understanding the layout more intuitive.

A friend uses a baritone ukulele as a substitute for guitar when travelling. I haven’t really looked at comparative sizes but that might be another option.
