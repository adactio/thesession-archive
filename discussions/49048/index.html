Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

Just a question from a perennial neophyte. Why are A whistles not used in sessions for A tunes? I saw somewhere that the problem was that it would be out of the octave and would require “octave folding”, which raises a second question, What is octave folding?

Thanks in advance to anybody who cares to respond.

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

Tunes in A sometimes (mostly?) have notes way below the A, say low E. (This sometimes suggests that they are in reality G tunes that are played one step above.) If you’re using an A whistle, you don’t have the lower notes - and by “folding”, you simply play the notes in the higher octave instead. It’s not the end of the world if you do that, but it might sound a bit off. Also, many tunes and keys/modes can be played on a D whistle.

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

Just in general, Irish/Scottish/US fiddle tunes often have the fifth of the scale as their lowest note - low E in A tunes, low A in D, low D in G. Doesn’t mean you can’t play a tune in A on an A whistle, but a point to keep in mind in selecting a repertoire.

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

in a way, A whistle and D whistle are the same, with left and right hands swapped around. notes F#-E-D are on the left hand for A and on the right hand for D, articulations/ornamentations you would play with right hand on D, you would play with the left hand on A. makes a difference if you are right-handed or left-handed.

for the other hand, C#-A-B are shifted by one finger as you move between A and D (and Gnat becomes forked-fingered). if you figure out this off-by-one-finger thing, you can trivially modulate tunes between Gmaj and Amaj on a D whistle (G# is Af forked-fingered xx0xxx).

for completeness, the G whistle is again off-by-one finger for F#-E-D left hand (C# becomes forked-fingered Df xx0xxx) and you gain a good Cnat (useful for playing with singers and wagon-wheelers).

in other words, moving between D, A and G whistles is a good workout for the brain.

"Richard, isn’t it more accurate to say that the “home key” of an E whistle is E.

I’ve always felt, after over 40 years playing uilleann pipes, wooden flute, and whistle that the home key of D instruments is G Major.

Fingering-wise the crossfingered C natural is more, well, natural for me to finger. The “open” C# on flute and whistle has to be “anchored” in some way and has less scope for ornamentation that the crossfingered C natural.

Interestingly the D uilleann pipe’s regulators are in the key of G, not D.

Many years ago a friend (who is an orchestral musician, conductor, and composer) and I were listening to an uilleann pipe album (the piper using a set in D) and he pointed out that every piece on the album was in the key of G.

So when first learning a non-Irish trad tune in G Major I’ll first pick up a D whistle, in D Major an A whistle, in A Major and E whistle, and so it goes. It’s surprising how many Irish trad tunes in D Major are actually easier to play on an A whistle, which also allows the playing of notes below “Bottom D”.

I say “non-Irish trad” because in that genre there’s been generations of musicians adapting tunes in various keys to work better on D instruments.

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

“Well, they’re called “D” whistles for a reason.”

Let’s see how much reason is involved.

On Irish whistles and flutes an instrument is named after this note xxx|xxx the “six finger note”.

But saxes and orchestral flutes are named after this note xxx|xxxx the “seven finger note”. (Or the upper-hand thumb key note, which I don’t have a way to type here.)

And clarinets are named after this note xxx|000 the “three finger note”.

Thus an orchestral “C flute” and an Irish “D flute” and a “G clarinet” (they do exist) all give mostly the same notes with the same fingerings.

xxx|ooo G
xoo|ooo B
xxx|xxo E

etc.

In other words, as you go across various woodwinds which fingering is used to name the size of instruments is fairly arbitrary.

(“Six finger note” etc are shorthand names and don’t necessarily reflect the modern Boehm fingerings.)

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

Heuristically, when playing in A, most whistle players play on a D whistle and half-hole the G# as best they can. The reasons why are
1 - that they see other experienced whistle players doing that (who are predominantly flute players who’d normally play on a keyed flute in D and have learned the tune for flute)
2 - that most whistle players aren’t carrying a roll case with whistles in all the keys, and have picked up whistles on an ad hoc basis

Those who have a good few whistles tend to pick up an A whistle before thinking about getting an E. So if they are not playing it on a D whistle with half-holing, they’ll be playing it on an A whistle.

I got an E whistle at a point when I was gigging a lot with a fiddle player who liked step changes up to A to close sets, and i found that for most of the ‘bright’ step changes (to A/E) this was the go-to whistle, so for me I learned (or re-learned) a bunch of tunes on E whistle as that’s what felt natural.

Nowadays my ‘minimalist’ session loadout is high C, D, and E (the high E is a Freeman tweaked), which can fit in a compact roll case. 99 percent of the tunes i’m likely to come across I’ll be able to play - the odd exception being things like Catharsis (ideally F whistle), the few common-ish tunes best suited to an F whistle.

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

“Highland pipers often avoid the D whistle and use an A whistle for such tunes - but we know this is because they have an unnecessary fear of a second octave.”

Ha! It’s so true.

I’m around Highland pipers (pipe bands, Highland Games, etc) all the time and what they want is to play whistle without having to bother learning to play whistle.

So they get a Generation Bb and try to play it like they would a Highland chanter.

But it’s doomed to fail, because whistles and Highland chanters work differently.

So rather than try to get them to actually learn whistle (like I did) somebody came out with a contraption that’s basically a whistle modified to use Highland fingerings. IMHO it’s a worthless mongrel, even as a crutch, even as a stratagem to avoid learning how to play whistle.

But also IMHO playing Highland pipes on a D (or Eb) whistle doesn’t work either, because as I explained above I think the open C# on D whistles is both clumsy to play and really limits the ornamentation.

Thing is, in Highland pipe music there’s a system of ornamentation that for centuries has impacted how tunes are structured and composed. One important aspect of this system is having available the same sequence of three “cuts” (to borrow Irish terminology) on the notes low G, low A, B, and C#.

So you can’t come very close to getting the feel of a vast number of Highland pipe tunes without having that capability. In this respect Highland pipers know “in their bones” that the tunes work better on an A (or Bb) whistle.

So…you need the same ornament-system for G, A, B, and C#.

And D is a bit of an outlier on the Highland pipes, in tunes generally being treated differently and ornamented differently that G, A, B, and C#.

Where to place the Highland pipe scale on a whistle to get all this?

I think it’s using good old G xxx|ooo as the tonic. Which brings us back to the E whistle! Because on a D whistle you can play all of the same cuts and pats and rolls on F#, G, A, and B while crossfingered C natural is a bit of an outlier in terms of ornamentation.

So if my gig was playing whistle along with Highland pipes all the time I would get a bespoke whistle made in E (for Scottish smallpipes in A) or F (for Highland pipes in Bb) with a flat 3rd, so the Highland pipe scale is fingered
xxx|xoo Low G
xxx|ooo Low A
xxo|ooo B
xoo|ooo C#
oxx|oox D
oxx|xxx (or) xxx|xxx E
xxx|xxo F#
xxx|xoo High G
xxx|ooo High A

Having E where it is there is especially nice because in many Highland pipe tunes there’s a lot of going back and forth between Low A and E.

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

>The song is in A, but the whistle used seems to be a D. What do you espert suggest?

It’s not unusual to play A major tunes on a D whistle. The only note of the scale missing is G#.

That can be solved by either playing ‘around’ it (substituting another note) or half-holing it. Some high D whistles have a cross-fingered upper G# – try sounding the upper octave note your whistle gives with all the holes covered except the middle two, and see what the tuner says. It might be a horrible screech, but if you’re lucky it might be a decent G#.

Nothing lost by giving it a go, specially as the tune might not even contain a G#.

Re: Using an “A” whistle for tunes in “A”

you can play in the key of Amaj:

D whistle - “A fingering”, G# is xx0’xxx (Aflat)
A whistle - “D fingering”, G# is 000’000
E whistle - “G fingering”, G# is xxx’x00

not all whistles are tuned for usable Bflat (x0x‘xxx) and Aflat (xx0’xxx). most whistles
are tuned for usable C#flat (C, 0xx’x0x). (fingering of whistles is usually named “as if it’s a D whistle”,
regardless of actual key).

for completeness, Fnat can only be half-holed xxx‘xh0, Eflat xxx’xxh. On baroque-tuned “1 key” flutes Fnat is a kludge, Ef uses the “1 key”.

this covers all the 12 notes of the 12-TET scale. there is people who play in every key on a D whistle.
