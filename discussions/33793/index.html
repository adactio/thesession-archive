Fiddles

I haven’t really had a chance to play too many fiddles in the couple of years I’ve been at it, but I found myself with some time to kill at a very cool little music shop this weekend. They had maybe three dozen for sale and I think I tried most of them. I picked them up more or less at random and played them before checking the price and vintage. They were all nice, and probably anyone of them would have been an upgrade for me. But there was only one that really tempted me. It was the closest one to the voice I hear in my head when I imagine what I would like my fiddle to sound like. Not a dead ringer for the sound, probably nothing matches the one in anyone’s imagination.

If I had to put it as succinctly as possible the sound I’m after is ‘old, woody, and mellow with a growl to it’. As I say, this particular fiddle didn’t nail that 100% but it became more and more apparent with every fiddle I played that it was the only one even leaning in that direction. The others were all very rich and focused and projected very well, at least the more expensive ones did. The less expensive ones were trying to be like that, only not doing it as well. Oddly enough, the one I liked was also one of the least expensive in the shop, not that they were flat out giving it away or anything.

I’m not trying to make too much of one random sampling of fiddles but this leaves me wondering if maybe ‘mellow woody growly’ fiddles are rare and/or underappreciated? Maybe I just have bad taste in fiddles? I don’t know. I plan to go find some more to play. In the mean time I thought I would throw these musings out there for you more experienced fiddlers to weigh in on.

“rich and focused and projected very well” is the sound that classically trained musicians are going for, generally. It’s why the more expensive ones were like that, and why, as you observed, the less expensive ones were ‘trying’ to do that. Well put. It’s difficult to make violins that do that well, and so the ones that do command high prices.

So where does that leave you? “Maybe I just have bad taste in fiddles?” I doubt it - your post convinces me that you know exactly what you want; it just happens not to be what the conservatory folks are after. Oh well - neither is Tommy Peoples. All kinds of traditional musics are known for rougher tone qualities and techniques than are considered acceptable in the classical world - look no further than Exhibit A : the wooden flute. So the very fiddle you want happens to be the cheapest? Good for you. Life won’t hand you very many victories like that - enjoy it.

I had a similar experience when I bought my current fiddle. I was trying all sorts of instruments in the shop when the salesman, seeing my lack of enthusiasm, said, ‘There’s this one…’ and pulled one from under the cash desk. I tried it and bought it more or less on the spot, despite its needing quite a bit of work -- mainly the top and back had come loose from the ribs in several places.
It was, he said, his daughter’s old fiddle, which she no longer played.
It is a Hopf -- one of the mass-produced models, I believe, as opposed to an original; but I suspect the reason I like it is to do with the larger size. I fitted a new ebony finger board and fixed the loose joints, and now it’s about as good as I’ll find, I expect.
One thing I have noticed in playing different models is to do with the place where the sound actually comes from.
With the Hopf, there is a sort of tennis-ball sized sphere, like an invisible speaker, just above the bridge (although I noticed the other day that when I change from G to G# on the D string, the sound moves noticeably about two inches to the left -- weird).
I have a cf fiddle where the sound comes from deep inside, through the f-holes -- definitely more plasticky than woody -- and another. where the sound seems to come from near the end of the fingerboard, which might be described as ‘cardboardy’.
Interesting topic.

Re: Fiddles

Wonderful to ask such a question!

How do you choose an instrument?

To best select your optimum instrument from a market, hire an expert, such as your instructor.

Otherwise, the first thing to look at is size. Surprise! not every Violin fits well every hand. And more again on how it sits on your shoulder area.

Satisfied with those considerations, listening to how each sounds as you try them is a great comparative guide. But I must share that I don’t bow them when checking on a store’s Violin stock. Instead, I like to plunk on the strings listening for width and depth of sound coming off of the table. Those that are deeper tend to be better, those that have a thin sound, like the last butter scrape you get at the end of a wrapper to put on your toast, are going to be ethereal and wispy.

Mentioning the different qualities of the sound each one produces narrows the field somewhat, and as you noticed for fiddle music a growl is a good thing, but perhaps not if you want to play Classical. I have noticed, though, no matter which quality the instrument, changes in the weather radically alters the sounds I hear from a Violin.

So watch out for that one by going into the store again to re-try when the weather changes!

I find that thicker back and sides thins the sound, and thinner back and sides has the opposite effect. To the point where it is excessive and unpleasantly loud. Too, such a thinned instrument will be extremely growly and screech on the higher strings with very little bow applied.

Strings too are another parameter of performance, thinner strings V thicker and so on.

But nothing can more alter performance than setup. That is why stores will hawk old screechers set up to sell! When the weather changes your Stradivarius will have a multiple personality attack and become a Lemonivarius! IOW if you are planning to spend a bunch then it is better to have an expert do your selecting for you, or in my case always buy new!

Elf, in your search for that sound in your head, make sure you check carefully for balance across all the strings, and pay special attention to the E string, and also the A. It’s easy to find a fiddle with a lovely deep bass on the G string and be utterly seduced by that. Much harder to find one with body and sweetness and volume on the E string, and in my opinion that is very important - more important, in fact.

Re: Fiddles

I’d agree with Ben that it takes a while to really evaluate a fiddle. Even assuming a good standard setup, a fiddle can sound so much different playing at home for a while, compared to what it sounded like in the shop. I think it’s important to be able to do this - maybe there’s a trial period, where you can buy the instrument and return it undamaged after (eg) 7 days and get a full refund.

The other thing is (as mentioned before) would be to have another experienced player evaluate it too.

As for relationship of sound quality to price, well that’s an area for a lot of dispute. A cheaper instrument may sound better to a particular player, and that may be down to personal choice - however, it could be down to not exploring (or being unable to explore) the full potential of a more expensive and better quality instrument.

Tone and volume projection - again another area where perception differs wildly, from the sound under your chin vs the sound a listener 20 feet away can hear. It’s often said said that some of the very high quality instruments don’t sound very loud to the player, but have great projection.

Bottom line is - it’s you who are shelling out the bucks, so providing the instrument is in good nick and set up properly, it’s your choice, and nobody else’s :)

Each year in April, in Manhattan, there is an huge international violin 3 day exposition called MondoMusica. Fiddles, bows and luthiers from all over the world. This is Disney World for the fiddler.
