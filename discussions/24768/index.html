Re: Rhythm description in words ~ Slides

A tune in 12/8 divided into 4 groups of 3 quavers or the rhythmic equivalent( such as a dotted crotchet or crotchet/quaver combination), and counted in 4 as for 4 groups rather than 12. The basic rhythm would consist of a crotchet bass note followed by a quaver chord inversion and this is repeated another 3 times for each bar. Note that there are more flamboyant styles of accompaniment for this type of tune. The obvious one would be Steve Cooney, and track 6 of Noel Hill’s “The Irish Concertina 2” gives an example of Steve’s approach. Jim Murray’s work with Seamus Begley is also noteworthy. But like Llig says you really need someone to show you how it’s done. Next time you or your friends are passing through Melbourne look me up I’ll be happy to show you how it’s done!

Re: Rhythm description in words ~ Slides

When I’m explaining slides, I juxtapose them with jigs, explaining how there’s a longer arc to the phrasing. I usually demonstrate by playing a few bars of the Connaughtman’s Rambles, which has a very 6/8 feel, and then maybe the first part or so of the Star Above the Garter, which (for me anyway) nicely displays the slide rhythm. I also try to point out what I picture as a slow up and down motion (in emphasis?) in the phrasing, that makes me think of horses on a carousel going slowly up and down as they go around. I don’t know if that last bit translates via the internet, but it seems to work in person. I think the key, though, is to play it so that they can hear the rhythm and phrasing.

Re: Rhythm description in words ~ Slides

Slides can be contrasted with jigs, but only in the sense that “they are both commonly notated in 6/8, but sound nothing alike, rhythmically, and so you can’t really rely on the stated meter of the tune and must use your ears”. (ie, you were right the first time, Ben, but good luck to you for trying to do it in language.

If your backers can get the hang of a polka, that’s the best starting point for them. Slides are almost the same as polkas, for the backing players at least - both have that two-beat feel and require above all a steady hand.
Confidence is paramount in the Kerry tunes, if you’re going to try to play guitar behind them. Play the wrong chord if you must, but absolutely believe in the chord you play and don’t stop for anything until they’re done. Anything tentative on a slide or a polka is wrong, period.

Incidentally, if you play the first part of “Teddy Bears’ Picnic” at the regular tempo it is played at, you will have a single jig. But if you take the tempo up a few notches you have a slide. Of course the second part of the song(:Picnic time for eveyone etc.) is a march.
Armed with that piece of info, your education in Irish music is now comlpete!

Both of the guitar players are new to the tunes. I think I am having a bit of success getting them to listen to the tunes. To a certain degree we two melody players had to just keep playing each tune (more than 3 times each, to give the others’ a better chance to listen) … and hope the backers were getting them in their heads. The bass player/singer is always good about singing the tune, so technically she could provide a 3rd melody instrument. But in reality the backers just want chords, so it might be that they don’t listen (to the melody) during a set of tunes (if that makes sense)… We go into learning mode, one tune at a time, slowly, oftentimes a single melody instrument, & likely one person singing the melody, calling out chords. For this particular session there are advantages to doing this, I think we always will. However, I think it often takes away from getting a sense for the rhythms (especially slides) the way it is when the tunes are played in sets.& up to speed.

Re: Rhythm description in words ~ Slides

Listening’s good. After they’ve listened a few times, have them try just listening to the tune against the root note of the tune for a while. Just have one of them play the bottom D or whatever while you go through the tune. That’ll help them hear where the tune moves relative to the tonic - if they’re trying to guess where the next chord is the whole time, all they’re hearing is their mistakes (along with everyone else’s).
