Getting Along With the Caller ~

It is essential for a good dance that the musicians get along with the caller. Personal preferences or feelings can get in the way of delivering the music that the caller desires and needs to be most effective. In a sense, the musicians are the backup band for the caller. Arguing with the caller at the dance, or working to undermine the caller’s efforts with inappropriate playing just because you may not like something the caller is doing, detracts from the dance. A caller has a hard enough job without static from the band. Let the caller know that you are prepared to work with them closely to give the dancers a good time. Let the caller know that if they don’t think a tune is working for the dance, it is alright with you if they stop the dance and ask you to play another tune. If you can, get together with the caller before the dance and find out what kind of tunes the caller likes and whether there are any problems with different keys. It is most effective when the caller and band have time to plan the entire dance and really match the music to the dances.

& - Stage Decorum ~

Socializing, running through tunes, and obtrusive tuning while the caller is trying to conduct instruction or a walk-through can be very distracting, both for the caller and the dancers. Try to be as unobtrusive as possible until it is time to play. Talk only as much as necessary to get set up for the next dance set.

Re: Getting Along With the Caller? (or not) :-/

Dani,
Pulling out V&P Williams for a lesson in dancing is a test in itself, especially when I read things like “Generally, using straight ahead major chords in a major key tune and minor chords in a minor tune will provide the greatest sense of forward motion. The substitution of ”modern“ chords for basic chords often is detrimental to the mood and movement of the music and must be done with caution.”
This is anti-intellectual yarbling.

…but to your original observation that:
‘In a sense, the musicians are the backup band for the caller.’
I’m not surprised they think this. Essentially, they’re saying that the dance is about the caller - and I believe this is fundamentally wrong. The caller is a facilitator that allows the dancers and the music to become one - leaving the caller to, finally, observe the overall art of the dance by backing out.

I’ve been a band leader for a long time, decades, and the quality of caller that I’ve worked with is not monolithic. There have are great callers - Susan Kevra, Lisa Greenleaf, Steve Zakon-Anderson, Rod Anderson, Eric Wieberg. There are great caller/musicians - David Kaynor, Bob Childs, Dudley Laufmann, George Marshall. And there are average callers, and, of course, miserably bad callers. To say that the band is supplicant to the callers leaves out the possibility that a great band may work with a bad caller, and visa versa. In the first case, the caller must be led by the band into a successful event. On the latter, the caller must assess the competency of the band and figure out (usually on the spot) what could possibly work with the assembled dancers.

While I agree that there should be little dissent on stage, (a band should have a leader and know the limits of the other players, and speak for them), it’s impossible to predict an argument. In my many years of playing for dances (well over a thousand), however, I can say that I’ve only worked with a few callers who should never be given a microphone. One, I remember,( I call her Sgt. Maureen), after a particularly lengthy instruction period - and a failed first attempt at the dance said “You people are really stupid!” - which cast a fairly unpleasant pall on the evening. Another caller, Joseph, who was a miserable individual to begin with, would shout derisive things to the band while we’re trying to get a groove going.
Another caller would constantly tell us to speed up, slow down, speed up, etc. - never allowing the band to concentrate on music (This person, by the way, was a particularly well known caller who was on the board of the Country Dance and Song Society). Another caller would consistently, in every set, tell us to play another A part, or B part, or A part, or half of an A part, etc. As you can imagine, I wasn’t likely to book that caller ever again. Most callers I’ve worked with have been just fine - some exceptional - a few, extraordinary.

I also remember one caller who said a band should never have a hammer dulcimer, or a bodhran. (Do you remember saying that? It was a long time ago.)

This is why good bands like to work with callers that they know - and then can work on the intimate details that you describe in the original rant.

But, to me, these “do’s and don’ts” sound like sour grapes - like coming back from a gig that, after all of the work and thought, turned out to be more difficult that originally planned. Not linking the tunes to the dance, someone yelling a profanity, and a band noodling through a teaching moment (drummers and mandolin players are particularly unaware of this), are all things that a great band won’t do. After all, given that all parties are experienced, it’s not about the caller, or the band, it’s about the music and the dance, when they become one inseparable, ephemeral experience, which has syncronicity, and eventually, pleasure as its outcome.

Re: Getting Along With the Caller? (or not) :-/

I used to work with a caller (singer, guitarist, harmonica player, pedal bass player, bandleader, manager) with a faulty thermostat. On a good night, he was fine (in fact, I applaud him for managing to perform so many activities at once - he would sing-call many of the dances); on an average night, he could be expected to get more than a little ratty with the musicians, often neglecting to back off from the mic when he did so; on a bad night, he would lose his temper with the dancers. That said, when he wasn’t shouting, giving evil looks or muttering under his breath, we got on fine.

More recently, I’ve been playing with a band using a range of different callers (whenever possible, choosing the most local to the gig). Each one has their own preferred dances, preferred tunes, preferred speeds, way of communicating with the dancers etc. Some are humorous, some serious; some are very picky about tunes and tempi, others not; some like to challenge the dancers with complex dances (regardless of their dancing experience), some like to cater for the lowest common denominator; some get agitated when the dance isn’t ‘happening’, others don’t let it bother them, as long as everyone’s having fun. We are fortunate in having a fiddler who is very familiar with the dances and is able to act as an interface between band and caller (I must confess that, with my poor spatial cognition and complete terpsichorean illiteracy, the best I can do is play in time. I know, I shouldn’t be playing for dancing - but when you’re offered the gig, it’s hard to turn it down).

No caller is perfect, but it seems to meet that the most important thing is to be able to communicate the figures to the dancers as clearly and simply as possible (as DsSS says above, being sensitive to the level of prior knowledge, speed of learning etc.) and holding the crowd’s attention, whether by being humourous, interesting or just likeable. Putting the dancers down for their dancing or learning ability, however lighthearted, is generally not a good idea - unfortunately, it seems to be a fairly common trait. There’s also a fine line between encouraging people to participate in dancing (something which is usually necessary at wedding ceilis, where most people are not there specifically with a view to dancing) and harassing them.

Re: Getting Along With the Caller? (or not) :-/

As a ceilidh band, we’re used to getting on with callers. Quite often, we’re out with a caller who we had never met before the evening of the ceilidh.

If a dance has a specific tune associated with it, we always play that, or at least, use it is the “lead” tune. And if we don’t know the dance, we usually ask the caller to suggest suitable tunes.

Yes, things usually work out very well - but there are exceptions!

We were out with a caller whom we’d never met before, and the evening was going reasonably well. Then the caller asked for a 32-bar waltz set. “Any particular waltzes?”, I asked. “Any waltzes you like”, was his reply.

The average age of the dancers was well over 65, so I picked out a set of old-time waltzes. The first tune in the set was “Mockingbird Hill”.

As some of you might know, that tune has lyrics. The dancers loved it! They were singing along to it whilst dancing. I made a good choice there, I thought to myself.

But after about 24 bars into the dance, the caller raised his hand, indicating to the band to stop playing. Cries of disappointment from quite a few of the dancers - they had been enjoying themselves!

“What’s the problem?” I said to the caller - “Is the tempo not right?”

“The tempo is fine”, came the reply. I just don’t like the tune - play a different one".

I won’t tell you what I said to that caller, put it’s not repeatable here ;-)
