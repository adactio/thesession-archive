Re: Playing along with a click

“Is this a bad practice?”

It’s a standard tool for improving your timing, regardless of what type of music or instrument you play. Why would it be bad practise?

A few things spring to mind here. In some sessions, the tunes “speed up”, sometimes with just one player, sometimes the whole group does. One reason is there’s not enough time left between ending the A part and starting the B part, even between phrases.

It’s easy be unaware of your timing problems (if have have any) and working with a click or a metronome is one way to spot errors and correct them.

Re: Playing along with a click

The metronome will force you to play the whole piece at exactly the same speed. While it’s obviously a technically demanding skill to acquire, having a great sense of rhythm is waaaay more useful than having a great sense of timing.

Because the metronome will force you to compensate any local deficiencies in rhythm by adding even more deficiencies in rhythm so as to make up for it on the overall timing, playing along with a metronome is of limited use in irish music (where you get zero points for being a brilliant technician if you aren’t a good musician).

You can, however, use a metronome as a diagnostic tool to figure out if there are specific places in a tune where you are speeding up involuntarily.

Re: Playing along with a click

Speeding up is either wilful or due to inexperience. The best policy is to listen to recordings of yourself and try to identify what caused the speeding up. Often, it’s those little runs of fast notes wot do it. If your lot have speeded up just a little over a whole set, well, never mind, eh? But speeding up a lot will lead to some people becoming uncomfortable with the tempo and a feeling of dissatisfaction all round. Have a word. Using a click/metronome to sort you out would be akin to putting stabilisers back on your bike to improve your riding skills, even though you can already ride.

Lately I’ve been trying to stop my foot-tapping. Quite a revelation it’s been - not doing it doesn’t make the slightest difference to my playing, I’ve discovered.

Re: Playing along with a click

“If you can’t identify your problem by listening to yourself playing actual music then there isn’t a lot of hope really. ”

That implies that some of us are born fully-fledged musicians (which has never happened, to my knowledge) and the rest needn’t bother. It might have been more accurate to say,

“If you can’t identify your problem by listening to yourself playing actual music then you need a teacher. ”

Listening and ‘feeling’ skills have to be learned just as playing does.

I agree, if you can’t play along to a metronome, then there is a problem - and the best way to overcome it is not necessarily by learning to play to a metronome. A metronome can give you *timing* but not *rhythm*. *Rhythm* has timing implicit in it, so once you have learned *rhythm*, you have learned timing.
Rhythm can only be learned by listening to and playing with good musicians.

Where a metronome can be useful is in highlighting points in a tune where there is a tendency to lose or gain time; this can happen in tunes that are a little technically challenging or have syncopated passages (if you play *those* tunes ;-) ). But this is purely for technical practice. If you have trouble keeping time on ‘easy’ tunes, then switch off the metronome and listen to some of this http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iNQzklicxoM

Re: Playing along with a click

Going back to the “speeding up” issue, there’s little test that can be done if you have a metronome, or basic drum track, and an audio recorder. This is just for personal discipline, btw. You’ll need a friend with you for this one.

The object is to see how well you sustain your tempo throughout the tune.

Pick your best tune, pick a comfortable tempo. Record the metronome / click track on the recorder at this tempo for the exact duration of one round of the tune, and include 4 beats for the intro.

Replay the track, give yourself 4 beats intro, and start playing.

Your friend now mutes the click, and listens on headphones, hearing both you and the click.

See who finishes first : you, or the click, and by how much.
