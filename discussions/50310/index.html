Guitar Intonation With Capo

Hey there,

I’ve been playing guitar for many years now and I’ve recently got into playing DADGAD accompaniment for Irish Tunes.
I’ve always just played in standard and would never use a capo I’d just play whatever chords I needed.
However I find with DADGAD you do need really a capo which is fine however I use a relatively cheap guitar (approx €200) and the intonation when changing the capo in the middle the set is extremely poor.
I’m currently using a clip on capo and I’ve seen the slip on capo reference on here before. It is time I invest in a better guitar anyways so what I’m really asking is
A) is there any specific capo recommendations and do they make a big difference
B1) is there any recommendations on guitars with good intonation with capos
B2) how much should I be looking to spend for a good guitar to hold up. Obviously a €2000+ guitar will be good but if I purchase an €700-€1100 guitar will I find myself looking for a new one again soon.

Hope all that makes sense. Feel free to ask me to elaborate if what I’m asking isn’t clear :)

Re: Guitar Intonation With Capo

I really like the G7th Perfomance capo as it isn’t spring loaded like some other designs so it doesn’t clamp down on the strings as hard..which can cause tuning issues in my experience. The pressure is also more evenly distributed across the fretboard.

That said, the big drawback for Irish music is that you can’t move it very quickly like you can with a spring loaded capo. I have seen accompanists move their Keyser type capos in a set if the tune changes keys. Not sure how you would manage that with the G7th. But they do make a ‘Nashville’ model which is spring loaded.

I highly recommend their capos though and I’d definitely be tempted to try their Nashville capo if you want a spring style.

As for guitars, I’ve been amazed by the advancement in inexpensive guitars over the last decade or so. I have a Martin X series and I’ve really impressed for the $500 or so it cost. You can definitely get a great instrument under $1000 or even $500ish these days. I hear great things about Eastman and Blue Ridge in the sub-$1000 range. Intonation is always a compromise as you move up the neck, I always retune after capoing if at all possible.

Re: Guitar Intonation With Capo

“cheap guitar, bad intonation”, yup, that’s the combo. but do check if problem is guitar or capo. using a guitar tuner, check if each string is in tune at each fret from 0th to 12th. if they are not, get a new guitar (or your band mates will complain about your out-of-tune guitar, eventually).

if guitar intonation checks out, the placement of the capo may be the problem. best if you put the capo right on the fret, same place where you finger it. if you put the capo between frets, it will pull the strings all the way into the fretboard and they will be out of tune.

P.S. how to choose your new guitar? shop by colour! (not). take your capo and your guitar tuner to the music shop, try every guitar, buy the one that’s in tune at every fret with the capo in every position. in order of priority: intonation, tone, looks.

Re: Guitar Intonation With Capo

It’s one of the problems with DADGAD - it’s very good for accompanying in D, but not as versatile as Standard.
I also find that DADGAD is much more sensitive to intonation discrepancies, perhaps because of the temptation to tune the open strings to just rather than tempered intervals.
Changing strings regularly helps. No capo put at the seventh fret is going to get old, worn strings to play in tune, without retuning.

As others have said, there is no reason that an inexpensive guitar nowadays cannot be made to play in tune all the way up the neck, especially after a set up from a good luthier. But if you need to change capo positions quickly, mid-set, then your kind of restricted to a (rather crude) spring loaded one or a glider type.

Even if your guitar is correctly set up for standard tuning, bear in mind that DADGAD involves tuning half the strings down a tone. This alters the overall string tension and can easily lead to differences in neck relief and intonation.
Even a cheap guitar should be able to be set up for correct intonation.
I’ve used Shubb capos for full and partial capoing for many years. Even with detuned strings I find them perfectly adjustable.

Couple of thoughts:

I love the G7 capos on an electric guitar. They don’t work well for me on an acoustic. They just don’t seem to clamp tightly enough. Maybe if you use very light strings, but in DADGAD, I doubt you do.

Someone mentioned fanned frets. Just to nitpick, they aren’t designed to correct intonation problems and give a just tuning. They’re designed to give different scale lengths to the different diameter strings, and improve tuning stability for dropped tunings. Doubt they’re made for acoustic guitars anyway.

You could try a variation on the James Taylor sweetened tuning, designed to improve intonation with a capo. I haven’t tried it with DADGAD, but it would be interesting.

Re: Guitar Intonation With Capo

I’m not over keen on capos but they are useful in certain circumstances.

When I played more guitar than I do nowadays, I found them useful for certain finger picking patterns and I note even many professional will do this if a key feels less suitable for their voice on a particular day. I’ve noticed, as well, they often change to a different key over a period of time or “as they age”. So, if they’ve worked out something very intricate and subtle, it make sense to retain the same picking style.

I sometimes use one for certain tunes on the mandolin. For instance, while it’s very easy to play Calliope House in “E” without a capo(I do it a lot) I also like to play it with “D” fingering with a capo and get the ringing effect from the adjacent strings.

Also, when I play along with Highland pipers with my octave mandolin, I often capo the first fret.

However, I don’t tend to use capos just for the sake of “making things easier” as it’s a “hassle” in more ways than one. e.g. you have to move or change them “mid set” etc. Also, tuning IS an issue as many people have stated and one usually needs to tweak things at the very least. Also. they can get in the way of your fingers as well.

As I mentioned, I sometimes play Ukulele for a bit of fun. In some situations, players will use capos “to suit their voice” if the key doesn’t suit. I deliberately try to avoid this as much as possible and usually “transpose” the chords to the actual key. Usually, this is fairly straightfoward unless it’s unfamiliar song or tune with the occasional unusual chord. However, even with the latter, it’s usually possible to substitute an alternative “on the hoof”.
I especially detest using a capo on a Ukulele. Apart from it not being necessary, the tuning issues are even more prevalent and it also excessively restricts your fingers too.

I’ve mentioned before that I like to play the harp as well. Now this is great instrument which certainly doesn’t need a capo. You just have to flick a few levers. What a pity you couldn’t do that with the guitar and/or other stringed instruments.
:-)

As some have mentioned, any acoustic guitar will have compromises with intonation no matter the tuning or set up. When I capo, I typically retune at that capo position and play out of that position. Re: Guitar Intonation With Capo

One point I’m always making to my students is to ensure that the capo is placed right behind the capo. This negates any differences of pressure the capo has in relation to different strings. Placing the capo in the middle of two frets means that the tuning is dependent on the capo pressing down evenly across all strings. By placing the capo right behind the fret it means that differing string pressure caused by the capo pressing down on the strings is taken out of the equation and the tuning accuracy is now dependent on the fret being in the right spot. This advice is particularly important when buying a guitar; if the guitar is in tune without a capo then it should be in tune with the capo correctly placed on, say, the seventh fret assuming you’re using a decent capo. For what it’s worth I use a Kyser Quick Change capo. The last one I had lasted about 20 years until the spring snapped, and that was used a lot as apart from Irish music, I’m also a bluegrass singer( you would need to have a very broad definition of singing!). I also have a G7 capo I’ve had for about 5 years and find it excellent. Both capos use high quality rubber components and strong, long lasting springs. Paige and Shubb capos also have a good reputation, a lot of my bluegrass picking buddies, both banjo and guitar players, swear by them, but I’ve not owned one . I own a Martin HD-28 and find I can capo as far up as the tenth fret and the tuning remains true and accurate.
As for your instrument tuning, I usually play in standard tuning but I sometimes use DADF#AD and although it doesn’t make as good an acronym as DADGAD, tuning wise it’s pretty close and I find I can use a standard light gauge set of strings(.012-.053 Phosphor Bronze) and they hold their tuning. For open tunings I play a Maton FG100. Maton are a Melbourne based guitar company and make fine guitars.
Just for the record I have no connection or endorsements from any of the products mentioned above.

Re: Guitar Intonation With Capo

The problem with DADGAD is that when the need arises to change key while in the normal course of playing a set of tunes, which almost always involves key changes, moving just about any capo on even the best neck is going to require at least some minor tuning adjustments.

Albeit he wasn’t playing in DADGAD, but their are quite a few videos on YouTube where Tim Edey is using one of those old-style Dunlop elastic capos and sounding wonderful while doing so.

For years that capo was the only affordable option for most guitarists. Even the bluegrass great Doc Watson used one. When Shubbs came along they were simply too expensive for most of us. Now there is a seemingly infinite variety of capos made. An apt analogy might be made to the world of tin whistles. Just a few decades ago the only options were Generations and Clarkes, now a plethora of different kinds can be purchased. Yes, great musicians sounded amazing while using Generations, Clarkes, Shubbs and Dunlops, but now we mere mortals can possibly sound better than we might otherwise while using the newer more expensive tools at hand.

Re: Guitar Intonation With Capo

Also, I notice mandocello8 talks about putting the capo directly on a fret. I’ve never done that, but most of the time I have a capo somewhere near fret 2 or 3, sometimes up at 5. That means I’m using the capo where the distance between frets is near maximum, and therefore the string is bent by the capo a minimum. Putting a capo up at fret 7 or higher, would be where frets are MUCH closer together, and automatically the use of a capo there, if you’re putting the capo between frets, means the capo is going to be stretching the string, perhaps sending the general tuning “sharp”. Perhaps wound and unwound strings react differently to that, and you get some strings out of tune more than others? Is the capo causing a string pinch that prevents good fluid tuning?

For the type of capo positioning I use, I always tune the guitar with no capo, then put the capo on. I never start tuning with a capo on. Once the capo is on, I check the tuning, but it usually seems fine.
