Re: Session Wreckers…

I’ve seen “session” wreckers show up aplenty at Open Mic events. They’re usually drunk and not the musicians though. If the musician is the session wrecker, I’d say it’s more a matter of them wanting to hog the stage or microphone for too long, to spend too much time talking to the audience because, isn’t it SO romantic for them to be a singer-songwriter to save everyone’s soul.

I’ve also been to events where it’s been a great discovery and joy when people with different instruments are blended together. It’s a thrill for the musicians to be suddenly playing in a new context and have fun with it, it’s relaxing and the crowd usually has more fun. It’s nothing like, “Hi, we’re Johnny Traditional And The Sametones and we’re here to play 17 variations on the National Anthem of Henry VIII’s Silver Spittoon for your everyday Saturday afternoon education and entertainment.”

I noticed the “Obey” graphic above. Isn’t Obey a brand of Scotch whisky? By the way, I once went to a Scotch tasting course, 2 hours of taking a taste tour around the main regions of Scotch making, and as a taste comparison, they also had a bottle of Jameson Irish Whisky there. I recommend to never mix the two types of whisky. Your taste buds can get confused, turn to cold mud and it ruins the experience of both. Some things, like music, can greatly benefit from diversity and spontaneous blends, while some things don’t. If you mix different types of Scotch or Irish whiskies together, that’s called a “blend”, if it turns out with a nice taste. Isn’t a good “session” as likely to include a good spontaneous new blend of things that are in harmony, as much as any well-practiced band that plays standards? Harmony via cooperation is the key; musicians should have a good attitude towards mixing things up with all kinds of other players and other music, as the situation provides each time. I REALLY don’t want to go to events where some stodgy self-righteous stick in the mud is getting vocal about what’s “proper” or “traditional” or “play it properly”. And be considerate of everyone around you and don’t hog the mic or be a pig with solo time. A musician that does a little showing off is great, as long as it also doesn’t mean hogging stage time or unfairly shutting out others access to the audio spotlight. That’s why they serve beer; to grease the wheels of cooperation!

For me, the term session wrecker implies a total crash, the kind of thing where the person has to be asked to stop whatever they’re doing because it actually is wrecking the entire session. Or the rest of the players are too polite to do that, so they start walking out, one by one. Not just a minor annoyance.

In the minor annoyance category I’d place things like someone hogging the sets a bit too long with tunes nobody else knows. Or someone insisting on singing a song where the instrumentalists have to sit on their hands, but it’s just one or two songs in the entire session. Minor annoyances that don’t make you want to get up and leave.

In the “wrecker” category I’d put the guy who showed up at a local mixed Scottish/Irish session with a giant homemade banjo bass, with a kick drum used as the soundboard. It was freakin’ loud. He had been attending local OldTime jams, and tried to play alternating I-V notes to accompany the tunes, completely out of rhythm. I was the one who eventually had to speak up and tell him to stop, it just wasn’t working. I tried to give him some pointers, like listening to some Lunasa albums with Trevor Hutchinson on bass. He never came back.

Robert, your notion of a “happy session” pdf doesn’t usually work. Why?

First off, every session is different, with different expectations and norms. A behavior that’s acceptable in one may be taboo in another. Even the same regular session may have different norms on different nights, depending on who shows up.

Second, the w@nkers who wreck sessions won’t read it.

Third, the ones who do read it won’t abide by it, or they’ll invent new ways to ruin an otherwise fun session.

That last point leads to encyclopedic lists of do’s and don’ts. For example, this three and a half page list (as a pdf!): https://www.irishmusicsocietyofoulu.org/media-files/General%20Session%20Etiquette.pdf.

Re: Session Wreckers…

Kenny, that’s grand, thanks for sharing again.

I couldn’t read much of that “Sessions from Hell” thread, would do my head in. But the upside to this thread about session wreckers is that it may help someone to speak up, civilly, to curb behavior that would otherwise ruin an evening of craic and good music.

I’m of the stripe that believes communication is paramount. If someone is hogging sets or playing out of tune or time, or playing so loudly the rest of you can’t hear yourselves, and so on (especially if any of this happens week after week), pull them aside at the earliest opportunity and have a civil conversation.

In most cases, it helps to focus on the behavior, not the person. Behaviors can be changed.

If the response is uncivil, take the high road but be firm—if the disruptive behavior continues, ask them to leave.

It’s usually up to a session leader to play this role, but it helps to also communicate amongst all the regular players so you have a quorum. The leader speaks for the group, not just for him/herself. And well beforehand, make sure you’re on the same page with the publican regarding session etiquette and who’s responsible for drawing lines in the sand.

In the end, if you don’t (1) explain the etiquette when need be and (2) give the boot to anyone who won’t comply, then you’re enabling the wreckage, and likely the eventual demise of the session. Yes, often there will be blow back. People who miss unspoken social cues also tend to flail in more direct social interaction. But ask yourself which is worse—a one-off disagreement or week after week of disruption?

If you’re not the session leader, and someone else’s behavior is bothering you, you have at least three options. First, talk directly with the person—start off with a friendly approach, but be clear about the nature of the problem. If that doesn’t work, explain the situation to the session leader, or to the regulars as a group. If that too fails, find or start another session.

That’s more or less my approach.

Personally, I hate having to talk to anyone about their behavior in a session, but it’s what’s required sometimes for the overall health of the session, both immediate and long-term.

As a session host, to keep the players you want there to keep coming every week, you have to deal with transient disruptive players quickly and ideally with compassion.

The compassion thing is hard.

You do get the occasional person who really doesn’t understand how sessions work, and once you gently explain the structure and basic guidelines on behavior, they step up their game and become contributing members of the session.

However, that’s the vast minority in my experience. The disruptive players are usually there with either their own agenda (singers who start solo songs every few sets, backup players from another genre who insist they can back traditional Irish music because they’ve owned a guitar for 30 years) or are way high on the Dunning-Kruger spectrum about their playing, which others find disruptive.

For those folks, not many of those I’ve ever had to have a conversation with have returned, and to be honest, if they can’t behave like adults and be sensitive to a few basic commonsense guidelines like “Play the tunes you know, don’t play the tunes you don’t know”, nobody wants them there, so “Mission accomplished”.

Yeah, I’ve got a fair number of people out there who think I’m a “session Nazi” and a complete a-hole.

But you know what, they’re not coming to our session. Again, “Mission accomplished”.

It’s why we’ve been able to keep a session going at the same pub now for 25 years.

Re: Session Wreckers…

The problem is that observing session etiquette is absolutely essential to a good session, but no one ever talks about it. We expect visitors to absorb it out of the air. You can’t blame first-time visitors for not even being aware that a session even has its own rules, when what they see is that people just turn up and play music together. It doesn’t help that the session’s rules have probably never been explicitly drawn up, let alone written down, they are just a standard of behaviour and expectation which has arisen over time.

When they transgress these unspoken rules no one tells them, they just seethe in the corner or leave the session themselves. The offenders carry on, having a great time and unaware that they’re doing anything wrong. It becomes self-perpetuating. The only solution is for someone to speak up and explain it to them, but that can be difficult, especially if you’re British! This is probably easier where they are completely incompatible with the nature of the session, and where you don’t care if they never come back, than with someone who might be able to contribute if they can only be set straight.

Re: Session Wreckers…

WhistlingFionn, yes, there are instances where a kind word in private works miracles, or simply helps someone find a more viable path to sessioning. We once had a gifted violinist, completely unversed in trad, sit in with us, playing a pricey “soloist’s” violin that was louder than the next three fiddles combined. He picked up on all the dirty looks aimed his way as his down bow/downbeat approach murdered any hope of pulse, flow, or lift. At the end of the night, as I was packing up, he quickly decamped. But he was waiting outside the pub, and he approached me, saying, “That didn’t sound anything like the session I listened to a week ago. Was it me? What was I doing wrong?” I said something about lift and nyah, he expressed interest, and I offered to meet with him to go over some bowing ideas. We ended up meeting once a week for about three months, and when he again sat in at the session, he mostly blended in (I spoke with him later about nixing the heavy vibrato). He’s still a violinist, and plays professionally with an orchestra, but now, years later, he’s also a fine fiddler, not just convincingly in Irish trad but also Old Timey and bluegrass. He’s greeted warmly whenever he pops in to that session now.

I truly wish such stories weren’t the rare exception to the rule. Straight away, this young man showed commendable awareness and humility (despite having more technical chops than the rest of us), and the fact that he took the initiative to ask for help spoke volumes about his open-mindedness and willingness to at least explore the possibility of learning something new. (He also helped me improve my vibrato.) That’s a person who can become a “session maker” (see https://thesession.org/discussions/49078), someone who brings their own talents to the table who also respects the music, appreciates the subtleties, and is open to connecting with the other musicians.

Of course, this is a two-way street. Some sessions put off an unwelcoming vibe to newcomers, and that may result in a vicious circle. It never hurts to begin from courtesy and kindness.
