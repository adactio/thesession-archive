Re: Advice to improve fiddle rolls.

Listening to other instruments can be helpful (especially when you play with other instruments) but there are fundamental differences based on the physical restraints of instruments.
Flutes and pipes (and whistles), for example, can roll the A (the note equivalent to the open A of the fiddle) using the note below. This is not possible on the open string of the fiddle, so a particular “open string roll” is employed (and possibly used less often than an A roll on wind instruments).
I’ve always used the third finger for the upper note of a first finger roll on the fiddle (as do many) and some use the second finger. The reason I use it is because it gives a ‘snappier’ sound (for want of a more descriptive term). Other instruments vary in which notes are sounded in rolls (a piper may roll on the A using the d above (the back hole). There are “open style” pipers and “close” or “tight” pipers too, and the rolls sound quite different between those styles.
So, the instrument itself can affect where you would place an embellishment. On that basis, it might be more advisable to listen more to good players of the same instrument.

↳ Posted by Weejie on December 18th, 2011.

Re: Advice to improve fiddle rolls.

I agree 100% with Weejie’s recommendation to use the 3rd finger to play the upper cut when doing rolls on the 1st and 2nd fingers. I too find it gives a snappier, poppier sound, all the better to emphasize the pulse that a roll is meant to do.

That said, some fiddlers do use 2nd finger to cut rolls on the 1st finger and still manage to get all the crispness and pop you could ask for.

In my own experience, what really helped my rolls was focusing on doing really crisp, clean, poppy cuts, and not moving onto rolls until those cuts were spot on.

So instead of woodshedding on a 1st finger B roll on the A string, I spent a lot of time playing just B2{d}B in place of the roll (B{d}B{A}B. That upper cut is where the majority of the pop and pulse comes from.

Some fiddler get plenty of pop without much movement in the finger making the upper cut, but when Kevin Burke first coached me on them, he emphasized making a big backswing with the ring finger, like taking a full backswing with a golf club, the better to really whack the ball off the tee.

Then you swing down, overreaching the string and drawing your fingertip across the string, not straight down on top of it. So your fingertip grazes the string (never pressing own against the fingerboard) and continues its follow-through arc toward the palm of your hand.

I’ve since noticed that Kevin uses a far smaller motion now than when he first taught me back in 1984 or there abouts. A more efficient motion now. But it doesn’t hurt to exaggerate a little at first and then gradually reduce the motion to the least you need to get the sound you want.

Hope this helps.

Re: Advice to improve fiddle rolls.

The rhythm often isn’t the same for a fiddle player. Yes, fiddle players generally do tend to swing a bit more than whistle pipes and flutes, and yes, this may well be because of the bow. But I don’t think that’s an excuse.

I think this is one of the reasons I’m championing listening to the music on other instruments. The mechanics of playing the fiddle can often get in the way of the sound you are wanting to achieve.

I prefer that more evenly stressed sound, it’s much more fluid to my ears, much more fluid and flexible and therefore more creative. Fiddle players often get themselves a little straight jacketed by things like that strong down bow on the back beat, it’s not something I like. And one of the things you hear a lot in fiddle players is them playing with much more swing than they realise. They think they are locking in really tight with the flute and the pipes, but they are not.

So yes, if you love the sound of Mary Bergins’ rhythm and phrasing, then play like that on the fiddle.

Re: Advice to improve fiddle rolls.

You’ve identified the sound you want, with Paddy Cronin, so that’s the first and biggest step I think.

You might want to obsess on that recording for a while. Days if not weeks. You can copy and paste the URL into youtubesnips

http://youtubesnips.com/

and extract it. Save it to your computer as a video file, or convert to an audio file. Put it on loop and listen. Listen in your car. Wear headphones at work if you can. You might even want to just focus on that beginning, the first few phrases of The Pride of Rathmore. If you’re on a Mac with Quicktime, you can slow it down to half speed easily: command+K will open the audio control panel.

You can do exercises for rolls: do a roll for your index on the low G, then ring finger, then third finger. Repeat on the D string, A string, E string. Practice the open string cran type rolls which I find hardest of all. But not too much drilling, I think. What’s more important is the context—where the roll hits, crunching up the smooth rhythm.

Lots of info on this site, do a search. Will Harmon has written some great stuff. Look for his “train” analogy.

Hope this helps. Good luck!

Joe
