Fiddle advice….

Found on another forum….
"There’s a huge disparity in the amount of rosin different people use. Someone did a test (reported in one of the journals, maybe CAS?), in which they rosined a bow and then tested its stick-slip friction, tonal qualities, etc., then played it for a gazillion hours. Guess what? A few strokes will last for something like a week, depending on how you play. I hardly use any rosin at all. Just a couple of strokes of Hill dark once in a while, and I’m completely satisfied with the bite I get. Try it. You might be surprised. Now if you do fiddling, you might have to use white spray paint to get that authentic look, but you sure don’t need all that rosin.
I rarely rehair either until too many hairs are broken. I just clean the hair with alcohol if it gets dirty (don’t get any on the stick). Just keep your fingers off the hair, especially near the frog. I don’t break many hairs either. Either I have gotten better at bowing since my childhood, or I get better hair now. Don’t know. After you clean a bow, you need to rosin a lot, to get the rosin distributed over all the hair. But then just take your thumbnail and stroke it across the hair to knock off the loose stuff. Some people “whiff” the bow through the air to do this…"
I agree with all this except for the bit about the spray paint. I just use a paintbrush and whitewash for that authentic dirty fiddle look.

Re: Fiddle advice….

Two common problems that break hair are catching the bridge with the bow (particularly if you use those plastic tube bridge protectors that often come with E strings). If that is the problem it will be mostly hairs on the side of the bow nearest your face that are snapping, and if you look at the end of the broken hair with a magnifying glass it is a clean break - the hair has just snapped. The other problem is insufficient tension for your playing style. If the hair isn’t tight enough the stick hits the string, and the ribbon of hair gets mashed between stick and string. If this is the problem it will be mostly hairs in the middle and outer edge that break, and under the magnifying glass the broken ends look split and frayed.

Over rosining is a crime that many, many fiddlers are guilty of. When you apply rosin the heat of friction between block and hair is sufficient to melt the rosin, so that it actually coats the hair (there are some electron microscope pictures on the interweb somewhere, but I can’t be bothered to look for them.) The dust occurs as the rosin coating on the hairs shatters and comes off, what is actually on the hair is a homgeneous coating, not particles. The best way to remove excess rosin is to slacken the hair, stick a pencil or something between hair and stick, push faily hard against the hair and run it up and down a few times, so that it ‘kinks’ the hair along its length and shatters the rosin coating, you can then wipe the dust off with a soft cloth.

In extreme cases washing with alcohol is ok, but unless you actually soak the hair and clean it very thoroughly you always get left with some rosin which is partially dissolved by the alcohol and sticky. As the alcohol evaporates the hairs tend to stick together. It’s best to leave it a couple of hours for the alcohol to evaporate completely, then comb the hair to separate the individual hairs before re-rosining.

Don’t ever wash the hair with water, or any water based solution (it won’t do any good anyway, rosin doesn’t dissolve in water). When a bow is haired, the hair is initially soaked so that it relaxes. Once the hair has been tied and spread into the ribbon on the bow all the hairs are inevitably slightly different lengths and thus tensions. So once dry the hair is run over a flame, which causes the hairs to contract to exactly match the distance between frog and tip, and when the hair cools it sets at that length so you land up with even tension. If you ever wet the hair it will relax back to its original length, and you’ll land up with a bush of hairs that are all different tensions.

Re: Fiddle advice….

Flaming is an absolutely essential part of the process. Even if you were to manage to get all the hair at absolutely even tension when you tie the hank, when you put the round hank into the tip or frog, and spread the hairs into a flat ribbon they have to follow different length paths, so you land up with uneven tension. Of course, the hair is still wet at that stage. As it dries it will shrink and take up any slack, so that the hairs are now all the same length, and everything looks good, but the tension is uneven - the hairs that have shrunk least are tighter than those that have shrunk a lot. If you give the bow back in that condition your customer might initially think you’ve done a good job, but when the bow is left un-tensioned for a while the hairs relax back to their natural length and the bow goes fuzzy. To prevent that you have to set the hair over a flame, it is an essential part of the process

Re: Fiddle advice….

Anyone wanting to learn to hair bows should seek tuition from a skilled craftsman. If you just want a rough idea of how it is done there are a number of discussions on Maestronet, between highly regarded professionals that you could look up, and even a few video tutorials scattered about the net. I have yet to find anyone who suggests you can get away with not flaming the hair. About the only thing Weejie is right about is that for an untrained amateur mixing flame and hair is a recipe for disaster, you need to be shown how to do it, just ‘exchanging correspondence’ with a bow maker isn’t normally enough. But I wasn’t suggesting anyone should flame their own hair, I was simply explaining why it is a bad idea to wash bow hair with water.

As to the rest, yes hair is hygroscopic, and it changes length according to humidy - that’s why the bow has a screw adjuster. But the point is that if you set the hair over a flame initially, all the individual hairs are set to the same length and expand and contract equally, so that the tension remains even across all hairs, even if the whole ribbon gets longer and shorter.

Setting and relaxing hair is something so basic I though most people would understand without further elaboration. It has absolutely nothing to do with “arrector pili muscles” or grannies under hairdriers. Think about what happens when you curl hair with curling tongs. You use heat to set the hair into a curl. But the next time you wash your hair you wet it, and it relaxes back to its original straight condition. Exactly the same thing happens with bow hair. It is simple.

How you store bowhair doesn’t really matter, The changes with humidity take place very quickly. A couple of years ago I played in Glasgow Royal Concert Hall, which is very heavily air-conditioned (the hygrometer in my case was reading 38%). When I took my bow out of the case it was normal, but within twenty minutes it had tightened up to the point where even with the frog fully forward it was still too tight. leaving the bow outside for half an hour returned the hair to its normal length. If you talk to an upholsterer, they will tell you that they regularly re-use horsehair that is 300 years old. That hair will have been through all sorts of humidity changes during its life, yet all it need is a quick wash to remove the accumulated dust and dirt and it is as good as new, there is no question of it going brittle or losing its spring. The only thing that matters as far as humidity is concerned is what is happening in your workshop as you fit the hair to the bow. If you don’t have humidity control and you re-hair a bow on a damp day (or you don’t let the hair dry completely before making the second tie), the hair is likely to be too long.

Re: Fiddle advice….

“If you heat hair it tries to contract - If you hold it straight it contracts evenly and you get a nice straight bow hair, in the case of curling tongs it can only contract on one side so it curls. ”

Curling tongs result in a temporary set. There are two kinds of bonds regarding hair. Disulphide bonds and hydrogen bonds. It’s the disulphide bonds that keep your hair in the shape dictated by the shape of the follicle. If you want to straighten curly hair, you use a “relaxing” chemical treatment. If you want to curl straight hair, you use a “perming” treatment (“perm” because it stays that way for a period of time - “permanent”), usually involving heat and chemical(s). These treatments alter the disulphide bonds. If you wet the hair, and then use a means of shaping it, you are breaking the hydrogen bonds. If you dry the hair, it results in the hair holding that shape only until humidity (or wetting it again) brings it back to the shape it was originally. Try it. If you use heat, and dry out the hair so that is dryer than the humidity of the environment, it doesn’t make it hold its shape any longer - it probably loses shape again sooner. You also run the risk of damaging the hair. It’s pointless. Your YouTube videos only show that many people haven’t got a scooby.
A slow drying process does not damage the hair and the hair shrinks just the same. It’s why pros use that method. The hair is kept flat because it is held under tension over a flat facing at the tip, and a flat wedge and ferrule at the frog. Any notion that you are “setting” its shape by heat treatment is blown out of the water by looking at the way professional haidressers treat hair.
Whatever shape you make it, it will last diddley squat.

↳ Posted by Mark M on January 16th, 2015.

Re: Fiddle advice….

“A slow drying process does not damage the hair and the hair shrinks just the same.”

There are clearly two different mechanisms involved in drying hair and flaming it. To start with the difference in length between a soaking hair and a completely dry one is just a few mm, but if you flame a loose hair it will contract by about 2 1/2" (and it will stay at that length only changing by a few mm with humidity, until you actually soak it in water), secondly the flaming process is so quick, a couple of seconds to pass the whole length of the bow through the flame, there isn’t time for anything to dry (most people air dry the hair before flaming anyway), the hair contracts instantaneously as it passes through the flame. And finally, if you try flaming wet hair it will contract, and still be wet when you’ve finished, so clearly it hasn’t contracted due to drying out.

Even the very best bowmakers inevitably get left with a few ‘waifs and strays’ that don’t shrink sufficiently during drying. These are very easily dealt by just lingering on them for a fraction of a second whilst flaming. How do you deal with them - do you cut them all out and leave yourself short of hair, or do you start the whole job again?

I dare say there are some people who don’t flame hair, but the vast majority of them (including most of the worlds top bow makers) do.

Re: Fiddle advice….

I don’t think hair becomes brittle or looses elasticity with age - if you look at the horse hair used in upholstery it lasts for centuries with no sign of degradation.

If you look at the structure of hair, there is a central cortex of long smooth fibres of keratin, which make up the bulk of the hair, but it has a thin sheath of keratin scales, in the case of horse hair the sheath is about 8-10 scale thick. My own take on what happens is that as rosin comes off the bow it pulls scales off with it. Once you’ve lost all the scales the hair no longer takes rosin for some reason. That may be down to something as simple as the smooth cortex not generating enough frictional heating to melt the rosin off the block.

Old bow hair….

My current bow was re-haired after thirty years of having being been played. My complaint wasn’t with the hair itself. I had rehaired it myself many years ago (1977), when I knew next to nothing, and I left the hair a bit too long to be tensioned easily. I had just washed the hair when my friend offered to rehair the bow. He’d have been able to shorten the hair but he said it would be nearly as easy for him just to put “fresh” (his word) hair on the bow as a present to me. Apart from the new tension I was able to achieve there wasn’t a huge difference in playability.
Another bow that I had rehaired this year was done with hair that I had bought when I worked at a music store in the mid 1970s. That hair must be at least 50 years old. The bow is perfectly useable and plays nicely.
So…. the hair on the first bow had been played on for over forty years and had no inherent problem. The hair on the second bow is at least fifty years old and it plays fine. Neither hair shows any age. It would be interesting to know how old the hair is on some really old 19th. Century bows, and how well the bows play.
