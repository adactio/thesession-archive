Re: Curious about the use of sampling in traditional Irish music

All sessions are a little different, so it is difficult to say how your local session will welcome a midi keyboard set on “mandolin”. An easy-going session in which you are a long-time regular might welcome it as a bit of fun. A more serious high-level session in which you are a stranger might not even allow you to play at all. In general, Irish tune sessions are dedicated to acoustic instrumental music, and amplified or electrified instruments are not terribly welcome. Exceptions do include keyboards, but I’ve always heard them set on a standard piano voice. Harmonica and sometimes mandolin players will also occasionally have small microphones and amps that are unobtrusive but help the instruments to be heard in a loud session.

Re: Curious about the use of sampling in traditional Irish music

Here’s the main reason not to do it (aside from the immediate reaction you’ll get, at most sessions). Learning how to play this music is all about listening to how other, better players do it. And how they have done it through the years.

You break that link when you try to play these sampled sounds on a keyboard. All you can do is trigger the bare note, and this music is far more than just bare strings of disassociated notes. You won’t be able to improve your playing by listening to great Irish musicians playing low D whistle, mandolin, or whatever, because none of the actual mechanics will translate to hitting keys on a keyboard. You have no access to the finger articulations on string, reed, and woodwind instruments that make them sound “Irish.”

As just one example, you have something like the buttons on a concertina or box accordion on a keyboard, but what are you going to do about the rhythm pulse of the bellows? Your keyboard doesn’t need oxygen, so you’ll learn nothing about how phrasing on a whistle or flute is built around the need to take a breath every once in a while.

I’m not averse to “using the available technology” in other contexts. I have a Midi guitar setup that triggers samples, but that has only shown me how difficult it is to actually play in an idiomatic way when triggering notes with a completely different instrument. I use the Midi guitar mainly just for triggering things like upright bass simulations or synth pads, and it’s for other styles of music than Irish traditional. I would be laughed out of any of the local Irish sessions if I tried to bring my Midi guitar rig, and for good reason.

Re: Curious about the use of sampling in traditional Irish music

“I don’t think Reg Hall lumps a wood and metal upright piano around with him. A magnificent accompanist with a real ear for what is needed.”

There’s a good reason why an exception is made for digital pianos: (some) people like piano accompaniment in a session, but not all session venues have a piano - and even if they did, pianos cost money to keep in tune. With a digital one, you can have your piano where you want it and you know it’ll be in tune. (…Fine, until there’s a power cut.) Another point is that this is one keyboard instrument imitating another keyboard instrument; the better digital pianos have weighted keys and velocity-sensitivity that closely mimics that of a real piano, so the player can achieve articulation that is not far removed from the real thing (even though it will always sound like it is coming out of a speaker, not from strings and wood). A keyboard trying to imitate a bowed, plucked or wind instrument is a different thing entirely.

“Also the Bothy Band employed a keyboard dressed up on tour.”

The Bothy Band’s use of keyboard (Clavinet - electric clavichord) is a different case, as they were performing as a band, not in sessions. I wouldn’t mind a Clavinet in a session, actually, at an appropriate volume and played with the sensitivity of Tríona ni Dhomnaill - I may not be representative of the general session-going population, though. Besides, I don’t put a Clavinet (an amplified stringed instrument) in the same class as a digital sampler.

Re: Curious about the use of sampling in traditional Irish music

@TheBlindBard: If you want to get up to session standard any time soon, you’ll need to stick at one or two* instruments - for a few years, at least. (If you let something as trivial as a broken string put you off, what hope is there?:-)) If you’re serious about learning Irish Traditional Music, you’ll make it a lot easier for yourself by learning on one of the more established instruments in the tradition, since those are the instruments on which the tunes and playing styles have evolved.

*I’m of the view that it is to your advantage to learn more than one instrument - even if you only play one of them to session standard - since it gives you a broader perspective on the music and divorces the *tune* from the *technique* required to play it. Too many instruments, however, and you are likely to find that you never reach a high standard on any of them (I know from my own experience). Like you, I started on mandolin and whistle, progressing to fiddle after 10 years of playing mandolin.

I’m just having a bit of fun with y’all…

I’d never seriously consider using my own iOS instruments in a session regularly. I have demonstrated them at our annual tionol and local sessions as a party trick/freak show, but that’s the extent of it.

It’s a lot more than just the sound, it’s the remoteness of the experience for any of these electronic emulations, whether my apps, or the vPipes, or modeling guitars and accordions. They absolutely have their utilitarian uses, as convenient practice aids or to help with the technical needs of recording or specific performance environments, but are fundamentally contrary to the intimacy of this music, particularly as I experience it in sessions.
