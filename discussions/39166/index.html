purpose of fully-lining a flute head-joint

There are two aspects to this issue:
(1) why fully-lined headjoints became popular in the early 19th century. They were mentioned and condemned as ‘shrill’ in the baroque flute era (Quantz 1752), but in 1889 were described by Rockstro (A Treatise on the Flute) as having ‘been adopted by all the English flute-manufacturers, and most of the performers, for the last seventy or eighty years’ [i.e. since 1810-20]. Rockstro writes that the lining ‘augments the brilliancy and the power of the tone of a wooden flute, but it causes some slight diminution of sweetness and flexibility. Its chief advantage lies in its endurance, an unlined wooden head-joint being liable to crack and to change its calibre more than any other part of the flute, on account of the heat and moisture to which it is subjected.’

It was about this time, I believe, that Charles Nicholson became very popular. He advocated a reedy tone and used flutes with enlarged tone-holes and fully-lined headjoints (Rockstro, para. 536). However, I don’t think that brilliant and powerful necessarily equate to reedy, although they may facilitate this. Rockstro himself thinks that the lining prevents cracking and change of bore dimension.

(2) why fully-lined head-joints are still popular. Is it simply a carry-over of 19th century practice or it is the increased volume and brilliancy? I don’t think it is likely to be for the prevention of cracking as many present-day makers think the exact opposite and Terry McGee has noted that virtually all 19th c. flutes with fully-lined head-joints have had cracks in these.

’The slide:
The great advantage of the METAL TUBE in the head joint, for regulating the pitch of the instrument, is so well known, that no flute should be made without it. (Lindsay was a flute-maker in London. He seems to be referring to the fully-lined head-joint in his reference to a metallic tone, but his reason is the tuning-slide (which only requires a partially-lined head-joint).

Re: purpose of fully-lining a flute head-joint

It is a somewhat complex argument, and let us consider that a brass tube lined wood head joint goes back into the early 19th century. Yes, the brass tube lining does enhance the brilliance of a flute’s tone, but perhaps due to temperature a brass tube lining is also known to crack a flute’s wood head joint. I have two eight-key wood flutes, one in D and one in C, made by one of today’s top makers and I had him make a supplementary unlined wood head joint for each of them. The unlined wood head joint has a somewhat more mellow tone but if kept clean and properly oiled then I can depend on it for a lifetime of use.

And then along came Theobald Boehm of Germany in the mid-19th century with his non-wood, all silver flute, which really did not become popular until redesigned by Louis Lot of France in the later 19th century.

I have an all silver L.Lot flute from the 1920s and wow, it can truly sing!

From Patrick Olwell

This is a minimally edited comment on the question that Patrick Olwell send me a while back. The grammatical faults are all mine. The essential information is from him.

Which Headjoint is Best?

People starting out playing Irish traditional music with the simple system wooden flute are sometimes on unfamiliar ground: there is no standardization of the instrument, as there is in the construction of silver flutes or violins. While the majority of flutes are made of African Blackwood, which many people have settled on for it’s unique tone and durability, there are lots of opinions and choices regarding the specifics of the headjoint. Some players prefer to play a headjoint as much like the original Nicholson headjoint as possible. These were fully lined with brass tubing, and featured a “thinner” wood head tube than the earlier London-made flutes. This thinner tube means the embouchure “chimney” is not as tall (or deep) as on earlier flutes. While it can make an amazing sound in the hands of a skilled player (this sound was part of the revolution Nicholson helped bring about) in my experience such a headjoint is not always as easy to play for a beginner or intermediate player. A taller chimney seems to allow more “purchase” in obtaining the sound.

The embouchure, or mouth hole size, also changed during the Nicholson period. Circa 1820’s they were usually smaller, at around 9.6x11.4 mm; by the 1840’s the mouth hole had grown to more of a “modern” size, somewhat like 10x12 mm. This sounds like a minor change, but the larger size can have a big effect on playing.

What’s even more confusing is the discussion of fully-lined heads and half-lined. All of the flutes made by London makers during this period (1820-1880), with the exception of Monzani, featured fully-lined heads. Part of this new flute sound was a more “metallic” (for want of a better word) projection. Everyone who heard Charles Nicholson play was astonished that the flute could do what he did, and most players were “brought over” to this new school. Nicholson, in addition to playing with great volume, was also capable of producing a whole palette of tones. One feature of his playing was his ability to play expressively at quieter volume, unlike the flute player at your session who is driving you crazy. Meanwhile, in France, the fully-lined head never caught on, and all headjoints were “half-lined.” Makers used a tuning slide of two pieces of metal tubing, but didn’t extend it up to the embouchure hole, which was all-wood. (Quantz had said in his 1750 treatise on the flute that a lined head had been tried, but “rendered the sound harsh and disagreeable”). French flutes also featured the earlier very small fingerholes, like a Baroque flute with keys, which played with a lighter, more nuanced tone.
So all during this time there were two different schools of playing, with different aesthetics (American flutes, principally made in New York City, fell mostly between these two) . I prefer a fully-lined head for session playing, as I feel I get more projection with less effort. My son Aaron prefers his half-lined head, and says it is as loud or louder, with less effort.

Further confusing matters is the unfounded assertion that a lined head will always crack, or crack more often, since the wood is thought to continue to shrink. In my experience this is not the case; a piece of blackwood is not like a black hole. It will not continue to shrink, and will do so only while it is losing moisture. Once fully dried blackwood is very stable. It’s true that gluing a metal tube into a tube of wood is a cockamamie design from an engineering standpoint, since the wood does move somewhat with the change in humidity, and the metal doesn’t. However, with a reasonable amount of care one will have few problems. You have to be careful traveling from the Caribbean to your home in the desert, but an all-wood flute with no metal tubing can also crack under these conditions. The early advertisements for the new lined heads maintained they wouldn’t crack, as now the moisture could not get at the wood of the headjoint.

We are planning a “blind” test with a group of blindfolded players and a number of headjoints, lined and unlined, to see if it is possible to tell the difference. We’ll let you know the results.

Re: purpose of fully-lining a flute head-joint

I’ve examined, handled, and played hundreds of vintage flutes, and it seems to me that the extremely high percentage of cracks in the lined sections (head and barrel) and extremely low percentage of cracks in the unlined sections speaks for itself. I don’t know how many times I’ve seen a display or collection of a dozen or more old flutes, every metal-lined section of every flute having a crack, every un-lined section being whole.

And the additional problem is that the headjoint crack usually goes right through the blow-hole.

I don’t have any scientific proof of anything, but coming from years of playing Baroque flutes and “Irish” flutes with unlined heads makes me prefer the way they play. Indeed my flute for a couple decades was a c1860 London-made 8-key cocus flute with an unlined no-tuning-slide one-piece head made by Casey Burns. For me it was the perfect flute. (The flute’s original head and barrel were split, and had been crudely repaired at some point in the past. I had Patrick Olwell re-cut the blow hole which improved it greatly, but with the new unlined one-piece Casey Burns head it was better yet.)

Now who is to say that all those old flutes would have had cracked heads anyhow, even if they were un-lined? Because the head is bombarded with far more moisture than the rest of the flute. I’m guessing that that is one of the motivations for lining the heads: protecting the wood from extreme moisture.

On Highland bagpipes the blowpipes on vintage bagpipes are often split, and they were un-lined. A few modern makers line their blowpipes with metal or plastic tubing. Other modern makers have their blowpipes made entirely of plastic, so often do wood ones split.

Re: purpose of fully-lining a flute head-joint

I think the thing to bear in mind is that 19th Century flutes were not constructed to play in pubs. There’s no question that the metal lining helps the flute project better in an ensemble playing in a concert hall, or even on a bandstand in the park. If one is making a flute to play Irish music, my suggestion would be to make it with large holes, a tuning slide, and no lining in the head. How to cut the embouchure hole is a mystery to me, but it is critical to the overall sound - much more so than whether or not it is lined, in my experience.

One thing you notice when you start taking the heads and barrels apart is that they were scored with raised burrs to help hold the liner with shellac (or whatever glue) to the wood. When the wood shrinks around the liner due to loosing moisture often the crack follows a line of burrs. Not always but often.

Re: purpose of fully-lining a flute head-joint

Provided that you use the right oil it really is impossible to use too much oil, inside the bore and without, well, OK, you do not want excess oil dribbling out onto the floor, so do swab out any excess oil. And then make sure that the oil can not and does not saturate a flute’s tone hole pads. Use a bit of plastic sheet, etc.

The topic of just what oil to use is legendary and so many pages of internet script have been written about that. However, let me give you my opinion about the best oil for wood flutes. Use Cold-Pressed Linseed Oil, available at your local artist painting supply store or even at your local natural food supply store. BUT DO NOT use boiled linseed oil from your local hardware supply store, as that is a different product.
