Re: Is Irish/Celtic music equally, differently or comparatively complex compared to European classical music?

It is nowhere near as complex, and that is the point. I love classical music and trad equally, and for different reasons. Some people think simple=easy=bad, which is why complex reels are the be-all end-all for some while polkas are “for beginners.” These people are wrong, in so much as there is no one ideal, and complexity is not an end unto itself. Trad is musically simple, which allows for wonderful subtlety, variation, and interpretation (which, in different ways, are also present in classical). Most importantly, it allows for a much wider range of performers, hence the term “folk” music.

Re: Is Irish/Celtic music equally, differently or comparatively complex compared to European classical music?

ITM is not complex at all. The patterns within each form (reels, jigs, hornpipes, etc.) are set and predictable. Unlike Old Timey music, however, there are a lot of notes and so it is hard to just jump in at a session and play tunes you formerly did not know. More notes does not make any genre of music complex.

Classical music is very unforgiving. You can’t be competent or even good and be much of a pleasure to yourself or others; you have to be at least very good. With Irish, you can be fair and hold your own. You may not make records, but neither will you make people angry at a session if you get most of the notes and can play at tempo. Or to quote a wise philosopher, “it allows for a much wider range of performers, hence the term ”folk“ music.”

↳ Posted by Ailin on January 15th, 2016.

Re: Is Irish/Celtic music equally, differently or comparatively complex compared to European classical music?

Diddley music, overwhelmingly, has regular-length phrases, much repetition within tunes, is mostly diatonic, is in a restricted set of keys/modes, and generally sits easily on the instruments that play it. It’s not difficult for beginners with at least basic skill on their instrument to be able to play it and join in. That makes it a very inclusive kind of music. Simple is a word with many a potential pejorative association. In fact, the simplicity is what makes it possible for the tutored and untutored alike to join in. That is a positive thing. It’s easy enough to get playing, but it’s then up to you to do the listening and to get the music under your skin, so that ornamentation and variation become second nature and in keeping, if you want to get good. With classical, on the whole you’ll need a higher level of proficiency on your instrument. The music will often modulate into distant keys and will be thoroughly chromatic, with unexpected harmony thrown in, and technique can have a vital bearing on the sound that comes out. And you won’t be able to have too much fun mucking about with the music, a bit of rubato or the odd cadenza aside. You’ll need to stick to what it says on the music stand, more or less, and find another way of putting your soul into it!

↳ Posted by David Levine on January 16th, 2016.

Re: Is Irish/Celtic music equally, differently or comparatively complex compared to European classical music?

Why the dichotomy?

Carolan met Geminiani in Dublin. Handel premiered the Messiah in Dublin. But, unlike Edinburgh or London, Dublin didn’t develop its own classical music scene. The Chieftains under the influence of Seán Ó Riada moved in the direction of “symphonic” folk. We also have pieces like The Arrival of the Queen of Sheba in Galway (De Dannan) and Bach goes to Limerick (Steeleye Span).

In Scotland, there is a broad overlap between classical and folk. William Marshall write in both styles and uses traditional themes in his classical pieces. Niel Gow played minuets for enjoyment. Scott Skinner was classically trained. A good Scots fiddler can play a Geminiani gigue as a guid Scots jig. Aly Bain is also classically trained.

Equally the reference to 19th Century Romantic and late Classical practice with rubato and strict attention to the score is another false dichotomy. Many coming into folk from Classical Music come from the Baroque (or earlier) eras. Think of David Munrow playing with the Young Tradition. Many Scots tunes are 18th century Baroque in origin.

Re: Is Irish/Celtic music equally, differently or comparatively complex compared to European classical music?

This may be more of an historical question than a “compare and contrast” question. The history of formal music can be easily traced back to Gregorian monks who first experimented with writing down symbols to represent sound. That is how we can listen and learn from music composed hundreds of years before technology gave us recording. The people from that tradition spent their lives studying and refining their craft to newer and higher levels. Add in multiple generations of that type of lifetime commitment to an art form and you get some pretty complex stuff. I am amazed how musical instruments like pipe organs were designed and built when life everywhere else was pretty primitive. Folk music came from farmers and tradesmen and ordinary people who worked for a living and played music because they loved it and to provide music for community functions and special occasions. This music was not written down and was passed down through generations like folk tales. We don’t know how much we have saved or lost and that is sad. The “classical” musicians looked with disdain on the simple “peasant” music until the 19th century when major composers like Franz Liszt, Tchaikovsky, Vaughan Williams and Bartok began to seek out folk music for inspiration. The traditional music of Ireland is great because it does exactly what it is supposed to do. If you are hooked on it like I am, you already know what that is.

Re: Is Irish/Celtic music equally, differently or comparatively complex compared to European classical music?

“A common sentiment, and dead wrong. The vast bulk of traditional music, in Ireland and Scotland, and in England, was music by and for the country house and those that could afford music. For sure, stuff filtered down to all classes, and there was always material, especially vocal material, circulating in parallel, but traditional music is essentially a relic of the landed classes.”

This is an interesting observation and one that I would be slow to dismiss, though maybe I think it could be qualified. If when you talk of the ‘landed classes’ in the 18th & 19th centuries, you include the bigger and more established tenant farmers. These people were not large landowners but comfortable enough and well able to host house dances and the like.

You shouldn’t think of the west of Ireland or any other area in these periods as being places of grinding poverty for all. There was considerable social and economic stratification, then as now. Any given landlord or estate had their stewards and larger farming tenants who they could rely on to be loyal. Under that there was a class of smaller tenant farmer often letting land from the larger tenants, some of these were poorer but others did ok. Under that again were the landless farm labourers and servants to all above, who kept the wheels of farming and commerce going. Then the destitute.

So the music was most likely the preserve of the bigger tenant farmers and some of their better off sub tenants, as well as being supported by the members of any big house. You’d have to go back a lot further to reach a time of Gaelic chieftains and their retained musicians - different era.
