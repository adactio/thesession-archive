Unknown unknowns

O.K., I know you can’t discuss things you don’t even know you don’t know about, but I think there’s a valid point in here… Please excuse a slightly meandering train of thought. Needs must, and over the past year I’ve been teaching myself DADGAD - but in the process, I got thinking about a wider issue. Curious to know others’ experiences.

What it boils down to is how some musicians pull stuff out of thin air that you didn’t even know was in there.

In the case of accompaniment (and this is where the DADGAD comes in) I’ve been watching lots of clips of players pulling the most weird, convoluted chords out of their guitars. For example, Caoimhín Ó’Fearghail here: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y1WBk-_nhok Adam Brown is another one…

Some of these people seem to have tentacles instead of fingers, and even when I can pause the video clearly enough to see, with my average/shortish fingers, I just can’t replicate them. Even using chord charts some of them seem physically impossible. But that apart, you can’t use what you don’t know is possible.

On the other hand, there are only so many notes in the scale, and what I *can* do is produce one or more chords for most of them, they just aren’t quite so many, and they don’t look as fancy. (I know about different voicings and have a growing number). So I began to wonder, how much of this is just showmanship, and how much difference do those crazy chord shapes really make? Just how many infinitesimal subtleties of voicing do you really need to be able to put behind a tune? As I said, there are only so many notes and phrases to cover. In some ways, rhythmic technique may be more important?

That said, a related issue concerns the harmonies that one hears. The limitations of the three chord trick are known; likewise the range of possible accomp. for any given tune is widely accepted - but the same thing still applies - some people just seem to hear interesting possibities that just pass me by. How do they do it?

Thoughts then extended the same principle to melodic variations. I’ve been playing these tunes for over forty years and am fairly saturated in the style. I am still gobsmacked at the ability of some people to pull complex variations out of tunes, that I never ‘knew’ were in there - such as Sorcha here (especially second tune) https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EHshhZjA5lk . In some cases you end up with a substantially different tune.

Once again, I suppose you can only work with what you know – but why do some people just find things that never seem to appear to others (including me)?

Is it raw talent (whatever that is), something in Irish water, or something they teach on Trad degree courses? Or is it something I ought to be able to learn???

Go to it!!!

Re: Unknown unknowns

As I’m primarily a fiddler, I’ll come at it from the melodic angle.

I tend to do a lot of melodic variations, and they generally come from two sources: (1) lifted from other musicians whose playing I admire, and (2) my own time spent living inside the tune, exploring all the possibilities and keeping what I like.

In either case, once I learn a “new” trick, I listen for other places to use it, in other tunes, perhaps modifying it to fit into other situations. In this way, even a fairly small variation can become a big idea, a concept that can be applied in many different ways. Sometimes I’ve borrowed that big idea from another musician, but sometimes it’s one that I’ve stumbled on myself.

For me, “immersion in the music” means more than simply listening to tunes and learning them. I’m always listening closely to how the players I admire interpret tunes and phrases, then work those bits into my own playing. The goal isn’t to play Jenny’s Welcome to Charlie or Winnie Hayes’ Jig just like Bobby Casey or Tommy Peoples or Martin Hayes or Philip Duffy or Liz Kane but rather to understand their variations and how those variations help to create a personal setting of the tune.

My own variations also come from one other place—out of the blue, playing in a state of flow. Sometimes my fingers and bow hand surprise me. Often this is in full flight during a particularly good session, fueled by a pint or a dram. I suspect this happens at all because I’m “at home” on the fingerboard and with a bow in my hand. It tends to happen more often on tunes I’ve known and played for 50 years, but sometimes it happens on a relatively unfamiliar tune, or one that hasn’t been dusted off in 20 years. I keep my ears open for these surprise detours or “mistakes.” If they suit the tune, I burn them into memory to tinker with again at home, to make them part of my repertoire.

Re: Unknown unknowns

I’m not a seasoned DADGAD player (mainly playing in Standard) but I recognise most of the shapes. Some of the shapes take a bit of getting used to but it’s important to realise that they are used for very good reasons, one of which is that playing the bottom two strings open on a D5 chord, like 000200 (or notes DADAAD) can sound a bit muddy so, for example 05x200 (with its four fret stretch) and the like is often used instead.

Sometimes you might want to pick out an F# in the bass before going to a G chord so you might use:
D/F# = 4x02xx to G = 5x04xx or 5x002x.
And, yes, you don’t need to play all the strings all the time. Often what you mute is just as important as what you play.

Re: Unknown unknowns

I don’t think it’s about showmanship in terms of accompaniment. It’s more about pleasing sounds, avoiding hand strain (no bar chords), economy of movement and maintaining (personal) interest.

Let’s face it, if you are playing in DADGAD you’re probably using a capo as fair amount of the time so playing a lot in D. If that’s the case it’s pretty boring if you just stick the same same shapes for each chord. Most players will (if they want to sound interesting and not die of boredom) eventually find lots of varying shapes and voicings (some adding “extra” notes outwith the basic chord) and routes from one chord to another with melodic or bass runs.

Re: Unknown unknowns

With regards to how to learn melodic variation, my process for developing the skills had a few steps:

First, I started with picking apart recordings and learning specific variations as if I was learning it as part of the tune. Then I would work on making sure I could play the base setting of the tune or the variation based on a whim without it derailing me. A helpful moment in that process was a class I took from John Carty at a camp about 20 years ago, where he taught a tune, and then also taught us 3 distinct variations to use on one single phrase of the tune. I learned a lot from that! I also worked a lot on smaller variations -- note inversions, double stops, different ornaments, tweaks to note timings, etc. Between all of those things, I started building a toolbox of things that I could call upon as the mood strikes.

There were two other things that really helped me in the process. Practicing tunes in non-standard keys, and playing tunes in different time signatures (most commonly playing a reel as a jig or vice versa). These are not things I would do in sessions (OK, maybe occasionally), just when I was practicing. What those two things did was help me develop my ability to have my brain in control of how the music was expressed (as opposed to just using “muscle memory” and playing the tune on autopilot…)

As my toolbox grew, and the more I was using variations, I started treating tunes more like stories, and less like a fixed string of notes. And similarly to how I might embellish a story as I was telling it, I would start to embellish tunes with melodic variations to tell the story slightly differently each time. At this point, it is not something that I necessarily go out of my way to try to do, it’s just something that happens in my expression of the tunes, and fairly often I come up with something that I think is really cool (and usually wish I could remember what I just did, but if it was cool enough, I’ll probably come up with it again…)

I hope that at least gives you some inspiration…

When they’re played every time… I find it helpful to embrace the fact that there are different settings of tunes, because there’s really no stopping it. And I find that using parts of different settings as variations is an easy way to build your toolbox…

Variation is most likely where the different settings come from in the first place. I know I have a couple tunes where what I consider to be the base setting of the tune is what I originally did as a variation on what I originally learned.

I should also note that big melodic variation is mostly a solo playing (or arranged setting) thing, and needs to be done judiciously in a group setting, like a session. It’s OK to have the most eloquent variation at the ready, but if it clashes at all with what everybody else is doing, there’s no place for it…

Re: Unknown unknowns

Ian, another thing to bear in mind is that, in order to stand out from the crowd, some performing and recording artists invent sizeable variations that may change a tune so much that their setting would clash with how the tune is more commonly played. That’s fair game when you’re soloing or playing with bandmates who are in on the changes.

Coming up with fresh variations that will work in a session is a different beast. Any change you introduce has to stay within the rails of the tune. I love that challenge—finding a sweet note or turn of phrase that jumps out as fresh without straying wide of the tune. Ideally, the variation enhances the tune in some way, draws out it’s true beauty.

Also bear in mind that when your mind’s blown by a particularly inventive, playful musician, chances are most of their variations are things they’ve played many times before. It sounds fresh and startling to us, but it’s likely old hat to them. We tend to hear other musicians’ playing against the baseline of our own understanding and playing of any given tune. The first time I heard Fergal Scahill throw in a chromatic bowed triplet (e.g., c^/d/d^ e2), it caught my ear, I sussed out what he was doing, and I tried it in a few tunes. In the end, I decided it sounds too jazzy for my tastes. And I’ve since noticed that he sprinkles that trick into many of the tunes he plays—chromatic bowed triplets are one of his signature bits. My hunch is that he borrowed them from hearing his brother and Martin Howley do them liberally on banjo and mandolin—they suit the overall sound of We Banjo 3.

I’m not sure if my own experience will be of help, but I will put it down anyway. To me there are various choices that influence what chord I will use.

The most basic form starts with strumming, usually this is backing and so a complete sound that matches the breadth and loudness between chords is a priority, with occasional excursions possible. This in effect is using the most strings possible of the guitar for any chord, so that there is bass presence available, so that lack of fineness in strumming doesn’t sound odd notes. Bar chords are optional, more where needed only, maximum open strings is for the depth and follow of the sound. Various chords can be played up the fretboard like this also

Synchopated strumming is on bar chords or tightly grouped fretted layout, with practice synchopated playing can also be achieved strumming over only a couple of strings, by muting all with a finger, or by muting with right hand.

Then with finger plucked accompaniment, if there is a steady bass rythm priority is to making sure those bass notes (usually two for any chord) are available, and the rest of the chord must fit stretch wise from that, choosing a nice balance of sound for the other notes. I don’t overdo full bar chords because unless you have a light stringed guitar they become tiring, especially near bridge, and because moving from bar to normal chord or playing tends to introduce a lag.

If it is not with a strong plucked bass rythm, then I go more for a nice balanced range of notes, often adapted to include embellishments. So chords are based on that and can be anywhere but tend towards lower half of fretboard, within reach of each other. This is still backing to my thinking though, but more musical, and choice of chord is quite unrestricted.

Then, if I am playing main melody clearly, plucked along with other strings or part strummed, my approach changes. Here I rely on my classical training and other experiencd to choose where on the fretboard I will play, what other notes to be combined to be played together, when and how to strum or introduce rhythm or bass. There aren’t any rules, being consistent and varied at the same time, the choice of chords are whatever achieve that and that are within reach of each other also, they are often not standard chords but choice of notes of a chord that happen to be near the melody and reachable, sometimes they are a chord pattern that most are familiar with, but often not.

Also to remember is that there are many forms of strumming, that goes from plectrum, through to thumb and all fingers, to flamenco or jazz style where individual finger strumming or circular motion is introduced along with choice of plucked bass, through to portuguese guitar style where single finger will pluck melody by flicking each way as well as strum when chosen, and so on. All of these can be adapted to different genres of music.

There is just as much room for being creative as anyone could wish for with all of that, but how is eventually up to anyone to discover for themselves, continuing from what is already known as well as learning new methods of playing - the possibilities, scales and chords, slowly become apparent to the point where a player feels confident in finding and combining them at choice.

If that is the direction of their playing.

Re: Unknown unknowns

Moe to the heart of Ian’s questions, let’s translate some of the variations in my jig examples into “concepts” that can be applied elsewhere.

|fdf fga| - I think of this as a little rocker pattern that can easily be varied by one note in more than one way. It’s essentially a notey substitute for f3, yes? So we can play it fef or fdf or fgf or faf and so on.

|AFE DFA| - A cousin of the rocker idea, the E here is a passing note (rather than playing AFD DFA). To my ear, passing notes help create a sense of fluidity and flow.

|ecA AB/B/c| and |dB/c/d fed| - The triplets (bowed or slurred) also act as passing notes, but you get more of them, which creates a sense of momentum up or down to the next note.

|cA^G A2=c| - more passing notes, but these are accidentals, which really jump out in a tune that’s otherwise clearly in one mode. I tend to use these sparingly, especially in sessions. Here the G sharp is jangly, while the c natural sounds sweet. You don’t have to use both.

|dB/c/d eag|fdc d2e| - the “eag” bit is similar to a step, but I’m very conscious of using this idea to lead up (or down) to the ending of a part, or the start of the next part, or any other important note. If you’ve been playing the phrase |dcd efg|fdc d2|, it’ll sound surprisingly fresh to play |dcd eag|fdc d2| instead, coming to the g from the opposite direction.

|cAB Adc| - I think of this as creating steps in the melody, and it can be highly effective to turn repeated notes (cAA A) into steps (CAB A) or to emphasize or invert the upward or downward scale direction of a phrase (CAA Adc|B…).

finally
|AB{d}B Bcd|AFD DFE|DCD EFG|FDC DAc|back to Part A - A small bit of doubling, going down an octave. Rather than playing a whole part down an octave, just a slice of it to come around the turn from one part to the next is a nice way to vary the sound without being overbearing.

Re: Unknown unknowns

Looking over the video there, what I see is quite standard chords played with a capo. His playing is more agile than it is octopus, and he concentrates on bass. The embelishments I hear and see are on bass notes. He plays using thumb to fret notes (again, bass orientated chords) and his hold of the guitar is a grip with thumb over neck to steady the left hand, this helps with agility (allows fingers to find destination) when a more relaxed overall posture is accepted. There are many very talented guitarists who use thumb over neck, and will also bring their hand around for more complex playing naturally.

What I don’t see there is much stretch. Compare this with classical guitar and there the thumb is behind the neck opposite to the fingers on the board. It is work to learn and seems stiff to start off with, in fact whole posture is based on facilitating that, with hand square on more or less to the fret board. There you have maximum stretch though, both across the board and along the board, the trade off being that the guitar is held relatively still for the left hand to find its way accurately. You can still play all kinds of music this way, it just doesn’t seem so relaxed to look at, but playing is as relaxed, unless of course you are wanting to move around a lot while playing. It adds a kind of poise to the actual playing also I think.

Hi Ian, I would add the observation that if you don’t cleave to that style of backing then keep looking for a player to emulate. When you find one you do cleave to, figure out how they get the sounds and rhythm they do, and what style and what ‘rules’ they are following.

I personally find Caoimhín’s playing very much worth studying, and with all respect to yhaal house, the progressions are anything but random-- every note serves the melodic line in some subtle way. No tritone substitutions needed or given!

To dive a bit deeper, he’s got a capo at fret 5 to accompany an A dorian tune and a more or less C major tune (with no F naturals in the melody). So the chord fingerings are those of E dorian and G. He is tuned to dropped D as CreadurMawnOrganig pointed out, but he is using a lot of partial chords behind a strong single note on Beats 1 and 5 typically. A few hammer ons. You can plot the contour of his backing by just listening to those notes which are often inversions of the implied chord. It’s a style that can easily be dialed back if you prefer the guitar to be inserting itself less in the fiddles’ conversation. But it’s a type that’s well suited to the way these tunes flow and it lets the melody and rhythm shine through always.

Have fun on your backing journey! I’m on the reverse journey-- trying to make a go of fiddle after backing tunes for umpteen years….

Reverend, glad you like my suggested meanders.

One way to “manage” the sheer number of possibilities is to vary only the bits that seem to want it. By that, I mean the repetitive bits. Think along the lines of the |E2 BE dEBE| snippet in Drowsy Maggie. Twice through Part A and you’ll play that snippet four times, three times through the tune and you’ve hit an even dozen iterations of that snippet. Yes, it’s a signature bit of the tune, but still it begs for variation.

Default:
E2 BE dEBE|E2 BE ADFA|
Variations:
|E2 BE dEBE|BedB ADFA|
|E~B3 d~E3|E~B3 ADFA|
|BE E/E/E dE E/E/E|BE E/E/E FADF| or just stay on the B: |BE E/E/E BE E/E/E|BE E/E/E ADFA|
|E2 B,E DEB,E|BEdB ADFA|
|E2 B~E3 BE|E/E/E Bd ADFA|

I’d be happy to sprinkle in two or three of those, not necessarily all five.

And it doesn’t mean you can’t vary other bits of the tune, but that snippet’s not a bad place to start.

Some players (even top tier musicians) tend to focus their major variations on just one part of a tune. So you can throw some bigger detours into the B Part, say, but leave the A Part (or vice versa) more or less stable. Or you might vary the A Part one time through the tune, then revert to the default A Part and vary the B Part the next time . So you don’t go completely off the rails.

All of this assumes that variations are what you’re after. Sometimes it’s bliss just to play the bliddy tune. :-)

Music is subjective to my view, always. Whether everyone appreciates a kind, style or level of music played or only the player enjoys creating a sound, makes no difference.

When social acceptance, approval or competition are added then we are talking of range of ability, because we are judging. A player might also judge themselves against another, or what they would like to play but not manage to. This judgement is also subjective but it is more tangible, because there can be forms of concensus, or a simple comparison between two versions is possible in terms of technique (but not content).

Limitation is what you cannot play, ability is what you are able to play.

Musical limitation is what cannot be played by a player and so does not exist because it is not played, musical ability is what the player is able to play, and so exists.

Physical limitation does not affect musical ability, a player is able to play what they are able to.

People play as they are able to, their ability might increase or decrease for any number of reasons.

The rest is expectations, wherever those come from and however they are handled, but no one is owed to be able to play at any level at all.

Re: Unknown unknowns

“I’m experienced with this music - and yet it still beggars me that there are people who can pull things out of a tune that I didn’t even hear in my mind’s ear.”

This brings up an interesting point. Similarly to David’s post about Michael Gorman, I don’t know how much of my variation I would say lives in my “mind’s ear”. Big melodic variations have two different forms for me. Some of them are things that I learned from a recording or came up with on my own during practice, that I have subsequently practiced. Like something I might have done for one of my recordings. But most of the variation I will play is not planned ahead, doesn’t exist as a fully formed thought before it is played, and is something that flows out of me as part of the emotion I am trying to convey in the tune, or a twist on the story of the tune.

When I first started having spontaneous variation appear in my playing, it freaked me out, and I worked to suppress it in some cases. But over time, I was able to nurture it as a skill, and learned to trust that whatever I am going to play is going to be OK. (That’s not to say that every variation I play is a masterpiece… at least once a session, I will do something that I have to laugh off with a “wtf was that supposed to be?” And some nights, the variations never really flow well…)

At the advice of Caoimhín O’Raghallaigh from a festival years ago, when I was first getting interested in playing more variations, I started doing practice where I would simply play a single tune that I know well repeatedly. Like for 15-20 minutes, or longer. And each time through, I would try to think of new variations. After a few times through the tune, I might have exhausted all the things I could think of. Caoimhín’s advice was to keep playing the tune even after I had done everything I could think of. After several times through that felt really boring, I would get into a bit of a trance, where playing the tune was effortless and in just letting it flow, new things would start popping up in my playing (alongside variations I had already done). Then I could start trying to guide the variations a bit more.

Another exercise I would give myself is to start varying transitions/pickup notes, and resolves of tunes. Often times, the pickup notes are entirely up to the player’s imagination to try to get one part to flow into another part. And I would start trying things that were different than how I had learned the tune. And for the resolves, you can often take the last phrase of a tune, which is most likely leading you to a big finish at the tonic. Since you know where you’re going (and the destination is the most important part of that phrase), then it’s not too difficult to “find a different way home”.

And finally, I will say that the most important exercise I do that helps with my variations is to compose my own tunes. I have always written tunes -- not because I believe I’m the next Paddy O’Brien, but because composing tunes is a really great way to learn more about how the music is put together, and to exercise my creativity. Are all the tunes I’ve composed great? Not by a long shot. Some of them are pretty good, though, and a number of them get played around here. When you’re playing melodic variations on the fly, you’re basically using your creativity and composing on the fly, which is pretty different than having something fully formed in your “mind’s ear”.

My hearing started to get a little dull at age 50, with the first signs being unable to hear speech in a noisy environment, and gradually got worse over the years. Still, if you look at the curve of deterioration on my successive audiograms, it’s steepest in the first 5 years, then the curve lessens for the remaining 15 years. A bit like the path that deteriorating eyesight takes.

Still on the positive side, my chops are pretty good, and technically outside of the “fiddling” range (broadly speaking), thanks to some heavy-duty classical training earlier on. I’ve not lost my passion for playing, so that’s a massive help too. I know some guys in my position who have just knocked it on the head completely, sadly.

As far as variation and improvisation goes, that’s pretty good too, although I need to maintain two versions (the original and my own) so that I don’t stray too far away from the melody and annoy my musical neighbours at sessions if I inadvertently play the variation!

I also now play viola, so I can play the higher notes accurately, whereas on fiddle I’d be struggling to hear them clearly (all notes are a 5th lower, of course).
