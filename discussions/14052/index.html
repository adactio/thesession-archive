Re: Hey Doc, it hurts when I go like this…

Nearly everybody goes through this at some time, and it can reappear at any stage of your growth as a player. So don’t let it get to you.

Here are some tips that have worked for me to get off to a better start:

1. Instead of forcing a tune out, just play whatever tune is already on the tips of your fingers, coming out on its own. This is how I warm up, whether playing at home, rehearsing for a gig, or starting off a session. For a lesson situation, ask your teacher if you can warm up first on whatever. Take a few times through whatever tunes pops up just to get in the musical frame of mind. Then, with the queue empty, the tune you *have* to play will usually come out easier.

2. Lilt through the tune--out loud or in your mind’s ear--before you play it. If you can’t get through it that way, you’ll likely hit some sketchy patches if you try to play it. But sometimes it helps just to get the first few bars going in your head before launching the bow. This is the old “Turn brain on before engaging mouth” bit of advice. It’s important to “hear” the tune this way at a fairly laidback tempo--maybe even slower than you intend to play it.

3. Even great players will noodle out the first bar or two of a tune, even on stage, just to get the brain warmed up and check their tuning, readiness, etc. It’s an accepted practice in most musical settings, even for performances, and certainly in less formal situations.

4. When you are ready to launch the tune, start slowly, lingering on each of the first 2 or 3 notes a bit before building up to the tempo you really want to play at. This gives you time to think ahead, and it sounds dramatic and “in control,” which impresses the hell out of anyone listening, even if you aren’t really in control. :o) It also serves as a brake to accidentally launching into a tune faster than you can comfortably play it.

In the long run, you will eventually find time to relax and look around in between all those notes buzzing past. Not sure how to describe this really, except that the tunes start going by in slow motion, with lots of “breathing space” between notes and between phrases, so you can collect your thoughts and let your imagination wander to what it wants to do next. You’ll find this extra time even when you’re playing at breakneck speeds, and everything becomes easier. Took me 20 years to get there, but I was a sporadic, mostly self-taught (deaf leading the deaf) learner, so most people pick this up more quickly.

Hope this helps.

Re: Hey Doc, it hurts when I go like this…

Ahhh…getting to the heart of the matter. Kevin Burke likes to relate a compliment he got from mandolin virtuoso Jethro Burns, who watched Kevin playing once and said, “He’s the epitome of physical relaxation and mental fierceness.”

There is a mental zone musicians (and athletes and surgeons, etc.) go into that is a blend of laser-like concentration and total relaxation. Like meditation, I’ve found that it’s worth consciously pursuing and “practicing,” if it’s possible to practice such a thing.

The trick is to *not try* to find the zone, but instead to simply let it happen. Most of us have had the experience of being in the zone, whether scoring a goal in sports or getting lost in music or painting or skiing or running, etc. In the zone, the body performs expertly, perfectly, without an apparent conscious thought getting in the way. And the second we notice that we’re in the zone, that awareness sends us crashing like Icarus back to earth and our usual lead-footed, wooden-handed adroitness.

So one way to learn how to find the zone and stay in it is to tease out what it feels like when you’re there. When you’ve had a zone experience, as soon as possible reflect on what it felt like--feel your breathing and muscles when in the zone, think back to how empty yet focused your mind was, try to recall how effortless everything felt. Focus on these feelings, and try to make them as visceral as possible so you can remember them the next time you pick up your fiddle and let your breathing, muslces, mind, etc. find the same states again.

This works when you’re actually in the zone too, it’s just harder to not let the awareness take you right out of the zone. So try it in tiny doses. Eventually, part of your brain will allow you to register the zone state without disrupting it, or at least keeping the disruption to a minimum, and then you can slip back into the zone. For me, it took repeated practice to do this. It’s not easy to learn to be aware of when you’re not mindfully aware.

I’ve noticed that it’s close to the same state as falling asleep. I’m a night owl and a bit of an insomniac, so now it’s easier for me to go into the musical zone than it is for me to fall asleep some nights. But the two seem related, and one has helped me with the other.

At some point, it becomes easier to be mentally “fierce” while in the zone--acutely focused on the tune unravelling before you--while letting go of the physical motions and techniques needed to do what your mind’s ear is creating next. As a musician, that’s an extremely pleasurable, enjoyable, powerful place to be. I highly recommend it. :o)

Re: Hey Doc, it hurts when I go like this…

Danny, I knew that book woul pique your interest--it is right up your alley, but bear in mind it’s written for a lay audience. Still gets at the essential bits of how our brains process music, based on the best available research to date.

In contrast to Bannerman’s cited problem (which seems to afflict all of us at some point), I’ve noticed that many people in the early stages of learning to play this music struggle more with simply recalling whole chunks of tunes. They may not hear the phrasing in ways that fit the structure of the tune, and so it’s harder to piece those chunks back together and string them into a continuous whole. I suspect this may be what Kennedy is grappling with.

On the plus side, bear in mind that this is usually a *recall* problem, not a *retention* problem. In other words, the tune is stored in your memory somewhere, you’re just having a hard time retrieving it in real time, with fluidity and grace.

And this also suggests ways to address the problem. In addition to the ideas I posted above, another idea is to spend some quality time sussing out the phrasing of a tune as you learn it and play it. The “call and answer” approach works well here--playing one phrase as a “call” and the next as the “answer.” I’ve also heard this described as playing both halves of a conversation. Think of each phrase having a distinctive voice or question-and-answer inflection.

If you’re having trouble hearing the phrasing (realizing that most phrasings are somewhat arbitrary--you can find lots of short phrases, or link these together into longer phrases, or take the holistic approach and hear entire halves of tunes as “phrases”), this is where I think it helps to work with your teacher/mentor/another more experienced player whose style you admire. It’ll be fairly easy for them to break a tune into the phrasings they hear, and to show you how they interpret the notes that hitch one phrase to the next. Eventually, you can learn to “visualize” a tune and its phrases to your own liking, and change the phrasing to keep it interesting.

Pay particular attention to those “hitching” notes--the ones that link one phrase to the next. Whenever a tune strikes me as “hard to recall,” I slow down and ferret out those linking notes--pick-up notes, bridges, etc.--and make sure I know precisely which notes they are and how they fit into the tune. It’s amazing how recalling one simple pick-up note can unleash the whole next part of a tune. ***Those singular notes are your best friends. Get to know them well.***

After a few years of such conscious effort, these skills become more or less reflexive, and all this happens on its own when you’re in the zone. Be patient yet persistent and you’ll get there.
