But that feels so unnatural. Anyone know any other tricks to get the wrist loose? (wise cracks welcome of course).

Re: Tips for the Fiddler on Breaking the wrist

This is one of those things that for me seems to fall into the category of “just do it” -- either you’re breaking from the wrist, or you’re not. The wall thing and the chair arm thing are all tricks to make you do something that one rationally should be able to do by simply concentrating on it -- but of course rational thinking only goes so far when human beings are involved, practically speaking.

Watch yourself in the mirror, play exceptionally slowly while concentrating on only bowing from the wrist, etc. The tricky part, of course, is to find a way to break from the wrist that still keeps the bow parallel to the bridge. For me, at least, it seems to require a very loose grip.

I worked on this all last summer, with some long distance help from Will. It made me crazy for a bit and totally trashed my playing for a while, but it was really good for me to get another batch of tricks to use.

Re: Tips for the Fiddler on Breaking the wrist

Just trying to keep your elbow against your side, with minimal movement will help you to loosen up your wrist. When I read this post I tried to do a video clip to show optimum wrist movement, but soon realised that playing with a stiff wrist looked almost identical to playing with a relaxed one, unless you really exaggerate the slackness (smooth snake-like motion) when changing bow direction. I refrained from doing this, because although it may look good, it serves no useful purpose, and encourages ‘lost motion’ when playing up to speed. A popular misconception, the ‘snake-wrist’.

The amount of wrist movement needed is really quite small - just enough to be able to draw the bow from heel to tip, keeping it parallel with the bridge. Any more than that amount of movement is just wasted energy - bad news for playing up to speed. Also, don’t forget the slight amount of movement the bow makes within your hold of thumb + 2nd+3rd fingers.

Re: Tips for the Fiddler on Breaking the wrist

Salt, I’ll throw some ideas and exercises at you, but from my own experience trying to loosen up my wrist, I’d say the first thing you have to realize is that it’s an ongoing and probably endless process. So be patient, and let yourself just *be* at certain stages along the way so that you can really absorb the lessons of each stage before moving on. And realize that how we think about and visualize the movements we make helps to shape those movements.

Right. One way to get the wrist flexing is to do long, slow bow strokes and feel yourself leading with the wrist. Specificially, you want to start any up bow imagining that the top of the thumb-side of your wrist is leading the way. And when you start a down bow, you lead with the spot just off your palm, where the base of the thumb meets the wrist. (In tech terms, you lead up or down with the scaphoid bone.) Using your wrist this way allows the bow to keep traveling while your hand starts to change direction. Another way to think about this feeling is that, on an up bow, your fingers continue to “pull” the bow up even after your wrist has started to head back down. And on a down bow, your fingers continue to “push” the bow down even as your wrist starts to head back up.

Which gets us to an even more specific exercise that focuses on the fingers. Place the hair on a string somewhere between the middle and tip of the bow. Start with your wrist hanging from your arm like the head of a goose, and as relaxed as possible. Now very slowly do an up bow, using only your fingers to “pull” the bow upward. Your index and middle fingers and thumb should bend at the knuckles. I once heard a classical teacher call this “making mountains” because your knuckles buckle up like a mountain range. You’ll know you’ve reached the end of the up bow when your fingers can’t bend anymore (don’t force it--you want them to stay relaxed). Then start the down bow by gradually straightening your fingers and thumb. You reach the end of the down bow when your fingers are as straight as they can go (without forcing it). So you end up doing up and down bows that are only about 2 to 3 inches long, and your forearm never moves--it’s all in the fingers and wrist.

Finally, you can do the same sort of thing but this time just the index finger and thumb move the stick, and each bow stroke is only about half an inch long, slowly back and forth. Think about relaxing your wrist and hand, using as little “grip” as possible to support the stick, and working toward making the movements as fluid and smooth as possible. What you’re aiming for is a very fine coordination of the index and thumb, where each joint folds and unfolds in a smooth, steady sequence from kunckle to tip and back again, like a flag rippling in a light breeze.

What helped me the most was concentrating on the smallest movements of index and thumb--to get them to work well, my wrist had to be loose and flexible, so that was a natural byproduct. Brian Conway talks about this as playing “close to the stick,” where the movements that control the stick come from the parts of your fingers that are closest to the stick--one spot on the side of the pad of your thumb, and the spot where your index finger lays acorss the stick.

But I still go back to long slow bows tip to frog now and then, still looking to improve control and relaxation. I’m also a big fan of repeating “effortless” as a mantra, as a way to relax and not focus on getting it “right” or “perfect.”

Hope this helps.

Re: Tips for the Fiddler on Breaking the wrist

Great suggestings here. Two other tips I was taught and pass on to beginners.

First try try holding a roll of TP between your bow arm and chest as you practice bowing from frog to tip. This will force you to break your wrist.

Another exercise, start at the frog with the hair of the bow turned so you’re staring straight at it. The wood of the bow should almost be touching the strings. As you down bow , rotate your wrist so that by the time you reach the tip, you are looking straight at the wood which should again be almost touching the strings. Concentrate on keeping the bow in the sweet spot between the fingerboard and the bridge. Reverse the rotation on the up bow.

Try both of these techniques with scales, arpeggios or just open strings. Do this several minutes each day when you practice. You should soon get the feel of it and it will translate into your playing naturally.

Re: Tips for the Fiddler on Breaking the wrist

“A popular misconception, the ‘snake-wrist’.”

But it looks so spiffy and dramatic on stage.

I’ve got a few nonmusician friends who like to tape televised musical performances they think I might like, to show to me when I come over. They all started out with something along the lines of “You’ve got to see the way this guy moves around and all the energy he puts out!” Very impressed by the stage theatrics of the thing.

But my critical comments have pretty much gotten them trained properly; and now they say things like “You’ve got to see the way this guy hardly moves and how little energy he puts out!”

I think what made the lightbulb really go on for me, which fortunately happened in my youth, was reading a bit in a guitar book about how amazed the author was by the delicacy, effortlessness and lack of obvious motion in Clarence White’s flatpicking when he was backstage warming up (and mentioning as an aside that Clarence cut his own picks from thick Plexiglass to reduce the motion needed to move from one string to the next).

What really rubbed it in was getting to sit on Earl Hines’ right hand for an evening (metaphorically speaking). There was so little motion of his fingers that you could hardly see it at all, even just a few feet away from them, but all this music just poured out of them.

By the time I got to do the same with Johnny Cunningham I was already enlightened enough to truely appreciate the effortless grace and minimization of motion that resulted in tunes flowing out at hypersonic speed while Johnny himself seemed in a state of near somnolence (it was near the end of a long weekend of performing and he just wanted to sit down and play for awhile for his own enjoyment for a change. Isn’t it funny how musicians relax after playing by playing some more?).

But I think most people, including many musicians, will continue to be impressed by (and seek to emulate) the dramatic movement, instead of real technical skill and proficiency, and thus be unable to even recognize it when they see it.

Maybe I should start carrying around a prop fiddle to bust up or something.

“Anyway. the exaggerated rotation is an exercise only. . .”

I’ve learned from recent experience that when posting about playing I have to put <this is an exercise only> tags (metaphorically speaking) around things that are only exercises and not what you seek to emulate in actual playing, especially since some of my suggested exercises can be a bit unconventional if I feel that’s what’s needed to do the trick ,which, more often than not, is because I feel the “blockage” is psychological in nature, rather than truely physical. Where the mind leads the body will try to follow, even if the body can feel the “wrongness” of it. Unconventional and/or exaggerated actions can help tune the mind in to where it should be leading, or get it to just plain get the hell out of the way like it should.

It also helps people to understand that their body knows a thing or two about what’s going on and that they should pay attention to what it’s telling them. It seems to me that simply learning to “feel what one feels” is the most overlooked skill in musicicanship ( and any number of other physical activities).

Of course I also recently responded to the suggestion that you should “ask your body” with the retort, “I asked my body, and it told me that more chocolate and Jack Daniels is the answer to everything,” so there are obvious limitations to that strategy.

Sometimes the mind needs to take control. :)

Re: Tips for the Fiddler on Breaking the wrist

I often close my eyes when I’m learning a tune because it’s somehow easier for me to follow along if I’m not distracted by watching the other player. I even did this some when Brian Conway once taught three of us a tune and wanted us to imitate his exact bowing. You don’t have to see the bow to know where the single strokes and slurs are. So, in support of what Michael is getting at (in his usual inimitable way), yes, putting a visual screen between teacher and student, though it may sound counterproductive, is not always a bad idea. I’ve had students who were so dependent on sight reading the dots that the only way I could help them with ear training was to make them close their eyes so they had no visual cues.

In noisy situations (a class, say), however, it can help to peek at the teacher’s fingers and bow hand for clues.

And of course I’ve learned a fair amount from *watching* people play. There’s nothing wrong with using visualization and mirroring to acquire and refine a largely aural skill. Seeing can be an adjunct, an asset that complements hearing. They don’t need to be mutually exclusive, even if sometimes it does help to shut one out to concentrate more fully on the other.

Re: Tips for the Fiddler on Breaking the wrist

I’d also point out that much of the advice given here had nothing to do with how the bow hand *looks* but everything to do with how it *feels.* I’m guessing most experienced players associate how the music sounds with how it feels to play it--in very specific, detailed ways. We learn to be aware on a deep level of the movements and positions of our muscles and joints and skin--it’s our bodies’ feedback loop. We know that it feels “like this” when we make a sound “like that.” So I could pick up a fiddle without strings and accurately finger the tunes, even though no sound would corroborate my intonation. And I can play “air fiddle” with no bow or fiddle at all, and my right arm goes to the right angles for playing on the E string, then the A, then the D, and so on.

In short, paying attention to how our body feels when we play is what allows us to learn how to replicate the sounds we want and avoid the squawks and stutters.
