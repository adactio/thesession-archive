The Little Things.

I just rented a violin for my other younger cousin(11-year-old), and she had her first violin lesson this week. The teacher went over with me what she showed her in the lesson, so i’m working with her through the week, same as her sister that I mentioned in my last post. As she’s setting up, she goes… “do you have one of those silencers?…”, a mute of course ーmine being a heavy mute. So I show her mine and demonstrate the difference in the sound. And, you know… the mute was such a big deal for me personally. It was just one of those little things that ended up affecting things in a big way. I mean, it’s just a little hunk of metal that weighs just a few ounces. And yet, it made all of the difference in my own progress with the instrument.

When people ask me what it was that really helped me get along with the fiddle, I always tell them about the mute. “The mute means that I can practice wherever and whenever I want to.”. And being able to practice “whenever” or “wherever” for me is a priority of the highest order. Maybe it’s my piano background and having long periods in my life where I couldn’t play or practice because there just wasn’t an instrument around. Before all of the mobile apps and computer programs, cheap electric keyboards, or knowing that nursing homes and rehabilitation facilities almost always have a piano; there would be times when I never even saw the face of a keyboard.

I know, I may be being a bit dramatic and romantic about it, but it’s become so endearing to me. I remember the shock and disappointment when I realized I had lost my first mute. And I remember the satisfaction I felt when I finally replaced it. So, I’m wondering if any of you have had experiences with something seemingly small or insignificant that changed your music for you.

Re: The Little Things.

Perhaps this does not qualify as a *little* thing but it was certainly a game changer. When I was 17, my mother came home with a mandolin she had bought on a whim in a junk shop. She was not in the habit of spontaneously buying random objects, but she revealed that she had always had a secret desire to play the mandolin. Being priced at £15 (the year was 1991), it was, unsurprisingly, not in playing order and sat untouched in a corner for over a year. Then I decided it was time to do something about it…

I boughr a new set of strings and, after putting them on, discovered that the machine heads were shot. I managed to find a replacement set in a small shop off Oxford St., London (where I learned the term ‘machine head’, after establishing that it was
not an electronic tuner I wanted), and fitted them at home.

When she bought the mandolin, my mother had been given the vital piece of information that it was tuned the same as a violin. So I consulted the Oxford Companion to Music’s entry for ‘violin’ and tuned it to the piano. The fretting and picking were not too much of a challenge for me, as I already played some guitar. After tinkering around for a while and figuring out some basic chords, I soon realised, because of the strings all being the same interval apart, that scales were constructed from repeating finger patterns. It was a short leap from there to my first tune (I don’t remember what it was), and the rest, as they say, is history… The mandolin was to become my main instrument, and continued to be so for over a decade, until it was insidiously sidelined by the fiddle.

A little thing by size, but not quite a little “thing”. I had trouble hearing myself at sessions and basically couldn’t keep up with my right hand side (accordion) . It was just too heavy to keep up the volume for multiple sets.

Re: The Little Things.

Jerone, your “little things” theme really strikes home with me. Forty or more years back, before tossing in the daily grind to seek fame and fortune in the fabulously and notoriously wealthy world of flute making (er, what?), I worked in Community Radio here in Australia. (Known as Public Radio in the US.) Mostly as the Engineer, but sometimes in other roles too. It was a great experience, the benefits of which I am still reaping.

When you are “the presenter” in radio, you obviously have to keep an ear on what’s going out. Otherwise embarrassing things happen. Like you have an important studio guest, but you forget to “bring up” their microphone, so it sounds like you’re talking to yourself. When you “open” a microphone, the monitor speakers in the studio are automatically muted, so you have to put on headphones to keep track of what’s going out. But, hey, it’s a 2 hour time slot, who wants to be enclosed in “cans” (as we called them) for two hours! Especially if you have studio guests, so you want to be able to interface with them about the next topic.
So, it’s cans on, cans off, cans on, etc. And where do you put the cans when not in use? They clutter up the workspace horribly, and typically choose to fall onto the floor just as the current track is ending, leaving you grovelling on the floor, trying to open your microphone, your headset over one ear but not the other, and your studio guests wondering why they bothered to come in at all.

After an on-air shift where that issue became really clear to me, I resolved to do something about it, and spent quite a bit of time sitting in the studio, going through the motions, looking for the ideal solution of where to hang the headphones when not in use. And finally cracked it, made up a suitable clip and screwed it in place. And stuck a note on the desk, drawing presenters’ attention to it.

Well, the week after was extraordinary. I got so much feedback, all positive. It was a “long-felt-want”. And it was fun to watch how quickly the presenters got used to using it automatically. Which was a bit relieving, as I had been wondering if I was being a bit obsessive about it! But clearly no.

And the moral of the story? You can spend a thousand dollars or more on a new studio CD player, or some other piece of high-end studio equipment, and you might get back a few positive comments. But come up with a better headphone clip, and you instantly take on hero status! Little things matter.

Re: The Little Things.

The simple realisation, somewhere along the line, that I would never be able to play like somebody else, and accepting that.

For example, at the level I am at, I can learn a tune from a recording in a couple of days and be playing along to that recording near perfectly at the end, knowing how to emulate every little detail. However when I then play alone it doesn’t “sound” like the recorded version.

The same goes for when I hear professional bands play covers to the minutia. I might here music from the entrance of some performance and have to listen closely for a moment to figure if it is an original recording being played or live cover. Even when live down to a detail, there is always “something” missing somehow.

So I learn a tune as close as possible to a favourite version, and then just start playing it as I like to, as I feel, and I find that is the most rewarding. “Probably not as good” as the original, but then who is to judge and I know that my own playing is at least acceptable.

Re: The Little Things.

One of my musical “little things” was a cylinder of silver tubing, about 5mm long. After playing whistle for some years, I thought to go on to flute, so bought a metal one from the local music shop. Hmmm, I thought, looking at the complexity of the instrument, I’d better get a few lessons.

By the time I organised lessons and showed up for my first, I’d actually worked out the fingering and how to blow it, and could play simple tunes. But the teacher was appalled, I was so flat. She showed me how to direct my air jet upwards, across the embouchure hole, instead of downwards, as I had instinctively found gave the tone I wanted. The new tone was awful, but I persevered, in the hope that it was an important learning experience. It didn’t get significantly better, and the teacher wasn’t going to let me get away with playing flat.

So, I took to the end of the head with a fine hacksaw, sawing off about 5mm. Now I could use my favoured embouchure, play in correct pitch, and still have a mm or two spare on the slide if needed. Win, win, win.

The teacher was stunned the following lesson, and said “you must have really been putting the work in”. I nodded and smiled. I don’t remember if I ever showed her my little piece of head tube.

A few years later, on the other side of the world and totally unbeknownst to me, an Albert Cooper was working at Rudall Carte, and struggling to understand why so many really good players complained about the flutes he was making for them. So he started experimenting, came up with the Cooper Scale, and the rest is history.

Re: The Little Things.

Heh heh. Yeah, it’s a reminder that back in my day, say late sixties, early seventies, flute making had descended into a production line corporate nightmare. The factories were pumping out instruments that hadn’t changed much - certainly hadn’t changed enough - since the old high-pitch days. And, if other players at the time were like me, we were inclined to blame ourselves for the shortcomings. Why couldn’t I play my flute up to pitch when my teacher could? And why doesn’t my flute play an accurate scale? It’s clearly my fault!

Fortunately for me, I was capable of reconciling my apparent shortcomings and knew enough physics to know what to do about it. You wonder how many might have just given up in disgust and taken up lawn bowls instead.

But well done, Albert Cooper. He wasn’t a well educated man, but he must have had an enquiring mind. And soon worked out that the customers were right and that his employers were wrong! Woah, dangerous situation! And worked out the solutions and changed modern flute history. That’s enough to be proud of!

Another takeaway for me (“another little thing”) was to learn that the embouchure approach favoured by (or was it forced upon?) teachers at the time was not the embouchure approach that made the sound I wanted to make. So it prepared me to go looking for what would make that sound. And that eventually lead to my article: http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Getting_the_hard_dark_tone.htm

Nothing much there perhaps for those who have grown up immersed in the world of Irish flute playing, but I get a lot of positive feedback from those like me living on the fringe edges of the diaspora.

Re: The Little Things.

My “little thing”: quality synthetic materials. My fidil and feadóg are carbon fibre and my bow is carbon fibre with Zarelon hair.

There are no woods, varnish, hairs, or metals to maintain or worry about, aside from the steel strings that are meant to be replaced anyway. I take care of things just as well, but not having to think about humidity, rehairs, and the like are not a concern and those kinds of little things add up to a big thing eventually.

This is a personal thing: I am not a performer for others, so the benefits of being weather resistant and stronger are not felt by me. Zarelon also does not break with aggressive playing either.
