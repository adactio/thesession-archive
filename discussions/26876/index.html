Re: Would you say this is too fast?

@ ⒹⒿⒻ…
Maybe this will help:
A phrase in grammar is (more or less) a group of words that conveys meaning, but does not make a whole sentence, for example, ‘the hair of the dog’.
In music the principle is the same: a phrase is a small group of notes that seems to have a natural beginning and end, for example (from The Kesh): D|GGG G
It is up to you to decide where the ‘joins’ are, how you connect the phrases, how big or small they are, and how much emphasis to give them, and the process is by feel rather than analysis, just as the stresses on words come naturally in speech. Think of a young child reading from a book, and you have an example of poor phrasing.
If someone says to you, ‘How does that first bit go?’ what you show him will normally be a phrase. There are places where you just wouldn’t stop, because it would sound wrong, and places where you feel as though you have ‘reached the end of the bit’.
How you shape the phrases can have a huge affect. Think of a smiling young woman in a restaurant patting her stomach and saying ‘I’ve had enough,’ and compare it with the same words in the mouth of a battered wife grabbing the nearest frying-pan.
Where you divide the phrases also changes the character of the tune:
‘Women,’ say men, ‘are useless.’
Women say, ‘Men are useless.’
The reason midi files sound so dead is mostly because they have no phrasing. The notes come out in succession, all correct and in order, but not relating to each other, all the same volume or mechanically stressing the same beat in each bar. It is by varying the volume and/or varying ornamentation, by changing the quality of the sound, by holding a pause or note that the phrases are defined. It can be quite a subtle variation, like the speeding up of a bow or a squeeze of the bellows, and it can be a dramatic pause or musical flourish.
However you phrase a tune, keep it more or less consistent, otherwise you sound like a bad actor, but feel free to explore. Select your favourite musician, and listen carefully for the little changes in volume, pauses, silences, stresses and flourishes that define the phrasing. But remember that’s his version: yours might be different. Phrasing comes from inside -- how you feel the tune -- rather than from careful planning.

Re: Would you say this is too fast?

If it feels (sounds) rushed, then it is, no matter what the speed. When someone who is in control of the tune plays fast, you don’t notice that it’s fast. You might think it sounds like 100 on the met, and then you find out it’s 118 or so.

One other thing about phrasing. I once read that phrases are like questions and answers. So you hear the first phrase of a tune, say over two measures, and consider that the question. Then comes the next phrase, also two measures, and that’s the answer.

This is all relative, too, so the first two phrases might together be considered a larger question, and the next two the larger answer. And this goes on up to A parts and B parts.

I just find it a nice way to interpret what’s going on in the tune.

↳ Posted by Jmbu on February 24th, 2011.

Re: Would you say this is too fast?

I think this is yet another musical paradox.

Beginner musicians don’t get the question/answer structure, and play reels “flat”

Gradually, you come to understand how the music is structured, play the phrases as logically “written” (as Jmbu suggests), add some of the swing and lift that characterizes irish music. But it’s sort of “mechanical”.

Hopefully for all of us, once we reach this stage, we learn to “unlearn” the regularities we put in our music. Or at least I hope it will happen to me some time soon :/
