Re: guitar accompaniment chord progressions for most common keys

Short answer: there aren’t any.

You need to suss out the chords for each tune, it’s monumental at first but like any other thing gets slightly easier with each attempt, it will become second nature after a while.

I found it helps to be able to play the tune, if you know some theory it’s helpful, but if you play the tune ‘chord shapes’ that show up in the melody will give you hints as to which chords go where.

Pick a tune, try to learn it, then get feedback from someone who’s been playing the music for a long time. Repeat.

Re: guitar accompaniment chord progressions for most common keys

There isn’t really an answer to this. You should place yourself next to a good session player and listen to what they do and ask them some advice. Most would help - some won’t. You cannot really get this stuff off paper very easily, but you will get away with 3 chord trick stuff (played with great sensitivity and quietly until you are considered as a support for the other players) for a lot of the more standard sessions fare. You MUST be able to hear where you put either i, iv or v obviously. Some tunes in the Irish tradition oscillate between key chord and the chord a tone down (bass player speak sorry). For instance Eminor and Dmajor. The Old Grey Cat (strictly speaking from the Scottish trad , but hey!) does this - some players would then go down to a Cmajor chord back to Dmajor then Eminor.

In most sessions, the common keys are Gmaj, Dmaj, Amaj, Emin, Amin, Bmin, although your ears have to be open to tunes in different keys. Guitarists who think they know the tune and the chords and therefore stop really listening to the other musicians can be a pain to play with, so this is really important. Best to learn in a session from a well respected session accompanist rather than from a book. Use your ears and decide who is good and contributes to the session and emulate them.

Re: guitar accompaniment chord progressions for most common keys

As has been said above, there aren’t really progressions that get used over and over again, like blues or rhythm changes in jazz. Be aware that the harmonies often won’t move the way you expect them to if you’re used to rock/pop/‘folk’. Especially watch for that I-bVII-I (i-bVII-i) type movement that’s so common. Also be aware that changes of chord happen in odd places in this music, not always only on the first or third beat like you might be used to. Also (there’s gonna be a lot of also’s if you do this long enough) know that there won’t be a specific required progression for a given tune like there would be in other styles, but rather there will often be several different chords or progressions possible. This is why often sessions will encourage only one chording instrument at a time, to avoid risk of clashing. I like the above suggestions that you learn some of the tunes; you can even learn them directly on the guitar if all you want is to understand them better and you aren’t holding out hope of being heard in a session. Have fun!

“Identify the key of the tune, and simply play that chord, if you can hear if it’s major or minor, add the third, if not, the root and 5th should suffice.. works for me.”

This may be partly tongue in cheek, but it’s good advice.

In general, please don’t try to play every chord you hear. Keep changes to a minimum, and sense where the “important” ones are and listen for those at first. Also, you don’t always have to change an entire chord -- sometimes moving a note or two is better. My feeling is these tunes want to flow, not shift all over the place. So the more smoothly you can support the tune, the better … including not playing at all if you sense that’s the right thing to do.

Also, be aware of dynamics -- be very sensitive to the melody players so if they quiet down, you quiet down.

Re: guitar accompaniment chord progressions for most common keys

I’ll have a shot at this. This approach is rooted in me starting out accompanying trads with open tunings, mainly DADGAD; but the idea can be transferred to Drop D and Standard quite easily. Realise that I’m not talking purely about ITM here - I used to accompany a broad range of world music, from Arabic to Scandinavian to German to Breton to self-written based on all those influences. (Jack of all trades, master of none ;)) But it’s given me a pretty broad idea of how to go about this.

Here’s the idea (assuming one knows nothing at all about the key or changes): say you know/hear the base key is D (forget about minor/major/other modes for now), - you groove to that chord with as many roots and fifths as you like. Then you start exploring which other notes work with the tune while sticking to that chord. For example you can start out by seeing whether you want a sharp or flat 7 (ie. 1 fret or 2 frets down from the D - C# or C respectively). Next you pull the same trick with the 3rd (3 frets up for minor, 4 for major - F or F# respectively). You mix those notes in with a droning D chord, going 2 frets down from the root for a single, grooved-up beat within the bar, so you’re not actually changing the chord you’re playing (technically, you’d be playing a D5b7 for a few strums, for example). With those two notes figured out you’re getting a pretty good idea of the key you’re in.

From there you can start expanding your chord range. Say you’ve figured out the tune is in Dm, broadly. Once you know the target notes in certain parts of the tune, you can aim for chords within the key that feature those notes. There’s 3 easy choices here (because, in normal 1-3-5 chords, your target note can be any of those three). Imagine the target note you’re going for is a C. Here the easy options are C major, A minor and F major (C is the root of C major, the third of A minor, and the fifth of F major); in my experience those three chords will usually work over that specific note if you got the key right. Once you get a hang of it you can substitute chords on the spot; for example, you could play the entire 2nd A-part of a tune in D major with a B minor chord instead.

Then there’s certain tricks that usually work once you know the key. In minor keys, a common trick involves the chords based on the 6th and 7th notes of the key. If you’re in Dm, try playing that for 2 bars, then Bb for 1 bar, then C for 1 bar. Another option is 1 bar of Dm, 1 bar of C, 1 bar of Bb, 1 bar of C.
Of course, these are dependant on the tune, but once you’ve gotten an idea of where the melody goes it’s quite easy to insert one of these.
(At the risk of moving too fast: for extra drama you can substitute every 2nd or 4th C for an Am. Alternately, you can play an F instead of the C, then drop down the bass a single fret to E after half a bar, landing on D again for the first bar of the next cycle. Note how this ties in with the substitutions I mentioned in the paragraph above?).

In major, a common trick is that of the I-IV-V, mentioned in an earlier post. For the sake of arguing, let’s say you’re playing a tune in F major (not very common I think, but bear with me). The IV and the V would be.. Bb and C. You can try out the above tricks in what is called the ‘relative major key’ of Dm - starting on F and moving to the other two chords. If you’re playing in D major, you’d have to figure out what the IV and V are in that key, of course (hint: it’s G and A).

The next step would be to incorporate snippets of the melody into the bits where you move from one chord to the next, but that’s a whole different story.

So, that’s my take on the subject. A lot to chew on, perhaps. I’ll be the first to admit I’m no expert, but this is how I started out, and it’s never let me down.

@ Jeff - G is a great example of a key that works relatively tonally in Irish. What I was trying to get to was that in some other keys, esp. E ‘minor’ or A, the hierarchy you delineated won’t work as well. Modes, somebody above said (correctly). The flat seven chord (e.g. D in E, or G in A) has an importance in this music that it really doesn’t have much elsewhere, which I’ve seen confuse many accompanists.

One of the things that I learned early on, and still greatly enjoy about this music is that you have to develop such an intimate feel for each tune, and it goes way beyond merely knowing the notes. It came as a surprise to me about three years ago when I discovered how hard it was for me to back myself up on guitar. I’d just assumed I’d have no trouble with it, yet when I tried even two chord accompaniments (by recording) it was stuffing up my fiddling. The thing is, I believe, that in ITM, knowing the ‘right’ chords is mostly just not enough (just as merely knowing the notes is not enough to play the melody right). By itself that knowledge won’t get you there. You need just as much of an intimate feel for each individual tune as does the melody player, and for me, despite my 50 years of guitar playing, I estimate that this would take me about two-to-three years of further commitment to my guitar. That’s why I stick to my fiddle;- it’s easier. I have a lot of respect for good guitar backers in ITM, but I’m serious when I say that anyone who wants to do it really should be committed (along with the bodhran players).

Irish Minor tunes are primarily Drian, then maybe a fifth of them are Natural minor and theres no harmonic minor and very very very few if any in the melodic minor. Sometimes D# IS used in E minor but only as an accidental, chromatic or varation or whatever you want to call it.

Thus in Irish music in Minor its mostly the raised 6th and flat 7th that are used.
Otherwise there is either no 6th or a flattened 6th and the flat 7th still applies.
If you’re talking about anything else you’re talking through your hat.

The major Vchord in minor is only ever used rarely as a variation on the minor V and usually sparingly only, in the instance that you either want a tune to sound old or else sound slightly cheesy or flashy.
