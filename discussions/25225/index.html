I think it can be identified, loosely defined and explained.

It started out as a way of referring to a certain group of sounds which give Irish and related music its character. Bent notes and cuts, and similar. They come to have an emotional effect, and the name for the sound starts to be applied to that emotional effect.

Some people seem to find it difficult to pick up nuances like this, I suppose that’s only natural, it’s a complex skill like learning a language, or acting, and people vary in their ability to do that.

Re: Rhythm and Nyaa.

Maybe this will help:

In both language and music, we use inflections to create meaning. In instrumental music, this “meaning” is abstract rather than literal. E.g., going up in pitch at the end of a phrase does not imply that a question has been asked, as such a rise might in some languages. So any correlation between the musical and linguistic inflections of a given culture must be tenuous at best--music is *not* really a language, and the rules of communication through it are different.

Yet both are (predominantly) aural. So some overlap, however subtle, should not come as a surprise. And a culture’s music does indeed rely on conventions--a “lexicon” and “grammar” of sorts--to build it’s tunes. Otherwise we would not be able to distinguish Irish tunes from French ones, etc.

What matters most when trying to play an instrumental music with authenticity is your understanding of that music’s conventions, regardless of any possible linguistic (or other cultural) influences.

As to *why* the language of a culture might affect the music of that culture (in terms of both composition, but especially the playing of it), perhaps it is something about the thought processes of the speakers/players. When we *think*, we generally *think* in terms of our language, our ideation is in the language that we know. As each language has meter, perhaps there is an unconscious, or perhaps in some cases a conscious, application of that linguistic rhythm to the way in which those who can do so, compose music, and in the way in which it is played. The “rhythm” of languages can vary vastly, and what one language speaker might “hear” as rhythm, may not be “heard” as such by a speaker from another language, and hence some differences in rhythmic styles could result, simply by virtue of the rhythmic definitions which a player has inherited from their native language.
