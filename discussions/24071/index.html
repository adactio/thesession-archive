Re: A little music philosophy for contemplation….

If by language we mean something with syntax, there’s little to suggest that other species use language. I’ve seen interesting reports on certain species of monkeys which seem to have the most rudimentary syntax - inverting the order of two signals alters the meaning of an alert call - but nothing that suggests negation (a dog can say “woof”, but it can’t say “not woof”) or wh-question formation (a cat can perhaps meow in a questioning manner - this is anthropomorphisation - but it can’t meow “which mouse would you like to eat”) or relative clauses (a bird will “sing” to mark its territory, but it can’t sing “the territory that belongs to me”) or any other actual language.
So no, language is not (to the best of my knowledge) used by any other species, except if we make the courteous inclusion of certain monkeys which can combine some signs.

Re: A little music philosophy for contemplation….

“…. abandon immediately that music is a species of language,-- which is not true,-- and to subsitute for that the converse idea that language is a species of music”

Possibly a nice poetic conceit, but what would this mean? Certainly the direct mapping of music onto “language” is only vaguely helpful - there’s no semantics, for one thing, and no syntax for another, Bernstein’s charming lectures notwithstanding.
However, there’s plenty to suggest that music is learned in the same way language is, using similar mechanisms - that is, not “learned” but “acquired” by a process more guided by internal switches than by well-meaning teachers.
This would lead to the speculation that music is an offshoot of language - pitch and rhythm are the first things acquired by a child, in the womb, as it is developing its linguistic faculties, so it seems fair to suppose that we’ve repurposed those elements to a more purely artistic purpose. But neither is “a sort of” the other, in any serious sense.

I don’t have class any more. I’m only half here right now. The other half of me is currently reading “The development of institutional care for ‘idiots and imbeciles’ in Scotland.” Luckily I don’t have to take social studies classes any more. Such is the postgrad life.

The whole idea of the “paradigm shift” came out of a book written in the 1960s, which I cited above. Thomas Kuhn basically suggested an alternative account to the positivist narrative of scientific knowledge. The positivist description is that scientific knowledge always moves in a forward direction, towards “truth,” towards “objectivity.” Kuhn argued that you have these paradigms, theories or laws describing the natural world, and one or more may be dominant at any given time. But due to factors, many of which may be social, political, or institutional (the latter really are just different takes on social), these paradigms can shift and you eventually get a new dominant paradigm.

Then after Kuhn’s revelatory book, everyone and their mother started applying the concept of the paradigm shift to everything else on the planet. No problem with that. I do it all the time. It’s a handy intellectual tool for describing how knowledge and conceptual frameworks change over time.

Of course you can look backwards, at the history of anything, you can observe these paradigm shifts. Any culture would have an intellectual framework for how it understood a given thing and social and environmental factors would eventually bring about a change in how that thing is understood.

BUT, and what I meant when I said there were no paradigms to shift, is that you can’t really say that this guy in the mid 19th century was arguing for a “paradigm shift” when the concept just didn’t exist. More importantly, he wasn’t really attacking an existing paradigm, insofar as I can tell. Nowhere have I read, in the psychological literature around the time he was writing at any rate, anyone arguing, “Music is a species of language.” Someone may well have have said it, but if you’re being all Kuhnian about it, you can’t say that it was a dominant paradigm.

"Perhaps the most effectual step a man can take in ridding himself of the clouds which darken most speculations upon these matters is to
abandon immediately the idea that music is a species of language, — which is not true, — and to substitute for that the converse idea that language is a species of music.
A language is a set of tones segregated from the great mass of musical sounds, and endowed, by agreement, with fixed meanings. The Anglo-Saxons have, for example, practically agreed that if the sound man is uttered, the intellects of all AngloSaxon hearers will act in a certain direction, and always in that direction for that sound. But in the case of music no such convention has been made. The only method of affixing a definite meaning to a musical composition is to associate with the component tones of it either conventional words, intelligible gestures, or familiar events and places. When a succession of tones is played, the intellect of the hearer may move; but the movements are always determined by influences wholly extraneous to the purely musical tones, — such as associations with words, with events, or with any matters which place definite intellectual forms (that is, ideas) before the mind."
