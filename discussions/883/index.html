Och, bb -- from that Kun site…

Elbow: Tendinitis in the elbow - also referred to as “tennis elbow” - can be caused by chronic stress on the tissue that connects the forearm to the elbow area after the tendon has already been torn. Other causes of elbow tendinitis include sudden stress on the forearm, and incorrect grip.

If tendinitis has already occurred, treatment should be approached very cautiously. Ice should be applied immediately, while stretching and rest can also help. Medications such as acetaminophen may be useful, but they should only be taken under the care of a health practitioner who is familiar with musicians’ injuries. These medications tend to mask symptoms, rather than treating the problem, and can affect co-ordination and fine motor control when playing.

Some doctors say injections of steroids are the best short-term means of relieving the pain and swelling caused by RSIs such as tendinitis. They say that if the injections are used cautiously, and not more than three are given in a year, the treatment can be very effective. Still, steroids can mask symptoms. And according to The Sportsmedicine Book, “there is medical evidence that steroid injections into a tendon will weaken it and make it more susceptible to tearing.” (p. 107) But this problem is more likely to affect weight bearing tendons, such as the Achilles, than those string musicians might injure through playing.

Doctors also might recommend immobilizing the area that’s affected by using a splint or a sling. However, immobilizing the muscles can weaken them considerably and it can take a long time to re-build those muscles.

Injuries to a flute player…

Flute has been very tough on my body. When I first began, I was playing slow tones for hours on end, with my body locked in a tight twist. After about a month I noticed my left hand growing numb and I had shooting pain in my upper arm. I went to a doctor who told me I had pinched my radial nerve at a point in my neck, and the only relief would be about 3 months of rest. So, I had to stop playing for 3 months!

Six months ago, I started losing strenght in my left arm, I couldn’t even lift a book with it. I was diagnosed with tendonitis and bone spurs in my left shoulder, and went on cortisone and physical therapy (with a possible operation in the future). That condition is now better, but it is not gone.

Physical injuries are a large part of being a musician. There is a part of the mystique that says we should play through the pain, that pain is part of the dues we must pay. But, I must say, it never occured to me when I began playing flute that it would be difficult for these reasons.

--Eliot

Can I just interupt about tennis elbow again.
For what it’s worth (to bb). I heard a lot of rubbish (I subsequently realised, from “experts doctors”) and tried a lot of treatments which made it worse (one expert had me believe that it was a nerve in my kneck which was the trouble and went on to crack and wrench my kneck until it hurt too!). I expect every case is different but I can give a brief account of the theory of the treatment that worked for me:
The pain was caused by the muscle right where it joins the tendon being in spasm following a fairly minor injury and repetitive use preventing healing.
The good treatment, (sustained thumb pressure on the sore spot for about a minute until I couldn’t stand the pain, followed by relaxing the whole arm, done several times, once a week) fooled the muscle into relaxing (after the pressure was off) thereby bringing it out of spasm.
Between treatments I rested the arm, which wasn’t difficult ’cos it felt like a huge weight of pressure and tension had been lifted.
After a month or so it was more or less back to normal and hasn’t been much trouble since.
I hope this info’s useful to somebody, I’m sure every injury’s different, so maybe a lesson is not to listen to just one “expert”.
Hope you don’t mind me sticking my two bits worth (nose) in!

Re: Injuries and conditions

This thread may be the most important contribution that The Session makes to traditional music.

It seems that anybody who has played for any length of time has had some repeated stress injuries. Symptoms such as numbness in the fingers or hands is a warning signal and is easily treatable if the issue is confronted early enough. They almost always are a result of performing repetitive tasks in an ‘unnatural’, or uncomfortable way.

Here is an easy way to find the ‘natural’ shape of your hands.
Look at them when you are relaxed! When out walking, or whatever, look at your hands hanging at your side. There is a natural curve to the fingers, and the thumb hangs opposite the first finger. The wrist is almost straight in line with the forearm.
Look at them often, until you can relax your hand into this shape at will. Then take your right hand, and turn it over, palm up, still maintaining this natural shape, and lay the bow across the fingers, allowing the frog to contact the tip of the thumb. Find the position, where you can hold the bow, and stay as close to this natural shape as possible. That is the position of most efficiency, where your hand is best able to function, and least likely to be subject to RSI.

For the left hand: Turn the hand over, palm up, again, and lay the neck of the fiddle across the fingers, so that all fingers are in contact with the fingerboard. The thumb will fall on the neck, likely not projecting above the fingerboard. Without changing the shape of the left wrist and hand, (and helping to lift the fiddle with the right hand, also) lift the fiddle to your shoulder, and tuck the chin rest into place under your chin. This will find the most natural position for the fiddle on your body, and, again, will allow the hand to function optimally. Allowing the fiddle to swing out in front of the body, or to hang lower, will result in suboptimal performance of the left hand. Suboptimal positioning results in a lot more work to get the same results. You might as well fill your boots with rocks and try running a marathon!

The other big problem is applying excessive force. It is easy to get into the trap of squeezing the neck so hard that cramps form in the hands, forearms and shoulders. It takes conscious effort to work to reduce the pressure we are exerting with our fingers. Again, if the shape of our hands is optimal, we can often find easy ways to reduce the forces. i.e. I had trouble getting my fingers to make some double stops, and had to contort my hand by squeezing the neck to get my fingers into position. This created all sorts of timing problems, and intonation problems. By changing the shape of my hand, eliminating the ‘collapsed’ wrist, I was able to form the double stops much more easily.

I don’t know much about the other instruments, but the same principals should apply. Find the most natural shape for your hands, and work with it until it feels natural. The effort required to get your hands operating in an optimal manner will be repaid by the saved effort of trying to make great music with suboptimal mechanics.

As far as injuries are concerned, I had a carpal tunnel operation done on my right hand, and was preparing for one on my left hand when the symptoms began to return to my right hand. I was lucky to find a local doctor who had to abandon a career as a concert pianist due to RSI. He was disappointed with the health care that was available to him, so he decided to become a doctor, and opened a clinic in Hamilton to treat musicians with injuries. (His name is Dr. Chong) He set me up with a physiotherapist who showed me some stretching exercises, and stressed the importance of finding mechanically sound ways of doing repetitive tasks.

I have also torn both shoulders, my left forearm a few times, and am currently dealing with a bit of tennis elbow (work related, but not severe.)

I still have a tendency to play with my fiddle in front of me, which puts extra stress on my left shoulder, and left wrist. With my current tennis elbow, I can definitely feel that I can improve my habits.

BTW, stretching exercises work by making the muscles ‘longer and skinnier’ rather than ‘short and fat’. This makes more room within your body for the neural pathways (those things connecting your fingers to your brain). A compressed nerve sends false signals to the brain, hence the sensation of ’pins and
needles’. So stretching works by allowing the network of nerves and nerve endings to do what they are supposed to do. It can be a big help toward achieving optimal performance, it feels good, and takes only a few minutes while you are waiting to play.

Hope some of this is useful
Scotty

Re: Injuries and conditions

Um…I’d just like to add another point of view here. I’ve suffered from tendon and joint problems most of my life, in part due to a genetic iron-overload disorder (topic for a whole ’nother thread on “hemochromatosis” known as the “Celtic Curse” because it affects those of celtic ancestry way more than other lineages). While most people may be helped by stretching and other “standard” medical treatments, some of us--for various reasons--are better off avoiding stretching all together. (A highly respected, wise old sports doctor was the first to tell me to *quit* stretching, and my joints are eterenally grateful.) Also, most anti-inflammatories don’t touch what I go through, and there’s some evidence that acetominophen, ibuprofen, and stronger NSAIDs can damage connective and cushioning tissues such as tendons, fascia, and cartilage over the long run.

For a few of us, pain *is* an unavoidable part of playing an instrument. In fact, *any* repetitive motion is likely to cause problems, no matter how gentle or biomechanically “correct.”

That said, there are ways to live with the situation, adjustments that allow me and others to play. I know all of the above posters mean well, and I don’t want to discredit your advice--but I think it’s important to recognize that some of us must follow different paths. I know you meant no harm, Glauber, but saying that playing should never be painful unfortunately ignores a basic and unalterable reality for some people, and may add an unnecessary load of self-doubt, discouragement, or even guilt. We play or we don’t play, and either way hurts, so some of us choose to play as best we can. I hope this isn’t the case for bb, but I’m sure there are others out there struggling to play despite unavoidable pain.

I’ll go ahead and put this here, rather than just email bb, because hemochromatosis really is more prevalent among people of celtic genes, and gee, there might be one or two of those perusing this site, eh? Up to 25 percent of all celts are likely to have the disorder, and unless you get tested, you won’t know you need treatment till it’s too late.

Basically, hemochromatosis is a genetic disorder that affects your metabolism. If you’ve got the double gene complement, you absorb dietary iron even when your body doesn’t need any more of it. “Normal” people pass off the excess. Those with hemachromatosis stockpile it in their bone marrow (where iron is usually put to good use making red blood cells), and also in and around the liver, heart, pancreas, brain, etc. The problem is that too much iron is toxic. You end up with liver and other cancerc, heart disease, diabetes, all kinds of un-fun problems. It also tends to cause arthritis, and researchers theorize that the iron bonds with calcium phosphatase to form microscopic crystals that cause inflammation in your small joints (fingers, toes, wrists, spinal facets) and possibly in soft tissues such as tendons and cartilage.

I was relatively lucky. Through a job-related health screening they spotted high serum (blood) iron levels when I was about 34. My doc did follow ups and made the diagnosis. Nowadays you can get a gene test with a small blood sample. It costs around $80 to $120 US depending on where you live. (Some people recommend paying it out of pocket rather than letting your insurance cover it because the insurance company may decide you’re a higher risk for all those health problems mentioned above.) I’ve also heard stories about patients with so much iron that they set off metal detectors.

Treatment is simple--they bleed you. (I remember thinking, “Great….leeches.”) Just like giving blood, they stick a needle in a vein and take a pint of blood off. How frequently this has to be done depends on how much excess iron you’ve got, and the rate you tend to absorb it (the rate can vary from person to person). Keep your iron levels normal, and you’re back to just a “normal” risk for those cancers and other end-organ diseases. It helps to avoid cooking with cast iron skillets, and I don’t eat iron-rich cereals (it’s crazy that they put so much iron in stuff like Grape Nuts and some breads). But it’s important to catch it and treat it before any serious damage occurs. Most people don’t start having iron-related problems till they’re in their 50s or so (sometimes later for women because of menstruation). Doctors have told me that at least some of my arthritis and other aches and pains are due to the iron.

If you’re of celtic descent and ever get a blood test that shows a high serum iron level, or even an elevated hematocrit (percentage of red cells), ask your doctor about hemochromatosis. Other ethnic groups are also at risk. I’ve seen reports claiming that this is one of the most common genetic disorders. Obviously, it runs in families (my son has it) and can skip generations. It was commonly missed in previous generations because the people died from one of those other diseases, caused by the iron overload.

There are a number of web sites with more info--just search for hemochromatosis. If anyone wants to contact me for more info, click on my member profile and send me an email. I’ll give you my regular email address then.

I was, for a while, a member of a fight/stunt troupe. We did cowboy shows, pirate shows, all sorts of things (I was an air man, which was a lot of fun -- and we made jokes about the fact that the lady marshall was Chinese), even once going and teaching a bunch of strippers how to box and wrestle without killing each other. Really, they were -- knocking out each other’s teeth, spraining things.

Dale Girard, our director and choreographer, had a motto/dictum that he was always exhorting us with: “No pain, no pain.” I’ve remembered that motto ever since and apply it to all sorts of things.

I don’t know why, but there’s this sort of macho thing amongst many musicians (mainly classical, but I’ve seen it everywhere) that’s not unlike a lot of stunt men that somehow injuring yourself or having pain from practising is glamorous or desirable, or some kind of sign how dedicated you are. (Not you, Will! Medical conditions aside, I mean. You’re a player in-spite-of, I guess!)

I was once around a symphony doctor (during a stint with Opera Colorado as a stage manager) who talked about this, ranted about it, really, going on about how unnecessary it all is. He said that there’s ways around most pain suffered by musicians. I don’t personally know what they all are, but I assume he knew what he was talking about.

As an aside, the stripper gig gave rise (as it were) to a funny story -- Timothy, who was tall, thin, and had a very deep voice, went along to the first meeting with the strippers. They walked in, and a perky, busty blond stripper bounced her way up to Timothy and squeaked out, “Hi!!! My name’s Candy!!!!!” Timothy looked down at her, swallowed, and in his subterranean rumble said, “Of course you are.”
