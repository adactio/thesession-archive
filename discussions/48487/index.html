Re: Session etiquette - minimum participation to play a tune?

How can you know how many other people know it if you don’t play it for them?

And if no one introduces new tunes at the session, how do you grow the group’s repertoire?

One option is to occasionally get together with another musician or two from your session just to learn one another’s tunes. That way, you’ll have critical mass when introducing those tunes to the larger circle.

Also, much depends on the skills and preferences of the people in your session. At my current local session, people are quick to pick up a new tune, so it’s not unusual for someone to launch a new tune only to be asked to play it six or eight times in a row so everyone has a chance to learn it and chink it in place.

Some other sessions I’ve been to over the years didn’t take to this approach.

When I happen to play a tune at a session that no one else seems to know, I try to gauge the interest. If at least one person is trying to pick it up, I’ll play it at least three times, more if they’re on the verge of getting it. If no one tootles along to learn it, I might switch to another tune after the second go-round.

Nowadays, it’s as likely that people grab their phones to record the unfamiliar tune, so I end up playing it as many times as it takes for everyone to get their audio clip.

At least once a session, I will bring out a tune I’m pretty sure nobody else knows. The good thing about that is that most of the players in both of my sessions are tune hounds, and there’s a pretty good chance that people will ask what it is, and within a week or two, there will be 3-4 people playing that tune… You have to be careful that you’re not doing it too much.

I will never play a whole set of tunes that nobody else plays, though. If I start a set solo, I make sure that the other tunes are more commonly known. But if you can make a great transition into a known tune, then people have a positive recollection of the tune they didn’t know… And if I start something that I thought other people would play but they don’t, I will often only play it twice before moving on, unless people are really engaged in listening to the tune.

Re: Session etiquette - minimum participation to play a tune?

Think it through. Use common sense. Be considerate.

If the session is new to you, and you to the others, better to avoid tunes others may not know. Establish yourself with tunes that are more common, after listening and getting used to the vibe, and understanding that every session has its favorite tunes and sets.

If the session is known to you, it is more likely that you will know tunes the others like to play. Plan the set: start with the new tune, and follow it with tunes more familiar to the others that you know they play. If others do not join in the new tune, or do not try to play it by ear, move on to the other tunes after the second time through. If the others are picking it up by ear, play it three or even four times, and then go to the more familiar tunes.

Folks learn by the repetition. And have fun!

Re: Session etiquette - minimum participation to play a tune?

If I’m trying to introduce a tune for the first time that I think might be picked up by the sessioneers (i.e. a tune of the type generally played in that session - not an oddity or party piece*) I will try and make a point of starting a set with it and going into something that I know is well-known, so that others play along. That helps to move it on from being a solo performance into being a collective playing event. If the new tune is a runner, people will either try and join on as you go, or ask the title, or request the dots, and from then on it will gather a head of steam and become part of the usual repertoire. If none of these things happen, you know its not likely to take off in that session.

* An occasional oddity/party piece can create interest and variety and be welcomed by the company, but if you see people look at each other and roll their eyes, you know you’re doing it too often.

cancion, if you’re nervous about soloing at the session, you might benefit even more from teaching a new tune to one or two of your session mates in the comfort of your own kitchen. You’ll have to play it solo for them, so they know what they’re going to learn, but then you can repeat phrases for them while they play along until they get it. Good ear training for all, and it’s great practice for yourself to get accustomed to soloing and leading a tune in a less stressful setting. Over time, you’ll grow more at ease playing this way, and maybe someday it won’t feel so daunting at your session. In the meantime, you’ll at least have accomplices when you go to introduce a new tune to the whole circle.

Re: Session etiquette - minimum participation to play a tune?

Just because no one plays a tune doesn’t mean you can’t try to introduce it to your session. Otherwise a session gets stale and you’ll never know who might like it as well.

If I’m going to introduce a new tune to a session, I’ll usually add it to one of my sets and play 2 others that some/most people would know. That way it doesn’t feel like you’re doing a solo all the time and keeps people engaged. I’d say 60-70% of the time it works, even if there are people who aren’t interested in it at all.

It also helps if you have a friend you know will learn and collaborate with. I have a few close friends who I meet outside a session and just practice tunes together. It helps with “tunecepting” (Inception movie idea here) and getting others in on the tune.

This is why I also find it’s so important who you play tunes with. If you’re at a session where everyone is “in it for themselves” who are only interested in playing their set and ignoring everyone else as a person. then you’re never going to have people to truly grow and play with. Being present, engaged, and connected to people at a session, will mean your new tune you’d like to play will be more receptive than playing at a battleground where all it’s “everyone for yourself”. And same goes for you receiving and learning new tunes from your friends as well.

Cheers,

Once through may not be enough for others to pick up on a new tune. I will often dive in quite early and start adding drones or chords and perhaps harmonies before building up to the melody itself (however I am usually playing English or mixed sessions, where there is more latitude compared with some Irish sessions where the focus is strictly on the melody). However some people like to hear a new tune in full first and may not want to start joining in until at least the second time. Just because they haven’t tried to join in the first time through doesn’t mean they’re not going to try, but they need to be given more time.

If I’m introducing a new tune I will play it at least twice. If no one is making any attempt to join in after two I’ll stop or change to something they know. More usually someone will join in, and then I might play it for more than the statutory three times to give them more chance to get into it.

Re: Session etiquette - minimum participation to play a tune?

In my experience, if you regularly session with the same circle of musicians, you eventually suss out what tunes each person knows, and which ones are their “go to” tunes, their favourites. I try to make a point of noticing if someone is sitting out most of the sets, waiting for tunes they know. I’ll either ask them to play one of their tunes, suggesting a tune if they’re on the shy side. Or I’ll start one of their tunes myself.

It’s also helpful to play tunes at the outset of the evening that everyone has in common, so everyone can get warmed up.

A good session anchor will do these things, but you don’t have to be the anchor to develop these abilities, and anyone in the circle can extend the courtesy of being inclusive, encouraging people to participate. If most people in the circle behave this way, it can become the culture of the session, making it welcoming and friendly.

All of this is especially helpful to beginners and guests, but it’s also a nice courtesy to even the most advanced players. Play or suggest a tune you know they love to play, to launch a tune that’s particularly suited to their instrument, perhaps a tune they’ve written themselves (many tune composers are loathe to start their own tunes but gratified when someone else does. It’s usually less about pride of authorship and more a matter of simply enjoying playing the tune).

Is this a special skill? Perhaps it’s more a mindset, not unlike remembering the names of your friends’ spouses and children. All it takes is caring enough to pay attention and a decent memory (as we get older, the latter can be the limiting factor).
