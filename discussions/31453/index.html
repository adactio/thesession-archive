Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

I will answer as best I can, but it would help to know if you have played wooden flute at all and if playing trad is your intent.

Here are some key points:

1. Wooden flute is a different animal from silver. The bore is conical, not cylindrical. There is no lip plate. Your fingers must completely seal the holes. I find it takes more air.

2. Keys make absolutely no difference in the quality, but I would be unhappy with less than eight keys because I use them. They are costly, though, so you might consider between four and six keys to save money.

3. I prefer wood and blackwood is the most popular for new flutes. Antique flutes are often in cocus wood, which is scarce today. Antique flutes are a bit of a crap shoot, but good ones appear all the time. Check the Chiff & Fipple website. You’ll have to be patient if you want to score a good antique. I have two beauties.

4. Be prepared to re-learn the instrument. In trad, you do the articulation primarily with your fingers and throat (glottal stops), not your tongue. When you stop tonguing, you suddenly realize that Irish-style playing is not just about sounding Irish: If you don’t tongue, you literally have to have some way of articulating notes and phrases, so expect a learning curve for mastering these techniques.

5. Here’s the least controversial thing I can tell you: Do a LOT of listening.

6. For learning tunes and technique, this is IMO the best site on the internet.
http://irishflute.podbean.com/

You’ll get to a ton of the tunes you’ll want at tempo and slowed down so you can hear exactly the techniques you’ll want to master.

Cheers.

Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

Keys on a simple system flute give you the accidentals - they still have six open holes just like a keyless flute. You can play most tunes on a keyless flute and I would recommend starting with a keyless flute.

My first teacher helped by demonstrating one of the major differences - blowing. To get the hard reedy ‘Irish’ sound you tend to blow more down into the hole than across it. This also makes the note flatter so you have to compensate by pushing the tuning slide in a bit. Good tone takes effort but it’s worth it.

Second the advice to listen as much as possible - you cant make your playing sound Irish if you don’t know what Irish sounds like!

Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

There are a lot of different ways to play Irish trad-style
flute. Probably the two main schools of playing - broadly
generalising - are the ‘huff and puff’ style and the flowing style.
Some people use a lot of rolls and other finger articulation -
others use very little. Some people use tongue - others stay
away from it. Some people use a lot of throaty articulation -
others don’t.

There are fans of all these variations and they can be passionate
about their preferences - this is the ‘right way’ this is ‘wrong way’.
You have to figure out what you like best and what comes easily.
I’m not a big huff n’ puff fan myself - it can get mechanical to my ears.

Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

Madeline, I always try to impress upon people who play Boehm flute who are thinking about taking up Irish flute that there’s no real reason to switch instruments.

As a wise old man told me 30 years ago “an instrument is merely a mechanical device. The MUSIC is in the person.”

There have been many fine traditional Irish fluteplayers who played the Boehm flute (or in some cases another system of mechanized flute, such as Paddy Carty playing a Radcliffe System Flute).

The point is that it’s more about the style of music than the instrument. An experienced orchestral fluteplayer, if they pick up an Irish flute, will still sound precisely like an orchestral fluteplayer. Likewise a good traditional Irish fluteplayer, if he picks up a Boehm flute, will still sound precisely like a traditional Irish fluteplayer. In other words the instrument does not magically imbue the player with any sort of stylistic approach.

Which is not to say that there aren’t valid reasons for getting an Irish flute, especially a keyless one! Because the Irish woodwind tradition was formed upon three instruments with more or less similar fingering (or one might say analogous fingering) the wooden flute, the whistle, and the uilleann pipes, and when one plays upon these instruments one is guided somewhat onto the traditional path at least as regards the available notes.

Orchestral/legit/classical fluteplayers have much to unlearn when they take up playing Irish flute. I’ve taught many Irish Flute workshops over the last 35 years and it is extremely difficult to get some “classical” fluteplayers to drop certain habits. One of the main ones is to stop tonguing every note and to approach the music as a “flow” in other words having a legato basis. I’ve had fluteplayers who were completely incapable of playing legato, and with these people I’ve had to resort to the strange-sounding method of having them play while keeping the tip of their tongue pressed against the roof of their mouth. Only then did they start playing with the “flow” I was after; more importantly they could hear for themselves how they now sounded “more Irish”.

Another Boehm habit that’s hard to break is finger position, specifically keeping the fingers in “guide position” a very short distance above the key (necessary on the Boehm flute to avoid a clacking noise when you press keys). When the fingers are held that close to the flute body they can’t accelerate enough to play a “pat” with the correct sound, which is dependant upon speed/velocity.

Anyhow if you want to get an “Irish flute” I say fine, if you want to learn Irish style on the Boehm flute fine; but the style is the key thing. You’ll have to learn a new approach to articulation and breathing and phrasing.

A high-quality wood keyless flute would do you just fine, or a polypenco/delrin flute which is cheaper but heavy in the hands.

One disadvantage to playing the Boehm flute as opposed to a keyless flute (which is more or less a wood tube with six holes in it) is that the Boehm flute has over 20 places where it can potentially leak, and goes “out of adjustment” on a regular basis. A friend who is a professional Boehm fluteplayer takes his flute in once a week for adjustment.

The opposite was the simple Irish flute I played for several years, three sections (head, body, foot), keyless, no tuning slide, unlined head. It had only three places it could potentially leak, the two tenons and the cork. I could bang that flute around quite a bit at sessions or gigs and not worry about it.

Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

Michael beat me to it. I still sound like an Irish trad player when I pick up a silver flute. (At least until I mess up the fingerings and then I sound like an eejut.) That said, most of the crossover classical/trad players I know do eventually get a wooden flute.

“What kind should I get?” The three makers I like best are Pat Olwell, who is highly regarded on this side of the pond, Michael Grinter and Hammy Hamilton. They’re expensive flutes, and all three have waiting lists. Also Dave Copley, Giles Lehart, Eamonn Cotter, Martin Doyle, Chris Wilkes, and Terry McGee make nice instruments. Casey Burns and Walt Sweet come up a lot because Casey makes a budget “folk flute” and Walt makes an inexpensive delrin “Shannon” flute that folks like. I’m sure I’ve forgotten other good makers but there are plenty of threads to check back and read for ideas. You could also watch for a good antique flute, some of which sound great and often cost less than a new keyed flute. It all depends on what you’re willing to spend; the better flutes do cost more but you can make perfectly good music on a less expensive flute.

“What about open holes vs keys?” If you’re planning on playing sessions you’ll probably eventually want a keyed flute. Those annoying fiddlers will wander off into F, Dm, Gm, or C at the slightest provocation and then you’re stuck trying to convincingly half-hole the accidentals. Of course one could argue that listening to mushy, out-of-tune F-naturals is the price fiddlers have to pay for straying from the True Path of D and G. ;-) I play an 8-key antique flute, though I would be content with a 6-key.

“Does the wood vary?” Yes, there are subtle differences between woods. Most high-end modern flutes are made of blackwood becuase it’s dense, stable, and produces a good tone. You’d have to play the other woods like cocus or boxwood to see if you discern differences. If you want a pretty much indestructible flute, there are people who are fond of delrin. (I’m not one of them.)

Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

As one who started on Boehm and now plays both, I’d say:
1. Forget the whistle. You’ll probably buy a few, but generally I only use mine when in playing familiar tunes in strange keys (e.g. accompaanying a singer…). My D whistle (the direct equivalent to the D flute) hardly ever gets out to play. Concentrate your energy on the instrument that you actually want to play.
2. Get a wooden flute. You can play this music on a boehm, but I find the ornamentation a lot easier to get right with the direct contact of my fingers on the holes. And yes, my Boehm also has open holes, but it is not the same… Also, slapping a Boehm mechanism around percussively can lead to a need for more frequent maintenance which can get expensive.
3. The sound is different, and that comes partly from the flute, but mainly it comes from the player aiming to achieve something different. The basic rule of listen to what you are trying to emulate and then listening to yourself still applies. I can get an “ITM”y sound out of my Boehm and a clear “classic” sound out of the simple sytem flutes if I want.
4. I find having separate instruments helps me also keep my head clear on how I play them. I play the simple system flutes for traditional Scottish and Irish music and I play classic on the Boehm. Just the feel of what I have in my hands gives me most of the signals that I need as to how I should be playing at that particular moment.
5. A keyless flute will do MOST of your traditional music needs. I would certainly recommend one for starting. I bought a very good 6 key flute as my first wooden flute and although it is a very good flute, it is not the perfect match to what I want in a flute. I now have “my” perfect keyless flute and have ordered a keyed one from the same maker. But that took a number of years playing on this type of instrument and also trying out a number of other flutes so that I had enough experience to judge what I was looking for.
6. As for keys; sooner or later you will want them. Hopefully by then you will have enough time on a keyless to know on what flute you want them. The FNat key is very useful. I like a C Natural thumbhole which is not traditional but a very easy option for someone used to a boehm flute. Scottish music will be much easier with a G# key. EFlat is not really achievable without a key, whatever some people may assert. The foot keys might be useful, but again, not really if you are planning on only playing trad on the flute. And you always have the Boehm as a back up option if you really _must_ play _that_ tune with a low C natural or two.
7. Many things are seen as “bad habits” in the classical world, and indeed often have their justification there. e.g. keeping the fingers close to the keys makes really fast fingerwork easier and not bobbing around in time to the music makes it easier to keep your embouchure stable and in the right place on the flute. However, as already mentioned above, the percussive fingering required of many traditional ornament REQUIRES you to move your fingers quite a lot further from the flute than would be acceptable in classical training. And this is dance music so bobbing around in time to the music is acutally a fairly sensible thing to do. With all these issues, accept that what is “plain and simply wrong” in one of these worlds is not necessarily so in the other and indeed may even be necessary. In my experience, both sides are prone to arrogance about the wrong-headedness and folly of the other way of doing things. Some things really are worth carrying over, e.g. in my experience many traditional players often neglect their breathing and breath support (not the really good ones). Be prepared to listen to the experts and take what works for you.
8. Yes, some traditional players play with the flute head supported on the shoulder. I am amazed that they can and I have no intention of ever trying - I am sure I would end up hospitalised in a day or two. Still, they seem to manage ok (see Conal O’Grada for example). There are other variations on holding the flute too but I would recommend holding the flute the way you hold the Boehm flute - it works and you are used to it. (Interestingly Conal said in a workshop that he finds holding the flute on his shoulder provides a reflection of the sound from his body back into his own ear which changes the way he hears himself when playing. I tried it and it does, butI’m still not going to change my grip!)
9. Learn to play tunes without sheet music. I am not saying sheet music won’t be part of your learning process, but it will be a diminishing one. Eventually you will find yourself playing tunes that you have never seen the sheet music for, but that have somehow found their way into your brain through the ears. (This message board will debate the role of sheet music endlessly if you let them.). You will also find it easier to play your classic repertoire without being chained to the music stand which is very liberating.
10. Most important. Listen to lots of this music. Remeber, you are trying to sound like that, so if you can’t hear how it sounds then you won’t be able to play it.
11. Sessions are fun and they can be good for y0ur playing, but they can also be frustrating and can lead to you knowing and playing a bunch of tunes badly and too fast just to “keep up” with the rest of them. Also play along to recordings by good people. Use slow-downers and sometimes transposing software to do so. e.g. a lot of Matt Molloy’s recordings are on EFlat flutes - If you don’t have one then using a computer to drop it down to be playable on a D flute is also a good option.

Have fun.

Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

I should point out that we’re using “Boehm” to refer to four quite specific aspects of flute construction

1) the Boehm fingering system
2) covered keywork
3) the cylindrical bore
4) the use of metal rather than wood for the tube

and these three do not necessarily go together!

In fact the original Boehm flute, his 1832 model, had a conical bore, wooden tube, and open holes, in other words retained the very things which make the “Irish flute” sound “Irish”.

And flutes with Boehm fingering but having a conical bore and wooden tube were made throughout much of the 19th century in England. Rather rare and expensive today, but it is quite possible for somebody to play a flute that uses Boehm fingering that has the same tone as an “Irish flute”.

Also flute were made in the 19th century that retained the old fingering system but had cylindrical bores and metal tubes- not “Boehm flutes” by definition but sounding like them.

Both types and several others come up on Ebay fairly regularly.

Re: Transitioning from silver flute?

When I read Boehm in the context of this discussion, I understand what you will find played in modern orchestras and taught in most schools. Metal tube (usually, but expensive modern Boehm flutes can also be made of wood…), cylindrical bore, Covered keywork, with or without holes in the keys for some notes. By far the most readily available instrument in the side-blown family nowadays.

Yes, _some_ can play ITM authentically on Boehm Flutes. I just find it easier to do so on a simple system flute. And _most_ people I know who _only_ play Boehm flutes have not yet gone far enough down the road of ITM understanding to sound right whatever they might be playing.

Boehm was looking for uniformity of tone throughout the scale, accurate tuning and generally power. He achieved this by having all the holes (except c#) about the same diameter as the bore, which was cylindrical. Putting the holes in the acoustically correct place for the notes and then adding keywork to make it possible for normal human hands to cover these holes. His keywork is also designed to have all the holes closed down to the active tone hole and then all holes below that open for ideal venting. By and large it works.

The simple system flute is acoustically very varied throughout its’ scale due to the size and position of the holes; for example, E sounds very diffent to D which sounds very different to G… This makes the playing on such flutes sound very distinctive and many Irish tunes use this tonal difference for emphasize, e..g rythmic honks on low D. To achieve this effect on a Boehm you would have to vary your blowing - possible, but harder. On the other hand playing most notes in tune most of the time is a big advantage of the Boehm:-)

In terms of the actual tonal quality, I can get an ITMy sound out of the Boehm and a classic sound of the wooden flute. However they still sound different when I play a tune on them.

@Richard, Moving while playing is a personal thing I would not feel the need to correct anyone who was either moving or not moving while playing. Interestingly, I have followed the exact opposite route; Boehm->Irish flute->Scottish Pipes - maybe Uillean are in my future somewhere?
