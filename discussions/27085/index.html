Traditional fiddle teaching - still alive

An orchestral colleague of mine, not a fiddler, was touring the West Coast of Ireland last summer and went into a pub in Connemara. As he was having his drink an old man came in with a young lad, apparently his grandson. The old chap went up to the bar and asked for “a beer and fiddle”. The barman duly pulled the beer and produced from under the counter an old fiddle and bow and passed them over. The old man and his grandson then went off into a corner where the boy was given a fiddle lesson.

My friend was very impressed by this example of traditional teaching at the heart of the community.

Sure traditional music is a minority interest in Scotland and to a lesser degree in Ireland.

But exposure to traditional music is something that crosses everybody’s radar living in either county. It’s simply not possible to grow up in either without coming across it, school, functions, just walking down the street. The “Trad” scene maybe relatively new in it’s present form but not the music, it’s ingrained in the culture ffs, wether we like it or not.

That a kid goes to the pub with his grandfather to learn a tune isn’t surprising to me but I’d speculate that the reasons they headed out to the pub in the first place is more to do with the grandfather rather than some pure drop sentiment. Rather the “just a drop” sentiment, pure or otherwise, ha ha.

Your far more likely to find this going on behind closed door, or hear it through an open window. My neighbours boy is learning the pipes directly from his father and since he’s moved on to the goose I can hear his progress from the seat from which I type this, if I’m sitting quietly.

The most moving experience I’ve ever had playing music was when the day after the night before, hungover and bleary eyed we called into a house to retrieve a jacket that had been taken home by the wrong owner. Anyway the woman of the house asked us into the kitchen and gave us some rather welcome tea whilst she raised his good self. The jacket was returned, the confusion laughed at, especially given the size of the man compared to the jacket he’d mistakenly lifted. The talk turned to fiddles.

The woman had played a little in her youth but her fiddle was in a sate of disrepair, she got it when prompted and within around 10mis one of the fiddlers had it singing out a strathspey. Just then the door burst open and three little girls ran in and started highland dancing, well the smallest two were copying the elder. It was just the most natural thing, no prompting no cajoling on the part of the parents. The elder girl was obviously a good dancer and the two younger kids were obviously headed that way probably once they started school.

The point of the wee story above is to illustrate my point, and whether we take a minority interest in the music or not, is irrelevant, it’s something that is just there. An active interest will always be the minority position.

So much conjecture about my one little post (never mind leoj’s subsequent inability to answer a simple question).

For Hurler and others lamenting the way discussions unfold here, I wonder what you think you’re contributing to it by presuming to know what others are really thinking when, in fact, you’re half a globe away and have never met. The easy and more friendly thing to do would be to ask. (And not as a form of passive aggressive baiting a la leoj.)

While we’re at it, “it’s not a handbag. It’s a satchel. Indiana Jones carries one.” :-D (If you don’t get the joke, you need to get out more.)

So here’s what I was thinking:

A life lesson? I was thinking that no one ever pulled me aside as a kid and took me down to the pub and showed me a tune on fiddle. So the kid in the OP may someday realize that he grew up in a culture where the music and this way of passing it on is knit right into community life. Where a fiddle behind the bar is commonplace. If he travels, he may also realize that some other cultures aren’t like that. (If I walked into any one of the bars in my town, it would create a scene to ask whether they had a musical instrument behind the counter, and could I borrow it please to teach a kid a tune, over there in the corner. People would think I was crazy. It would very likely annoy the patrons and probably lead to being ridiculed and maybe even escorted out the door.)

So if the kid in the OP story ends up playing traditional music, he’s likely to have a very different sense of how the music fits into his life and his community. For playing this music, I’d say he’d have a much more ingrained understanding of music being part of the community (and not something left to the radio or professionals) than if he hadn’t had the experience grandpa has given him.

It reminds me of something Martin Hayes said in response to an interviewers question (and I’m paraphrasing here): “Why is the music so strong in Clare?” And Martin’s answer was along the lines of: “It’s strong in Clare because the people there believe they have the music.”

This isn’t some romanticized, “charming” notion, but a plain truism. “Whether you think you can, or think you can’t, you’re probably right.”

Because of his experiences with gramps, the kid in the OP story is more likely to grow up thinking he *can* -- he *can* learn and play this music as an utterly normal, not-odd, part of living in his community. With a little talent, woodshedding, and luck, he may come some day to see this as an advantage, a source of conviction that helps him to play with “heart,” “soul,” “nyah,” -- all those hard-to-define qualities so many people here crow about finding in their favorite players.

Is this profound or trite or both? That’s up to each of you, eh? I don’t really care. Either way, it strikes me as a nice little life lesson. It’s interesting to me because I’ve heard a number of people tell stories about growing up with the music that are the same stories the kid in the OP will be able to tell some day. How utterly normal it seemed at the time. Funny, the people I’ve heard these stories from--the people who felt compelled to tell them because the stories were meaningful--include a long list of today’s well-known, brilliant, powerful musicians.

Maybe no one else sees it that way. Fine. Maybe that makes me odd. No worries. I’m accustomed to being labeled as “different.” That’s okay. I’m much better at being myself than I am trying to be someone else.

Re: Traditional fiddle teaching - still alive

Almost without fail I come away from reading one of your posts thinking “Well he has a great gra for the music, but he doesn’t quite get it.”. But then why would you? Even with years of playing trad you’re not coming to it as someone born and reared to it. I’m not trying to establish any hierarchy between natives and blow-ins, placing one above the other, but there are things the stranger just doesn’t know. As anyone moving into a new townland will tell you it doesn’t matter how long you’re there and how much you establish yourselves in the community, the natives may even treat as one of their own but there’ll always be things that are unspoken or you hear but don’t fully understand. Like not teaching polkas to beginners.

In this case it wasn’t about what the “life lesson” was actually going to be (though it was pretty easy to guess) but rather the idea that the cameo might be “life lesson” at all. For me, that’s a fundamental misunderstanding of how children come to trad here in Ireland. The process isn’t profound, it just is.

You used a movie quote so let me try one in return: “This is a very simple game. You throw the ball, you catch the ball, you hit the ball. Sometimes you win, sometimes you lose, sometimes it rains.”. It seems to me that you’re over-thinking it, putting yourself in the lad’s shoes in a way that does doesn’t ring true, at least with me. I’ve taught trad for going on forty years, must be a thousands children, and they just don’t exhibit any of the angst one often sees in your posts (including the one above).

Why does it matter, well for better or worse any websearch to do with trad brings up thesession.org. Yet none of the “big voices” seem to have been born and reared in Irish communities. On lots of topics that doesn’t matter. In fact many here know more trad “facts” than most of my neighbours. The thing is, there’s so much more to trad than playing tunes. In fact they’re the least of it. It’s on the intangibles where the wheels so often seem to come off the wagon here. In many cases poster from here seem to struggle to get their point across, sometimes it’s their inarticulacy, sometimes their bellicosity. Even with that jig character and big_tab one can sense the struggle to express the often inexpressible. So a consensus of sorts forms around a single easy perspective, one that sometimes reflects practices outside of Irish communities in American and the UK but be at odds with those inside or over here. It’s not that one perspective is wrong and the other right (if it’s anything, trad is a very catholic church!) more that there’s no balance.

There was a paper floating around last summer, out of UCC or UL I think, entitled “Is Will Harmon now more influential than Willie Clancy?”. It wasn’t actually about you per se, instead your name was used as a catchy proxy for the internet as a whole. It’s balanced and measured as one might expect but it turns out that in a world where privacy boundaries are shifting, where “information wants to be free” Irish musicians generally remain reluctant talk seriously about trad’s underlying value systems and what it means to them. It’s changing, slowly, (for example, there are a couple of interesting Irish-based trad blogs now) but there’s still a bit of the primitive’s “if you take my picture, you’ll take my soul.”.

As for “the people I’ve heard these stories from--the people who felt compelled to tell them because the stories were meaningful--include a long list of today’s well-known, brilliant, powerful musicians.”. How does one respond to appeals to vague authority. Sure musicians are grateful that they were brought up with the music and that it allowed them to make a living doing what they love. Sure they’ll talk of early influences. But “life lessons” . . . no Irishman or Irish-American would be caught dead using that language because a) it’s poncy psychobabble, and b) it suggests that the the music is somehow separate from the community, from the land, from the culture. Some sort of special intervention. As someone once said that Martin Hayes said “It’s strong in Clare because the people there believe they have the music.”. No-one born and raised in a trad community needs a “life lesson” to play music.

From one of the study’s interviewees “I’m a doctor. I know how my respiratory system works but I don’t have the words to tell you how I learnt to breath or what breath means to me.”. I don’t think it’s a “life lesson”, it’s just life, it’s what we do.

Re: Traditional fiddle teaching - still alive

Many thanks, everyone, for those interesting and informative comments.

My grandfather didn’t take me to the pub to teach me the fiddle, but what he did when I had my first real bike with racing handlebars (I was 11 or 12) was to teach me in some depth how to maintain it, not just tires, repairing punctures, brakes, chains, gears, and the like, but more advanced stuff like building up my own wheels from scratch. He would have liked to have shown me how to braze up a lugged frame, but, being retired, he no longer had the equipment so he talked about it instead. He also taught me about posture on the bike and how to train for races. which I did a few years later. I like to think he would have made a great violin teacher if he had been a musician.
