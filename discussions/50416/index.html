Whistle tuning woes…or something

Someone, a whistle player I sometimes play with, regularly gives me flack about the tuning of my whistle. Decent wooden whistle. Was not that cheap. When I blow the whistle into my tuning gizmo, it says it’s fine. A = 440. The head is shoved as far into the body as possible (don’t take that the wrong way), so it isn’t getting sharper unless I saw off the end. I know, it would be ideal if I could sharpen it when needed, but my horses like being expensive, so…not happening anytime soon. But lots of people play bloody Generations and Feadogs and whatever that aren’t tuneable at all.

I also know you can blow it flat when feeling uncommitted to that tune you’re trying to recall, but everyone does that. Including him. When you remember it and gie it yaldy, it’s fine.

Had that discussion today. Not the first time. I pointed to my gizmo (well, my iphone) and reiterated that it says the whistle is fine. He said, “You need a new whistle.”

My line: “I have some vet bills to pay off. You want to help me out with that, I’ll get a new whistle.”

No one else in the session has said anything.

Re: Whistle tuning woes…or something

Frankly, this sounds like a dude with a problem, and the problem isn’t the tuning.

Can you two play a tune together, just the two of you? That should show up any spots where your intonation differs - it won’t tell you who’s “right” (if that’s a thing) but it’ll tell you whether one of you is playing something different from the other. But probably it’ll sound fine when the two of you play together because you’ll just sync up without thinking about it.

I think this is kind of a thing with whistles. There are definitely whistlers that I have to listen carefully to when we play together. I don’t know who’s “wrong” here, but I can hear the fingernails-on-blackboard clash in certain places unless I’m very consciously trying to come to where they are. There’s also another fellow at my session who also plays flute and whistle, and I’ve noticed that we generally don’t play whistles at the same time - possibly an unconscious reaction to this sort of situation. (or possibly a conscious reaction on his part, I suppose)

I suspect that whistle intonation, barring extreme cases, has very little to do with “the whistle is out of tune”. As others have said, air pressure and lip have a lot to do with it, and you’re not going to manage those consciously. You just have to listen and lock in to someone, preferably another whistle player - even if they’re playing a bit out, it’s going to sound a lot better if you’re both out the same way than if you’re a bit out from each other, particularly in the upper range.

Re: Whistle tuning woes…or something

I fear that sometimes we fall into the trap of being too concerned about exact tuning. Of course, there is such a thing as plain out of tune, that’s not in doubt. But at the other end of the “in tune or not” spectrum, things are not so clear.

We are not doing a musical equivalent of painting by numbers, looking for the exact blue for the sky. It’s different for every instrument, naturally, but in general a note is more like a real artist’s brush stroke. The note starts somewhere, perhaps moves towards the arithmetically correct pitch, maybe wobbles a bit and quite possibly moves right off, up or down, at the end. That’s where the life comes from.

And that’s before we even start to consider the perception games played by the human nervous system. Many of us will know that if, for example, you have to play an upper-octave, one-or-two finger down note on a whistle, and you *know* that it is at risk of being flat, you can start WAAY flat and then slide up towards the target. Even if you don’t quite reach it, if you do this right the human ear will hear you as having played the note without a problem.

In other words, while these aids are useful (very useful, imho), they still don’t tell us whether it actually *sounds* right to a typical listener.

In even fewer words, it’s an art.

Some older people won’t notice because they can no longer hear it. Tune against a sustained reference pitch in relative quiet: no issues. In the middle of a big session, with voices and laughter in the mix as well: incredibly tough, and impossible for some.

Whistles played against one another are okay, you’d have to have lost most of your high frequencies not to hear that. But in a noisy pub session I suspect I could sit between two fiddlers who weren’t quite in tune, and either need half an hour to work it out or not realise at all. Frequency losses are usually different in each ear, and discrepancies become so normal that you don’t necessarily pay attention.

It’s a problem for everyone else, of course, and difficult if people aren’t aware how much hearing they’ve lost. If someone told me I was out of tune I’d regard it in the same spirit as them telling me I’d left my jacket behind when leaving the pub, but some folk might be quite prickly.

Re: Whistle tuning woes…or something

“Got to tune the way you play.” Got to play in tune. Everyone in this discussion knows that with whistles and flutes there is more to playing in tune than tuning it, blowing it and covering the right holes. I guess that if we can hear what we are trying to play in tune with clearly we do give it the attention (or do it automatically as per Michael Eskin’s last sentence).

Contrary to what WhistlingFionn said I find playing in tune much harder on whistle than flute - probably having done it less, being an octave above most other things and having trouble accepting a lack of dynamics. I go through the motions of tuning but in a room at any normal temperature I know where the tuning slide should be for A 440 and I am checking how to blow. If the instrument is cold I expect to have to push it a bit for the tuner and the other instruments to start with. If after a few tunes I feel I am having to blow it sharp or flat I then nudge the tuning slide (or check if I have accidentally
moved it).

I don’t claim to be good at playing in tune, and RTTA often shows up some dodgy notes (those sharp C naturals really don’t do it for some people and I need to honk the bottom D more) but once the tunes with other people are flowing a tuner is not much help. Ears and a feel for how I’m blowing are.

If I didn’t mind drawing attention to myself it would be interesting to record myself with a clip-on mike on one channel and the session on another. Then analyse at home. May try it at a kitchen session sometime.

For sharper, you need to shorten it, using knife, file and sand paper. I am a flat blower and I had to shorten several flutes at the tenon joints. (you “undo” this by pulling the tenon out as needed). I would not do this on a gold whistle, but when you send a whistle for repair (“it plays too flat!”), take a guess what they do to it behind the curtains (hint: sand it shorter until it is sharp enough).

BTW, my Doug Tipple PVC G flute has the wedge and it plays in tune. really well made.

Re: Whistle tuning woes…or something

Sometimes whistles are out of tune. In a recent session I helped a whistle player who was nearly half a step sharp. She had no perception of it, but after the correction, others remarked on the great improvement in sound.

But sometimes a critic’s ear needs calibration. One of my best friends and bandmates too often criticizes “the fiddles’ intonation.” The _other_ fiddler is notoriously bad, which makes me feel like I’m criticized for his errors. But when rehearsing for one gig, she was insistent: “No, listen to your high A!!”

As it happened, my electronic tuner was running right in front of me. It showed my A was perfect.

She’s convinced her judgment is perfect (and she is a very good musician) but I say if the tuner says it’s A440, that’s what it is. _Musicophilia_ by Oliver Sacks has a section on people whose perception of pitch goes awry. Apparently it can happen to the best of musicians.
