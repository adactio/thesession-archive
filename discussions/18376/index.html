Re: 28 days to better ears

28 days is a good amount of time to dedicate to something. So we’re here to support you :-D

I used to actively practice listening. I have about 3000 tracks of Irish trad on the mp3 player in my car. I put it on random, which meant that I was hearing tunes that I had no idea what they were. I would actively listen to the shape of the melody the first time through the A and B parts, and then try to lilt them the second time through to see how well I listened.

Of course, you should keep actively listening while you’re lilting, to find the bits that you didn’t learn the first time through. If you do that as an exercise, you get much much better at it. Combine that with some proficiency with your instrument, and you’re well on your way to learning and retaining tunes very quickly.

Re: 28 days to better ears

>> Odd ~ wouldn’t you say?

I don’t find it overly odd that she would do that. I don’t really read music, but when I was starting out, I used to try to make sure that I had ABC files that were exactly the same as the setting of a tune that I had learned (by ear). I needed the reference, because I didn’t trust my brain to remember “how the tune went”. In just a few short years, though, I started relying more on my brain, and less on the “artificial memory”.

But a real breakthrough in my playing came when I stopped thinking of a tune as having a specific line of notes in it. As soon as I let go of individual notes, I stopped being annoyed by people that had different settings of a tune than I do. The shape of the melody, and ultimately where the tune is “going”, is much more important than what notes are in it. When you rely on your brain for remembering the contour of the tune, it opens up the world to you. For instance, you can find some wonderful variations to express the tune in a different way, play the tune in different keys, and change what you’re playing on the fly to more match other people that you’re playing with.

I’m not saying that I’m a master of any of these things yet, but it was a wonderful discovery that really made a difference in my playing.

Re: 28 days to better ears

Ben, I will send a PM shortly.

But I’ll also respond here in the off chance that it might be useful to others as well.

I was and am hoping to b helpful. Playing music with other people in a session is far too spontaneous and interactive (and *fun*) to work well if some of the people are playing from a written script. The mere presence of written notation will at best be a distraction from the full-time job of fully using your ears.

In short, “playing by ear” isn’t just having a tune in your head and remembering the notes in the right sequence. It’s much more about being able to listen accurately to what others are playing and respond and adapt to that. It’s about paying attention in real time to all the qualities of the music as it’s being played right here and now. What’s the pace, the pulse, degree of swing, mood, volume, are we on the beat or a skooch behind or ahead, is this the two part version of this tune or the four part, can I hear the mandolin or should I back off my volume a bit?

Since all of this can change moment to moment, “playing by ear” has to be “in the now,” immediate, and immediately responsive. Nothing on the written page can help with this.

Of course, this means that you have to really know the tunes. You have to be able to pull them up and play them, like telling a familiar story again, no need for a script to prompt you. All of us sometimes forget how a phrase goes or how a tune starts, or how the B part kicks off. We either don’t really have the tune yet, or it’s been eons since we last played it, or we’re eyeballing the bottom of the 12th pint and recall isn’t what it is sober. Sure, go home and listen to the tune again or scan some dots or abcs to jog your memory. But then play the tune a hundred times through without the page, layering in variations, till you have it. And play it at the next session 6 times around.

Think how silly it would be to play football if every player ran around carrying a copy of the rules and a spiral-bound playbooks. Likewise, sessions are a lot more fun without the reams of inky paper. :-)

4 years later ~ and glad you decided to come back to this and share

I’ve enjoyed reading the contributions here - ben, Will, and the football analogy. Well, here’s more, being my usual introspective and rambling self - - - but blaming you two for the following. ;-)

Ben, you used the word ‘conversational’. Think how that works best when you can also have a face before you, or even reach out and touch when appropriate. Conversation is not just about the words. If it were, then print would be sufficient. Then think about print and how easy it is to read it wrong, or other than the writer really intended, or out of context. The same is true with the music, if it is to be fully conversational. You need to see each other, to be completely open, without a sheet of paper or a book in the way of that full experience. Better direct communication, face-to-face. That doesn’t mean there isn’t still something getting across, being communicated, if imperfectly, when that dulling effect of sheet music is in the way. It can still have some fun and pleasure in it, but it is fragmentary, incomplete, dulled, like talking to someone with a duvet thrown over your head. You can hear, but even that is dulled, and you can’t see. Now plug your nose and with your eyes closed have someone feed you an onion and an apple, at random. With the nose closed you don’t taste everything. With the eyes closed you miss the visual pleasure. Without the sense of smell it is hard to tell an onion from an apple. Trust me, sheet music has a similar effect on reducing the full experience of music, of sharing it with others, making that fuller and more direct contact with the ears and the eyes…

P.S. That ‘stubborness’ of your friend’s, it is so common with folks that are dependant on dots. I’ve come across it often. There are ways to move beyond it, but like with any addict, they have to want to, and having a supportive and understanding friend, as you obviously are, helps… Whether they do or not, best of luck to you both…
