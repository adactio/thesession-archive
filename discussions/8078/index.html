UNDER YOUR SKIN?

A couple of very nice folks hi-jacked one of my threads recently to discuss Klezmer tunes. Now, I’ll be honest, this type of music I do not understand & even if I learn all the notes I just can’t seem to get my head ‘into’ this music, if you see what I mean, so they started me wondering…………

It reminded me of all those early years when I was learning Irish music.
I had actually lived over here in Ireland first, for almost four years, before going hame tae bonny scotland & takin’ up ITM as a hobby, so I had listened to it daily on the radio here, been to see the Dubliners & even attended weekly sessions, just to drink & listen, as a punter.
This meant that when I took it up, for me there was a connection.
I could think of the countryside, the places & the people etc etc as I played the tunes.

On the other hand, with Breton Music, I heard it for years but couldn’t get interested in, it seemed like very simple, ordinary little tunes, until I actually spent a summer in Brittany & ever since then I have absolutely loved their music & play it, & lots of other ‘French’ traditional tunes too, whenever I can, because now I have vivid memories of the place, the food, the dances, the girls etc etc

So, I guess my question is really for anyone who plays ITM but has never actually visited Ireland, or plays, [ the more complicated :-) ] Scottish Music, but has never ever been tae bonny Scotland, or the music of any country, for that matter, when you haven’t actually been there.

Now before you go off half-cock, No, I’m not digging up that old argument about not being able to play cause you aint been there, which is patently bo**ocks, I’m just curious to know how other folks get themselves under the skin & right into another countries music or a particular type of music.

I can do it, a bit, with Old Time music, but then the tunes are so similar, but Klezmer music, or the wild gypsy music of Hungary, or I even remember getting a tape from kenny of Chinese music after he had visited that country, & of course it was a delightful present & I enjoyed listening to the music, but I don’t think I could ever play that music, no matter how hard I tried, it just doesn’t make any real ‘sense’ to me.

So please tell me, how do you get under the skin of the music, ITM or any other type of music you play?

Ptarmigan,
You start this thread with a complaint about someone highjacking one of your threads. Imagine, someone highjacking someone elses threads, filling them with a bunch of voluminous sidetracks! I am shocked! SHOCKED! Not that YOU would ever do such a thing, would you? ;-)
Now to (kind of) answer your question, as an American, I have spent my entire life being influenced by a rich stew of all sorts of types and styles of music. That is why, even when I play traditional music, I play it with an American accent. Without even thinking, I tend to swing the rhythm and use jazzy inflections. Musically, we are the sum total of all our experiences and influences. As we are exposed to new things, that sum total changes and goes in new directions, but I am convinced that the old influences are never really lost, nor fully submerged, even for those of us who attempt to focus on a new style of music.
I guess what I am saying is, you can never completely get “under the skin” of a specific musical style, unless you have been immersed in it from youth. That doesn’t mean that you can’t play it, and enjoy it, and even become proficient in it. But we all bring our own personalities and styles to the table. And it is not a bad thing--isn’t the world better because we are all diverse individuals, bringing new and different things to every gathering of musicians? I love to play this music, and the fact that I do it with a New England American accent is fine by me--I couldn’t hide that if I tried. As the old sailor once said “I yam what I yam.”

Re: UNDER YOUR SKIN?

I wonder, if you don’t live in the culture of the music you want to learn, don’t you have to find mentors who are themselves immersed in it and can pass it on? That’s my experience--making every opportunity to tap into the Irish musicians whose paths crossed mine. Luckily, Irish musicians tend to be the roving sort, so it was easy to rub elbows with some great musicians and converyers of the tradition.

I also drowned myself in the music through recordings. Commercial cds, radio programs, and bootleg session tapes helped a lot and let me listen over and over, digging into the nuances and details. But this was secondary to listening to and playing with real people who had learned their craft directly from some past master.

In short, it’s more than just learning the instrument and the tunes. It’s about becoming an active link in the chain of music that’s passed from Scully Casey to Bobby Casey to Kevin Burke, say, or John Doherty to Mairead Mhaonaigh, or Martin Rochford to Cait Reed, etc.

You never know where you might run into a great Klezmer or Gypsy musician. One of the best Gypsy fiddlers I’ve ever heard was playing a summer-long gig at Disney’s Epcot Center. Imagine learning Gypsy fiddle and Hungarian from an A-Tier player…in Orlando Florida USA. You just never know.

I played a lot of different kinds of music before settling on ITM, i.e. renaissance, medieval, baroque, Middle Eastern, classical, jazz and rock. My introduction to ITM was hearing it on the radio (the Chieftains) and mistakenly thinking it was renaissance music. It was this misconception that plunged it into my skin like a hypodermic needle. It wasn’t long before I realized it wasn’t renaissance music, but the damage had already been done.

After that I pursued ITM like it was some sort of Holy Grail. No one in my hometown even knew it existed – so it was almost like a personal treasure. Eventually I met others who did know of it, but that didn’t halt my obsession at all. The pursuit of ITM then changed the course of my life, and this was all before visiting Ireland.

I moved to San Francisco because I knew there was an ITM scene there. It wasn’t long before I was taking lessons from Noel Hill and workshops with various fluters on tour. I worked hard to learn as much as I can, and I’m still doing that. The reward for all the effort is visiting Ireland and being able to participate in the music there.

My obsession for ITM has easily eclipsed all the other kinds of music I had dabbled with up until hearing it for the first time. Now I’m like an addict and it has consumed me and left me poor and without any real purpose in life other than drinking and playing tunes.

I played in bluegrass bands on the US East Coast for about 10 years. Luckily, I mostly played 5-string banjo, so it’s had little to no influence on my fiddle playing, which (other than a one-lesson detour into Old Timey) has always been focused on Irish trad.

Last year brought a wild experience to this isolated little town I call home. Eileen Ivers was in town so I went to her show. Enjoyed her virtuosity and personality, as always, even though I prefer my Irish trad without so much ‘world beat’ affixed to it. Also thoroughly enjoyed Ivan Goff’s pipe and flute playing.

Then I grabbed my fiddle at home and trucked down to the local pub for several hours of tunes with the granddaddy of Appalachian fiddle music ethnomusicology (and fiddling), Alan Jabbour. What a treat! We played a host of tunes common to both repertoires--Irish and Appalachian--both savoring the other’s approach to the tunes. Alan’s a great fiddler and a true gentleman, but that didn’t stop him from holding his own in the grand tradition of slagging each other, mostly about whether the Irish or American setting of a given tune was the better.

Dick, I’ll bet you *have* seen some Klezmer and Gypsy musicians and just didn’t know it at the time because they weren’t actually playing the music right then. It often amazes me how little we know about the people right in front of us, and how many beautiful secrets they might reveal if we just asked the right questions. Case in point--the other main Irish fiddler in my little town of 25,000, stuck in the middle of Montana USA. I’d known her for 4 to 5 years--coached her son in soccer, went on family hikes together, etc.--before either one of us knew the other played fiddle, and both Irish music at that. It wouldn’t be much more far fetched for you to learn that the postal carrier on your street is a talented Klezmer clarinetist…..

Heh, well my folk music roots are tied inexorably to the Texas old-time fiddling tradition. I’ve always loved the Americana-Irish style that comes along with all of the old ballads and songs that come down from the Appalaichan tradition, but I hadn’t ever really listened to Irish musicians playing Irish music. I’d always heard old-time musicians playing Irish-esque music. Wonderful but not the same. So I’m still very much in the process of trying to get the music under my skin. My school doesn’t allow for many opportunities to meet, play with, or listen to Irish musicians so I’m muddling along the best I can without guidance from anything but cds and sheet music.

I’ve always been fairly adaptable and I don’t find the cuts, rolls, etc all that hard to do, but the instictive knowledge of how to use them to dress up a tune isn’t there yet. So to the bowing differences are just now being realized. I enjoy a challenge though and since music is my life I don’t have too much of a problem adapting to listen for the nuances that tell me where to add what and when to add it.

My poor musically inclined hear has to be told to listen for music for enjoyment because I am first and foremost a critical listener, probably a result of learning my classical piano pieces by ear for the first two or three years of my lessons. Luckily for me that ear has been tuned to folk music and lets me hear the old-time tunes and pick them up in one full repeat of the song typically. Now I just have to do the same for Irish music, only without the benefit of having someone really close, I don’t have a car so distance is an issue, to take me under their wing and sometimes slow things down for me so I can find all the nit-picky little details that transform a piece into a tune.

Although my mother’s side of the family were Irish, there was no music as far as I know. My Scottish father played Highland Bagpipes and his father sang Music Hall songs semi professionally. However I didn’t know this ’til later in life.After the mock GCE exams at school there was a week or so when we could take records, games etc. into the classroom and whereas everyone else took Gene Vincent, Eddie Cochrane and Elvis, I used to bore everybody stiff with Hank Williams 78’s.
I remember hearing my first trad songs in a coffee bar in Nottingham in 1957, things like “The Great Speckled Bird” but it was not ’til 61 that I learned “Pit Boots” from Bob Davenport and sang it at the first folk club I attended in Bournemouth.
All my friends enjoyed and still enjoy the popular music of the 60’s but I couldn’t listen to pop later than 59/60 Rock and Roll changed about that time.
I had sung harmony in a Skiffle group as a replacement for a Tea Chest bass player who had gone down with ‘flu’ in 1956
and I think that period of 3 or 4 days of gigs sowed the seed for the rest of my life.
As for other countries trad music, they seemed to mean more to me than any of the pop stuff, although other than French and a couple of Eastern European tunes I never had the inclination to actually play them.
As for yodelling, did anyone ever hear the third Tobin brother Jimmy? It was quite a revelation after Nicolas and Paddy.
