Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

I have periods when I try and learn Irish fiddle from Kevin Burke’s Homespun DVDs and CDs. In them he states that there are rules to bowing…but he doesn’t know them, and that he bows a tune the way it suits him. Which is probably very Irish. Re: Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

Correct, depending on the player. They all have little tricks to make the tunes sound better.

But in general there are bowings used for different dances. You will find loads illustrated on YouTube by searching for ‘hoakum’.

There are two stages to learning to play trad on Violin. First one is just learning how to play the Violin, for example with the Suzuki books/CDs. The second consists of using a tutor such as Kevin Burke or similar to get a handle on the peculiar bowing used in Trad. You can usefully supplement that with more ‘bowing’ type books such as Matt Cranitch’s book The Irish Fiddle Book’. Matt is 100% traditional in style growing up in the south of the island in a musical home and learning the Classical Violin as well. His pedigree is one of the best out there. Highly recommend this to you even if you get but a couple of tunes out of it.

http://www.amazon.com/The-Irish-Fiddle-Book-Fiddle-Playing/dp/1900428679

P.S If you purchase this book please do get the CD that goes with it!

The book is very popular here in the USA and I know several who have used it to become excellent performers of Irtrad on the Violin. I use it myself and love some of his settings for tunes I used just bow any old way.

Another great book, I think, is Peter Cooper’s ‘Complete Irish Fiddle Player’ published by Mel Bay. This book has some lovely easy settings for common tunes you’ll encounter at sessions. His book goes very slowly from the simple updown bowing in simple jigs to advanced licks used in the harder settings. Again I would highly recommend this book.

But most important of all is developing good bow control because the music is in the bowing not the left hand fingering.

Happy fiddling :0)

Re: Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

The only ‘rule’ that can be said generally to apply, I think, is that in reels, beat 4 is usually a down-stroke, viz: one two three FOUR, one two three FOUR. To that can probably be added that the first and last notes of each part are usually a down bow.
Put the two together and you immediately have a problem:- ONE two three FOUR…
The way round it is usually by slurring the two notes preceding beat 4, which gives Irish reels that peculiar jaunty rhythm. It also results in the oft-seen phenomenon of the foot going down while the bow goes up (on beat 3, if you think about it). I believe it is not the slurring per se that is important, but it is the _change of direction_ of the bow that is the main factor, in that it facilitates the easy separation of beats 4, allowing a series of clean and distinct attacks which, over the length of the tune, serve as rhythmic anchors.

When I am learning a new reel, the first thing I do is consciously arrange my bowing so that beat 4 is a down-bow. I then go through the tune many times, playing every note as quietly as possible except for beat 4, which I play as loudly as possible. This fixes in my mind exactly which note falls on the beat, and also forces me to avoid the natural tendency to bow down up down up.
When I am more or less satisfied that I can do it, I go through again and ‘allow’ myself the odd up-bow where it feels right. The end result is different for each reel, but the general ‘feel’ is still there.

The same ‘rule’ could be applied to hornpipes; but given that their rhythmic feel is different, together with the multiplicity of triplets, the overall effect is not so obvious.

Polkas have their own rule, which is to group the beats two to a bow, with a pulse in the centre of each bow.

Re: Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

In the early stages of learning follow bowing marks or tutor’s instructions religiously, especially when the bowing pattern indicated seems unnatural or wrong. Later on you can completely ignore bowing marks and play in your own style. But the more different patterns and techniques you’ve learned in the early stages, the more varied and interesting your style will be. If you ignore bowing instructions and just play things the way that feels comfortable right from the start, you’ll land up always bowing everything the same way.

Re: Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

It’s not an exact science - any talk of “rules” is out of place IMO. Don’t forget that studying bowing indications in a book you are learning from sheet music, using primarily your eyes - and your ears maybe not at all. It is entirely possible to learn to bow and get a decent Irish sound primarily by listening, listening to audio recordings or live performances and trying different things to get the sound you want to achieve. The odd hint from someone or gleaned from watching a video won’t go amiss of course but this is a far cry from copying entire bowing patterns wholesale.

The other danger with any patterns given by others is that in trying to apply them you can easily lose sight of the fact that every tune is a melody as well as a, shall we say, device for imparting rhythm. You have to be wary of trying to make learned patterns fit tunes and be sure that the imparting-rhythm component doesn’t override the melodic component. The idea of working on every tune to make sure you have a down-bow on a particular beat is anathema to me.

@ Ben Hall -- to avoid any confusion (I hope!), this is the sort of thing I mean -- He starts with an up bow, and plays one TWO three FOUR, with two and four being quick, emphatic downbows. The pattern varies as he plays, which is pretty much unavoidable; but the general rhythm remains. It is the second of the downbows that I concentrate on in each group of four, in order not to fluff it while aiming to stress the first beat of the bar. If you are tapping your foot on beats 1 and 3, your foot is going up on 2 and 4 -- the opposite way to your hand.

Re: Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

I expect Kevin only included the detailed bowing because some students insist on it.

I think people get stuck on arbitrary rules like ‘always have a down bow on beat four’, or whatever. Perhaps it helps in the beginning, but ultimately such rules are little more than things your muscles remember. What does matter are the sounds caused by changing the direction of the bow. Cranitch’s book is especially nice since he has some good examples of cross string slurs, and slurring into the down beat, and such. Practicing some of these things can help prevent bad habits (built into your muscle memory) that might limit your ability to make the sounds you want. I recommend you avoid getting too stuck on learning ‘patterns’ of bowing -- although there are a few that are nice to know. Practice different directions for the techniques -- e.g., learn to do bowed triplets starting on the up bow. Ultimately, you will learn what effects you like as you hear other players, and you can use the ones you want.

Re: Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

I’ve participated in a couple of fiddle workshops with Kevin Burke over the years. He says that in Irish traditional fiddling, the primary role of the bow is simply the generation of sound, and its contribution to rhythm is relatively minor. He demonstrated an exercise where the player makes long slow bow strokes which cover multiple measures of the tune and bear no relationship whatsoever with what is happening in the music courtesy of the left hand.
He told us that whenever someone asks him how he has just bowed a phrase, he has no idea. He also says that he avoids laying more pressure downward from the bow onto the strings than the natural weight of the bow, which is a carefully-evolved device for making music.
That being said, his bowing technique is certainly second to none, and the pulse of his playing flows beautifully from the way he bows. His strokes do not mechanically follow the beats or the measures of the music, but the way they interact with the structure and rhythm of a tune gives his playing great nuance and musicality. You need to watch him, and not just listen to his CDs, to understand what I mean.

Re: Bowing rules in Irish fiddle music

I’m not of enough experience to speak as an expert, so I won’t presume to, BUT what I sense is that tunes should probably be treated on a case-by-case basis. Imagine if you prescribed to yourself a single way that you were going to bow all reels; imagine the difficulties, such as slurring through awkward string alternations etc. that you’d come up against!

Of course there are some common patterns. I was lucky to have a lesson with Kevin a few months ago, and the only specific pattern I remember him naming (passingly) was one I was already aware of, the ‘3-3-2’ - three quavers slurred, and again, then two bowed separately (though of course one of the 3s could also be bowed separately and the bow still end up in the ‘right’ direction by the end of the bar). Oh and also, I do remember him saying how he’ll often approach polkas in the manner mentioned by Gam above - slurred pairs of quavers, with a push on the second of each (Pete Cooper also suggests this in The Complete Irish Fiddle Player). I guess personal traits have a lot to do with the bow mechanics - everyone finds different things easy/difficult. I’ve noticed in videos how often Kevin ends a tune on an upbow, and of course, his off-beat driven style is formed of a lot of strong up-bows, whereas I’m sure many would assume a down-bow for the emphasis.

I find my own experience of working out a bowing varies; if I’m learning a tune from a single recording then I’ll often incline towards bowing it similarly too, often spending effort on trying to work out the slurrings by ear as well as the notes - not that I aim to appropriate anyone’s renditions, of course… If I know a tune more generally, either from back when I used tunebooks more often or I have multiple recordings, I experiment more and usually land on a bowing that remains my ‘basic’ for that tune, subject to variation of course.

Though I sometimes find that, having got into a pattern, I might bow it differently (through accident rather than variation) and prefer what I hear. I’ve recently had this with the B section of ‘Pigeon on the Gate’; I had settled into a fairly habitual pattern, and though I wasn’t sure it worked best I was being a bit too lazy to do anything about it. Nonetheless, a week or two back I, as if by accident, started bowing it a differently, which seemed to bring out much better accents, shape and lilt - all good things! So that’s how I generally play it now, though I shudder to imagine someone asking me to slow down and show them the pattern, because it seems to happen so automatically in this case (perhaps because I stumbled upon it) that I feel I don’t know exactly how I bow it. (Any thoughts on the amount of consciousness one actively applies to slurring/bowing?)

Sorry, this is getting long-winded - difficult to communicate this stuff by text alone. I guess my main thought is that though no tune could surely be said to have a ‘best’ sequence of slurs, separates etc., the bowing is most important in bringing out the character, shape, distinctive features or whatever of each tune, and all kinds of different patterns work in different applications. It’s enlightening to see how different two renditions of a tune can sound through bowing alone, which is the exciting thing.

How I bow depends on a couple things:

The Scottish fiddlers do seem to be technically better and play in a more considered, structured musical way. At least the ones in some of the Scottish fiddle competitions I’ve seen. Plenty of the contestants are clearly classically trained and are good by all accounts.

Some of the performances I’ve seen are quite lovely.

But then I also think that Irish reels sound better than Scottish ones. I always think the Scottish reels never really get going or hit that sweet spot in terms of rhythm and lift like some of the better Irish players can.
