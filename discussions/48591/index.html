A fiddle tutor once advised me that it didn’t matter a great deal if you played a slightly different setting in a session as it would all “blend in” somehow.

Even although I highly respected this woman as a musician(I’ll not name her), I wasn’t convinced that this was strictly true and having looked at some of the outrageous variations for some tunes on this site I think I was correct.

However, in many cases a different setting may well “blend in” but you have to learn to adapt. Either this will mean “finding” the correct notes on the fly or playing something which will fit it.
Of course, if you will be playing with these musicians on a more regular basis, you should learn their setting too. This needn’t necessarily be a conscious effort, of course, and if you are fairly experienced player it should come naturally.

As far as deciding which is the best setting, I tend to go for one I like which is often the one I’ve first heard or which attracted me to the tune in the first place. I may learn different settings later on and my own chosen setting may(often does) develop or change over the years.

Re: Morrison’s jig, versions

“Bum notes” are a different thing, of course, but for most of us it is a case of playing a “third” note instead of the one above or below in the scale or vice versa. This won’t usually be noticed as it should be in harmony with the correct note.

As I say, I tend to do this with many tunes over time and often only realise what I’ve been doing when I check the music. I’m sure this is how many of the different settings have developed over time.

There are other subtle things which will often work e.g. length of the notes …a crotchet as opposed to two semi quavers or triplet . For instance, some fiddlers might like to play a long note but mandolin and banjo players will often break down a longer note into triplets etc.
Again, this usually works OK as long as the overall timing isn’t affected.

Re: Morrison’s jig, versions

> When I looked it up here in the tunes list I found multiple listings, none exactly like the version I was looking for, but most had some vaguely similar phrases but over all, not really the same tunes, not even compared to each other.

Looking at the settings of Morrison’s jig on here, they’re actually much closer than you think! One issue may be that you’re not used to thinking about music, standardization, and variation in the way that it’s used in Irish music.

https://thesession.org/tunes/71

Let’s look at the first two bars of a couple settings

#1: |:E3 B3|EBE AFD

#2: |:E3 BEB|EBE AFD

#3: |:E2 E B2 B|E2 E AFD

#4: |:E>FE BEB|EBE AFD

Yes, all four of these have different notes. But believe it or not, most Irish players would see these as essentially the exact same! If you played all of those together in a session, it would sound perfectly fine.

If you break it down beat by beat (in other word, every grouping of three eighth notes), you’ll find that each grouping starts with the same note. In #1 and #2, the first grouping (1st beat, 1st bar) is just E for three beats. In #3, it’s 2 beats and then re-articulated on the third beat, just a simple rhythmic variation. And in #4, it’s embellished by quickly moving up a step to F# and then back down. The ear will hear that as very similar to staying on the E for all three beats as the other settings did.

The second grouping (2nd beat, 1st bar) in #1 is B held for three beats. In #2 and #4, that is embellished by going back down to the E. Most people hear this first bar of the tune as one that fits in an E minor chord, and so BEB is basically suggesting that chord to the ear. Similar to the move up, then down a step on the previous beat, the ear will hear this as functionally equivalent to staying on the B for all three beats. #3 does the exact same re-articulation for the B as it did for the E.

The third grouping (1st beat, 2nd bar) in #1, #2, and #4 does a similar alternation to what we just saw, only this time rather than BEB it’s EBE. Again, the ear will hear that suggested Em chord and it will feel similar to staying on the E for all three beats. #3 does that same re-articulation trick it has done for every beat.

The fourth grouping (2nd beat, 2nd bar) is the same for all four settings.

So again, even though the settings all look different they will sound very much the same in these first two bars. You can do this kind of analysis for every beat of every setting, and you’ll find they have a lot more in common than you think! Most settings of Morrison’s posted here could be played simultaneously without much bother. From what I’ve heard around sessions, #1 is closest to the “standard” version, but don’t let that stop you from learning another one!

Re: Morrison’s jig, versions

I have said this before*, but a signature characteristic of Irish Traditional Music (not universal, but pretty much inevitable when played in a session setting) is *heterophony* – i.e. many musicians playing the same melody ostensibly in unison, but each with their own small variations, without any pre-arranged consensus on who plays what. The result is that sometimes there is perfect unison, sometimes harmony and sometimes dissonance. This creates, in my opinion, a constant cycle of tension and resolution, which propels the tune forward; this is analogous to how, in chord-based genres of music, tension and resolutions it the harmonic progression are the propulsive force.

*Apologies to anyone who has read this, almost verbatim, countless times before – and if anyone thinks it is complete nonsense, I would be very interested to hear your counter-arguments.

Re: Morrison’s jig, versions

I usually try jackb’s version first because his notation is clear and straightforward. Some people try to notate every roll and cut or (worse) notate hornpipes with dotted “dots”.

So in the Morrison’s example, jackb (as do many of the others) show a dotted quarter note: 3E 3B, which most people roll which is notated explicitly by other contributors, or a flute/whistle player will play as E-breath-E. Since the rolls are idiomatic to the tunes, you don’t need to be explicit which just makes the notation more complex.

That does introduce the problem that people who don’t know the genre but are good sight-readers, will play as notated in the simple version, and it doesn’t sound right at all: “That’s not how Morrison’s goes!”

Re: Morrison’s jig, versions

Pretty much every tune you will find in this tradition will get played in different ways by different people or in different situations. This is an “aural” tradition, meaning that it is primarily passed on by ear, not by any form of notation. It’s a wonderful resource that we have many different settings notated in the tune archive here (and elsewhere), but early on in your experience in this music, it’s important to learn to not think of any particular setting of a tune to be “definitive”. Even modern tunes that were composed by someone will be played many different ways *by the person who composed it*!

So there’s no getting away from this. Your best bet is to embrace the diversity in the way that tunes are played. The way I encourage my students to deal with this is to have what you consider to be the “base setting” of any tune you learn. Generally, you can consider the way that you learned the tune (whether it be from a person, from a recording, or from notation) to be your base setting. That doesn’t mean that this is “definitive” in any way, other than to your basis of how the tune goes.

As musicians, we are expected to play the tunes differently each time. You’re not necessarily doing big sweeping melodic variations, more like you’re expressing the tune differently each time, suggesting different emotion from the melody, highlighting different phrases, or discovering some fun note inversions or ornaments that tell the same story in a catchy way… These variations are things that diverge from your “base setting” of the tune, but you can always fall back to the base.

So having all of these different settings notated can be a major plus, because you are shown different ways to play the same melody, and you can steal those ideas to incorporate into your own playing. One person’s base setting is another person’s variation. And over time, you will end up developing your own style of playing and varying tunes. And you will learn to spot when people are doing something different with a tune when you’re playing with them, and you’ll adjust what you do if it clashes with what other people are doing. (What inevitably happens when two experienced players that aren’t familiar with each other are playing a tune together is that they’ll both spot that the other player plays the tune differently, and they’ll both adjust the next time around, and still clash the second time… but that’s a fun moment that brings nothing but smiles, and maybe a quick word about which way to play it going forward…)

When you’re first starting out, you may not be at a point where you can easily adjust what you’re playing to match other people. But the key is that you notice that there is a difference. The next time that part comes around, you can try playing quieter to see if you can better spot what the difference is. But if the other players are more experienced, it’s more up to them to adjust to what you’re playing.

Late to the party.

If a dozen musicians (with experience in the tradition) would learn a new tune from one single setting, chances are that everyone would play the tune differently after a while (and probably every time they play it thereafter).

At the same time, if they all would have individual “home” settings of a tune, chances are that they’d play smth “in the middle of the road” if they were at the same session.

In short - with experience, one will deviate from the assumed generic “standard” version (or Urtext … ), and the same experience lets you adapt and adopt to whatever is going on around you.

As simple as that.
