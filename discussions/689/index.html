Re: Fiddle Classes at Willie Clancy

Well, I’ll tell you what I was told, but understand there are PLENTY of fiddlers that do not do what James Kelly does and they sound great doing it.

1) You can make a louder sound on the fiddle by either Increasing bow speed or bow pressure. James Kelly chooses pressure over bow speed for his playing style, which he learned from his father, the great John Kelly.

This requires a PERFECT bow hold technique to accomplish. James has an unusual bow grip as he places his thumb on the frog of the bow. He says it’s a better fulcrum for pressure. I’ve been experimenting with this and it does have some merits! Lastly, a classical player who attended the class suggested I should curve my thumb into the bow (as you might when you pick up an object from the ground). That has helped my control greatly. I’ve noticed that the thumbs of some good fiddlers do have a degree of curved thumb.

2) Tunes are ‘built’ by allowing played notes to echo or ring onwards as you play the next note.

3) Irish jigs are played ‘straighter’ than I thought they were. Instead of DAH-da-da, they are played like dah-du-da. (If that made any sense!)

4) Complicated triplet runs (not bowed triplets or scratches) require lighter pressure on the bow to help them sound crisp.

Anyway, hope you’ve been able to get some free time for yourself Zina.

Jill/Zina,

Not sure what more to say about ‘building tunes’. More than once James Kelly reprimanded the class for thinking too liniear. If I can paraphrase him, he said ‘It is not one note, and then another note, and then another note after that!’. He drew a diagram showing how the sound of each note should overlap the note that follows. He didn’t mention chord structure.

I’m just beginning to work with this, but I can tell you from listening to James Kelly that this technique allows the music to sound more complex and interesting.

The oddest thing about all the sessions in Ireland was how slowly the tunes were played. Compared to everybody in Texas, Boston and San Fran, these guys in Ireland and moving through molasses. Roughly 1/2 to 3/4 slower than ceili dance speed. Even the Germans and Swedes held the same pace. Why didn’t anyone tell the Americans?

Anyway, the ‘building tunes’ or ‘layering notes’ method would likely benifit most when tunes are played at a much slower pace than at the pace most Americans play this music.

Hope that helps.

As to speed, it depends really on the session. I’ve been at some Irish sessions where the music whipped along every bit as fast as in the States. I personally love the sessions best where the music tempo rises and falls, depending on the music and the mood. Sometimes clipping reels along is just the best! Sometimes taking the time to wring everything out of a few tunes is perfect.

I’ve mentioned my cathedral organist friend who feels that the “right” tempo for any piece of music is the tempo that you play it best at. I’d guess that applies here!

I quite like the thumb on the outside bottom of the frog…I don’t usually use it, I seem to lose a bit of subtlety when I do, probably from lack of practising with it that way (oh, and lack of good playership, I’m sure!), but it is a great way for me to remind myself to relax my bowing hand; I tend to use it when I’ve discovered my hand aching from clenching up while I was concentrating on other things. The new bow has a different wrapping from my old one, in a different place; I need to either rewrap or change where I’m holding the bow; the tip of my thumb tends to go numb. Perhaps the Kelly hold is in order until that problem goes away…
