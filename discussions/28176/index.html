Instrument personalities 2

Lately i’ve been given the opportunity to tell people how i feel about the instruments i’ve heard and played. Guitar really inspired me. A girl asked me if i played Guitar and this is what came out…

“Yes, i play guitar. It’s a really sweet instrument. It’s timbre is really bright and rich in tone, but it’s so soft and gentle. But it can get loud and rhythmic. Perfect for storytelling. It’s also really intimate. One of the few instruments that you can cradle and cuddle up with. And it’s crisp voice is quite welcoming.” After this i started thinking about the other instruments. Then that thread “The quality of the different keys” made me think more about it.

This is all i could come up with…

“The Fiddle, being one of the more vocal instruments, is a *talkative, but very *charismatic type. It likes to *ramble and *gossip, but it’s also a good *sweet talker. It could sing if it wanted to, but it’d rather *lilt, and unlike me, it knows how to *deliver a joke. I’d rank it’s charisma alongside the jazz instruments.”

If your instrument was an entertainer, and you had to write a review about him/her, what would you say?

Re: Instrument personalities 2

I’m fascinated by the psychology behind this nonsense.

I think this kind of personification can only come from people who, for what ever reason, can’t seem to get to grips with their instrument. An inanimate object is, by definition, inanimate … and only becomes animated when controlled by the animator, i.e. the player.

Maybe there’s a confusion in peoples’ minds over the meanings of the words “fault” and “faulty”? It seems quite plain to me that provided your instrument isn’t faulty, then any shortcomings (or, of course, any positive things) in the music made on it is entirely the fault of the player. But how people turn this simple fact around to say such nonsense as “My mandolin is rather quiet and will never dominate the conversation” is astonishing. Is it the mandolin’s fault that it’s quiet? Is it the fault of the mandolin? Is it a fault of the mandolin? Is the mandolin faulty? Surely you don’t have to be that bright to realise the mix up?

Mandolins are quiet. End of.
And any use of the instrument must, of course, embrace this characteristic, but what fascinates me is how a characteristic of an inanimate object can be confused with a personality trait.

I’m afraid the no “pretty raven lady” could ever take the edge off of the negativity I have such bloody rubbish.

“I think Ptarmigan has the right idea and we should return to discussing how all instruments have distinct personalities and whether or not these instruments (or the people who play them) need psychiatric help.”

Ok, well since i started the thread, i’ll say this.

All instruments sound different because they and are made differently and played differently. They all have different timbres. They all make music in different ways. They are all made out of different materials. And they all have different pitch as well as timbre ranges. Which is why i say they have different personalities. Is this really a ridiculous idea? No, because people have different voices. Their voice boxes are shaped different, as well as their bodies. And they have different accents and talk at different pitch ranges.

Pianos are stringed percussion instrument, whose strings lay in a big wooden box and are struck by mallets from pressing down on keys. It is one of the few instruments that reach from the bottom of the bass clef to the near top of the treble clef. The strings are always hit the same way. It also uses three different pedals to influence how the keys sound. Damper for quieter, Bass Sustain, and Sustain. All of these things influence why the pianos sound the way they do.

Lets move to the other stringed instruments,

Violin/Fiddle, whose strings lie outside of a small wooden box, and are bowed, and touched to change pitch so it sounds significantly different from a piano. Has a high pitch range because of it’s small size. Violas are lower, then Cellos are lower.

Other stringed instrument. Guitars, mandolins, bouzoukis, all also have strings that lie outside of a wooden box. But instead of them being bowed, they are strummed or picked. Not only this, but they have different types of strings as well. Nylon, Steel, Gut, and others i don’t know about. They all vary in sizes.

Now to the instruments that are blown into. instruments that have no strings, aren’t in wooden boxes, and aren’t plucked and strum. Pipes and Horns. I don’t know much about these. All i can say is, they are all shaped different, some have reeds, some don’t. Some are made out of wood, some metals like brass. All of these things influence how they sound.

Then you have the free reeds. They use keys and blown air, but no strings.

Drums. Bang Bang, Boom Boom, Pop Pop, Click Click… whatever.

So as you all *ALREADY KNOW*, all instruments sound different because they are made differently, and played differently, which is why i say they have different personalities.

We had a chat the other night about how quite a lot of the people we knew who play this music are engineers, scientists, and IT folk.

On thinking about it, I realize people personify nature and inanimate objects all the time. If you’ve ever read any mountaineering books, ever, and people are describing epics that involved storms, avalanche, rockfall, and the like, they frequently call the mountains angry, vengeful, indifferent to suffering, all sorts of things. I’ve also been known to seek out “friendly” climbs. Half the time you don’t even realize you’re doing it, such as when you say things like, “The weather is being a bastard today.” I do it with the pipes as well: “They’re just in a mood today. This is as in tune as they’re getting.” Or on a good day: “They seem fairly happy.”

In all of the above examples, I don’t think the personification is overly romantic and stupid; I think it’s just a way of relating to your environment (even as disparate things as an avalanche falling on your head or some out of tune bagpipes). Some days you get glorious sunshine in the mountains, some days you get horrific weather and things falling on you. Personification seems to be a way of coping with the changeability and occasional ferocity of nature.

Overdoing the anthropomorphication is bad on any level, even when dealing with animals. With an inanimate object like an instrument, the only damage you do is to yourself - you sound a bit numptyish. The most useless people at training horses and dogs are those who completely anthropomorphise them and assume they are just four legged children. That results in pretty badly behaved horses and dogs.
