Re: Memorization tips

There are plenty of approaches to memorizing tunes. (Google ‘memorizing music’.) My approach is to listen to the tune enough to be able to hum it, and then learn to play it phrase by phrase. Play it frequently enough to get it into my fingers as well as in my head. And then go back to it regularly, to reinforce the memory. It comes down to listening and practice. The amount required of each depends on the tune.

Re: Memorization tips

If you think about (non-ITM) music you know, let’s say something you listen to on the radio and with which you can sing along, did you ever sit down and try to “memorize” it? No, probably not. You just heard the song enough times that you knew it. At first you were able to sing the refrain, then maybe later on, the first verse, then the second verse, and then there were a couple words which you just could not understand, so maybe you went and cheated and looked up the lyrics online, and eventually, you could sing every word.

Same principle applies here, I think.

You have to listen to whatever tune it is that you’re trying to learn over and over, until you can hum it. Then you can try to play it, or play along with the recording to which you’ve been listening. You’ll probably be able to play a lot less of the notes than you think, so go back and listen some more, play it again, get a few more notes, repeat. That’s mostly how I go about this. It’s not really “memorization” in the sense of learning the tune note-by-note. For some tunes, when there are notes I just can’t quite hear or get, I will take a look at the sheet music to see what they are… analogous to Googling the lyrics for a song.

Re: Memorization tips

‘Playing by ear’ is a funny term. Certainly for the vast majority of session players, if they join in a tune, it means they already know it. Most sessioners wouldn’t say they had a tuned ‘memorized’ but if they say they ‘know’ it it means the same thing. They’re not just picking tunes up on the fly, most of them.

I think a word about different learning styles is appropriate here, too. Visual, aural, kinetic type words and concepts. For me, and for many other players, I know a tune best by feel, not by sound or by what notes look like on a page. Obviously the notes on the page are not the music, that’s as true in ‘classical’ as it is in Irish, but for more players that just me they are a good way to pick up a new tune, IF you already understand the style, which only comes from listening (this is also true of ‘classical’).

To learn lots and lots of tunes, learn one first. Then another. Soon you’ll know fifty thousand.

Re: Memorization tips

There is a distinction between ‘memorising’ and ‘knowing’.
The former implies, as David50 says, remembering what note comes next, and is not a very good way to learn tunes. It’s like memorising a list of somebody’s good and bad points in the hope of getting to know him, rather than spending time in his company. You might get a rough idea of that that person is like, but you won’t really ‘know’ him.
I sometimes liken it to telling jokes -- you can hear a joke, then tell it to someone else without ‘memorising’ it -- you just remember it. You know what the joke is, rather than what the words are and in what order. Similarly with a tune, as you learn it, the notes group themselves into shapes or phrases, and the phrases arrange themselves to form a general overall pattern that makes the tune.
When you are first learning it, a new tune might appear as just a sequence of notes. but as you play it (or listen to it) more and more, the internal feelings that go with the shapes will become apparent, and then the overall structure, which is what you remember when you know the tune.
Sometimes, of course, you can get side-tracked into playing part of another tune; but that doesn’t happen too often, and once you recognise it, it is easy to leave yourself a mental note such as, ‘It goes up here’, or, ‘D then G, not D then A’, or as in the joke analogy, ‘after he takes the pills,’ or whatever tripping point is.
It boils down to cferrie said: ‘Practice practice and more practice’, but I would rather say, ‘play play and more play.’ Think of the tunes the way you would your friends -- first you have to meet them, then you have to get to know them by having conversations, not just by memorising their details (much as Google would have us think otherwise). Some of them will turn out to be attractive but quite shallow, and others will have hidden depths that aren’t immediately apparent. If you spend enough time with them, you will remember all you need to know.

Re: Memorization tips

It’s not remembering you want, it’s forgetting - *forget the dots* (for now, anyway). Learning tunes from sheet music will not help you much at this stage - you *must* learn to learn by ear. Chances are, you already can.
To quote David50 above:
“I just have to know the tune the way I know how to sing ‘Happy Birthday’.”
My guess is, you never had to learn Happy Birthday - either words or tune - from a book; furthermore, I expect you could probably play it on any of your instruments without looking at the music and without much, if any, ‘working out’.

Granted, most tunes you’re likely to be playing in a session will be a bit more complex than Happy Birthday (some of them not really that much more complex, in fact). But it is only a matter of refining the same skills that enable you to sing or play back the simplest of songs. If you are taught to rely on reading music, these skills very likely get neglected and thus are not fully developed.

The way to develop and refine your skills is simply to use them: find a recording of a tune you like, listen to it through a few times and see how much of it you can play back (maybe none, at this stage, but it lets you know where you’re at). Then stop and start the recording as many times as necessary to fill in the gaps. (You could write it down as you go, if it helps - I used to do this). By the time you get it, you’ll have listened so intensively that it will probably be pretty well ingrained. If you can’t recall it a day, or an hour, later, it will probably only take a few notes to prompt you. The more tunes you learn this way, the quicker and easier it gets.

When you get to a session, take a recording device with you (N.B. It is polite to ask the other musicians before switching it on). Then you have a new set of recordings to learn from at home. You will find, eventually, that you can play tunes you never even intended to learn, just through hearing them repeatedly over a period of months or years.

Re: Memorization tips

The best memorization tip I can give you is learn the tune by ear in your head. Get some recordings(which is very manageable) or listen to other live players(which is more of a luxury than just a convenience).

7 repetitions is the magic number for long term memory! Something I do to make sure a tune sticks, is to try to recall it back to memory after not thinking about it for a while. Like, listen to the tune a few times, then turn off the music, and try to think of how the tune went note for note. Then allow yourself to lose focus for a few minutes. Then try to recall the tune to see how much of it you still remember. It sounds like a lot but you can easily do this entire process several times within a few minutes.

If you’re extreme about sticking a time in your head, keep recordings of you really close by, so when you can’t recall it, you can easily go back to listen to the parts you forgot.

Also, after know a bit of the tune, try randomly lilting it, and then check your accuracy by listening to the recording of it.

Another thing I do is I learn my tunes in sets(or I make up my own sets of tunes that I like to put together). That way, if I remember the first tune, I also remember the 2nd and 3rd tune.

Last thing, all you have to do is pay attention to how well you’re recalling the tunes. If you’re bad at recalling, listen more.

Re: Memorization tips

Well there’s research on this topic…which basically says, as do most musicians, that input is all (as it is with acquiring a language). The more you listen, the more you can do. Music, like language, is procedural, not declarative, memory, so all those ppl who say “listen by learning” are right.

We have to distiguish between two kinds of learning:

a) motor skills. Only way to get these is through practice, and they are relatively easy to get.

b) “getting” the tune, ie having a mental soundtrack of it-- mostly done by listening to it. To “understand” a tune (i.e. get it so it feels familiar) you need to hear it (and be focused on it) around 20 times to get to the automatic recognition stage. Once you’re there, you’ll need another 100 or so repetitions to be able to play it (assuming also some practice in getting fingers mouth etc to “do the moves” on your instrument).

One of the reasons why practice helps us acquire is NOT because we are developing “muscle memory” or anything like that (MM matters only a tiny bit)…it’s because, in playing it, we are also HEARING it. All the practice in the world is useless if you don’t have a “feel” for how the tune should sound.

Honestly, the best things to do are

a) go to sess MINUS cell phone (so you stay focused); listen to what you don’t know; ask names of ones you wanna learn
b) go home and get dots for ONE tune per week to learn (after listening to it a few times for “feel”). Learning too much at a time is bad.
c) anything else that you like, find it on youtube or get mp3 or whatever, and listen to it a ton.

The most magical moment is when you find yourself playing along to a tune and you realise you’ve never sat down and learned it :-)

Re: Memorization tips

To help memorize the music faster, get your eyes away from the page sooner. I say it’s great to learn from sheet music, as long as it’s a tool you are using in addition to ear training. But you will get more benefit from sheet music if you play through reading it the absolute minimum number of times possible before moving your eyes away, look at a wall, and try playing the tune as far as you can get without looking. Do this before you think you know it and be patient with yourself.

As you are looking away from the music and playing think to yourself about the patterns in the music. Is there a particular phrase that acts like a hook and draws your attention and triggers your memory? Is there a certain part that doesn’t make sense to you? Try playing variations or listening to a few good recordings to try to hear what the nonsensical part sounds like. Ask friends (“what do you play here?”).

Another thing I do is I learn tunes from albums I own. I make a point of going to Custy’s Music online and ordering a few CD’s every couple years or so. I focus on CD’s by solo trad musicians. I live in the country so I often drive long distances. I have had the same CD from Caitlin NcGaibhain (I do not remember how to spell her name though) in my car CD player for months. It plays non stop when I am driving for hours. I guarantee you I can hum along to every tune on that CD, I can also begin lilting the next tune before the track switches. It begins playing in my head immediately in the following silence of each track. I don’t play most of them but I guarantee when I finally learn them I will not struggle with remembering the tune after that point. I also find I make less mistakes when I learn this way. First listening to the recording of the tune over and over and over.

Re: Memorization tips

Also as creature said. When you learn by the listening method, you might find it helpful to write your own sheet music transcriptions. I do this because after I’ve listened to a particular setting by a particular player or players many times over on a CD or even at a session, I find that the sheet music I find online is not 100% accurate to the setting I want to learn. I find having a good basic sheet music of a tune helps me learn it and memorize it (because I know when to look away) so for pretty much every tune I learn I write it down with ABC in ABC Explorer. I don’t go back and read them very often but just the act of writing it down for yourself does magic with your memory, the same way it does when you take good notes in class. Many college students that take extensive classroom notes, will barely read their notes, because by writing down things that they heard they have imprinted things into their memory. Writing can trigger our brains into focus.

Also because I am generally obsessed with tunes I find myself humming tunes throughout the day. This is also another very important aspect of the learning process. Play even when you are not playing, keep a few favorite tunes on repeat in the back of your mind. Imagine the tips of your fingers on the instrument as you hear each note in your head. Do this while walking/driving/shopping and even when you are trying to listen to somebody speaking to you. Don’t tell people you didn’t hear what they just said because you were thinking of a tune. This is important. This is just for you.

And the more you repeat any learning exercise the easier it gets. That’s the beauty of it all. If you practice right, it will be no time at all to become a master of your instrument and repertoire.

Re: Memorization tips

I read sheet music, so I depend heavily upon doing so, but I also listen, and my hearing is getting better. If I’m already familiar with a tune it is much easier to learn/memorize/know (LMK) than one that is new to me.

I was taught a usable method for LMK’ing a new tune by my violin teacher, Tom Obrien (of St Louis, MO, USA) - Start your practice session working on the tune you want to LMK. When you have reached your usual stopping point, whether a set time, a set number of repititions, or even a point where you feel you aren’t making headway, stop playing it and go on to something else, but keep playing. After chasing the tune out of your head by others, return to it and play it some more, and then END YOUR PRACTICE session. The tune you want to LMK must be the first and last tune for each practice session.

Repeat these steps for three days and you should have the tune LMK’d. If not, go it another day. It works. I make the new tune one of the others that I turn to after the first go-round with a newer one, which cements it in place.
