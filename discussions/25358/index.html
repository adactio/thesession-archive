Identity crisis!

I am normally just a lurker/stealer of tunes here, so please forgive me for a bit of a ramble, but your comments would be welcome… (at the risk of re-igniting that long discussion about ETM from Nov. last year ;-) )

I have been playing Scottish/Irish music on the mandolin for around 30 years - now to a reasonable standard. Long ago, I press-ganged a few mates (mostly classical musicians) into playing a few gigs; they lost interest after a while, but I was hooked…

I have been a ‘lone wolf’ almost ever since, (mostly due to a lack of people wanting to play the same music - all efforts to find them failed…) and my music nearly dried up entirely - only so much reward from twanging away to oneself on the sofa… Recently, the purchase of a Tascam recorder has brought things back to life as at least I can multi-track on the various instruments that I play (mandolin/octave/bouzouki etc.), and I have produced some respectable results, given the lack of ‘core’ instruments.

I was recently in Edinburgh and got into a session or two , which was really great (anyone reading who was at The Tass on 4th Aug?), and they mostly seemed fine, if a little surprised, about a Sassenach so keen on playing ‘their’ music ;-). Also just so much great music going on that it seemed like the promised land compared to what is available here locally… It was just so good to be able to play ‘properly’ again - made me realise what I have been missing. But it also reminded my just how indigenous trad music is to particular places or people.

Coincidentally, a few days later, I bumped into a guy locally who also plays mandolin, and I went to a session in Chelmsford as a result - fine, but I just couldn’t get very excited about the overwhelmingly English music being played. I was told there is some ITM played, but apparently it is very sporadic.

Having done some trawling, I came across that epic discussion from late last year about ETM, which mostly confirmed my own prejudices! On the other hand, both a sense that, as a through-and-through Englishman, I have always played ‘somebody else’s music’ and pure practicality suggest that I would be better off playing ETM, even though I don’t really want to!

The two of us are trying to set up a session locally here, though I suspect it will probably end up being largely ETM, which is his thing anyway, but won’t solve my own issue, nor the advice I have been given before of finding like-minded musicians by going to local sessions.

I have researched the scope for trying to find ETM of a type that could have some fire put in its belly as does I/STM but it doesn’t really seem possible - I guess it would have been done by now if it were…

Ultimately I would like to get back into some kind of occasional performing, but even trying to compromise and play a bit of everything to keep everyone happy somehow doesn’t feel right - as is widely known, a camel is a horse designed by committee… There is the issue not only of finding suitably-skilled collaborators but also finding people who want to hear the music locally, apart from as a novelty act at a wedding :-(

So what to do (short of relocating myself several hundred miles, which isn’t really possible right now)? Any ideas? Am I being too defeatist? Or too hung-up about not having the right background? And what do you all really think (especially the Celts amongst you) of guys like me trying to play ‘your’ music like this? Are we doomed only ever to be second-best? Previous attempts to solve the practical issues have just seen me back on the sofa twanging away again…

And incidentally, if there is anyone in the Chelmsford/Colchester/Braintree discobabe-zone out there who likes the sound of what I do, please do get in touch…

Re: Identity crisis!

ian stock - Rest assured English music can really zizz. You just have to find the sort that does, and people who can do justice to it.

For far too long English music was dominated by the stultifying clobbering of lumbering tunes by Ashley Hutchings and similar, and any sentient being gave it the bum’s rush. Things have changed, though. The younger generation have brought musicianship a long way on, and the advent of better melodeons than the Hohner Pokerwork has helped. Old manuscripts keep surfacing and getting published or into circulation. Much nimbler tunes - often 3/2s - are coming out of them than the 2/4 polkas that characterised ‘English music’ in the postwar folk revival. I reckon these were culled from a c20 English tradition that was very run-down, and that in earlier periods English trad was a lot more comparable to Irish or Scottish in what it was capable of doing.

I’ll stop a mo, and maybe come back with other thoughts, such as resources.

Re: Identity crisis!

ian stock - I can give you a Nick’s-eye view on music in the North-East, where I live (Durham City).

I’m (mostly) middle-class London English by descent: no Irish or Scottish background, anyway. Or Geordie, indeed. When I heard Irish and Northumbrian music in late adolescence, I just knew I wanted to play it and took it from there. I’ve been through my share of self-consciousness about difference in class or national background from those the music has most appeared to belong to. If you respect the music itself and are not just hi-jacking it to take the p*ss / make a splash with some band of wackoes / concoct a bogus identity, then as far as I see where you come from doesn’t matter.

Scotland and North-East England are not extensions of each other. Cross the Border either way, and you go into a markedly different country. If North-East politicians and others refer to Scotland, it’s invariably just to moan about Scotland getting a bit more Barnett money than the NE. But yes, there is musical input from Scotland. Quite a few pipe bands, fiddle and accordion clubs (a Scottish phenomenon), Scottish dancing groups, and an input of a lot of Scottish tunes into the Northumberland tradition in and after the Fifties as a result of the influence of Jimmy Shand (whose band was welcomed in Northumberland, because they were willing to adapt their playing to the rhythms and speeds the Northumberland dancers were used to - they differ sometimes from those in Scotland).

Shetland tunes in particular were taken up, because many have a rhythm ideal for the Northumberland ‘rant’ step. In a 4/4 tune, the emphasis of this is something like as follows:

..and-a | ONE TWO THREE, and-a | ONE TWO -etc.

Of course, N/land musicians could also pick up Scottish radio programmes, and go to gigs over the Border.

The fiddle and Northumbrian smallpipe tradition survived in Northumberland possibly because of deep isolation. Most of the county stayed archaic and rural, with no mass immigration. The gentry liked and kept up the smallpipes. I should add, though, that great pipers like Billy Pigg and Tom Clough came from the county’s industrialised South-East part.

The epicentre of much of the fiddle and smallpipes tradition, though, was Tyneside in the early c19. The pipes were developed and experimented with in various ways, and the ace fiddler and composer James Hill was active. The music simply lived on in the country long after it had been abandoned on Tyneside. For all its loneliness and distances, rural Northumberland always seems to have had an umbilical link with Newcastle and Tyneside.

The NE’s industrial areas - really, the rest of the region, away from rural Northumberland - were the ones that attracted the Irish immigrants, from the late 1840s famine years onwards. Many settled on Tyneside. They were accused by coal-miners of undercutting and strike-breaking and may well have avoided the mines by and large. What they did go in for, notably, was working in the new iron and steel works in County Durham and in or around Middlesbrough on Teesside. Their descendants are still there, in many cases maintaining a Catholic tradition.

There have long been an Irish centre in Newcastle, and I dare say there are others in the area. The Newcastle one certainly hosts gigs and has, at least, hosted sessions. I don’t know if there has been a continuing tradition of trad music played by immigrant Irish and their descendants here since the c19 or not - quite possibly. But it hasn’t been like New York, or Boston…

County Durham got Methodism, a superb brass band tradition, and choirs: trad effectively did not survive as a living tradition, and has been re-activated since the folk revival.

Regarding song, Tyneside has a characterful tradition of c18-19 ditties, well recorded by revival bands such as The High Level ranters; otherwise, a sombre ballad tradition dating back (at least) to the time of the Anglo-Scottish wars suddenly surfaced again in c19 Co. Durham to record disasters in the mines.

I’ll catch breath - may come back to sketch out the present scene.

Re: Identity crisis!

ian stock - I’ll try to summarise what has gone down in the NE, music-wise, in fairly recent times.

By the late 60s / 70s, there were huge numbers of folk clubs in the region. Stefan Sobell, living near Hexham, (re-)invented the cittern, which became popular with musicians in the NE and Scotland in particular. The High Level Ranters, though not copies of The Dubliners, publicised their local Tyneside / Northumbrian songs and music in a comparable way.

The Irish trad revival of the Seventies created devotees in the area, myself included. Some have Irish background, others do not. Some are seriously good musicians.This music has hung on in. Sessions apart, there is a thriving Irish dance scene, and established ceilidh bands have a great deal of custom. But many sessioners have nothing near where they live, and have to travel a long way to a session.

More recently, education projects like Folkworks, Folkestra, the Folk Music Degree course at Newcastle University and a lot of work in schools in Northumberland have considerably upped the numbers of kids learning to play trad and of adults going to classes to start playing or to improve their game.

As far as I can see, the musical approach in much of this is pretty eclectic, with tutors coming from all over and the gamut of traditional instruments being learnt. I imagine each student sets out to imitate the player or band who grabs him / her the most…

The Irish trad revival has put an Irish accent into a lot of music played in the NE, including an uptake of simple-system flute, whistle, uilleann pipes and Irish bouzouki. It has been ‘base camp’ for a number of players. But it is one of various ingredients in the music. The tunes and playing-styles these days often seem to come, not only from Northumberland (Willy Taylor’s fiddling, especially) but from Scotland, Shetland and Canada, with Scandinavia, France and the USA thrown in.

Apart from Irish immigrants coming to the NE, the region in general had little historical contact with Ireland - although the Protestant settlers may well have included people from the NE. But many over the years have left the NE to emigrate to the New World. Cumbria in the past had more contact with Ireland, because in the c18-early c19 Whitehaven was, relatively speaking, a much more important port than it is now.

The North-East - as far as my experience tells me - is trad-friendly. If not played monstrously or obtrusively, Irish, Scottish and related traditional music seems to go down well. It seems at home here.

Maybe I can share one heart-warming story, though: for reasons that do not matter here, a few years ago I booked Flook to play to our sixth form at school (in the days before public sector cuts!) There was a huge amount of bravado/scepticism/condescention amongst the students in the run-up to the performance - but as soon as Flook started to play, the place just fell silent as the musicianship shone through. They particularly liked John-Joe Kelly’s bodhran playing.

They played for twice as long as intended and took loads of questions from a genuinely impressed audience. One of my finest ever professional moments - and proved my theory right, that what did happen would happen. I chose Flook mostly through a personal connection, but also because I felt their identity would show that it is not all old men with beards and sandals ;-)

The most memorable thing was the confusion and curiosity shown by this commercially-hardened audience that these people played music not to be rich or famous but just because they liked it. I especially remember one student asking them what other artists they listened to - and then being baffled by the fact that they had never heard of any on the list that was the reply! They just couldn’t get their heads round the fact that some people just do different things…

I know I made at least two young folk enthusiasts that day, one of whom, having just finished her A levels, has spent the summer at the festivals and has just taken up the melodeon.

Re: Identity crisis!

ian stock - I’ll append a few more notes on the NE, and its instruments.

The NE is not at base a ‘Celtic nation’, as some assume. After the Romans it was thoroughly dominated by Anglians (Anglo-Saxons), who in turn were made subject to a Norman ascendancy. Scandinavian settlement was largely limited to the South of County Durham.

By the early c11, the Kingdom of Scotland had reached pretty well its present border, overrunning a sizeable Anglian population in Southern Scotland and also adopting its language as the official one of the court. So, a strain of common culture between Southern Scotland and Northumbria is sometimes to be seen. But the relationship between England and Scotland, as states, was dominated by distrust and war for hundreds of years. This governed life near the Border.

The Border ‘reiver’ clans lived in freakish, lawless conditions. They did so by subsistence farming, raiding and rackets. Their loyalties were quite situational. Their many surviving ballads (some with tunes) seem to come from one people rather than from representatives of two different national blocs. The accession of James I/IV to both Kingdoms put a stop to the reiving.

In the Middle Ages the harp was prized by the Scottish court. It also engaged ‘Inglis’ (English)bagpipers, according to one document. The Great Highland Bagpipe was invented in the c15, I have read. This was a Gaelic development, I assume, and presently adopted in Highland Scotland and Ireland generally.

I think the early English bagpipes and the harp were fairly certainly in Med./Tudor Northumbria, though I know no evidence except ‘Harper-’ place-names. The harp was popular with the ruling class in England too, and certain NE tunes seem to me like quintessential harp tunes.

From the c18 onward far more is known about local instruments, and examples survive. By then, bellows-driven bagpipes were being developed. (Bellows-blown bagpipes were a French innovation, on the Pastoral Pipes of c17+.) Two main lines were followed up:

(1) The Border Pipes, conical open-ended chanter, open fingering, scale identical to the GHB;

(2) The Northumbrian Smallpipes, cylindrical closed-ended chanter, closed fingering, scale originally one major octave, subsequently extended through making use of keys.

The Border pipes seem to have been in widespread use across Southern Scotland and Northern England. What this seems to demonstrate is that the GHB scale was seen as the most versatile range of notes one could have on a chanter played solely by fingering open holes. So knowledge of the GHB had got well around - although the instrument itself had been suppressed in Ireland and in Scotland after Culloden. (It was adopted not that long after, however, as a military instrument).

The Border pipes could/can function at volume as a dance music or gathering instrument (though obviously not as loud as the GHB). Their playing died out in the c19, but has been revived. Possibly the rise of the fiddle and free reed instruments, or the domestic or pub piano, saw them off. Or the shell-likes of the Victorians simply found them uncouth.

The Border pipe repertoire, resurrected from manuscripts, again seems to have a kind of unity to it: it’s difficult or impossible to say which side of the Border a lot of the tunes come from. In contrast to the surviving English and Northumbrian trad of the later c20, which was very much Major in key, a lot of these tunes are modal.

The N/brian smallpipes I imagine were based, scale-wise, on some basic English mouth-blown bagpipe. It was not long before the scale was extended by adding keys to the chanter - I would say, to give some point to playing the thing at all, but possibly to enable it to rival or accompany the fiddle as an indoor performance instrument. The one-octave phase of the instrument left a legacy of agile tunes that occasionally give the weird effect of being well over an octave in scope! A smallpipes chanter with 7 keys can cover most of the present-day repertoire, running (if it’s a G set) from low D up to second-octave b with a c# thrown in; more keys can get you low C#, C nat and B,, and/or more accidentals. I use the G scale as an example as it’s familiar, but the N/brian smallpipes are actually mellower and more generally played in F.

As far as I’m aware, the N/brian smallpipes tradition has lasted unbroken. But it did not colonise a wider area, in the era before the folk revival.

I’ll take a break, and maybe come back concerning other instruments, etc…

Re: Identity crisis!

English takes on folk/trad have, I think, been conditioned by the fact that its original home has been in rural or comparable settings where close-knit communities have stayed put for a long time, and that the great majority of the population - folkies included - haven’t lived this way for a very long time.

By “comparable settings”, I include mines and seafaring - callings where coping with the natural world dominates the community’s life over generations, as in rural occupations, at any rate when farming was more labour-intensive than now.

In County Durham, there were plenty of songs about pit-work in the coal-mining communities, and particular disasters were commemorated by local balladeers such as Tommy Armstrong. (The verse of some of these is remarkably fine.) And these were handed down. Of course not everybody would sing or know them, but people in general would know *about* them. Traditional singers like the Elliotts of Birtley recorded them, revival singers have sung them, and the texts and tunes are not hard to come by.

In contrast, if a Tube crash killed thirty people it would be one day’s news. Even if they all came from one block in London, the population of the block would fairly soon be markedly different what with people coming and leaving, and have no collective memory to speak of of the event. It would only live on as a personal tragedy in the minds of various scattered individuals.

So yes, I think English folk labours like very much else under the imperatives that a highly urban environment almost inescapably imposes: Seize the moment, make a splash, get noticed, find a hook to catch the attention of people in a mass, take the acclaim and the money, shake the people off again, bolt back to your retreat or bubble-world, start out again…I am not aiming to be cynical here: it really does seem to be like that.

Re: Identity crisis!

ian stock - I’ll try to say a bit more about NE music.

To recap on ethnicity - the ‘Celtic nations’ *did* contribute to the NE population, but in the modern era, as Scottish / Irish / Cornish / Welsh people came to work in the industries and duly assimilated as North-Easterners. Rather earlier, there was apparently a distinct Scottish community in Newcastle. Many Borderers - probably from both sides - came to work and settle on Tyneside.

The rather subdued profile of naturalised Irish culture in the NE may owe to many of the immigrants having been scattered among small towns, possibly inducing them to assimilate fairly quickly. The ‘critical mass’ of a large community embedded in a sizeable city was maybe never reached, even in Newcastle. The descendants of the c19 Irish will be doing, or playing, all manner of things - but for the most part not trad!

Instruments, again. The fiddle became a favoured vehicle of the dotted 4/4 hornpipe (the Harvest Home-type rhythm), first popularised on Tyneside in the early c19 by James Hill. He came from Eastern Scotland, and (acc. dow, a Session member) the dotted 4/4 hornpipe had probably originated there a little before. It became very popular as a solo boot or clog dance for guys wishing to demonstrate their prowess in pubs, on top of suitably resounding platforms, and spread countrywide. The fashion passed but Hill’s tunes remained in circulation among trad players.

The 3/2 rhythm, previously used for hornpipes of whatever kind, went obsolete after the 4/4 came in. Used quite a lot in older NE trad and indeed in older English music across the board, it is being played again more in sessions.

I gather there’s the odd difference in bowing technique between NE fiddle and one or more other traditions, but not being a fiddler I cannot elaborate.

There was no whistle or flute tradition *as such* in the rural trad, though the odd such instrument might be used by someone who had one to hand. Billy Ballantyne played piccolo, Billy Conroy played home-made whistles. Clips of both and other N/brian trad players are to be found on the FARNE website (Folk Archive Resources North East), although one does get the impression that the same handful of tunes is getting played rather a lot of the time.

Nearly all the revivalist concertina-players I’ve seen here have played the English sort, but I don’t know whether this reflects local usage in the past, or whether concertinas were played much at all, whether in pubs or in parlours or in concertina bands.

The pub or parlour piano was widely used for sing-songs and house music/dance parties (I would like to think it still is…)

As for accordions/melodeons - Northumbrian music, like Scottish, needs or at any rate benefits from a bass vamp, whether the provision is basic (D/G melodeon, e.g.) or comprehensive (piano- or big button-accordion). The basic B/C accordeon doesn’t provide this, so stays around the Irish music where it is a favoured instrument. Julian Sutton, a local player who can turn his hand to various genres, uses a B/C/C#.

As for strings - as everywhere else, guitarists came in from Beatles / pop / rock and roll / R&B…there was a big R&B scene in particular in postwar Tyneside. In the folk club boom there really were a lot of good singer-guitarists about. A bewildering range of mandolin-type instruments are played. I mentioned the cittern - its re-invention in the NE is really probably a matter of chance, but to my ears anyway it absolutely goes with Northumbrian music. I believe it lends itself particularly well to playing in C and F - the usual keys in which the N/brian smallpipes are played - but may be quite mistaken here.

I think that covers most of the instruments!
