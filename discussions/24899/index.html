…and not forgetting the 12th century eye-witness commentary on Irish music:

http://www.standingstones.com/giraldus.html
Geraldus Cambrensis. 12th century on Irish music:
(He was a Welsh writer I believe):

“I find among these (Irish) people commendable diligence on musical instruments, on which they are incomparably more skilled than any nation I have seen. Their style is not, as on the British instruments to which we are accustomed, deliberate and solem but quick and lively; nevertheless the sound is smooth and pleasant.
It is remarkable that, with such rapid fingerwork, the musical rhythm is maintained and that, by unfailingly disciplined art, the integrity of the tune is fully preserved throughout the ornate rhythms and the profusely intricate polyphony—and with such smooth rapidity, such ‘unequal equality’, such ‘discordant concord’.
One must note that both Scotland and Wales, the latter by virtue of extension, the former by affinity and intercourse, depend on teaching to imitate and rival Ireland in musical practice.”
And so on it goes.

There are no recorded Irish dance tunes until about 400 years later - and when they did appear, it wasn’t in a way that suggested thousands of them had been lurking unrecorded in oral tradition; most seem to have been composed not all that long before they first got written down. Fleischmann’s book made a good try at documenting what happened.

What makes Irish music interesting isn’t venerability resulting from age (most of it isn’t very old), but vitality from ongoing re-creation. Nationalistic obsession with ethnic origins misses everything that makes it a joy and an inspiration.

By the way, I think you’ve read a little too much into the concept of genetic memory. The neural encoding that allows the ‘common experiences of a species’ to become incorporated into the genomes are biologically, not culturally, specific. For example, the inherited ability to use language is one shared by all members of the species, while the specific language an individual learns to speak is culturally determined. Likewise, the inherited responsiveness to music as a stimulus, and the associated ability to learn music, are governed by the same determinations. Therefore the style, or tradition, or form, of the music learned within a culture is not pre-determined by genetic inheritance, rather it is formed within a specific cultural context.
