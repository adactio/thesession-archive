Re: Not County Styles, but Nation Styles.

My personal take on it is that the traditional music of the various parts of Ireland and Britain together forms a distinctive, easily recognisable bloc with altogether more in common, taken overall, than sharply separate.

I can’t help comparing it, in this respect, to the ‘Insular’ style of art and lettering in manuscripts produced in Anglo-Saxon, Pictish, Gaelic and Irish scriptoria round about the c8 AD. Maybe ‘Insular’ wouldn’t be a bad label for this music.

The nature of the dances (I’m not on firm ground here, I’m not a dancer…), the rhythms and structures of the tunes and the keys they are usually played in, are things held in common. Also, the diatonic / modal nature of the vast majority of the tunes.

Re: Not County Styles, but Nation Styles.

Probably the best way to understand the different styles of music is to look at the different styles of dance - a set dance where the dancers are travelling round the room needs a very different rhythm to a step dance where the dancers are kicking about on the spot. And Irish or Scottish dancers leaping in the air need a different rhythm to flat-foot and clog dancers rooted to the ground.

The tunes themselves aren’t important - many of the same tunes occur in all the celtic traditions. It’s the way you play them that defines the style. And the way you play them is dictated by the style of dance.

Re: Not County Styles, but Nation Styles.

“Seriously though, many performers will use the word ‘Celtic’ to describe themselves. Because traditional music has no boundaries it’s not easily labelled. So many musicians don’t have a problem with the label ‘Celtic.’”

I have a problem with the label “Celtic”.

Traditional music has lots of boundaries, and anybody who’s done any serious study of one or more of the myriad traditions should have a pretty good idea of where they are. Musicians cross these boundaries often enough, but I’d like to think that they are aware of when they’re doing so. Jerry Holland once mentioned that he would never dare play an [Irish] roll in front of one of the older Cape Breton fiddlers (though he certainly might elsewhere). Irish musicians know that jigs and reels have certain rhythms, and they violate those conventions rarely and carefully. Pipers know their “backstitches” from their “crans” from their “cuts” and “rolls”, and Cape Breton and Scottish fiddlers sure as hell know how to play a strathspey, and will be able to instantly recognize when another fiddler doesn’t know how (though their idea will likely differ from that of a fiddler from Donegal).

That’s the problem with the “celtic” label. While the grouping category might be a necessary evil when shopping for recorded music, or even for advertising your band, it is being deliberately ignorant of all the details that made the traditions worth listening to in the first place.

Without those details, you get lumped in with stuff like this:

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yOaoUx0Mc0Q

Which, in all honesty, isn’t anymore “celtic” than Boston’s basketball team.

↳ Posted by minijackpot on February 16th, 2011.

Reels, jigs, etc.

To get back to your original post, no jigs and reels are not confined to Ireland. There are 6/8 jigs (single & double), 9/8 jigs (hop & slip), and reels throughout the Isles-- ‘Soldiers Joy’ is an example of a very-widely spread tune, from Scotland to Wales to America to Norway play versions of the tune. There are also hornpipes (mostly 2/2 or 4/4 like reels, though Britain has 3/2 hornpipes, as well.) Continental tunes are also shared, like 3/4 waltzes and mazurkas, and 2/4 (or 4/4) polkas. Slides (12/8), similar to single jigs, are peculiar to Ireland (the SW specifically). And that’s without getting to the strathspey/schottische/highland/fling/german/barnadance spectrum of tunes you’ll encounter MOSTLY in Donegal and Scotland… and then are rants and some others, you’ll find plenty of those here. ;p

Barndances, Germans, Hornpipes, Highlands, Flings, Highland Flings, Schottisches etc.
https://thesession.org/discussions/4149#comment84822

General resource:
http://www.irishtune.info/rhythm/

Keep in mind these are rhythmic distinctions. The contours of the melodies are often what makes something “sound Irish” (or Scottish or sound Welsh, etc.) There is a whole big world out there, just don’t get too lost. ;p Good luck!

“OH! WOW! AIRS! I neglected them! They would be the Perfect place to start, am i right?”

I’d say no, not a perfect place to start, unless you mean playing out the tunes to songs you know. The pieces referred to as “slow airs” are actually very difficult to play well, and they’re usually the last thing you learn. They’re based on the old singing tradition, and if you don’t have that in your ears it’s hard to understand why they sound the way they do.

If you hear some fiddler playing an air that grabs you, and it stays in your head, then there’s a reason to learn the tune, and that’s also a way to learn the tune. But buying a book of “101 Great Slow Airs” would be a mistake - they don’t transcribe well at all. If you need an analogy, it would be like transcribing a raga - everything that matters about the performance is stuff that you can’t get on paper.

On the other hand, if by “airs” you mean you have a song in mind and you can reach into your head and bring it out, definitely go for it. That’s a great way to find your way around the instrument. And if you learn that tune and you play it slowly, and a little freely, a little rubato, put a little of your own thing on it, I don’t think anyone can really complain. Just go easy on the reverb, and avoid the synthesizer backing, at least until you can get the dry ice and the stage lighting to go with it.

Re: Not County Styles, but Nation Styles.

“Air” is an old word for a tune, a melody. As in “Londonderry Air” (AKA “Danny Boy”). A song is a lyric combined with an air, or a tune, or what you will. (That’s why it’s wrong to refer to a jig or a reel as a “song”).

The old style of singing, which is the source of the instrumental “slow airs”, inclines to highly-decorated melodies with the tune and the phrasing subtly modified to suit the lyric. (Compare that to something like the Broadway show tune tradition, where the lyric is typically much more tightly tied to the melodic strain, even if the tune is interpreted with some swing) The instrumental tradition, then, is inspired by the vocal tradition, and could be seen as an homage to the vocalist as musician, and also to the lyricist as poet.
Lacking that inspiration from the vocal and poetic tradition, the playing of a slow air would be a pretty empty thing - just a set of notes, played with arbitrary timing and phrasing. So it’s pretty critical that you understand what you’re playing when you play an air. You’re playing a song, whose words, even if not sung, are present, and must be respected.
This is made a little more difficult by the fact that the words are typically in Irish.

If you like the sound of slow airs and you want to learn them, you absolutely should do that, but the place to start is not with fiddlers. The place to start is with the great singers - Joe Heaney is a name you’ll hear a lot, but I’m sure if you ask for suggestions, you’ll get a good list of great sean nos singers. Even if you don’t learn Irish, it’s possible to study the lyrics and get an idea of what’s going on in them, so you can at least know where the sensible phrases are - you don’t want to break up words in ways that don’t make sense. To borrow an example posted here recently: Imagine a singer who came out with “Some where o -- ver the rainbow blue -- birds fly”.
