Re: Ornamentation

What SmashtheWindows says. It’s not like you have a “tune” over on this side of the room and “ornamentation” on the other side of the room and you say “hey let’s put these two things together, why don’t we?”

By definition, there is very little “ornamentation” in Irish music, because the definition of “ornamentation” is something superfluous to the melody which is added to the melody for decorative effect. (Look it up.)

There’s little of that in Irish music. Rather there are motifs or devices such as rolls which on the fiddle, flute, pipes, whistle are integral to the melody and drive the melody along (I’m talking reels and jigs here, mostly).

The trick is to attempt to remove all of these motifs and devices, remove all the so-called “ornamentation”, which on many Irish instruments leaves you with something that doesn’t sound very much like traditional Irish music.

Re: Ornamentation

Off and on I see something along the lines of what Richard is saying and it never fails to baffle me. Richard was the first person local to me that I ever heard play ITM on flute (my instrument), and he deeply impressed me, so my post here is in no disrespect to him or anyone else that feels that ornaments are a part of the tune, but I must respectfully and emphatically disagree. Two facts: if one learns a tune from a written source, such things as rolls may or may not be indicated, but by and large, what you get is the bare-bones framework of the melodic line. If you play as written, the tune is most certainly there, but it won’t sound Irish at all. It is up to the player to add what we refer to as ornamentation. How Richard defines ornament is correct, but if he feels the word by definition is inappropriate to what we add to the tune to create the Irish style of playing, that is more a failure in applying the right word (ornament) than a problem with what is actually happening regardless of what we call it.

Fact number two is that one simply has to listen to various players play a tune to hear how differently ornaments are applied and realize that the skillful use of ornaments has much to do with skill and style in playing.

Let me add that, as a flute player, many ornaments I use are as practical as they are artistic. Some ornaments allow one to get a breath, something fiddlers don’t need. Ornaments also allow me to avoid playing two notes of the same pitch in succession so I can avoid tonguing that can make the tune sound choppy and stylistically not how Irish flute players generally play.

So, if Richard prefers “device” or “motif” to “ornament,” I sympathize with his desire to be more precise with his musical terminology, but “ornament” is a time-honored term of art: We all know what we are talking about when we use it, even if it is not the best word. I think it’s pretty evident that ornaments are not a part of the tune until the player puts them there.

“there are motifs or devices such as rolls which on the fiddle, flute, pipes, whistle are integral to the melody” ~

Completely disagree! Nothing is ‘integral’ except the basics - melody - rhythm… Some styles and players do not use rolls at all. This is an old hammer, some folks sold that ‘it has to be’, almost as if it is nothing without it. The melody has always stood on its own, and handled well but simply, with drive and spirit, it can electrify a dance floor and excite an audience without the ‘extra’ twiddles. This kind of narrow view of it all, as ‘one thing’, is almost tyranny. If you don’t roll there, at such and such a point you’re doing it wrong. If you don’t play rolls, well, you’ve missed it all together. I’ll stick with the Christmas tree analogy. It’s always a tree, and we’ll keep with something of a living origin, say a fir, but how one chooses to decorate it is individual choice. Some love it laden down, and even use that spray snow, so the tree can almost be an afterthought, just a framework for all the decorator chooses to do to it. And those people will often opt for something synthetic, even pink, or aluminium. Or, as with others, on another extreme, they might even like a live tree, which we have, and decorate it in a way that the decorations are scattered around but aren’t cluttered, you can still recognize the tree, and the ornaments are just gentle proof that someone has ‘adorned’ it in their particular way, in whatever mood they were in at the time. The same is true of the music, for some ~ sometimes one does it one way, and sometimes another, and it generally never repeats ‘exactly’ the same way with every visit. That’s part of the joy of taking it to heart ~ with appreciation for the music, rhythms and melody and those joyous choices, that ‘freedom’ to make it your own, with respect for the tradition it comes from, and under the influence of those we’ve learned from.

Roll if you want to, but you don’t have to… ~ ‘c’

Re: Ornamentation

There are different approaches to ornamentation. Some ornamentation adds notes which are not part of the melody. Another, different style of ornamentation uses percussive, rhythmic articulations which can be less about adding notes (or pitches) but more about the melody. In the former ornamentation & melody are relatively distinct. In the latter distinctions between ornamentation & melody are ambiguous.

Re: Ornamentation

I don’t really think that rolls, cuts, etc are part of an individual Tune, in that ‘a roll HAS to go here or it’s no longer the tune’, but ornaments or whatever you want to call them are most definitely part of the Music. It’s the style.

It’s a little like an accent in one’s voice. I can hear music, and tell by the way it’s treated whether it sounds like traditional Irish fiddle music or not. If you play with a lot of double stops in 3rds I’d say you might have an American accent to your playing. If you play with pretty straight rhythm and a lot of vibrato I might think you had a more classical ‘accent’.

A significant part of that is ornamentation. Not the whole part of it, of course, like everything it’s a number of factors that come into play and you can take some or several away and still have that thing be recognizable. As ceolachan says, you can add more or less ornamentation, or even take it out entirely but if it still has the lilt and lift then off she goes.

If you want to use the salt analogy, consider that it is seldom essential to the dish in the same way burgundy wine is essential to beef burgundy. The dish would have the same name with or without the salt. The salt is not essential, it is an embellishment, as are ornaments in a tune. To advance the notion that ornaments are an integral part of the tune is to say the tune no longer exists without the ornaments. Given that no two players use the same ornaments, that comes near to being impossible unless one simply imitates another’s performance.

Re: Ornamentation

STW, your link to Grey Larsen’s piece (and I have his book) is telling. In his discussion of the terms “ornament” and “articulation,” he tries to make a distinction by saying (as I infer) that the ornament is decorative, whereas articulation is functional. What I think he misses is that one person’s articulation is another person’s ornament. He may use a cut or strike to start a tune to give a clear articulation, but I may use a roll or a cran for the same purpose. I think it is a mistake to confuse the necessity of using some device for a series of notes as thinking of the device used (ornament?) as being “part of the tune.”

Re: Ornamentation

Grey Larsen also says: “The word ornament implies a musical element that could just as well be left out, leaving the essence of the music perfectly intact. Many ornaments used in Irish music do fit that description, but there are others that do not, that are essential or intrinsic to the life of the tune. Stated another way, there are places in many tunes that cannot sound “right” without the use of ornamentation.”

I do not think that an ornament could “just as well be left out”. However, nor I do I think that an ornament is “essential or intrinsic” to a tune.

I do think that you can talk about ornaments separately in a technical sense, that is “how do you play a cut/roll/cran/triplet on a whistle/fiddle/shaky egg”, so that we can share and learn how such an effect might be achieved and then applied in practice.

Regarding their use/non-use and placing within a tune, I don’t see ornaments as an “addition” or an “embellishment”. I see them as part of it. If I put a cut on a note, I do it because that’s “just what goes there”, making it *my tune* - the sound and feel *I* want.

Each to their own really.

For me, the best example of ornamentation (added, notated and explained) is in Breandan Breathnach’s ‘Cold Rinse Over Ireland’, Vol 3. :)

There’s a table at the beginning showing how ornamentation is applied, on fiddle, whistle and melodeon.

On many of the tunes, the ornamentation has been added to the printed music to match the actual playing of the tune by a specific player.

At the back of the book, there’s a list of the tunes, the player, and the name of the recording.

As an example, there are are least four tunes transcribed from fiddler Frankie Gavin, and the tunes’ dots and audio can be matched up on the CD “Masters of Irish Music” by Frankie Gavin and Alec Finn.

Looking at the printed music, it’s really obvious by the markings where the ornamentaion is - and if you listen to the tracks on the CD, it’s pretty much spot-on.

3. 3. Why aren’t you playing what’s written down for the tune?

“Published fiddle music is usually a only a skeleton of what we play, often lacking bowings, dynamics, ornaments, variations, or even chords. Tunes are usually written unswung, with one full repeat of the melody line (usually two eight-bar phrases repeated—once through most square and contra dances). Variations, beat placement, and bowing syncopations are implied and change with style. Most of this “performance practice” couldn’t be read by the majority of fiddlers. Defining techniques are learned as part of a style, and applied to the tunes as a spoken accent is to a language.” **
