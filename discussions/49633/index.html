Writing out reels wrongly

I was playing for a ceili last night and it occurred to me that we write out reels wrongly. The count that the dancers are dancing to - 1, 2, 3, 4 - counts groups of four notes. So a bar should consist of four of these clusters of notes. But we write out reels in such a way that a bar is only two of these groups long - in effect, we write out reels in 2/4.
Not only that, but if you count a bar as four of the beats that the dancers move to, what we normally call a 32-bar reel is actually only 16 bars long.
Not that it matters, of course - the sheet music does its job of presenting us with the basics of the tune either way. You could write it out without bar lines or time signature and not a great deal would be lost, given that you need an understanding of the music to be be able to play a tune from sheet music anyway.

Re: Writing out reels wrongly

The notation and beat counting conventions have evolved to suit musicians, not the dancers. I think it’s just easier to perceive many consecutive notes of the reel in shorter bar-phrases and count beats by half notes instead of quarter notes (100-120bpm instead of 200-220bpm), which also makes it much easier to tap your foot. I’ve had to explain the latter part to a classically trained musician who described the reels as being played at 200+ bpm speeds – it’s possible to count them like that but it’s just less convenient, so we divide that beat count by 2. Same story with the polkas: you can in theory make them go past 300bpm by counting every quaver as a beat but many metronomes don’t even support tempos that high.

Note that we have just the opposite story with the slides: the modern convention suggests notating them in 12/8 (which works visually fine with the sparse dotted quarter-quaver groups). But the dancers always count the number of slide bars the same way they do jig bars. It’s just more convenient to them – and when playing for the sets, it was never a problem for us to divide by two to convert :-)

There is no “wrong” way to notate or count beats in a traditional tune – there are more or less convenient ways to do so depending on your purpose. For the purposes of reading sheet music, I think having 16 notes per bar would’ve been a nightmare!

P.S.: Another example: there are various ways to notate “crooked” Bulgarian tunes and many folks go with a gazillion / 16 to avoid having to change the time signature every few bars out of convenience. But local trad musicians don’t perceive the tunes like that, they never count as 15/16, 33/16 or even as 7/8 – instead, they count in combined groups of two and three beats (replace that with mnemonic 2- or 3-syllable words if you like). Re: Writing out reels wrongly

Any system of notation is conventional, and the use of staff notation to record trad tunes is no different. Conventional systems by their nature are hard to change intentionally, since they are not defined by a standard but are accepted by consensus. They are also hard to keep constant, for the same reason. The spelling system used in English is not what anyone would have designed if they’d sat down to get it right, but it works. The set of symbols used in mathematics are an awful mess, but people still do math - and so forth, and so on.

Since you clearly can read reels as written and I’m guessing you have no trouble playing for dancers even though their count is different from what you’re reading on the page, I’m guessing that the notation for reels, for you, falls into the category of “could be better, but it works”, which is pretty much how everyone else sees it as well. So unless you’re looking for something about which to be a harmless eccentric, I’d suggest it’s best to leave it and get on with playing tunes.

@ELCBK, yes, you can absolutely add accents and phrasing in your ABCs meant for educational content – and yes, keeping the notation simple for reading is very important when sharing your transcriptions with the wider community, such as The Session! There’s no contradiction, it just depends on the context – for example, I do add emphasis in ABCs for beginners and indicate phrasing/bowing for advanced fiddle students but I would never post those transcriptions here. For someone scanning through tune versions, this sort of information density would be off-putting at best. You can find all the currently defined symbols, including accent / emphasis, in the ABC notation standard and experiment with them in your software of choice to see if it supports what you need. Re: Writing out reels wrongly

Just to illustrate what I mean, here’s the first bar of St Anne’s, written out in the way that I think would best convey the way that the tune is played:
http://www.maybewecould.co.uk/images/st_annes.jpg
The pulse is at 110bpm (this is obviously arbitrary but gives some sense of the pace). It’s in 4/4, so a bar will be four beats at 110bpm. The musician can already get some sense of what’s going on rhythmically.
Each beat - each tap of the foot - is clearly visible on the page as a cluster of notes, with the pulse of the music falling on the first note of each cluster; in playing the tune, the musician would try and give some sense of this, perhaps a slight accent on the first note of each cluster, or a very slight lift or separation between the clusters.
For a musician who is used to reading music but has never encountered this type of music, all that is conveyed at a glance.
As I say, all this is purely theoretical - for the way I use sheet music, the conventional practice is perfectly adequate. But I don’t think that the conventional practice actually conveys the correct musical meaning.

A reel has a pulse that you can tap your foot to or dance to, and I feel that the sheet music should show the musician that pulse as clearly as possible - it’s such an important part of the music. The conventions of staff notation allow for this - the main pulse of the music is shown as the count of the bar, and the notes are grouped into clusters that make up single beats.
The musician can then create the pulse in their playing in lots of different ways. I gave a couple of examples but there are lots more possibilities and as you say, no one way of doing it is ‘correct’. I think it’s fair to say that if the pulse isn’t at all discernible when someone plays a reel, they aren’t doing it justice.

Re: Writing out reels wrongly

As I’ve mentioned before when these topics come up, when I was doing a lot of Hollywood studio work it was interesting how the arrangers wrote everything in 4/4, probably for ease of sightreading.

So the stuff equivalent to jigs would always be written in 4/4, each beat having a triplet.

That of course meant having half as many bar-lines as Irish Trad arrangers would write.

Ditto with the stuff equivalent to reels: in 4/4 but each beat having four 16th notes, and having half as many bar-lines as Irish arrangers would write.

So that’s how outsiders to Irish Trad conceptualised our rhythms.

(I never saw a Slip Jig-type thing in a Hollywood score, but I’m guessing they would write it in 6/4 again with a triplet on every beat. Oddly, in one film we had a group playing Slip Jigs for Irish Dancers dancing Slip Jigs, and the music supervisor removed us from the soundtrack and substituted music in 4/4 which, to them, probably fit the dancing better though which to us looked completely wrong.)

Also in Hymnody it’s interesting how they’ll often notate old Hymn tunes in 4/4 though they clearly have a different underlying rhythm. Thus, in 4/4 the main beat shifts from place to place in each bar and the tune seems irregular. However if they placed the bar lines to fit the rhythm every main beat would be on a “1” and the whole piece would be perfectly regular.

When tunes are written in 2/4 and counted “1 & 2 &” the “1 &” and the “2 &” are sometimes swung. Polkas vary in rhythm. Can a 2/2 tune be swung like that ? If so it’s an argument for writing reels in 4/4 because then the 1-2-3-4 are almost always evenly spaced in time as that meter would imply. But the ‘ 1 &’ etc are typically swung.

When you see an experienced player on stage switching between tapping 2 to a bar and 4 to a bar it seems to be about emphasis, not timing.

So my interpretation is that the emphasis in a reel is usually two a bar but the metre is four to a bar.

What cues would the 2/4 version give to a musician to suggest a different way of playing the tune?

When you tap every other beat, you’re feeling/hearing it in *2/2*, not 2/4. This might sound pedantic but it does have implications when it comes to how things are notated. A 2/4 reel would be written with a lot of 1/16 notes, not 1/8 notes as it is written in 4/4 and 2/2.

The difference doesn’t mean much in trad; if we had settled on 2/4 rather than 2/2 to write it in, the feel is largely the same. This is because trad is not really a notation-driven music, so the notation is descriptive rather than proscriptive.

If you’re asking, though, what the difference is between feeling it “in 2” (either 2/2 or rewritten into 2/4) or “in 4” (4/4), that is about phrasing and emphasis. Feeling it in 4 IMO makes for a much more plodding, less flowing sound that brings to mind the sorts of slow beginner sessions where everyone’s reading from a sheet. Feeling it in 2, grouping all four 1/8 notes into one “beat,” makes the phrasing a lot more driving/flowing, and ultimately danceable.

The most obvious example of this is probably in slides. If you listen to good Cork and Kerry players play slides, there are two beats in each 12/8 bar. In other words, each beat has a grouping of 6 1/8 notes. When others play slides, too often they play them more or less like jigs, with groups of 3 1/8 notes rather than 6. Completely different, and IMO much worse, sound.

The bottom number in a time signature just gives information about the notation - 4/4 means ‘there will be four beats in each bar, and each beat will be shown as a crochet’. Only the top number relates to the music, the bottom number is about how the music is going to be shown on the page. So musically, 4/4 is the same as 4/2, it’s only the notation that differs.

Will, when said there is no reference, that was in response to your idea that the crotchet was somehow linked to heartbeat.

As a nipper I used to enjoy stumbling my way through the Adagio of Mozart’s Cm piano sonata (Köchel 457). It’s presented in common time with a tempo equivalent to 30 crotchets/quarter notes per minute. That’s about seven or eight times as long as a crotchet lasts in a normal reel.

My argument is that how composers notate their work is down to fashion within the genre. For example, it used to be far more common (than it is now) to notate Scottish reels in 2/4 (Scott Skinner, Charlie Sherrit, etc.). The way they are notated now doesn’t alter the way they are played.
