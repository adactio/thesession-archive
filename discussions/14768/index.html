Re: Mandolin speeds

One of the best bits of advice I ever heard on increasing speed is to practice slowly - dead slowly 10 times through or more until the tune’s physical pattern with your hands and fingers becomes an act of it’s own volition. Then speed up until you hit your top end. Repeat regularly as needed. The other challenge for me in obtaining speed was to learn not to strangle the livin’ sh*te out of my instrument the faster I tried to play. Tight muscles and having to think about the next series of notes or triplets is a sure-fire way to kill fluidity and speed.

However, I also feel compelled to throw in my two cents - speed is over-rated compared to feel. As a banjo player I’ll take Angelina Carberry’s smooth easy going style over lickety-split speed merchants any day. Best of luck to you.

Hi Ian,

That was myself who stuck that clip of Declan on youtube, its from Galway a couple of years ago - hes some player!

I played mandolin for about 2 years and then decided to learn fiddle instead, but anyway.

Declan is a monster of a player , technically he’s the best Ive ever seen though, sometimes the super speed reels lose their appeal as they can all sound so similar, (different tunes should be played a different speeds which bring out their individual sound and beauty). Thats my thoughts anyway.

Anyway back to the point, after trying to learn mandolin for about 2 years Im convinced 99% of the technical aspect is the way you hold the plectrum. If you hold the pick like a guitar player you will never be comfortable at playing fast triplets with ease. You have to hold it like Chris Thile (get his tutor video!) or Declan which is a closed hand position (like Blairs thumb of power!) with the pick sticking out.

Changing your pick hold can be really annoying but its worth it if you want to plat fast triplets which just roll of your plectrum with ease. And do other stuff. Some of the great (classical) mandolin masters of the past also used the same hold.

On the left hand speed, try holding down the strings just hard enough to get a clear sound and keep everything relaxed, you may also want to look at your general posture, your arm, wrist angle, place of your thumb and experiment to see what works best for you.

(You could also work on finger buster drills too if you wanted, theres a few on the web if you search around) And remember Declan started off with the kerry polka etc like the rest of us (even if he was 5 or something!)

Hope that helps.

I don’t consider myself to be that great a player but I can “play to speed” in most situations. However, it really depends on how well I know a tune and/or the company at the time.

So, when you say that you play along with bands such as Lunasa, De Dannan and so on, I presume you mean their CDs? :-)
It’s actually relatively easy to do this with time and practice although you’ll obviously concentrate on your favourite tunes.

However, I think the problem is(with me, anyway) that in the “big bad world” of sessions, the other players won’t necessarily play the tune as you’ve learned it from the album. It might be even faster, have variations in the melody, more or less ornaments etc. Even the timing might be different.

So, this does tend to throw you(us) a bit. You have to learn to adapt to what’s going on around you. This may mean altering your normal approach and technique to a particular tune. There’s not the same scope for “stretching” notes on the mandolin so you might have to add a triplet or something. Also, the ornaments you’ve already learned might not work very well with what’s going on around you.

I’ve found that there are (and probably always will) certain tunes that I just can’t manage very well on the mandolin in a session even although I can play them very well in another situation or if I lead them myself. Others, of course, are “less suited” to the instrument although the really good players will tackle just about anything.

Re: Mandolin speeds

Learning to relax is important. Remember that you don’t have to have your entire arm froze up to hold something firmly between a thumb and a finger. You should be able to shake your entire arm like it was made of rubber without dropping your pick. On your right hand, stop occasionally and make sure you can freely wiggle your thumb. If you can’t, you’re not relaxed.
---
Use a metronome, set it to one or two beats per bar. Start slower than you actually need to play the tune comfortably. I’m going to have play the same notes but apply weird accents in varying patterns, and then add dotted rhythms. It will force your brain to come up with a few different solutions to obstacles that weren’t obvious to you, at slower tempos where you can deal with it.

1. Accent any note falling directly on the first beat of a measure and the halfway point. Really overdo it - double forte on the 1st note of each measure and piano for all others. For ex. “ONE and two and THREE and four and” for 8th note run in 4/4. Speed the tempo up a bit if it seems easy.

2. Turn #1 above upside down and accent the last note at the end of the measure.

3. Play the piece like it was written in triplets and accent the first note of each triplet. Doesn’t do much for waltzs, but you don’t have speed problems with those do you? For ex. “ONE and two AND three and FOUR and” for 8th note run in 4/4. It’ll drive you nuts after the first bar when things don’t line up like you’re used to. That’s the point.

4. Play the whole piece in a dotted rhythm (if it isn’t already) with the first note dotted. Don’t worry about accenting here.

5. Turn #4 around and play the first note short and dot the second note.

6. Play the piece with a swing feel.
---

If you don’t have constant runs in the same rhythm, change them all to the most often appearing note. So if the piece has mostly 8th notes with dotted half notes mixed in all over the place, play the dotted half note as the equivalent number of eighth notes.

Dump the ornaments at first. I’ll send a 2nd post on a trick for those.

You can practice your scales an arpeggios this way too. Probably should have started mentioning scales.

Playing with the dotted rhythms will slow down half the notes and speed up the other by a factor of two. Think of it as simplifying which notes you have to play fast by cutting half of them out. If you have to shift anywhere, really work the shift with the dotted rhythms to get them clean and faster than you actually need them.

Re: Mandolin speeds

Speeding up ornaments…

I learned this on violin, so it may not “sound” the same as you are doing it, but the mechanics will work. You’re going to completely eliminate your right hand and teach your left hand to fret with velocity.

Start with a trill: f# and g on the E string, 1st position. Plunk your middle finger down on that g and let it ring. You didn’t plunk hard enough. Raise it high enough above the string that you can pick up some speed on the way down. You should hear your finger percussively hitting the finger board (sorry, “fret board”) in addition to hearing the note. Now don’t lift your finger so high in the air this time and get it to make the same noise. Now try it with the index finger on the f#. This is the hardest finger for me. I found it very unnatural to lift it up. Once you can make a decent slapping sound with both fingers, play the trill very slowly in this manner. (Leave the plectrum in the case - no right hand). Yeah, you’ll have to pick up the index finger for each f# note and you normally would just leave it down. Pick it up anyway and make sure you can make both fingers slap down in tempo. That’s basically it. Now trill with the other fingers too. Start slow. Enjoy the pain in that fourth finger. The good news is that you can lift it up higher to get it moving faster.

Forcing you to make that totally unnatural slapping sound requires you have a minimum velocity in your fingers when you fret a note. There’s no escape and you know immediately when you get it wrong. No, you don’t play with this kind of pressure all the time, it is an exercise. But you do need to play with this kind of velocity to play an ornament cleanly at a fast tempo.

Now apply same exercise with your mordents, turns, grace notes, etc. You can also use this method to pound fingerings for shifting into your brain.

Another trick for trills is to play them in measured steps and incrementally increase the number of steps (you can use the plectrum again, if you didn’t lose it) while playing at the same tempo. For example, a trill over a quarter note you could play in 8th notes at first. Count the number of times the top finger lays down as you do it (count 1-2). If the piece has a lot of trills, play them each trill with the same measure (as 8th notes). The next day, play them all in triplet 8th notes (count 1-2-3 on each trill), The next day, play them all in 16th notes (count 1-2-3-4 on each trill). A couple days later play them all in 32 notes (count 1-2-3-4-5-6-7-8). A week later, let ’em trill as fast as you can go.

Re: Mandolin speeds

>…change them all to the most often appearing note
Simplify the rhythm to a consistent value, like straight 8th notes.
So if you have:

X:14
M:2/2
L:1/4
K:C
||d/-c/B/A/ G2|c e e/-d/ e||

Replace it with:

||d/-c/B/A/ G/G/G/G/|c/c/ e/e/ e/-d/ e/e/||

Work it until you can play it faster than you need, then go back to the original.

This is also a good trick to get past a really complicated syncopated run. Simplify it until you get the notes down, then all you have left is to leave a few notes out.

How would I define a “swing feel”? That would take a whole other discussion topic. I’m referring to a root element of jazz music. This is where you play (what would be notated without any syncopation in rhythm) somewhere between a dotted rhythm and a triplet, depending on the melody and the tempo. More importantly, emphasis changes from the 1st and 3rd beats to the 2nd and 4th beats. That almost dotted rhythm lag drives you right into 2nd and 4th beats Every jazz artist does it a little differently, but when done right, you’ll get drawn in and find you need to move along with it. Getting back to velocity exercises: the point is to find yet another way to turn the accents in the melody upside down and force your brain to deal with the same melody in a new way - getting it irrevocably into what a lot of people call “muscle memory”. I tend to think of it as actively programming your subconscious so you can do it by reflex - without thinking about it.
