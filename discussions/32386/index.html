“Squeeze-box” (and other naff lay expressions)

I’m something of an antiques aficionado and often watch the various TV programmes which cover that topic. This morning I was watching a programme called “Antiques Road Trip (originally BBC production, but now re-hashed on the “Yesterday” channel).

One of the items purchased was an English concertina: circa 1860, pokerwork rosewood ends, 48 keys, bone buttons, four-fold bellows, original hexagonal mahogany case . The dealer and (so-called expert) informed us that it was a “squeeze-box”, and the most important thing to look out when buying one was damage to the pokerwork ends!

Was it an English, and Anglo or a Duet? He didn’t know, or (if he did), he didn’t say.
Concert pitch or otherwise? Number of bellow folds? Condition of bellows? Brass reeds or steel reeds? Type of buttons? Condition of mechanism? Do all the notes play on both the push and the pull? We weren’t told.

He did point out the oval plate bearing the maker’s name - but stupidly he didn’t tell us what the name actually was (and the camera was too far away to pick it up.) Possibly a Wheatstone or George Case model.

I hate that expression “squeeze-box”. No player would ever use that term. Admittedly, one might just say “box” – as in “I’ll bring my box along to the session.” “Queeze-box” doesn’t even make sense. If you just pushed the bellows, and never pulled them, you would very quickly run out of air - and therefore tune.

That dealer was no fool though when it came to haggling over the price. He beat the un-informed shop owner down to £75 for it. It was subsequently bid right up to £150 when it went to auction. But still a bargain for the second buyer (assuming that it was in concert pitch and in good playing condition, I would estimate its retail worth at £300-£400).

Rant over. But not quite. I don’t like the term “tin whistle” either. True, they used to sell tin-plated whistles - but never whistles made from solid tin. Neither do I like the term “penny whistle” – especially given that fact that some of these instruments can now cost hundreds of pounds. If anyone says that they can play a “penny whistle” it’s a pound to a penny whistle that they can’t …

Re: “Squeeze-box” (and other naff lay expressions)

The point is that “tin” in common parlance means not tin (Sn) but sheet steel (the kind that “tinkers” worked with) , so the Clarke-style tin whistles at least are accurately named, being made from rolled-up “tin.” “Tin fiddle” is therefore also correct.

Concertina players are generally very defensive about mislabelling of their instruments, and this is perhaps understandable - esp. when they get referred to a type of accordion. ;-) However when I am asked what is that thing I am playing I’m quite happy to say “squeezebox” rather than “bisonoric chromatic diatonic button accordion.” People are generally satisfied and I am spared the trouble of further tedious explanations.

@D.J.F. If only one of us had known about it when it was on sale in that antique shop, we could have bought it for only £75 … sigh. That shop was in Yorkshire - so not so far from you!

As to value, (judging from its four-fold bellows and its bone buttons) it was probably just a budget model of the period. I would guess that it was brass-reeded too (steel-reeded instruments are louder and more responsive to the fingers, especially important when playing fast tunes).

Vintage concertinas with nickel-plated ends/buttons and fitted with steel reeds are usually much better quality instruments and fetch a lot more money. And for ITM, the Anglo concertina is the preferred instrument and really good one will costs £1000s.

My caveat was that it was a bargain at £150 providing that it was in concert pitch and in good playing condition. If it wasn’t, it might cost hundreds of pounds to sort it out. And it would still be an instrument with only four-folded belows.

A few years ago (based only what I saw on the TV) I would have guessed about £400-450 for that instrument - if it was right in all respects. But the recession has impacted on prices, and dealers have had to come down quite a bit in order to make sales.

To a point, I would agree with you, Jeeves. In common parlance, if someone says: “tin can” what the really mean is a steel can - plated with tin.

No-one would be able to sell a steel whistle - it would rust. The original Clarke whistles were made of steel, plated with tin to resist corrosion. These days cheap steel whistles (e.g. Generations) are usually nickel-plated.

So (if anything) it’s tin-plate whistle - not tin whistle. In any case, most competent players just say “whistle”. Especially so if its made of wood or some alloy.

My grandfather always referred to his instrument as a ‘squeeze-box’ as opposed to a the ‘gut-box’ that other people play.
