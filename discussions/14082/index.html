BS SS ~ Both are usually valued by those of us who dabble in those dark arts. So, please, don’t speak in such a sweeping and inclusive manner unless you can back it up. ;-)

I studied it longhand, with pen and ink, and despite being a lefty, which, I promise, made it hell. I spoiled a few manuscripts, and the manuscript paper wasn’t cheap… I was glad to see the software option come into creation and have used quite a few of those over time, currently with a few left on the computer here, but mostly using Sibelius, but by no means professionally.

I also love the ABCs, and taking either course for starters is workable, from ABCs to dots, as happens in the processing on this site and others, or starting with the dots and then picking up the ABCs.

On a personal note, it is hell trying to get the five lines of a treble staff and then make the spaces and lines and bars and flags and all that hullabaloo, and on a beer coaster, when you’ve got a couple of pints in you and an active musician tight in either side… So, don’t tell me that’s the place to bother with dots. It ain’t. ABCs were made for such situations, and even rainy days with little bits of paper or palm sized notebooks…

I love them both, and I can’t think of anyone in my experience that would condemn the dots as evil, or that would claim ABCs are the only way to go… Just to clear the waffle…

There are loads of freeware and shareware programs out there, but also, if you do a search of the ‘Discussions’ you’ll find this subject is not new, mostly the answer has been ‘on hand’ rather than via some software option, but the later exist. So, after trying the stock of things out there in freeware and shareware land, there are also the ones you can get that cost, used in schools and the like, for example, from the folks at Sibelius, who gobbled up smaller fish and then got gobbled up themselves, there’s “Musition”, just for starters ~

Re: Reading the dots

If you already read ABC, you can get your feet wet with standard notation by printing out the sheet music generated from a tune’s ABC, then writing in the ABC beneath it. Gradually, shift your focus from the ABC to the standard sheet music as you get to know the tune. Also, listen to the MIDI while trying to follow the sheet music. Depending upon your instrument, you can add position and/or finger numbers to the sheet music to help you along.

Re: Reading the dots

Whilst I think that musical notation (of any kind) is merely a *tool* for recording music, and is *not* the music itself, I would say that staff notation is, in my experience, the most effective and versatile means of providing an accurate viual representtion of music. Yes, there are certain ‘givens’ that need to be learned about in advance, such as note values, clefs, time signatures and key signatures. But, beyond that, it is just a spatial representation of pitch - a relationship that is already engendered in referring to a note as ‘high’ or ‘low’.

Tablatures are fine as a kind of ‘idiot’s guide’ (I use the term figuratively, not to suggest that all those who use them are idiots - in fact, they leave me perplexed) to playing an instrument, but they are instrument-specific, do not bear a direct relationship to pitch (for example, on an instrument such as 5-string banjo, where the 4th string is lower in pitch than the 3rd but the 5th is higher than the 4th - and any stringed instrument that is not tuned at equal intervals) and do not have any means of accurately representing timing, aside from borowing note symbols from staff notation.

ABC notation is capable of conveying the same degree of detail as staff notation, but it is essentially an encoded form of the same and, as such, whilst it is a very convenient way of entering tunes into a computer, it seems to me that reading it requires a greater degree of intellectual processing than reading off the staff.

Re: Reading the dots

I learned to read music while learning to play the flute. I found it easier to learn by treating the dots as if they were tablature. (a note on the middle line means one finger down, the line below means 3 fingers down, etc) If you try to remember the letter of the note at the same time (the middle line means one finger down and that means B, and it sounds like this), it’s too much to keep in mind all at once. The point is to be able to play from the dots. Knowing the letter right off doesn’t help. You can get to that later.
Now that you know the written note and what it sounds like, you’ll be able to read better.
It’s great to be able to read a manuscript and hear it in your mind without ever having heard it before. I love being able to sight read.
