Session or gig?

What is the difference between a session and a gig? I’m based in the UK.

↳ Posted by Molly on May 25th, 2025.

Re: Session or gig?

In the trad context, a session is an informal gathering of musicians to play tunes, generally for no pay and under no binding expectations to appear, though some sessions pay a few anchor musicians to be there.

A “gig” usually means a scheduled and paid performance by a solo artist or a small band.

Basically, a session is a bunch of musicians playing mostly to each other, while a gig is a bunch of musicians playing to an audience.

Re: Session or gig?

(1) A gig is a contractual arrangement. Punters pay money to hear music and the musicians are expected to provide a reasonable amount of music. (2) A (true) session may have a non-playing audience, but it’s essentially there for the musicians themselves. They can come and go when they want and play as much or as little as they want. (3) But as some previous posts have stated, there are some “sessions” where organisers or musicians are hired by pub or venue owners to play to an audience while giving the impression that the event is actually a form of no. 2.

Re: Session or gig?

Sometimes it’s hard to tell the difference but, as others have said, the set up of the session is more informal. The participants may still play to and interact with the “punters”(Not everyone likes that word ;-) ) but they aren’t really considered to be an audience as such.

Also, there is no set list and while many tunes and, sometimes, songs regularly crop up and are even expected in some sessions, it’s still usually a fairly spontaneous affair.

However, a *true* session is basically a bunch of “musical friends” meeting up to have a tune or song. It might not even have been planned or it could be a regular occasion. If the latter, it is usually expected that a core number of musicians will usually attend. It may either be voluntary or for a small payment and/or hospitality‘’’free drinks, food etc.

As has been discussed, the lines between session and gigs can get blurred especially if the management has expectations that the musicins should be there to entertain.

There is also the “closed session” scenario where only “invited musicians” are allowed to take part. This has often been described as a “gig” scenario. However, a closed session may still be just as informal and spontaneous as any other session and nothing like a gig at all. It’s just that access is restricted.

Oh, as to having to visit the toilet during a gig performance, I’m sure there’s subtle ways of leaving the stage so that the audience doesn’t even notice. e.g. if another musician is doing a solo, a singer is being accompanied by just some of the band members or not at all, or there is some other arrangement.
I quite often see musicians coming and going on stage in such situations but, from now on, I will be wondering if they are actually leaving to “pay a visit”. ;-)

Re: Session or gig?

As a tangent, the ebb and flow of the word “gig” has been interesting to watch. When I first came across the word, it was only ever used in the context of a musical performance, and in the sense of a job, not in the sense of an entertainment. So “I have a gig with my band on Thursday”, but not “I’m going to Bob’s gig at the Flaming Toucan next Friday”, and not “I have a gig slinging coffee that pays the bills, but really I’m a poet”. At some point, I noticed that my Irish friends would talk about a “gig” as a musical performance they’d go to, not something they’d be paid for (“Are you going to the Altan gig next week?”), and I also noticed that in older usage, people certainly used “gig” to refer to regular and nonmusical employment. (I think Phil Lesh talked about having a “gig” as a postal carrier in the early days of the Grateful Dead, for example). All of which was perfectly normal flex for a word, connotations and denotations shift across language communities.
But then the rat bastards got hold of it, and “gig” became shorthand for mechanized wage slavery of the Uber/Lyft/Mechanical Turk sort, which sort of took the word out of my usage. The “gig economy” is a reversion to the worst excesses of unregulated capital, and I don’t want to have anything to do with it - they can have the word, I’m done with it. So I no longer talk about “gigs” at all, which is probably fine since I no longer look for (or indeed would accept) opportunities to play music for money, but if I did I’d probably refer to it as a “show” or something of that sort.

Anyway, it seems that the idea of a session is a pretty fluid one, it hardly seems necessary to bang on this dead horse any further. We’re looking at a Wittgensteinian “family resemblance” scenario, not something that has a precise definition that applies to everything that’s referred to as a “session” (or if you prefer, using the lovely back-formation “seisun”).

Re: Session or gig?

As others have said, a session can be seen as a “gig” for anyone paid to be there, but generally I tend to use the terms to describe openness. A “session” is something where someone not part of the group can join in, a “gig” is something where a certain set of musicians is contracted to play and they don’t have others join them.

The lines get blurry, obviously. Many sessions aren’t open to just *anyone* who comes in, there’s a certain expected level of skill, or even an expectation that you know one of the musicians before they’ll let you in. Playing in Eb is a way a lot of musicians maintain an “open” session while still limiting the number of people who can join in.

And then here in Ireland you get into the fact that even the “sessions” are often set up by the bar as enticements for tourists to come in. The place I play most often has an overhead mic to bring the sound all throughout the pub, despite it being an “acoustic” session. There are paid leaders, but anyone is allowed (and generally encouraged) to join. So it’s a gig in the sense that people are getting paid, but it’s got that openness that (for me, at least) is an important part of the session feel.

Re: Session or gig?

The microphone over the table scenario is, in my experience, one of the worst ideas anyone has ever come up with and I wish bars would stop this nonsense. The problem is, it’s never going to make the sound better, since the microphone is always going to pick up a good chunk of the background noise that it’s supposed to be competing with, so it’s just adding to the overall noise floor, which means that people are going to talk louder (because after all, they’re there to have a beer and hang out with their friends) so the musicians have a harder time hearing what they’re doing. The only thing I can imagine an overhead mic being useful for would be if the bar wants to provide a live web feed of the session for some reason - that might work, I guess. Not well, but it might work a bit.

If you’re at a pub that makes this mistake, I suggest you try asking the bartender to turn the overhead mic off for a night and see what happens. My prediction: the place will be quieter, people will enjoy themselves more, and the musicians will be able to hear each other - all in all, it’s going to be a massive win for all concerned. The only possible downside for the bar is that with people not having to shout over the speakers, they might not get quite as thirsty quite as fast, and so they might not go through quite as much beer - but I don’t know that anyone’s cynical enough to suggest that a bar might intentionally degrade the session experience just to sell a few more pints…

I would disagree with Jon Kiparskey about the usefulness of a sound engineer in an overhead miked session. If you simply hang a mic over the table then yes, you will have all the problems with amplified background etc. that people have mentioned. But someone with a little training as an SE will know what type of Mic to use (hypercardiod or shotgun that is deaf to the sides, NOT a normal cardioid or omni that will pick up sound from the side equally with sound from below) and he will know exactly where and at what height to hang it so that the players are all included more or less equally and almost everything else is excluded. In that situation the arrangement can work very well (although he also needs to know about speaker throw patterns and placement, which is just as important as mic placement).

Re: Session or gig?

The 1st time this question {session versus gig} came up for me was sometime around Y2K.
Almost complete strangers & some familiar friends’ (by chance) came together & we all started a session.
The one thing we had in common was an urge to play trad tunes.

The bit about playing tunes & starting a session was the best thing. Better than I might have imagined. But then we had an opportunity to help with a fund raiser at the Fox & Goose in Sacramento, CA. What could go wrong?

Everything, well almost. Actually the event was very fun both musically & socially. It truly was. But there was also a bit of tension (lot of tension actually) right below the surface. The session itself was fairly casual. Yet, when the day of the *event* began peoples’ expectations began to collide. It was almost a trainwreck.
This is where a session is not a performance in the traditional sense takes significant importance.
We were a session; not a band. The poop began to hit the fan.

For the most part we survived. The sessions continued. But those gigs, well they continued. They continued in 2 ways.
#1: Gigs; which were never discussed during a full session. A few session members would put together a *band* & we toured. Those gigs functioned as bands would be expected to. I am happy to have experienced this performance aspect of playing trad music. But, no, the gigs were not sessions as we discuss them on the site.

#2: Gigs w/the full session. These ranged from having good fun (mainly playing fixed/rehearsed sets @ ceilis, barndances, weddings, anniversaries… to less fun, often emotionally challenging events with way to high expectations & egos involved.

For me the bottom line is sessions are not gigs. If you play a gig; focus is essential. That’s professional.
A session is everything that a gig will never be. A session exists only in the minds of those playing; & only in the now.

Sessions aren’t gigs. You’re not band members!
