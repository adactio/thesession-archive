Re: Need help/commisseration getting over another hump

Sometimes it’s good to think about technical details, sometimes it’s not. Learn to know when thinking about the air is helping you (when you’re having trouble with a particular phrase, and there’s a non-intuitive use of the left thumb that’ll help you get through it) and when it’s just getting in your way (when you’re in a pretty ordinary tune with pretty ordinary air requirements.
If you’re playing Miss Monaghan’s, and you’re thinking a lot about the air button, you’re probably going to convince yourself you’re doing something wrong - even though on that tune, there’s really not a lot you can do wrong as far as air goes.
On the other hand, if you’re playing Sullivan’s (polka), you’re going to find that without really thinking about the air, you’ll be all the way out when you come back to the top of the A, and have no place to go for that long stretch of draw notes, so you’ll try to do things like play the Es on the press, and that won’t work, and then you’ll think you’re not a very good player. So there you need to figure out that by playing judiciously and NOT trying to get any air in on the A part - because the f#s are so quick, it’ll only screw you up - you can make it to the strong f# in the B part, which, given both repeats, is enough to get your bellows closed up again.
Yeah, that’s one I had to think about, I cite it as an example of a place where it really helps - most of the time, though, if you’re thinking about that air button, it’s going to distract you more than it helps you.

Re: Need help/commisseration getting over another hump

The other thing, which I was going to mention is the observer effect - happens in sessions as well. You play the tune at home, and you make a few little slips, and you get by them, no problem. You play the same tune with someone else listening, and you hear the mistakes, and they trip you up.
Not only that, but at home, you can allow the little hesitations and hitches in the rhythm - back up and fill in that part you missed. You don’t even notice you’re doing it. But when you get playing with someone else, that back-and fill destroys the tune, and you suddenly feel like a bumbling amateur.

So look at the way you play the tunes at home - pick one set you like to play, and really listen to yourself playing it. Stomp the one, and make sure you’re always aware of it. You don’t want to always push the downbeat, but you don’t ever want to lose track of it. If you hear yourself making a mistake, you can either stop entirely, or keep going - the one thing you MAY NOT DO is “correct” it. Get rid of that habit - it’s one almost everyone has to get past - and you’ll find that mistakes don’t kill a tune for you, and you’ll be a much stronger player just for that.

Re: Need help/commisseration getting over another hump

Playing this music is 90% mental. The other half is physical.

Not to plagiarize Yogi Berra, but that statement is an appropriate way to explain the struggles that you feel. You can have the physical ability down, but what really makes the music comes from the grey gooey stuff between your ears.

I don’t generally have much performance anxiety in most of the situations that I play music in. At home, teaching, at sessions, performing on stage, etc. So that makes it all the more surprising (and annoying) when it does hit. Last time I was in Ireland a few years ago, I found myself locking up in sessions, barely able to play things that I knew well. Partially because I was playing with people who I wasn’t familiar with, and I had this sense that I was being judged on my ability, and that sent my playing straight to the toilet. (I’m sure that was happening, but no more than it would happen anywhere else. So it was mostly irrational, subconscious feelings that were killing me, and I hated the fact that I couldn’t control it!). It was also somewhat due to the fact that their rhythm, or speed, or whatever, might have been a bit different than what I was used to. And I wasn’t good enough yet at the time to be able to adjust my playing (at the same time that I was having issues with nerves).

I think there’s a bit of Zen that the great players have, where they can push aside the inner critic, and rely upon the knowledge that they can play the music well in all sorts of different situations. I have actually talked about this specifically with a couple of world-class players. I asked one player the last time he was nervous with an instrument in his hand, and he surprised me by saying “every time I get on stage”.

So how do you get past it? For me, it’s a matter of taking it head on, and working through it. Find the times when you’re playing with other people, and things are clicking. Try to actively think about what situational elements are helping create that feel. Simply understanding what factors are leading to that, internally, can help you recreate that feeling at times when you need it. Gaining some confidence in your own playing will go a long way to helping you get over the hump!

Another thing that can help is to look at how far you’ve come in a mere 3 years. Take some pride in that, instead of dwelling upon “how far you still have to go” (because, you’re really never going to get there! The journey is unending…)

Re: Need help/commisseration getting over another hump

You’re just learning to play, that’s all. Music is not just about mastering an instrument then learning some tunes, although you have to do both. If you’ve only been playing for three years, it’s not surprising that you can’t remember a tune you ‘learned’ six months ago. Your playing - the way you ‘think’ musically - is different now to what is was then. You are hearing different things, noticing stuff you weren’t aware of before.
As has been said here, other people don’t hear your mistakes the way you do. Here’s why:- you yourself know what you intend to play, so if you make a mistake it is obvious -- to you. But nobody listening knows what you intended to play. If you don’t otherwise falter, no-one notices except the most experienced players, who don’t give a monkey’s.
Learning to accommodate mistakes is part of the process. As long as you don’t repeat a mistake too often, it’s nothing to worry about, and can often be serendipidous. I will just add that this will happen to you again (and again!) at various future stages. It might help to have a couple of recordings of yourself, one made early on that reminds you how much you have improved, and another that you quite like, so that you can reassure yourself you are not a total eejit when it’s all going supermammary.
Something else that might cheer you up: your playing always sounds better to other people than to yourself, and here’s why: for a piece of music to work well, it needs an element of surprise, something that catches you, gives a tweak, makes you smile. But it’s almost impossible to surprise yourself because you know what’s coming. That’s why some mistakes can be serendipidous: you can sometimes give yourself a little surprise. And a smile.
