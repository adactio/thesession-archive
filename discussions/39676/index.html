Re: “Second” whistle/flute

Some professional recordings have been done with that, most notably that Paddy Maloney “Tin Whistles” album. Sesions would give you the boot straightaway if tou tried something like that.

Take the time to learn to play tunes in a traditional style and at the proper tempo, and then come round the local session. Don’t show up and try to shove yourself into what is going on there if you can’t hang.

Sessions are musical conversations, and the language is Irish traditional dance music. You can’t very well converse if you don’t speak the langauge.

Re: “Second” whistle/flute

If you want to play a second part, start a band or find a like-minded playing partner. There are no second parts at sessions; sessions are about playing tunes.

Just learn the tunes properly and play them slowly. Its not necessary to go full-blast on every tune. Find a slow session or whatever in your area if necessary. If your goal is to play at sessions, avoiding playing tunes is only detrimental.

Re: “Second” whistle/flute

Mals, how long have you been playing? It takes a long time to become good enough to play in a session, and there aren’t any shortcuts. Slow sessions are, in my opinion, a terrible crutch for newer players. My experience with them is that the players whom freqent them often cannot play tunes on their own, cannot play in a traditonal style, or can’t be bothered to listen to and memorize tunes.
And so they form these slow sessions to try and fix those problems. The rub of it is that you have to learn all those things through your own individual practice, so I have seen quite a few who never move beyond that level (Slow session people, no need to come and make a big fuss about it, here are exceptions to every rule and you know who you are and where you stand as a player better than I do).

If you are serious about playing Irish traditional music then the way to proceed is to find a good teacher of your instrument, in person or on Skype, take lessons and practice hard for a long time. By all means go by the local session and listen to the music but don’t bring an instrument and certainly don’t sit in the circle until you can play tunes on the sessions terms.

Another thing to remember is that these people, whoever they are, didn’t wake up in the morning and decide to antagonize you. Clearly what you are doing at the session doesn’t fit with what is the established norm and they are trying to let you know that.

If you are interested, pm me and we can talk and get you on the right track.

Wesley Mann
(Also a flute player, also in his 20s)

Re: “Second” whistle/flute

We all start at the same point and have the same challenge. Learn an instrument and a bunch of tunes.
What I suggest is you spend a year at home learning tunes and listening to good trad players . A session is a group activity . Think of a group activity that your familiar with and good at so then you might understand why a novice might not be able to just join in. Say football, would it be ok if a guy just joins in without knowing the rules or have any skill, but wants to kick the ball around too……
While your learning your tunes try to find a guitar player who is open to learning the chords and you can start playing together. Start with one tune and the pair of you learn to play it well together. Then add another. If you can’t find a guitar player or fiddler say, who’s at your level to learn with then just do it on your own. There are no short cuts to gaining skill….. Practice.

Re: “Second” whistle/flute

Hello Mals (and others),

With apologies, I don’t want to appear to criticise (yes, with an ‘s’, as per the Queen’s English!) anybody, but I’m tuned considerably differently to what’s been offered above.

I play sessions in Scotland and Ireland regularly, and overseas occasionally, and have done for almost 40 years since I was in my early teens. Some of the most unfulfilling sessions are those where the only fayre is non-stop high-octane jigs and reels, usually played too quickly. Conversely, a good session leader will manage the dynamics, the energy, the mood and the volume - including the volume of the others in the pub, who are often in noise competition with the music, especially when they get bored of endless driven thrashing reels.

A really good session lives and breathes by interspersing the jigs and reels with airs, gentle 3/4s - free-flowing and not quite strict ‘waltz’ tempo, a few good songs or at least their melody lines, key changes, time signature and tempo shifts, ‘solo’ melodic masterpieces with perhaps just one melody artiste and a good, sympathetic accompanist or two. (Yes, I know, that makes it a duet or a trio – but you get my drift.)

These slower pieces (ie proper ‘slow tunes, not the jigs and reels slowed down) are excellent for the new guys to learn, and they can be confident centre (yes, centre, Queen’s English again) stage while playing the standard ‘simple’ melody line. The job of providing the ‘seconds’ then falls to the experienced players – and if they’re not up to the job, it’s they who need to go and learn some more (about harmonies, accompaniment, chord progressions, etc). Let’s be clear: there is no such thing as “THE guitar chords” as quoted above; the real depth and quality of a good tune are enhanced tenfold by expert tasteful accompaniment which varies according to interpretation, skill and the required ‘mood’ of the moment.

Of course, for any tune’s key there are guitarists who will honk away with the 3 or 4 standard chords, oblivious to the other harmony instruments, and possibly hearing the tune for the first time so they don’t know it properly as they should. Sometimes it’s wiser for these types to listen more and LEARN the tunes to the same depth as the melody principals have. But then there are real maestros on guitar (mandola, cittern, bouzouki, etc), piano accordion and piano who will transform the ensemble to several planes higher with expert sympathetic accompaniment. And if your melody players, some of whom might profess to be ‘expert’ and advise you to stay clear of their session, can’t hear and understand the interpretation and where the accompanist is going, and deliver a ‘second’ line that fits accordingly, then maybe that’s an area of their own musicianship that they could develop for themselves. Perhaps even using the ‘slow’ tunes, so everyone’s a winner…..

So, Mals, my advice to you is this: go look out some superb slow pieces (for example Jay Ungar’s ‘Ashokan Farewell’; written by a New Yorker, and loved as a session favourite here in Ireland and Scotland!), and work up your skill, confidence and repertoire from there.

I know this works, and it’s how I’ve mentored musicians and lead sessions and session schools for years. Good luck – message me if want more beautiful ‘slow’ tune suggestions.

Gordon

I’m not clear on what sessions only play reels and jigs at breakneck speed (something I’ve never been privy to). Fundamentally what makes a session good is the players not the tunes, and good players tend to know a lot of tunes and (lately) seem to be interested in playing at a reasonable or even relaxed tempo. At this point it has to be some manner of logical fallacy to claim that if a session doesn’t want to have airs (generally solo pieces) played, then all they want is super fast reels with no variety.

Back to the op, what rubs me the wrong way about the question is the same thing as someone buying a bodhran or a guitar and, sans knowledge of tunes or ability to play with the correct rythmn, shows up to a session and expecting to be welcomed in. There aren’t any shortcuts. You have to learn tunes and you have to be able to play at speed to play in a session.

Hornpipes and Barndances are played at a slower tempo and are a good place to start. Jigs are easier to play than reels so progress to these.
