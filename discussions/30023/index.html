Re: Concerting vinyl LP’s into CD’s

Buy a PC, a record deck, some blank CDs.
Plug the record deck into the line in of the PC. Dowmload Audacity for free. Put your vinyl on the deck. Place the ‘needle’ on the record. Click the record button of Audacity.
When it’s done click stop on audacity. Go to file and click on export. Save to a new folder as wavs. Bung a blank CD in the PC. It will prob ask you what you want to do. Click on burn audio CD. Drag contents on new folder and click on start burn. CD pops out of PC and bingo!
Before you export the Audacity you may want to seperate each album track. So highlight one track and click on export selection instead.

Re: Concerting vinyl LP’s into CD’s

Converting LPs to CDs isn’t a difficult process, but as all these are available on CD, you won’t be able to get as clean a sound doing the process yourself. What stands in the way to make it less than perfect ~ the LP, that medium, however little it may have been used, the record player and cartridge, the wiring, the interface that converts analog to digital, the software that you process it through and how much processing you do to it. With inexperience this is usually too much, or not at all. Either way, you won’t get as good an earful than if you just purchase a CD of said LPs.

There are some inexpensive turntables on the market that will convert and transfer the audio so you can burn a CD from it, none of those are very good, are, in my mind, a waste of money and time.

I know there are many others on site here who have done this, besides me. Others will follow. I use a piece of kit, hardware, that is wired between my sources, turntable and cassette decks, and our computer. I then have software I use to ‘gently’ and carefully do my best to try to clean up the audio some before eventually ordering tracks and burning a CD ~ but ~ only on recordings where there isn’t an option to purchase a CD. I’d rather the latter where possible.

Re: Concerting vinyl LP’s into CD’s

You don’t want to plug a turntable directly into the line-in jack. You need to go through a phono preamp to get the correct equalization applied. If you have a turntable that already plays through an amplifier and speakers, you can take the line-out from the amplifier directly into the computer’s line-in, because it has already been properly equalized. I’m guessing that’s what yhaalhouse meant.

If you do buy a phono preamp, don’t get junk (like Radio Shack). It’ll sound crappy.

Re: Concerting vinyl LP’s into CD’s

A turntable on its own that comes as part of a sound system will need some sort of preamp. There are software packages that include the right stuff. A cheap turntable, which includes most of the sorts you see advertised in The Guardian, etc., will not run at constant speed (in other words, you get “wow”, which is impossible to edit out later), and will almost certainly run at the wrong pitch. The cartridge/stylus will not be of the first water either. You can fix pitch on your computer, but cheapie software that does this will serve to coarsen the sound quality. You will not be able to remove all the clicks and hiss, no matter what the software claims, unless you are prepared to accept loss of the high frequencies. Yuk. Having cast all this gloom, I can attest to the fact that great results are possible as long as your turntable and stylus are decent quality. More than good enough to produce enjoyable digitised copies, definitely. The best advice before you start is to invest in some damn good record-cleaning kit. And defluff your stylus after every couple of tracks! But ask yourself whether all this investment in decent kit, not to speak of time, would not be better spent scouring eBay, etc., for decent second-hand CDs.

Re: Concerting vinyl LP’s into CD’s

I’ve digified hundreds of LPs over the years, it’s pretty straightforward once you figure a few things out. I’ve always used Adobe Audition, which originally was called CoolEdit. You can still obtain free copies of the older versions. This is a Windows program, I’ve no experience with Mac stuff. With Vista the older versions of Audition don’t work, and the newer ones are much less to my liking; so I keep XP on a desktop machine for dubbery. I use a laptop most of the time, and use it as a jukebox, basically. Computers are fantastic for that.

With Audition I first dub the whole record. Then you highlight the section of a track you’re interested in, and go to Save Selection. I set Ctrl-D as a hotkey for this. Then you save the track in its dedicated folder. I used to name everything “01 - Motorhead - Ace of Spades” etc to preserve track/artist info but have found a better way of going about that; you just need to save the track with its title.

Then delete the selected portion; I expand the highlighted section to the beginning of the next track. Then highlight that next track. I could do much the same with other programs like Audacity or Goldwave, but stuck with Audition in the end, I just like how it works better. Audacity also would crash every other file I’d load, too.

Now, I just listen to music on computer/iPod anymore, no CDs. With these machines you might take an interest in what are called “ID3 Tags,” these preserve additional info about the file - artist/album name, genre, track number. I listen to music using a program called Foobar2000 that has excellent tools for entering tags. A great standalone program for tags is MP3Tag. These are both free. With these programs you can just hit “Auto track number” and those will be imposed in the order the files were created. You just enter Artist/Album for all files.

Why bother? Without tags on an iPod nothing will be sortable in any fashion, just a jumble of files set in the order you loaded them in. The tags let you load all the Planxty, or all the Blag Flag, or all the Hindemuth, etc. Fun fun fun.

Hope that helps/made sense.

Re: Concerting vinyl LP’s into CD’s

I don’t have any fancy setup for transferring from LP to PC. I take the headphone jack out of the stereo to the microphone input of the pc.
The nuisance is setting up the recording levels, but once you set it up the first time, make note of what you did (pencil mark on the volume control of the stereo, and approximately where the input level is in Audacity.) It will be good enough for all your transfers. The sound quality will be good enough - just what you’re used to. Remember that your recording will never be better than what you hear on the LP.
A bigger nuisance is dividing the recording into individual tracks. I use Windows Media Player. With judicious use of copy and paste, you can minimize the pain in naming every resulting mp3 file and then naming each track.
If you’ve got time on your hands, do it with the equipment you’ve already got.
