Aeolian (or natural minor). The same as harmonic minor, but with no accidentals.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

Some points for guitar backers.

Doriian mode tunes generally require only two backing chords.
Example: E-Dorian tunes need only Em and D

Mixolydian mode tunes generally require only three backing chords.
Example: A-Mixolydian mode tunes need only A, G & D

Additional chords to the above won’t necessarily be dischordant, but they will be out of keeping with the mode, and will probably (quite rightly) annoy the melody players.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

It’s complicated.

Sticking just to tunes readily playable on a D whistle, keyless D Irish flute, and basic D uilleann chanter with only a High C natural key, the limitations of which have exerted an influence on the traditional repertoire, and also sticking to reels and jigs, it more or less boils down to

D Major and D Mixolydian and a huge number of tunes that switch back and forth due to the C alternating between C natural and C sharp. Beware that it’s not at that uncommon for the note F natural to appear.

D tunes in a gap scale D E G A B C or in a pentatonic scale D E G A C or D E G A B where there’s no 3rd but seem to have a minor feel to most accompanists.

E tunes either minor or dorian or switching back and forth due to C alternating. Note that there are E tunes in gap scales with no G and thus no 3rd, which like the D tunes with no 3rd are thought of as being minor.

G tunes in either Major or Lydian or alternating due to alternating C. (Scottish pipe music has a fair number of tunes in G Lydian, which sometimes appear in Irish music in C Lydian. Note that with these Scottish tunes G is the tonic and A is the dominant, and accompanists often stick to those two chords.)

A tunes in dorian or Mixolydian or alternating due to alternating C.

(Scottish pipe music has a huge number of tunes in A gap scales A B D E F# G, A B D E G, and A B D E F# which sound minor to people and gap scale tunes in A B C# D E F# which sound Major to people due to an implied G#.)

B tunes in dorian or minor or alternating due to alternating C.

C tunes in Lydian. Not sure how many there are in Irish trad other than Rakish Paddy, which started out as a Scottish pipe tune in G Lydian but got transposed.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

You can have all the theory you want but, in the end, you have to use your ears (and be ABLE to use your ears).

I tend to think of trad tunes as being major (i.e., major third is present - Ionian or Mixolydian), minor (minor third is present - Dorian or Aeolian) or neither* (no third at all - gapped).
It’s very easy to hear the difference between Ionian and Mixolydian. Not quite so easy to hear the difference between Dorian and Aeolian as often the sixth is not prominent so treat as Dorian unless you hear otherwise (but maybe stick to the one and the flat seven until you are sure).

*A common GHB scale is 1-2-4-5-b7. which can be played on A, B or E (as the root). It’s often treated as minor but is “neither” really.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

The really old Celtic tunes in their original versions are generally pentatonic or 5-toned - to illustrate, the black keys on a piano produce a pentatonic scale - which does not coincide with later applications of the mediaeval modes or with modern scales. Pentatonic music is often referred to as “gapped” but the gaps are only from the perspective of later music based on the 8-tone modes or on our familiar 8-tone scales.
Interestingly, traditional Chinese music and northwest coastal North American First Nations music are also basically pentatonic. Many other musical traditions around the world.
You can play the usual setting for Auld Lang Syne on the black keys of a piano. Many, many other old tunes - for example the Rolling Waves Jig (check the 2nd setting at https://thesession.org/tunes/515).

D major, play chords D, G and A;
D mixolydian, play D and C;
A dorian, play Am and G;

D major, play D drone

D major, do not play

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

The issue I’ve noticed more with guitarists has been about rhythm and phrasing. Several things any backer needs to understand about accompanying tunes…

1. The music is melody lead, which means…
…A: Circular 4-bar chord progressions don’t work.
…B: Chord changes happen with the phrasing.

2. Rhythmic accenting(in the guitarists case, “strumming pattern”) is more important than harmonic vocabulary. It doesn’t matter how many chords you know if you don’t understand the difference between jig rhythm and reel rhythm.

“Learn rhythm first with your root chords, heck you’ll learn most of the chords you need to know between the common keys.” (A tune in DMaj gives a IV and a V, from it’s GMaj and AMaj chords, which is plenty for someone who is still learning rhythm.)

Sorry that this doesn’t address modes or guides for such, but reading the OP I figured these were important points to bring up.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

“No substitutions” implies that there is some fixed set of chords for every tune that are “correct”.

That’s a very narrow way to look at the tunes.

There are the notes of the tune. Often they fall into a specific key and mode. That determines the most obvious triads, but those triads aren’t the only chords available within that key and mode if you start doing suspensions or deliberate tension-inducing dissonances which often sound just fine in context.

If you back using a sparse chord scale against drones based style like I do on GDAD tuned bouzouki, it’s mostly “substitutions” as there aren’t that many fully formed chords.

I am a strong believer when taking advice on this music that when someone tells you “never” or “always”, always take it with a grain of salt, including this advice.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

Will says this is meant to be descriptive, not prescriptive: “There is no substitution in traditional Irish music.”

Yet in practice, the most highly regarded players enthusiastically play substitutions *for the very notes* of the tunes—such variation and inventiveness is the acclaimed hallmark of “the best” players. You can hear this characteristic of the music going back to the earliest recordings and it continues; it’s why most tunes have so many versions. And it’s enshrined as an admirable quality in the oft quoted adage, “The same tune is never the same tune twice.”

I can’t think of a reason why the same wouldn’t apply to accompaniment, from the outset. Indeed, a good backer listens for the melody player’s substitutions. For example, if in a G major reel, say, a fiddler has a habit of coloring a 2nd string B note by adding a 1st string g double stop, and then the next time around plays the B agains the open e string, any guitarist with ears should be playing some form of G major chord in the first instance and an Em in the second.

Of course, to sound good, all of this is best informed by immersion in this music and open ears and an open mind.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

The “no substitutions” rule is probably one of those proscriptions aimed at beginner to intermediate level accompanists in ITM to keep them out of trouble. After all, the overriding Golden Rule for an accompanist is “do no harm.” Which means don’t upset the melody players with a substitution that might force the melody in a different direction, or add an unintended flavor to the tune.

Once you have your head deep inside the music, then maybe you can add something to the session with substitutions. But don’t succumb to the temptation just because you’re bored with simple chords. Make sure it’s actually working to support the melody.

For many accompanists it may be something more appropriate to a band with worked-out arrangements than the more unpredictable flow of a pub session. I enjoy hearing creative substitutions in recordings by famous bands. Whether I’d enjoy it in a session depends on the skill of the accompanist. It varies quite a bit in my experience.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

“Guitar is just one instrument , why is it that its the guitar players who want to lead the changes instead of follow them ?”

If a guitar player even knows how to play chord substitutions, they probably learned it in another genre of music where it’s a common practice like jazz, rock, or pop music. So they might bring it into ITM and think it’s just the normal thing to do. That is, if they’re not familiar with the convention of “just keep it simple and don’t annoy the melody players!” :)

A box player, uke player, or bouzouki player isn’t as likely to be familiar with the idea because those instruments aren’t common in the substitution-friendly genres of music. So it’s usually the guitar player getting the stink eye when they get bored with playing basic chords, and start entertaining themselves with substitutions instead of listening to the melody players and supporting them as more of a rhythm instrument.

Piano of course is the other instrument where an accompanist might be familiar with chord substitution from another genre, but they’re not as thick on the ground as guitar players in sessions. At least in my area.

In his initial post here, Conical bore makes good points, but they’re a given for any player at a session—don’t experiment so far beyond your ken that you wreck the music for others.

Re: Is there an accessibe guide to the various modes of Irish tunes (mixylodian, myxomatosis, etc

I am late to this thread, but for me, the simplest explanation is that for any major scale, there are seven notes separated by a defined progression of tones and semitones (Tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone). Other scales can be made using the same seven notes, but starting on different notes. Each possible starting note gives a different mode, and they sound different to the ear because the position of the semitones relative to the starting note changes, even though the key signature and thus the notes do not change. Most people hear the Dorian mode as sounding “minor”, even though technically minor scales have their first note starting on the 6th note of the major scale.

The list below gives the position of the starting note of a major scale (1) and the name of the mode:

1: Ionian
2: Dorian (sounds minor)
3: Phrygian
4: Lydian
5: Mixolydian
6: Aeolian (minor)
7: Locrian

The modes most common in Irish music are: Ionian, Dorian (“dor”), Mixolydian (“mix”) and Aeolian.

The keys 0f major scales and most common modes in Irish music are:

C (D Dorian, Gmix, A Aeolian)
D (E Dorian, Amix, B Aeolian)
G (A Dorian, Dmix, E Aeolian)
A (B Dorian, Emix, F Aeolian)

Nevertheless, when playing with accompanists who aren’t familiar with the music, I find they aren’t familiar with mode names either, so I just say “minor” when the mode is Dorian or Aeolian. As noted already, many tunes are ambiguous, either because the 7th note changes, or the mode changes between parts, or both.

Yes 100% agreed!

This strikes me more as a Highland piper than as an ITM player. Because it’s common with ITM albums and videos and sessions to have harmonic accompaniment, while the Highland pipe band world revolves around competitions where only pipes and drums are allowed.

It’s so wonderful to hear a pipe tune transformed with skillfully chosen chords. Many years ago I heard one of the army pipe bands playing along with the military band (brass & woodwinds) doing Crags of Tumbledown.

Before the military band joined in it was just the pipes, and the drones force everything to resolve to A, especially when the melody is on A or C#. But when the military band began doing backing chords there’s a part where the melody is on A and the military band played D Major, another part where the melody is on C# and the military band played F# minor. It was beautiful and completely transformed the tune.

(NB I’m using the written notes, sounding pitch is a half-step higher.)
