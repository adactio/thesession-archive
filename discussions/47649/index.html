Going slow to go fast

“Go slow to go fast”. This is a statement that I’ve read many times, and it works for my guitar playing as well. Repeatedly playing a tune over and over until it’s in your bones and muscle memory has taken hold is the method that works for me to begin to play a tune on flute, whistle, and guitar with relative ease. However, I seem to reach a threshold. Clearly, we are not all the same. Our minds and bodies all work slightly different when it comes to playing music on an instrument. Often, I can hum a tune along at tempo if the tune is in my bones, but it seems that even then- after years of playing- that sometimes I just max out. Now, to be fair to myself, I did take an 18 year break from all things IrTrad. Guitar playing filled the gap. Now back for the past six months, I’m playing better than I did years ago, but I fear that being able to play at session tempo may be quite a hard task to accomplish. My mind can do it, but getting the fingers to play it at tempo- and cleanly- seem to be like it’s going to be a long, long road.

I’ve recently began going back over all the tunes I know and making sure that not only am I playing them accurately, but with better ornamentation, and better intonation. I’m working hard on phrasing because it’s so paramount in Irish music. I would rather play slowly with great tone and phrasing than play fast and poorly. However, I’d like to be session-speed capable one day too, even though I have no local sessions at this time.

I guess the point of my post here is this- do a resign myself to always being a more slow player, or do I continue down the past of “slow is faster” and just push the tempo up incrementally for each tune as I learn them until I reach either the desired tempo or lose control of the tune/instrument?

Thanks for suggestions

ET

Re: Going slow to go fast

The majority of the music we play in this tradition is dance music intended for playing at a lively tempo to get your feet moving. I don’t enjoy sessions where the music is played so fast that the dynamics are flattened out, "steamreeling’ the tunes. But a too-slow tempo for jigs and reels can be deadly too, taking the life and lift out from the other direction.

So I try to aim for common dance tempos -- say, 100-112 bpm for a reel (counted 2/2) -- even if it takes me years to get there, and I’m still climbing that mountain on a few of them. Six months to get your chops back isn’t very long, just be patient and keep working at it. Slow progress is still progress!

P.S. At home, I play certain reels as “slow reels,” the way some famous trad artists have done on recordings because I like how they sound that way. John Doyle’s arrangement of The Morning Dew for example. That’s one way to have your cake and eat it too, with faster tunes for sessions and slower ones for enjoyment at home as solo pieces. I just have to make sure I’m putting that approach in a separate category, so I don’t get lulled into not working to keep my tempo up on tunes that really “want” to be played at dance tempo.

Re: Going slow to go fast

I don’t think you need to resign yourself to being something that you don’t want to be. If you are interested in being able to play faster, it’s almost certainly achievable. The play slowly to be able to play fast thing is one part of the equation. When you’re doing that, you’re internalizing the behavior, or developing it into a neural pathway that knows how to play what you’re playing. And it’s an important part of being able to play any music.

But I am also a firm believer in the fact that to learn how to play well fast, you have to play fast to get good at it. And the best way I ever found for doing that was by playing in sessions with people that were better than me. You can get some of that by playing along with recordings. And we have all sorts of fun technology these days that allow us to change the tempo of recordings without changing the pitch. But there’s something about playing in person with people that makes it feel more urgent. In other words, it may be somewhat difficult for you to get your playing up to “session speed” without actually playing in sessions. But “session speed” is about more than tempo, it’s also about lift, lilt, and expression. The better you have internalized the physical motions required to play this music well on your instrument, the better prepared you will be to adapt to how other people are playing.

So back to the playing slow to play fast thing for a minute -- people tend to think of this so-called “muscle memory” as being a way to remember how a tune goes, and a certain percentage of the people who learn this music will learn a tune, relegate it to “muscle memory” and subsequently play that tune that way forever, without any real musical expression. They almost become like a computer and just spit out the tune the same way every time, which isn’t necessarily very musical. What I think you should be looking for with the slow, deliberate playing is building the neural pathways (“muscle memory”) that know how to play your instrument. You internalize things like how to reach a particular interval from a note, and how to play the ornaments that you use to make your tunes more musical…

Once you have a skill that you’ve really fully internalized, then executing that skill at a different speed isn’t particularly difficult. Here’s a link to another blog, which is written by a neurologist, who also happens to play clawhammer banjo. His whole blog is worth the read (if you can ignore the not so subtle promotion he is doing for his online banjo lessons). But here’s one post he has specifically about being able to play faster, and why the slow practice helps: https://clawhammerbanjo.net/the-immutable-laws-of-brainjo-the-art-and-science-of-effective-practice-episode-14/

Re: Going slow to go fast

abstract fast, abstract slow. put numbers on things. use the livebpm app to clock the speed of your local session. around here, session speed is 90 bpm for reels, 120 bpm for jigs. (it is the same speed in notes/second). nominal dance speed is 112 bpm for English Country Dances, 120 bpm for Contra dances, again, clock the speed of dance videos posted on youtube. Listen to players who play your favourite tunes fast, watch for what they do differently from you. if you want to play fast, play fast, but choose your tunes carefully, on mandolins, spootiskerry at 90 bpm is easy, catharsis (in Gm), not so much. get some lessons, tell the instructor “I do not want to learn more tunes, I want to play reels at 120 bpm, can you teach me that?”. There is teachers who specialize on this. Expect going back to basics, how to sit/stand, how to hold the instrument, how to warm up. for fast playing every little detail is important.

Re: Going slow to go fast

The goal of slow playing is to ensure your mechanics (physically moving your fingers, breathing, adjusting your embouchure, etc.) are accurate, effortless, and smooth. All three of these qualities are essential for playing cleanly and musically at any speed, and it’s too easy to skimp on any one of them when you’re struggling to keep up at speeds above your comfort range. So yes, keep doing the slow practice, but pay close, deep attention to every finger motion, every inhale and exhale, every flex of your embouchure being accurate, effortless, and smooth.

I’d go so far as to say, if you’re *not* paying attention to those things, then slow practice becomes nothing more than learning to play slowly. The point of slow playing is to give your brain time enough to attend to every nuance and detail.

I agree with Reverend that sessions with good musicians—those whose playing you admire and aspire to—is a great way to immerse yourself in the higher speeds (and lift and pulse, etc.) where dance tunes thrive. Before you do that, consider two skills that you can add to your slow playing focus: relaxing and “staying small.”

Faster music tends to sound more energetic, so most of us are tempted to make our finger movements more energetic, too. Soon, our fingers are flailing all over, which makes it harder to keep the pace, and then, trying even “harder,” we tense up. It’s a recipe for crashing and burning 8 beats per minute below the pace everyone else is sailing along at.

So one of the keys to playing fast is to stay relaxed and erase any tension (anywhere) that tries to creep in. Remember to breath not just to fill your flute or whistle but to replenish your lungs. Relax your feet, your legs, your abdomen, your throat, etc. Relax your arms and hands, your neck and face.

The second key is that, as you go faster, make your movements smaller. Don’t lift your fingers too far above the tone holes, don’t bang them into the flute like you’re trying to drill through it. Instead of gulping for air, sip it. Find the smallest, easiest, least amount of effort that gives you the sound you want.

You can practice these concepts while slow playing, to really focus on how it feels to play effortlessly and small, but you also have to test them at higher speeds. Slowing the tempo on YouTube clips is a great way to play along with your musical heroes, mimicking their ease and economy of motion, and then gradually increase the tempo. (Use the “custom” setting in the playback speed menu.)

And find a session, even if you have to travel a bit. The immersion really does help.

P.S. At a session, it’s also absolutely fine to tell the people you’re sitting next to, “I’m just getting back into playing, and I’m struggling a bit to keep up at faster speeds. Would you mind helping hold this set to a lope?” (Not every session will accommodate such a request, but many will, and it doesn’t hurt to ask. If they won’t help out, then find a more suitable session, at least till you get your chops up to speed.)

And you don’t need a full-on session to work on tunes and tempo. If you can find just one or two other decent musicians to play with, that’s a session, and they can help steady the tunes while you focus on relaxation, economy of motion, etc.

Also, mandocello8 makes a good point about the value of warming up. When Dervish or Lunasa come on stage and jump right into a blazing set of reels, rest assured they’ve been gently knocking the dust off in the green room for 30 minutes beforehand.

Re: Going slow to go fast

The bulletproof blog, Reverend, Conical, Gimpy, and others have said what I would: if your goal is getting comfortable with speed, practice playing up to speed as well as practicing slow. Practice precision when playing slow -- keep finger movements economical/small, as big movements will become flailing when going faster, and get good tone -- then play those parts faster. Keep relaxed.

I like focusing on the hardest couple of bars of a tune, practicing those bars slowly, then taking them up as fast as I can play them cleanly. If it’s getting messy, I dial the tempo back a few BPMs until I’m not flailing around any more. But focus on economy of movement and relaxation and get that phrase up to my target tempo -- dance tempo of 113 is a sane goal. Once I have the hardest phrase at tempo, the rest of the tune usually can fall in line pretty quickly!

Re: Going slow to go fast

Whatever you do to get up to the speed of a fast session, there are a couple of things I would ask you to remember (sorry if I’m teaching my granny to suck eggs here).

1. When fast music sounds lively, it’s not becuase it is fast, it’s because it is lively. Nearly every musician I met who could play fast and lively could play slow and lively too.

2. The biggest impediment I have heard to people moving up to a faster (but also lively) speed in a session was trying to put in more ornamentation than they needed (or were able to). If the fastest you can play that G roll is 0.3 seconds then don’t try to still play it when you need to play it in 0.2 seconds to keep the beat. I believe that transcription of tunes including too much optional ornamentation has led people to believe the ornamentation is all obligatory (and it isn’t). Play the basic tune to get up to speed. Put the ornamentation back in later, when you sound fast and lively.

PS: controversial opinion here: you should never speed up a tune that someone else has started. If they want to speed it up they will do it themselves. Otherwise you are heading for a mushy, unlively sound because people are not actually listening to each other.

Not sure if it applies to the flute/whistle as I play neither, but with the instruments I do play (tenor banjo/mandolin/concertina) one thing that can be common when people try to play faster is that they don’t let the notes sound fully, instead lifting their fingers/releasing the button too soon so that the note gets cut off and choked, leading to a very staccato sound vs. a flowing sound. Some folks don’t seem to be aware that they’re doing that, so they continue to play that way, leading to engraining those less than ideal mechanics. Just something to keep in mind when working to build speed up, and a good reason to get in the habit of recording your progress every so often to keep on top of stuff like that.

Re: Going slow to go fast

The mechanics of playing fast are often different to playing slow. The problem with always playing slow and never playing fast is that you can counterintuitively allow and cement bad habits that prevent speed, or just simply not learn what it feels like to play fast.

If we agree here, that ‘fast’ means ‘up to speed at a session’ rather than ‘faster than necessary’ - and then that playing fast is the aim, then there needs to be some fast practice as well as slow. The saying does not mean that playing slow will guarantee you can play fast - just that playing slow will improve your ability to play fast, when you do.

The two techniques I used most as a beginner:
- Playing a tune, slowly increasing the metronome
- Repeating 1 or 2 bars of a tune with a metronome (focusing on getting each 1 or 2 bars sounding right at speed) - then putting it together

All the while, trying to be conscious of what happens when you fluff up or sound less good. In general I would practice playing above your comfort zone at least a bit (if your comfort zone is below the required speed for your session)

Re: Going slow to go fast

I watched a Kevin Burke video on YouTube about this topic recently. He had a very different attitude towards the ‘go slow to go fast’ point of view, which made a lot of sense to me. He recommended practicing tunes fast, but in chunks. One phrase at a time. Keep playing that phrase until you can play it up to speed. His point was that you play tunes physically differently at a fast speed - your bowing is different.

This has already made a huge difference to my (fiddle) playing. It was a bit of epiphany to me, to realise that practicing slowly might be in some ways entrenching some bad habits. Obviously it goes without saying that you can’t play something fast unless you can play it slow. But it’s been an interesting experiment for me to try learning new tunes at session speed. I’ve tried doing this with a metronome set to about 115bpm, and it’s surprising how - by repeating each phrase again and again and again - I can play tunes at much faster speeds than I had previously thought I could. Not exactly an earth-shattering piece of advice of course.
