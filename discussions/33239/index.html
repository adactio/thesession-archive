Re: Sessions at so-called Irish pubs

I too bemoan the lack of decent session spaces but on the other hand have heard so many bad sessions over the years I can’t blame the pub managers for showing them the door. People can forget that they are in a business premises not a public space and treat a session like a club rather than a symbiotic arrangement by which they provide the entertainment in return for the performance space and the possibility of a few fringe benefits. It’s a chicken and egg thing really it’s hard to create performers and musicians if there’s no where to do it, inspire and learn.

I remember hitting a popular hotel session at one of the UK’s biggest folk festivals it had been taken over by about 20 accordion players, they were having a ball but the landlord was pulling his hair out, the bar had emptied at one of his busiest times and the lowest common denominator music was turgid. He was so chuffed that I turned up with my highland pipes he said get and play and get rid of that lot and you’ll not have to buy another drink. Needless to say that is exactly what I did. The rest is a blur but I do remember the bar filling up again quite quickly and with a variety of instruments that time by which I was content to limit myself just to listening and everyone else was probably content to let me too!

Re: Sessions at so-called Irish pubs

I come at this from both sides. I owned bars, restaurants, and nightclubs for quite some time years ago. I did a lot of things to support the local music scene (not trad at that point in my life), but you can only be so generous to the scene if it’s not making you money.

But as a trad musician, I know that, while it’s not everybody’s bag, it *can* be a desirable thing for an establishment, and can pull in a crowd. It depends on the place, of course. But one session I started years ago wasn’t even in an Irish pub. It was in a small town restaurant, and carried a novelty that didn’t exist elsewhere in town. It lasted for almost almost 7 years before the novelty wore off enough that the mutual decision was made to cut it off.

And there’s another long-running session in a college-town Irish pub near me, which is successful enough to draw a crowd, including college students on Sunday nights.

I think part of the key to success is what the pub is trying to be. Having a session can give a pub a sense of authenticity. Part of the key to success is the attitude of the players. Even when not getting paid, lots of players seem to treat it like they’re doing the pub a favor, and come off a bit like prima donnas. And finally, part of the key to success is communication with the pub, and their staff. Setting expectations, and getting them to understand that the session is not the same thing as “free entertainment”, or whatever. The session exists for the players to get together and share some tunes and crack. And if you set it up right, educate the pub, the staff, and the punters to an extent, and play good music, it really can be a success.

Re: Sessions at so-called Irish pubs

People sitting round a table with a glass of water each all night, making a mess, dragging their feet at closing time, not playing particularly well and generally ignoring everyone else, including the staff, are not going to endear themselves to the landlord or manager, whose house it is, and whose guests they are.
A significant number of people, on the other hand, who spend enough over the bar to pay for the staff, or encourage others to spend who would not otherwise, who leave readily when asked to do so, who are cheerful and entertaining to staff and other customers and who play reasonably well and generally act with good humour and courtesy, will be spurned only by someone whose sole purpose in life is watching sport on television.
A great deal depends on the size and layout of the premises, the business acumen and character of the person in charge, how busy (or not) and how well established the place is, the desired day and time, and numerous other factors specific to the area, such as passing trade, local offices or shops, etc.
In my limited experience, Irish Theme Pubs are a marketing ploy and nothing else, instigated by pub-cos, with a fixed idea of what to put in them and how to run them. The only Irish Theme Pub I’ve been in that was actually like an Irish pub, was one where the rain came through the ceiling into the bar. Sadly, they eventually fixed the roof.

Re: Sessions at so-called Irish pubs

I’ve started sessions at pubs over the years. The successful ones are ones where management is behind the music and the musicians - and supports them not only with free drinks, but free food as well. For example, The Harp in North Amherst, MA (USA) has a very successful and long running session on Friday nights that starts at 9 and goes til closing. So successful, in fact, that they’ve started a slow jam (for beginners) on Thursday nights.

I say approach a local restaurant/pub/cafe. It doesn’t have to be Irish. And explain what a session is and why it would be a win-win for the venue (increased traffic on slow days, etc). Volunteer to run it/manage it/market it (to take away the sting for a venue that has no experience with trad music) and show up on time and sober. Be consistent, patient, and work with management.

I’ve never heard of a session round here (southern midland England) that regularly provides musicians free drinks. One place the landlord brings out a jug of beer towards the end of the evening, when most people have already bought some drinks, at another he brings out some sandwiches and chips.

Most sessions have to happen when the pub would otherwise be relatively quiet, unless the session is really well known, and the pub is small enough to be filled by sessioneers and enthusiasts. (Know where I mean?!)

The crucial ingredient is surely musicians! Enough decent players who know each other and know that if they turn up they’ll get some decent playing. If the music is decent that keeps the landlord happy.
I’m surprised if anyone knows of a session that survives on freebies without the other ingredients.

I read many years ago Cairan Carson’s Last Night’s Fun..and,through the years,his section on the politics of the session have been etched in my mind- He hypothesizes that a “true” session will never be a money-making event, or a show attraction for bar. It isn’t a “show” And, many times, when a bar owner who isn’t really in touch with the music realizes this, the plug gets pulled. A session is about sharing tunes and chatting, and spending money on drink is not necessarily job 1 every musician-and a bar is in buisness to make money.

If the bar owner is in touch with the music, thats the key for having a bar supporting of a session. The bars where I am that have successful sessions- the owners know the music, and the musicans and provide a time for them… support is what it is all about- if a bar owner needs pursuading, it’s like a brick wall, so why not go elsewhere?
