Re: who would pay $2250 dollar for a low whistle?

Yes I’ve never seen a whistle go for anything near that amount.

But… if somebody is a serious player and/or a professional player, someone who has a $5,000 flute, or a $20,000 set of uilleann pipes, or what have you… and that whistle is hands-down the ideal instrument for them, why not?

Let’s not forget that professional musicians, who declare their music income, can write off instrument purchases as business expenses here in the USA. A studio musician might make $2,000 from a single gig (not initially, but those residuals add up) and if that whistle gives the composer the sound he wants, it’s well worth it.

I think the best-sounding Low D Whistle I’ve ever heard is the Copeland played by David Brewer. It sounds remarkably like a flute when he plays it.

This is going to be an oversimplification, so my apologies beforehand, don’t take it to serious.

(like I said, an oversimplification)

Re: who would pay $2250 dollar for a low whistle?

Even though an Irish Low Whistle has a lot fewer pieces than a saxophone, it seems to me that making a really good one is just as difficult to accomplish.

I’ve owned a large number of Low D Whistles from a large variety of makers, sometimes as many as a half-dozen from the same maker, and each one is different, and I’m yet to find one that does everything really well. Like all wind instruments, Low Whistles are a bundle of compromises. I would gladly pay $2,000 for a Low D Whistle that did everything excellently, or even one that didn’t have one glaring flaw.

1950s Selmer Mark VI’s have the same mechanical complexity as saxes made before and since, yet good players agree that there’s something special about them that no-one has been able to duplicate.

Re: who would pay $2250 dollar for a low whistle?

About the comment above that Low D Whistles are made from a straight piece of store-bought tubing, note here an excerpt from an interview with Michael Burke by Dale Wisely:

Michael: "The bore is not the same diameter everywhere from top to bottom. Most
whistles have at least a constriction at the top, but that is about it.
My low D has 5 bore tuning perturbations to pull octaves into alignment,
adjust for individual notes ease of overblowing and tuning, and to
strengthen the low end notes for that nice booming low end players like.
No instruments are pure cones or pure cylinders. In the case of my
whistles, I have used constrictions and expansions of the bore diameter
to tailor the responses of the bore to produce good tuning and output
volume."
