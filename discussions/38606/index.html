Re: Why do many disregard the notion of Celtic identity?

One of the reasons is that the “Celtic” identity has been so stretched from any original meaning that it doesn’t really mean enough to be useful. It could mean areas where Celtic languages are spoken, which would make Ireland, Scotland, the Isle of Man, Wales, and Brittany “Celtic.” Cornwall has revived their Celtic language, so maybe throw them in. Oh, and Celtic languages were spoken throughout England, too, and the musics share many similarities with Irish and Scottish music (there are even bagpipes!), so maybe we’ll add them in. What about the north of Spain, you say, they have bagpipes and had some Celts there at some point, right? Sure, why not! And in many areas of France there’s music and culture that can be traced back to the Gauls, so throw that in as well.

And so on and so forth, until you realize that the “Celts” once occupied most of Western and Central Europe, and rural musics and culture in those areas share many common bonds that may or may not be able to be traced back to some essential “Celticness.” The truth is, the fiddle, bagpipe, accordion, flute, and drum are all used in many European folk musics for dancing, and the reel, jig, and polka in particular are found in various forms across the continent. So for me, at least, the Celtic designation isn’t very meaningful unless you’re talking about linguistics.

Re: Why do many disregard the notion of Celtic identity?

Bigsciota has summed things up nicely. The term “Celtic” is most certainly useful in linguistic contexts when referencing languages which have derived from Proto-Celtic, or the associated populations int he same context. It can be useful in historical contexts as well, though things can become a bit murky even then at times, especially in regards to tribes that inhabited regions along the borders of certain historical populations of mainland Europe.

“Celtic” is usually a label used to sell things that fit a certain type of invented fantasy culture which usually has little connection to the actual culture, history, or traditions of the parts of the world usually considered to fall under that moniker. Or, it may be used as a catch-all, but at what point does that end, and where do you draw the boundaries? There really are no unifying characteristics that are generally agreed upon by most people who tend to use the term frequently.

Better to just specify what you are actually talking about, and avoid the term altogether unless used in specific historical or linguistic contexts. If you want to see the difference between how the words are used, or what sort of people use the words, simply type “celtic music” into YouTube and look at the results…

I see the word thrown around frequently in mandolin groups, usually by people who play Irish tunes as though they are Bluegrass (or play them in a way that resembles a very basic MIDI file). If you are going to use the word, it is best to not use it in relation to traditional music. It is generally a hallmark of people who have no clue what they are talking about.

Re: Why do many disregard the notion of Celtic identity?

“How is it any less solid then any other identity ? What characteristics unify it?”

My thoughts- The problem is that the Celts as a people and culture have a very wide and varied history that dates back at least to around 700 BC. They at some time or other occupied nearly every corner of what is now Europe. It simply is not possible to “connect the dots” across 27 centuries and draw some kind of linear progression of Celtic culture. As mentioned above, the Celts seemed to have originated as a people and culture around 700 BC in what is now parts of Germany and Austria. They spread across Europe rather rapidly. The Celtic empire at one point rivaled the Roman empire in size and scope. In fact, the Celts were a thorn in the side of the Romans for centuries, until around 100 AD (IIRC) the Romans decided they had enough and launched a crusade against the Celts. They made it their mission to wipe the Celts off the face of the Earth and might have succeeded if not for a few small bands of Celts that escaped across the Channel to the British Isles, where the Romans finally gave up chasing after them. A few pockets of Celts in Spain and France also survived the carnage. Over 27 centuries the Celts had their influence in so many areas and so many cultures that, today, one can just about define “Celtic” as whatever they want it to be. But if one could truly define today’s Celtic culture in some accurate, narrowly defined terms, I doubt that it would bear much resemblance to the original ancient Celts of Germany.

David E.

This probably just muddies the already murky waters, but I think there are several meanings of Celtic that get lumped together in conversations like this. There’s the ancient historical, tribal meaning, which is (as has been argued above) largely irrelevant to discussions of music and modern cultural identity. Then there’s the linguistic meaning, which starts to have some bearing on the modern state of things, since the languages still are (or recently have been) spoken in the places now considered Celtic.

The other meaning I see is the modern manufactured identity which somehow manages to include Galicia (Galician is not a Celtic language). This meaning/identity has to do with a fast and loose take on historical and linguistic details, and seems to be more about an identity created in contrast to the dominant culture (Galicia vs Spain, Brittany vs France, Scotland/Ireland vs England). There’s nothing inherently wrong with this, of course, but it’s problematic that these identity politics use a term (Celtic) that has real historical and linguistic connotations. That’s where it gets messy. What about Celtic for the historical stuff and Keltic for the modern stuff?

Re: Why do many disregard the notion of Celtic identity?

I think my most definite take on ‘Celtic identity’ is: “Something owned - to a greater or lesser extent - by those who know for certain or assume with high probability that they are of direct descent from people who lived in Britain or Ireland before the coming of the Romans and the Germanic peoples to Britain, and before the settlement of Vikings and English/British in Ireland: owned, that is, by those to whom such knowledge actually has any significance.”

This picture is rather like that of a huge flock of birds most of whom are together in a dense, substantial bolus whirling along through space-time, but many of whom represent a ragged, frayed edge to this. There are plenty of ifs-and-buts to the definition I put above, but I feel ‘Celtic identity’ is something that does have substance.

This definition roots ‘Celticness’ in the races who lived in Britain / Ireland in the prehistoric past, in the related Gaelic and Brythonic languages whose early forms they would (most of them, at least) then have been speaking, the formative and ongoing effects of the Insular landscape and climate(s?) they lived in, and the culture they had then, whose development over the ages seems to have been organic and persistent in enough ways; and, especially, in the fact that descendants of these people are still very much here, with the descendants of the languages I mentioned (for all that not all the people speak these), and their history and lore, feeding a lot of their consciousness, has been well recorded and researched to a very long way back.

One can in this wise isolate a distinct and definite pedigree. But it does not take into account some of those who might be in, or beyond, the “frayed edges”: historical arrivals into these societies who assimilated, people in mainland Europe whose remote ancestors may have been kin to the Insular Celts but who then became wholly Latinised, and so on. Not that being of any one known ancestral group ever, per se, made one person superior to another from another group. Different, in some ways, maybe… isn’t that supposed to be the spice of life? :-)

Yes, it would be. But that isn’t what people are doing when they talk about Celtic culture in a modern context. Ancestory has absolutely nothing to do with it, what we are talking about are communities that speak (or spoke until very recently) languages derived from Celtic language as opposed to the Germanic and Romance languages spoken in most of the rest of Europe.
