Re: Why is it that pint glasses, set in shuffling motion by massed foot tapping…

Shock is created by impact of one mass against another e.g. foot stomping while playing. The shock impulse caused by the impact travels through the table as a deflection wave. The shock will generally cause affected beer glasses to vibrate. Reduction in shock severity by use of suitable isolators e.g., a beer mat or fuller pint, is achieved by the isolators storing the energy of the shock through isolator deflection and subsequent release in a smoother form over a longer period with lower overall amplitude therefore your pint will be less likely to fall, or at least it will take longer to reach the edge.
I believe from my observations, that such vibrating glasses invariably travel toward the source of the initial shock i.e., the foot-stomper or in the case of massed foot stomping the most aggressive foot-stomper, thus some justice prevails as the pint belonging to the source of the most vibration will generally be the first to fall.

Preventative measures include the use of a beer mat, prohibiting foot-stomping and replenishing your glass.

Re: Why is it that pint glasses, set in shuffling motion by massed foot tapping…

“Simple gravity: the foot stamp slightly depresses the floor surface, and the glasses travel downhill.”

That assumes zero friction between glass and table - an unlikely scenario. It is the sudden downward motion of the tabletop that causes the glass to be momentarily ‘weightless’ (or to completely lose contact with the table). Thus, the friction between glass and table is reduced, allowing the glass to slide, for a short moment, in whichever direction gravity pulls it in - that being determined primarily by the slant of the table.

Re: Why is it that pint glasses, set in shuffling motion by massed foot tapping…

There are a number of reasons. Ultimately, I would suggest, gam’s is the main reason. Shock waves travel out from the source. When the table is suoorted by one middle leg, as is usually the case, the vibrations from the banging of your foot travel up the leg and out through the table in all directions.

When a table is supported by multiple legs, say 4, 1 at each corner then any instability will be caused by one leg being shorter than the others or slightly bent.

Add to this the fact that most glasses are placed around the edges of a table placing, over time, extra force on the edges, thereby making many of the tables slightly convex. Also, with the glasses round the edge it will be these areas of the table that will be the wettest.

There is also an element that glasses could be going in all directions.

You can analyse it based on individual pubs for local phenomena. In the Ben Nevis, to quote an example the OP is familiar with, the glasses always fall off the one side of the table, the side nearest the long bench against the wall. This is for a number of reasons. Firstly imperfections in the floor round there, secondly damage to that side of the table (check it out!) and thirdly, that has the greatest concentration of musicians and being on one long bench their force of stamping is probably combined better than that coming from the little stools on the other side. The main reason, of course, is that that table is just not even. Too many folk have leaned on it over the years and it slants in towards the bench.

Re: Why is it that pint glasses, set in shuffling motion by massed foot tapping…

The “bread always falls on the floor butter-side down” phenomenon has been explained fairly satisfactorily by taking into account the height from which it falls, the speed of rotation imparted to the slice of bread about its centre of gravity as it leaves the hand, and of course the gravitational acceleration. A mathematical analysis has shown that these can naturally come together to ensure in many cases that the bread hits the floor butter-side down. If the bread fell from a specific greater height it would doubtless tend to land butter-side up.
The pint glass falling off the table is indeed akin in some respects. However, I have yet to hear of a glass with a significant amount of Guinness falling off a table and landing on the floor without spilling a drop, although there have probably been some occasions when someone with unusually fast reactions has caught it in mid-flight (I once saw someone catch a falling wooden flute before it hit the stone floor).
