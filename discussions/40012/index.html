What’s a reasonable price for a workshop from a world-renowned player?

I’m organising a 2-hour workshop/masterclass by My Favourite Living Irish Fiddle Player and I’m not quite sure what to charge

… it’s in London (UK), and the venue needs to be reasonably central, so I have to factor in things like cost of the room hire, which ups the likely price a little more than the ideal… but would it be reasonable to charge between £15 and £20 for a ticket for a 2-hour workshop from a player such as, say, Kevin Burke or Tommy Peoples or Frankie Gavin or Sean Keane? I know I’d be prepared to pay that myself, because I’m a fiddle obsessive and these things don’t happen that often in London… but I’m interested to know what others think?

Re: What’s a reasonable price for a workshop from a world-renowned player?

A workshop is sort of a big preplanned lesson, where the instructor will have decided what he or she wants to teach in advance and will use that material as a vessel to impart some of his or her style and technique. A masterclass will have each student get up and play something and receive a short 1 on 1 lesson on what they played in front of the class. That way everyone attending can learn from each other and the instructor at the same time.

Workshops tend to be better for less advanced players, and masterclasses better for the more accomplished musicians.

Re: What’s a reasonable price for a workshop from a world-renowned player?

Isn’t this a fairly backwards way to prepare charging for a workshop?

Typically the artist has a fee, and there are other hard costs/overhead which can vary wildly depending on the venue (rental, insurance, sound reinforcement, etc.), and in most cases the presenter would like some funds for the effort. After adding all of this then you imagine a best case scenario and a worst case scenario for attendance, find a comfortable middle ground of numbers of attendees, and divide that number into all of your costs, and there is your admittance charge (rounded to a reasonable decimal).

Having been a presenter for a few decades I found there is a lot less fear when I know what my ‘nut’ is (nut being my break even point) as opposed to sitting with the artist and not knowing if there was enough money (and being embarrassed - which happened a couple of times - and making up the balance out of my own pocket).

Presenting is a delicate balance between good artistry, organization, and fairness. In other words you want your attendees to get what they paid for, and you want the artist to know that a gig with you is worth their time, and you want peace of mind that you’ve done your best and learned a little on the way.

Good luck.

Re: What’s a reasonable price for a workshop from a world-renowned player?

When I host concerts and workshops with top tier talent (John Carty, Cillian Vallely, Tony DeMarco, etc), my standard price is $40(USD) for a 2-2.5 hour workshop, but I include a seat at the concert in that price (which would normally be $20). That way, I find that it’s an easy way to get make people feel like they’re getting their money’s worth, and a lot of people that wouldn’t come to just the concert end up coming to both. It always seems to work out well that way for the artist.

Mind you, I am doing house concerts and workshops at my house, so I don’t take any of the money. I’m always happy to trade the cost of some snacks and drinks for the joy of getting a chance to spend some time and play some tunes with the players.

“I’ve experienced some brilliant players who can’t teach or communicate…”

I’ve experienced that a few times over the years.

I actually have a tape of one workshop where a brilliant player struggles for several minutes trying to describe a “triplet” (for which, evidently, he has no word).

I attended a workshop many years ago by one of the world-renowned players mentioned above which was entirely worthless. He sat there staring into space. Finally someone broke the silence and asked him a well-worded question about a specific point of technique. The famous player seemed baffled. Then the questioner played a portion of a tune the famous player had recorded which contained the technique in question (it was quite familiar to me- I knew the recording, had transcribed the tune). The famous player was still baffled. Then ensued an absurd scene with the questioner trying to teach the tune to the famous player (who seemed to be unfamiliar with it, though he had recorded it and played it on tour). Needless to say the questioner never gained any insights.

Then ensued another period of uncomfortable silence. Finally the famous player said “well let’s play some tunes” and the workshop degenerated into a session. This would have been fine had the room been full of ITM players who shared a repertoire with the famous player, but alas, the room had mostly players of Scottish music, who then began playing their Scottish tunes, the famous player trying to figure out the tunes on the fly.

Another Famous Player workshop I attended was odd in a different way. The famous player never played a note, or had anybody else play a note. There was no music at all. Instead, the famous player delivered an hour-long lecture about how beginners go about learning the music the wrong way. (BTW I had no argument about anything he said! It was all bang-on.) Without hearing anyone play, without knowing anyone’s skill level, he told everybody to buy a metronome and go home and practice. The class was stunned. (I can only hope that some of them took his advise, I know I did!)

A very useful workshop was given by a guy who spoke no English. Well, he knew one word, “after”.

He would hold a finger (getting our full attention) then say “after” then play a phrase of a tune. We got the idea and would play the phrase he had demonstrated. He was very good, very efficient, and we learned much.
