Re: Tuition for Irish Mandolin

I’m tempted to ask why you would want to (speaking as a long-standing mandolin player myself). Especially if, as it seems, you have had access to other instruments which will give a much better sound in this music.

I am self-taught and have never had any tuition at all, but it seems to me that there are three ways to go:

1) the worst is to emulate mandolin styles used in bluegrass etc. which are really not at all suitable for Irish or Scottish music. Don’t whatever you do play snazzy, syncopated chords on it unless you want to sound like a hoe-down.

2) you can derive a certain amount from banjo technique - in particular, types of ornamentation are similar.

3) for me the best is to take as much as you can from the fiddle. Mandolins to easily sound twangy or staccato as it is, without accentuating it by pretending you’re a banjo. Much better to aim for smoother playing - and for this you’ll need an instrument of sufficiently good quality to get a good sustain from it. Cheap mandolins just plink and IMO sound far worse than cheap instruments of most other types.

My best advice would be to choose an instrument that is more sympathetic with the music to begin with. As you develop, you will find there are lots of things a mandolin doesn’t really let you do - and just to add insult to injury, you will be invisible at sessions!

Still, if that lot doesn’t dissuade you, feel free to get in touch off-list if I can be of any help.

Re: Tuition for Irish Mandolin

Yooval, the first requirement for tone is to listen to the instrument you want to imitate. O.K., that’s not a good word, but you know what I mean. I listened to people like Kevin Burke and Martin Hayes. I also listen to a lot of Scottish fiddle, people like Aly Bain, Jenna Reid and Duncan Chisholm. Not all stricly trad, but they have the feel. I tried to adapt fiddle l/h technique where possible.

I use a small fake-tortoiseshell plectrum, quite rigid, so that it doesn’t twang or click too much.

Yes, I use hammers-on and pull-offs and have found it possible to get the whole of a triplet roll/slur out from one strike. Sometimes I use banjo-style triplets - depends what feels right.

Another thing I have been working on is keeping fingers in place for as long as the tune will allow, so as to maintain sustain for as long as possible. Another fiddle technique (I think less used on banjo) is double or treble-stopping - but with a single strike, equivalent to a single note, not strummed/tremeloed.

I should have added that another ‘inspiration’ instrument for slow tunes is the harp - it is possible to get an almost clarsach-like quality with some effort, using double-stopping and then something between an arpeggio and a single strum.

As I said, I think a lot of this has been possible because I have a very good instrument with a clear, loud tone and a relatively good sustain.

Re: Tuition for Irish Mandolin

I don’t understand all these suggestions that the mandolin isn’t suitable for Irish music.

I’m just listening back to recordings of a session this weekend, which included four fiddles, two whistles, two boxes, bodhran and guitar, and the leader playing mandolin. The one instrument that shines thruogh above everything else is the mandolin.

One aspect that people sometimes fail to grasp is that the Irish mandolin is, to all intents and purposes, a completely different instrument to the American mandolin. In the various types of American music, the mandolin is used as a soprano guitar. In Irish music it is used as a plucked fiddle. That requires a different instrument, different plectrum, and different approach.

Choice of instrument is a very personal thing, but probably the majority of Irish players favour flat top mandolins - because they give a brighter sound which cuts through. Oldtimey and bluegrass players almost always use carved tops, which give a rounder, more guitar like sound.

In American music mandolinists tend to use thin, flexible guitar type plectrums - which make strumming chords easier. In Irish music they tend to use thick, rigid plectrums with a round end and bevelled edge (like the wegen) - not only are they louder and brighter, but they also make the high speed Irish triplets and ornamentation possible (flexible plectrums cause timing issues, because they don’t release the string until you’ve gone well past it).

So in all, if you try to bring American style mandolin playing into ITM, it doesn’t work that well. But if you adopt the Irish approach to the mandolin, it’s made for the job.

Re: Tuition for Irish Mandolin

skreech sez:

“In American music mandolinists tend to use thin, flexible guitar type plectrums - which make strumming chords easier. In Irish music they tend to use thick, rigid plectrums with a round end and bevelled edge (like the wegen) - not only are they louder and brighter, but they also make the high speed Irish triplets and ornamentation possible (flexible plectrums cause timing issues, because they don’t release the string until you’ve gone well past it).”

skreech,
I agree with you about the (non)use of flexible picks while playing ITM on the mandolin, but I don’t know any Bluegrass or OT musicians who play with such a pick. For the most part (I assume that there have to be some players who use thin picks) they are using 1 mm or thicker picks. My current favorite is a Blue Chip .60 inch pick (or 1.5 mm) which slips through the strings and imparts plenty of energy to the double course and this pick has a lot of adherents.

It is almost impossible to get a full tone out of any mandolin with a thin pick because of the energy requirements of double courses on a mandolin require a more energy to activate the string. G strings on a mandloin are in the .040 inch range and a thin pick will barely move them.

I think we can agree to disagree about which mandolin is best for ITM. I find that a variety of mandolins can sound great in the right hands. Mandolins are fairly demanding when it comes to producing a good tone and some are louder than others. But I have not heard the holy grail yet.

I am wondering why you think that American musicians use the mandolin as a “soprano guitar.” Virtually every name brand mandolin player I have interviewed says just the opposite, that the mandolin “is not a little guitar” (Andy Statman, Roland White, Peter O to name a few - their exact words, I might add) rather it is a different instrument that needs a different approach. I suspect we have different ideas about what a soprano guitar is.

Mike Keyes

Re: Tuition for Irish Mandolin

“Do elaborate! (seriously…)”

Mandolins are not little guitars and they are not little banjos. As Chris Thile points out in his video they require more precision and a good right hand to play well. My own opinion is that mandolins have very specific requirements as far as developing tone goes and that you have to have technique that is very specific to the mandolin in order to develop a style that works.

This means learning how to strike the double course evenly, finding the right angle and area of stroke, developing a feel for the fingerboard and the fifths tuning while learning to play the music you are interested in. A lot of the basic technique is the same across styles and there are some techniques peculiar to the mandolin (tremolo for example) that can be added to any style.

All of that being said, elite level players such as Ian and Luke seem to have the mandocentric capability that makes the music sing. Most of it is in the right hand (Andy Statman and Peter O both say it is 90% right hand) and you can hear the clean clear notes, triplets and extension of the sound of the instrument that each bring, even through the noise of the crowd.

Both have very nice instruments which they use to great effectiveness, but I think you would hear the same with a cheap PacRim mandolin in their hands. The sound would just not be as full due to the limitations of the instrument.

Mike Keyes
