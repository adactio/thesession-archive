Just-tempered ears vs equal-tempered ears

The recent, and adjacent “Just-tempered instruments in ITM?” prompted me to start this thread. I guess there probably has been one like this before (?)

Basically, I’m asking, if you play in just temperament (fiddle, flute etc) is your ear more sensitive to ‘bad’ intonation? I’m thinking of (eg) the 3rds (regardless of what instrument you hear them on) - if the higher note is sharp, does is bother you? Do you even notice?

It would be easy to say that the ear of a fiddle player is more sensitive to this kind of thing, than (eg a guitarist or an accordion player).

Nice thought - but is it true? What if the only music you’ve ever played on fiddle is Irish trad, and in a session, with a big mix of instruments (and maybe you play mandolin too)?

If you’re a guitarist, and a pretty good one, who tunes to perfection in 20 seconds - do fiddle notes sound out of tune in relation to your 2/3/4 note chords, even when those notes are played by a good fiddler?

I do a lot of recording at home (on fiddle, obviously), and from time to time I do struggle with ET instruments like piano when used for accompaniment.

Just asking for thoughts, not having a stab at any instrument or musician :)

At the end of the day hearing is largely a completely personal interpretation of a mechanical process. Those mechanics are a function of the vibration of bones which although they are similar in almost everyone are highly unlikely to be identical or perform exactly the same for us all. One person’s in tune is therefore at the extremes of everyday perception highly unlikely to be the same as anyone else’s. Tuning is always going to be a bit of a compromise as a result. [*Good. I was hoping it would turn out alright. I first came across the concept from a training video by, I think, Perlman. It makes complete sense … if you’re a violinist, that is.*]

@Ben, I think it applies to fiddling too :) I don’t know what Perlman actually said, but I assume it’s along the lines of what Prof Sassmanshaus said in the video “which system to use when” (as in your link above).

In a way, that over-complicates things (not everyone knows that Bach Partitia) - you could just as easily demonstrate how a single note needs to have variable pitch depending on its context.

Take the B on the 2nd string - play that with the G just below until you are perfectly in tune. Now play the adjacent 4th (that same B) + the open E. The B will now sound flat and the 4th will be out of tune, so you’d need to sharpen the B to get the 4th in tune. If those three notes had to be struck as a chord, the priority would be for the perfect 4th between the B and E, over the G+B 3rd.

Same with the Cnat + open E - the Cnat (the leading note) needs to be sharpened fractionally.

Similarly, with the C# + open E, that C# (the leading note) needs to be flattened slightly.

Many fiddler players (including me in my early days) had absolutely no knowledge of temperaments, or even thought about them - but they did work out how to make the adjustments so that everything sounded ‘in tune’.

So it’s not just violinists :)

Re: Just-tempered ears vs equal-tempered ears

Bottom line is, anybody with a good ear will play in tune in the context he’s playing in.

Anybody with a good ear can hear the beats which occur in many Equal Temperament intervals.

A professional pianist I know once said “sometimes I wish I could get in there and tune the strings” when he hits a big Major 3rd and those beats sound. (BTW that’s how piano tuners tune the piano, by counting the speed of the beats. For a Just Intonation piano, you would get rid of the beats.)

And any brass player or choir singer with a good ear will adjust their intervals as they go along to make in-tune intervals.

But a solo singer or player of any instrument with a good ear will play in tune with their accompaniment, and if that’s in ET (keyboard, guitar) then they’ll adjust their intervals to suit that.

Not that these people are necessarily aware that they’re doing this adjusting. They’re simply guided by what their ears hear.

So I don’t think it’s an either-or thing. Pretty much all orchestral players float between the Just and Tempered intervals as they go along, because playing in tune is situational.

The Pure Drop people who make a big deal about Just Intonation…. well, I don’t hear them playing their B’s 16 cents flat, usually. It’s interesting in this regard to hear Paddy Keenan playing very tightly and in tune in unison with an ET fretted instrument.

Because of the sorts of gigs I usually do, where I’m playing the uilleann pipes in an orchestral or other ‘legit’ settings, I have my chanter in strict ET. I can play up and down the scale and not move the needle. Yet I can turn on the drones and sound just fine, the chanter’s F# blending with the drones perfectly. It’s just pressure adjustment, I suppose.

I don’t know why somebody would make a statement like that without checking the facts.

Equal Temperament is fairly recent, and keyboards were always tuned to Just Intonation, until in the 18th century people began experimenting with a variety of “tempered” scales (which were various compromises between JI, which is the only thing that sounded in tune to their ears, and ET, which their ears rejected).

There’s a huge Just Intonation movement going on now, and many people are tuning their pianos JI, and orchestras are recording works in JI, etc.

Joel Thomas has summed up the primary disadvantage of keyboard tunings other than ET - that it makes playing in, and modulating to and from, different keys, more difficult and/or impossible. And classical music after about 1800 depends pretty heavily on those different keys and modulations. Add in the whole-tone and octatonic scales that turn up in jazz and 20th century classical and meantone is just not going to work. People go on and on about that sharp major third in ET but when the cost of not having it is 1) retuning the piano every time, 2) not being able to modulate or play in Db, or 3) using an electric keyboard instead of a hammers-and-strings piano, I’m sorry, but I think the cost-benefit analysis is tipped pretty far in favor of ET, which is why it’s what’s used these days. As noted by posters above:
Tuning issues arise for each instrument that has more than one inherent fixed pitch.
Tuning issues arise when playing with others; tuning is complicated by inherent fixed pitch(es) in any of the instruments.
Aware, sensible musicians will adjust to the context, for the most musical result.

When playing fiddle alone, harmony trumps melody: I adjust harmony notes to produce subtones (resultants), and adjust melody notes to fit such harmony (such as someone mentioned above).
When playing with others, I adjust pitches to match others’ instruments. This may sound ridiculous, until you play with wind instruments. Playing jazz, I wondered why, when I played D on the A string, it was very flat to clarinets and brass instruments. The reason is that, tuned perfectly with the A string, fiddle D string is a flat D according to the pitching of wind instruments as well as keyboards and button boxes. So I have learned to play sharp in context to match such instruments. I have heard from some fiddlers that they won’t play with wind instruments because of “the tuning problem”. I choose to adjust to fit.

I know a violinist in a US orchestra who says that trying to play in tune with the orchestral winds is annoying. Also that the orchestra tunes to A442 (the current pitch of many orchestras, and perhaps why percussion instruments are tuned at A442). And after tuning, the violins retune/raise their G string: “How much?” “We don’t know.” Interesting. Moreso because they don’t have a standard for the G, which with today’s tuners would be easy to ascertain.

Half a life ago, I was playing fiddle at Centre Pompidou in Paris, where I met and played with ‘Arab’ musicians (apologies: they did not say where they were from). The violin player played quarter-tones but the guitar player did not. No one mentioned it. They taught me the beginnings of two pop songs, one of which has ‘B moll quart’ = halfway between B and Bb. This note pulls at the heartstrings. It is an exact pitch, and is different than ‘blue notes’ or other such I have played.

It was interesting to learn in this thread that wet reed tuning covers a multitude of personal and group tuning sins. This in addition to having an emotional effect and added volume.

I tune organs and pianos, which always sound unbearably harsh after I have done a proper job. I much prefer slightly out of tune pianos, with the 3 strings sightly different pitches for each note, resulting in a warmer sound overall as the various ‘out’ strings sound ‘in’ for each tonal context.

My recommendation for instruments with variable pitch is: When playing alone, play the most in tune with your instrument; When playing with others, play the most in tune with them.
Playing a variable pitch instrument, tuning is not a mechanical given. Tuning is a feel, so always aim to feel the best.
