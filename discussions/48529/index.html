If they aren’t coming at all, do you remember my discussion about experiencing “loss of acquisition”? Looks like it’s from a year ago, so you may not remember it; but I talk about how when learning Japanese, there got to be so much information to process that I “stopped learning”. New material would not process. There was so much backed up information that my mind needed to process, that it would not receive new information.

In a tune learning context, I’ve noticed that when I’m learning tunes in bulk, I learn them all slower, confuse parts of similar tunes easier, and am even liable of forgetting parts altogether. Also, and this is a big one: Tunes that I learn in bulk do not make it into my recall. I have to work them into recall separately. (By recall I mean being able to play them on the spot with no assistance.). I also don’t learn their names when learning them in bulk, which makes it even more difficult for them to make it into recall. These are all signs of loss of acquisition.

This is all remedied by pacing my learning and taking my time with individual tunes. I do not recommend learning tunes in bulk, and it is no longer something I practice. (I would consider “bulk” anything more than a set’s worth of tunes, the standard set being 3 two-part doubles.).

If you’ve made the mistake of learning in bulk, develop what you have and move on when there’s space for more. Sorry if it’s a separate issue for you and none of this helps; but this is the closest to “loss of acquisition” that I’ve gotten for music.

Re: Mental block with Reels, confusion!

“I listened to before, and trying to work on training my ear, and finding out that I am able to get a decent bit of melody without needing to constantly use the dots like I did before.”

Aww man, you’re ear-training? I’m sorry, I didn’t realize. Well that right there explains it, in my opinion based on what you’ve told us. Tunes, especially reels, are more advanced when it comes to ear training. I mean, sure they have a high accessibility being melodies as opposed to chords; But that doesn’t mean they’re always simple to acquire. A melody that goes by too quickly, or a melody that isn’t clear(bad audio quality, mix of instruments interfering with clarity, etc.) can present a real challenge.

There are a number of techniques you can use to pick up on anything easier by ear:

1. Only learn tunes that you already know. The trick about learning by ear is that crystal clear mental picture. If you don’t get clarity from listening alone, physical practice into the ear training can make it clear for you.

2. Keep the workload light. If you’re ear training, learn tunes slowly. Training your recall, it is the essence of being an ear musician and trying to pick up too many tunes at once is going to cause loads of issues for your recall.

3. Mix learning techniques in your practice. When I’m learning by ear, I do a number of things in the moment of practice to sort the music out. I listen to the music, just the music. I meditate on the music without playing it to see how much of it I understand. I test my understanding of the music by playing along with the source material. I test my recall by playing the music without the source material.

Learning happens with the music and away from the music, as well as with the instrument and away from the instrument. Sometimes you have to draw back away from your instrument and really listen to the music. Sometimes you have to draw back away from the music and really feel out your instrument.

Oh, and definitely what Will said: If you’re ear-training, focus in one instrument at a time. Instruments present a dialect barrier that will also cause a number of problems if you’re trying to learn multiple tunes at a time. Learn your absolute favorite tunes on multiple instruments to expand your technical fluency if you want to, but definitely chill on learning multiple tunes at a time on multiple instruments.

Re: Mental block with Reels, confusion!

This is going to sound woo, or too obvious, or whatever but:

Stop telling yourself that you’ve got a block on reels! That declaration of “can’t” can infiltrate your brain deeply.

I am still less than a year into this journey, and quite similarly, when I started I was learning jigs at much quicker rate. Finally I got to a dozen jigs and I was still wiping out on my first reel. It didn’t help that it was NOT an easy one (Dinny O’Brien’s) and I had the feeling that “if I can’t learn this one then I don’t deserve to go on to another one”. Thankfully I got over that.

And once I got the first 2-3 under my fingers, they started coming easier. A lot of times I had to use mnemonic devices to help remember them (Kerry Reel sounded like a reel version of the Killavil Jig, for example). It’s helped me to find the tunes with a simple structure, so that when you learn the first line successfully you’ve got 75% of the tune. I think it also helps me at sessions, if there’s a reel I’m intrigued by, to see if I can quietly get the ending phrase under my fingers before the ‘hup’, and that helps a lot with retention for some reason.

Finally, even if you don’t consider yourself a singer at all, humming along is phenomenal for building recall. Then you can be sure the tune gets in your brain and not just in your fingers.

I wish you luck - that moment when you make progress on something that was previously an obstacle, it really feels like flying.

Re: Mental block with Reels, confusion!

Part of the issue may simply be that reels have 33% more notes than jigs, so depending on how you look at it, that means that there are more notes to learn and remember. I certainly don’t think of tunes by the number of notes, I think of them in phrases, and so I don’t find reels harder to learn. (And in fact, I find them easier to play on banjo, because they’re even numbers of notes, so you can pick them with a simple down up, down up pattern).

One way to maybe test this out would be to try learning some single reels. While you can think of a single reel as being a reel where you just play AB AB, instead of AABB AABB, in reality, most single reels have a structure where the parts are actually half as long and then repeated. And then they will often have one part (or sometimes more) where the part will have a variation in the second half, or at least a different resolve on the repeat. So in general, you can think of single reels as having about half the notes of a regular reel.

Here are a couple of single reels which I teach to my students pretty early in their journeys:

Rolling in the Rye Grass: https://thesession.org/tunes/87
Throw Away the Keys: https://thesession.org/tunes/1540
The Hare’s Paw: https://thesession.org/tunes/1462

Maybe give one of those a shot and see how it goes…

Re: Mental block with Reels, confusion!

OP, completely unclear what your problem is with reels. but do know that you do not must have to play reels. play jigs, polkas, waltzes, an-dros, tarantellas, and if asked “do you play reel X?”, deadpan answer “i do not play reels”, “my teacher told me to never play reels”, “reels are boring”, “my copy of o’neil’s is missing the reels pages”, etc. most important, give a different answer each time asked.

I think your situation is valid and reels really are harder to learn and play than jigs:

1) reels have more notes than jigs, 8 per bar reel vs 6 per bar jig, so played at the same “notes per second” velocity, jig at 120 bpm is same as reel at 90 bpm. If you can play jigs “quite fast”, it means you can play reels only “pretty slowly”.

2) “easy” reels have simple structure of 2 groups of 4 notes repeating. “difficult” reels have all kinds of subtle rhythms going on inside. that makes them more difficult to memorize and more difficult to play with good phrasing and groove. The groove itself may be 4+4 or 3+3+2 or something else.

3) many recording musicians fill reels with ornamentation that obscures the basic melody and structure. many recording musicians play reels at dance speed 120 bpm (or faster) where it is impossible to catch them by ear. sheet music or amazing slowdowner to the rescue.

You do not say which reels you are working on that give you a hard time. It could be helpful if you are more specific. Perhaps you accidentally chose the wrong tunes. Some reels are truely difficult on mandolin or banjo, Catharsis, Exploding Bow, Wizard Walk. Some reels “play themselves” on mandolin or banjo, Tam Lin, Banshee, Spootiskerry. For flutes and whistles this different yet again, i.e. Catharsis is trivial to play on an F whistle or flute.

@BB, 1st tune, reel, 84-80 bpm, sounds swell, 2nd tune, jig, 115 bpm, good, 3rd tune, reel on banjo, 80 bpm, good, 4th tune, jig on banjo, 115 bpm, good. All good, I hear nothing wrong with your playing. Melody is there, rhythm is there, groove is there.

to me, the next step with these tunes would be to record them at the same tempo, but with a metronome.

you may discover that the metronome is hard to hear, that it jumps around randomly and that sometimes you cannot hear the metronome at all.

this is as expected. my solution is to use an extra very loud metronome, or to run the metronome through headphones. (as side benefit, playing banjo while wearing headphones will save your hearing).

when recording on vocaroo, definitely run the metronome in the headphones to make it inaudible in the recording.

after you can play your reels against the metronome at 80 bpm (jigs 115 bpm), bump metronome up to 85 bpm, rinse, repeat.

you may discover that to play at higher bpm settings, your technique, your instrument, your pick, your posture, your everything may require severe improvement. again, this is normal, everybody goes through this. people like Maddie Wittler and Sharon Gilchrist teach this side on things. the usual “how to play mandolin” youtube videos do not.

There is a slight discrepancy in jigs as we have 12/8 and 6/8 jigs. it depends on the length of the phrase. they can be notated either way but the subtlety is lost
all jigs are what is called compound time.
so a 6/8 jig is compound 2/4
9/8 is compound 3/4 and
12/8 jigs are compound 4/4
what this means is there is emphasis on the first beat of the bar.
so 6/8
is strong weak 2/4
9/8 is strong weak weak 3/4
12/8 is strong medium weak medium. 4/4

This is meter, different to rhythm though they over lap.
thats why reels and hornpipes are the same meter but different rhythm or feel. and of course there are many different subtle rhythmical variations within each .
if you take a 4/4 reel and make every pair a triplet , then it could become a 12/8 jig! or Hornpipe…… depends on the feel and melodic and chordal structure
i just want to point out this is NOT about notation, its the other way round ,notation is about this……

Re: Mental block with Reels, confusion!

OP, there’s no mental block, you’re just mistaken in expecting to pick reels up as quickly as you pick up polkas, jigs or hornpipes. That is not unusual at all. Yeah, yeah, there will of course be some character popping up saying “It’s no problem for MEEEEEEEE.” Ignore that. It’s normal to need longer to take in a reel. Reels are often more “notey” as the culchies put it.

Listen to the reel you want to learn for at least a couple of weeks--in the car, at home, coming and going, etc. I’ve read advice input to the effect that you should be able to lilt the tune before learning to play it, but I’m not a singer or lilter. However, listen for a week or two to “get it in ya.” It’s like a tenderizer. Hopefully on a recording with other stuff you’d like to learn. Once you’ve got a sense of it, slow down unabashedly as needed while learning the notes, without shame.

In the meantime, you should be listening constantly regardless of whether you have a target reel in mind. Irish trad with generous helpings of reels should be your sonic wallpaper. Tap your foot and sway a bit with the swing of the tunes, with special attention to reels, and do that as much as possible when listening. Over time, the reels will come to you faster. But there is nothing to be surprised, embarrassed or worried about if it takes a while.

As an extra exercise---this is weird, but if you can stand it, spend time listening to single-note melody music in 4/4 time that’s not dotted like a hornpipe, that runs in 16th notes or “cut time” like reels, from the Baroque (Bach, Vivaldi, etc.) and Classical (Mozart/Haydn) eras. Piano, Harpsichord, violin. Irish traditional dance music shares some DNA with that stuff. You’ll swing, phrase, and articulate the Irish reels differently, but it can really help get the structure of reels into your neural apparatus.
