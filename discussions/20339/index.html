Foot in mouth disease?

I was at a session the other night and the regular guitarist let another fella, who seemed to a friend of his, play his guitar for a few sets. He seemed to be playing a lot louder than the other but with the whole session going and in a noisy pub, it sounded fine. Then I started up a set, the first tune most people knew, and the second one they didn’t. All of a sudden I was hit by a train of chords -- the guitarist kept up the volume even though it was just him and me playing and he was playing all these creative, interesting chords with a really driving rhythm. I play the tune in question, Dowd’s 9, quite bouncy and swingy and had the strange feeling of claustrophobia, with a guitar louder than I was pulling me into a different sort of rhythm. Suffice to say I kept tripping up over the beat as I just couldn’t find my feet with all this going on.

After the set, I said quietly to my fiddler friend, “Oh, man… the guitarist…”

She said, “I know… he’s really good.”

I went, “Huh. I’m not sure I liked how it was fitting with those tunes there.”

She then said that he plays with a fairly well known band. Oops.

I have no doubts he was a good guitarist but I guess I like my accompaniment subtle.

Mad cow disease ~

“a driving rhythm” ~ too often accompanists take this as their aim, crackin‘ the whip and the rest of us cattle needing their direction. As to who is the ’regular‘ or not, if it’s your own session and there’s a guest, well, you lay out the welcome and you do your best, but it is up to the visitor to get the measure of your session and find a fit. If your the visitor, whatever your instrument, you’re the guest and it would be beholden of you to get a feeling for the way things go, you’re the visitor, and it shouldnn’t be about proving yourself or showing everything you can do. The start of this thread says the guest guitarist immediately took one of those ’upper hands‘, volume, and kept with it. If you’re playing over the volume of the person you’re accompanying you’re not showing respect, you’re ot listening. The idea is to strike a balance, more so when there’s only two of you. There’s no respect in ’driving‘ things they way you want them to go, and worse if you are supposedly ’accompanying’ someone…

Getting the gist and humour of a session doesn’t take anything more than good ears and a little consideration. It might also be the case that chord diarrhoea, another illness not unknown or uncommon, just might not have been appropriate for the setting. This is another case of ‘less is more’. It wouldn’t be uncalled to ask the question why, as it is my experience that too much usually is a sign of a need to prove something and can also be a case or someone more interested in showing what they can do than the music itself…

Before someone translates this as me being anti-accompaniment, hardly. I love a good chord, and a good accompanist, as rare as that might be. Also, I’d rather a ‘beginner’ with a few chords and reasonable rhythm sense, than a flash jackass…

This is also speaking in generalities, as none of us were there and we don’t know the situation first hand… But it reads as if this guitarist, in this situation, took the upper hand. Even if they were the better musician, they were the accompanist, and they were supposedly accompanying this person, not whipping them into shape, as they saw fit. It doesn’t sound like they respected that, or gave due consideation to the person they were accompanying. This situation isn’t rare, and I’ve seen it and experienced it, including percussionists/bodhran bangers, pianists, etc… I don’t need or respect that kind of cattle prod approach to the music…
