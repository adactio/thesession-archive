Re: Need Suggestions on Button Arrangements for Diatonic Accordion

Hi ichiba. Two points to begin with. One, (as you probably realise) any system of diatonic accordion has severe limitations. Two, you can make great music on any system of diatonic accordion - but (see point one) you won’t be able to do everything you might want to do.

So perhaps the best approach is not to worry about what might be the best system in theory, but to get hold of whatever system takes your fancy and work hard on getting the most out of it. I would recommend you start with something relatively simple and light - two rows, two voices, eight basses - and get stuck in.

As for choosing a system: look at what players you admire are using and make a choice. When I took up the box years ago I faced exactly the same dilemma as you, i.e. wondering about C#/D, B/C or D/G (and even C#/D/G). I chose C#/D and have never regretted it, but B/C or D/G would have done fine, too.

C#/D/G is a 3-row instrument: extra size and weight. Weight is a very important consideration in my opinion. Also, in most configurations, your C# row will be longer than necessary, and your G row shorter than you might want. (So no, I wouldn’t agree with your thinking, but you have to make up your own mind.)

As far as basses are concerned, I would advise you to go for the system you want, learn the right hand, and turn your attention to basses as you get more proficient. And then, just do whatever the limitations of your chosen system will allow you to do.

C#/D generally has a wider selection of chords - but only for playing in keys with two sharps :-) You generally have no C chord, which is, well a huge missing elephant in the room. But you do have B and F# which (assuming you have a register to take out the thirds) you can use as minor chords - wonderful to have not just for Bm tunes, but for tunes in D and Em also.

B/C has a C chord, so is much better for accompanying tunes in G. But no Bm or F#m.

D/G basses: yes, more versatile in general. But with a D/G you will be generally confined to the same repertoire that you can play on a D tin whistle. Think about it. Whereas with C#/D or B/C you can play any tune in the Irish repertoire (and in fact any tune at all with sufficient application) and you will never be stuck for an accidental.

Good luck. But remember, there is no diatonic system that will allow you to do everything. But you CAN make great music with ANY system. All that is needed is for you to work hard at it. :-)

Re: Need Suggestions on Button Arrangements for Diatonic Accordion

I was in a similar situation last year. I’m a recorder and whistle player who was mad enough to buy a diatonic button accordion and to teach myself, for lack of teachers or fellow players in my area in northern Germany.

My advice in short:
- Get a B/C box.
- Use the modern bass layout (http://forum.melodeon.net/files/site/BC21modernbass.gif)
- Pay attention to wet/dry tuning and make sure it’s not too wet.

The most challenging aspect for me as a novice player is bellows movement, or more precisely, bellows/keyboard coordination. A scale is harder to play if it requires more bellows direction changes. D and G scales are the ones you need most in Irish music, and these require more bellows direction changes with a C#/D layout than with a B/C layout.

See also https://blog.mcneelamusic.com/which-accordion-key-is-better-for-traditional-irish-music-b-c-or-c-d/

Things I wish I had known before I bought my box:

- Many instruments have minor differences from “standard” keyboard layouts. Make sure to get a detailed keyboard map.
- Wet tuning can be annoying for a well-trained ear or when playing with other instruments.
- 23 or 21 buttons don’t really make a difference for B/C. I do have 23, but I never really use the two lowest buttons.
- D/G (or other fourth-apart) boxes tend to be more chromatic than you think. Some have missing accidentals on the lowest buttons, others have a helper row with extra buttons (2,5 row layout).

By the way, forum.melodeon.net is a good place to ask any questions about diatonic accordions.

Paul Young has lots of videos with Irish tunes played on D/G boxes (and some on B/C)
https://www.youtube.com/@paulyoungfolk/videos

With D/G, there are more options of playing chords on the descant side (right hand), and you get instruments with 12 or even 18 buttons on the bass side. But as usual, options tend to please experts but confuse novices.

I totally understand where you’re coming from, Harald. When I started out I despaired of being able to manage fast bellows changes satisfactorily.

But with the benefit of some crucial hints from a good player, things got better quickly. And now, with the benefit of some years of playing (C#/D, but with lots of experimentation with playing in different keys, and especially B/C fingerings), I would caution people against viewing bellows changes as something to be avoided. You can’t achieve a decent level of proficiency on any system, B/C included, until bellows changes are as natural as breathing in and out. They are the breath of life, or the soul, of the instrument. (If you don’t like bellows changes, get a piano accordion.)

And in the D scale that you mention, in particular, there is a trade-off for the fewer bellows movements on B/C. You have to master the challenge of much greater finger dexterity, and I’m not at all sure that this is ultimately easier than the bellows challenge.

Any diatonic box player has to deal with both these difficulties - and the sooner you tackle them, the better.

They each have their challenges, playing B/C style on the C#/D box you need to learn how you manage your air much better because you are fully extended or out of air much more easily. But like Steve mentioned it’s not a matter of if you need to master these skills, it’s a matter of when.

I’d say if you’re used to a piano or similar instruments then B/C is probably easier because finger movement and on-and-off-row will probably be a non-issue for you.

If you’re used to diatonic instruments like the harmonica and also the whistle then the rows translate really well to what happens on the button box with a C#/D. For a whistle your main row is basically a D row while the C# row is cross fingerings (accidentals).

I’d say the presence of a teacher in the area and what (s)he plays should probably be the main factor though..

Re: Need Suggestions on Button Arrangements for Diatonic Accordion

It very much depends what you want to do. Semitone-apart systems now seem to be favoured for playing Irish music, but the left-hand chords may not be suitable for the principal keys. There are modified bass systems which provide better chord options, but chords are used only sparingly in Irish music anyway. Which right hand semitone system is best is hotly debated. Look on melodeon.net for more information. If there are any players whose style you like then perhaps go for the system they use.

The D/G system is less popular among Irish players, but Irish music can be played on it. If being able to accompany yourself with full chords is important to you then this is probably a better choice, but you might not be able to imitate some of what you hear semitone players doing, and you may not be able to play easily in some keys. Playing in A, for example, you may not have G#, or have it only at one pitch, although A Dorian is available.

Contrary to what someone said earlier I would recommend trying to learn both sides together, certainly if you go for the D/G system. With this, how you play the right hand is often dictated by your choice of chord, which determines the bellows direction. Often people who have learned only the right hand then struggle to bring in the left hand later. This may not apply so much to semitone boxes.

They all have their pros and cons, and all are compromises. You are right to ask questions, but getting answers may not leave you any less confused. Eventually you are going to have to make a choice, and it’s better to get on with learning to play than never doing so because you can’t decide on which system. People make great music on all of them, so it probably matters less than you think.

Ichiba - regarding that modern bass setup, it seems really strange/weird to have major chords everywhere. I think some setups have a few major chords and a few minor. Either way, this is why it’s suggested that you “tape the thirds” or choose a box with a register/stop to remove them - see this example:
http://www.saltarelle.com/sites/default/files/styles/large/public/irish-bouebe-o.jpg

(By the way, “semitone systems” refers to systems where the rows are tuned one semitone apart - B/C, C#/D, and any other combination you can think of. “Modified bass systems” refer to any other setup than the common ones. Peter Browne once presented a modified system where each of the eight buttons had a single note, no chords. There were Eb, G# and all that, in appropriate places on the left hand side.)

Re: Need Suggestions on Button Arrangements for Diatonic Accordion

I think jeff_lindqvist has already answered your questions to me.

All the semitone-apart boxes are fully chromatic, but how easy they are to play in the keys you need for Irish music varies. I think B/C is most common, but C/C# and C#/D also have their supporters. There are several different layouts for the basses, including modern ones which are more suited to Irish music, but anyway the chords tend to be used sparingly. I don’t play this system so I can’t advise but you will find plenty on melodeon.net. You will also find examples of keyboard layouts for different systems and tunings.

D/G is not fully chromatic, and although with additional accidentals you may have the additional notes you may be missing the necessary chords to play in some keys. Also the accidentals a the top of the rows are not conveniently placed and can be difficult to reach - having them on a third or helper row can sometimes be helpful.

For most people wishing to play Irish music the semitone system, usually B/C, seems to be favourite, but they tend not to use the chords much (I don’t know whether this is a matter of style or difficulty of playing). However if you want to accompany yourself with a full chord accompaniment then the D/G would seem better, and the option to take the thirds out of the chords will help you to play in other keys.

Re: Need Suggestions on Button Arrangements for Diatonic Accordion

Just a few words about my B/C box:
Just in case it’s not immediately obvious, B/C players play the same tunes as anyone else, so, lots of tunes in D and G, and if you have a box with the commonest bass/chord layout, you will have Gs and Ds on both pull and push (the older system had 2 Cs and only one D).
You have the facility to use chords I IV and V in several major keys, e.g. C/F/G if playing in C, G/C/D for G, D/G/A for D, and A/D/E for A. But as my teacher told me “chords come much later - get to grips with the right hand first”, and he was right!
I play a lot of Scottish tunes, so being able to play in A, including G#s, is useful.
And if you ever accompany singers, they have a habit of choosing keys like F or Bb: it is possible to play in those keys too, albeit not with left hand chords!
