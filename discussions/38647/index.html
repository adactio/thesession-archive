A practice method for problem phrases.

One thing that I’ve noticed over the years of my music learning, is that it doesn’t matter how difficult a piece of music is, there are always phrases that give me trouble. Whether it’s learning by ear or sheet, a practiced instrument or a less practiced instrument, there are always problem phrases. The only exception seems to be for the simplest, easiest music.

First, I ignored them. I figured that if I learned a piece as a whole, and only 2 or 3 measures/phrases gave me trouble, then eventually I would have played the piece so much that those phrases wouldn’t give me trouble anymore. But of course, I couldn’t have been more wrong. This was back when I was a teenager and after piece after piece, it just didn’t hold up. In time I realized that if you only practice through a piece once a day, when you never even perfected it, you only have one chance to get the phrase right. If you get it wrong and don’t reinforce the right way, it defeats the purpose of the practice.

Then I learned about the whole “A minimum of 7 repetitions is required for even a chance at long term memory” bit. So I figured, “Hey, 10 repitions sounds like a good number”. But was it really enough? No. No, 10 was not enough. Is 10 really that much more than 7? Is 7 even a good number? No, it’s only the minimum required for long term memory, but this barely scratches the surface of long term perfection. After working with numbers like 7 and 10, they just weren’t enough.

Random numbers don’t work for me, because I’m much more comfortable following a system. So one day I’m thinking, why not have a little fun with it and come up with a mathematical equation for how many repitions I should practice in a phrase. This is what I came up with.

M ÷ P = R

M = number of measures
P = number of pages
R = number of repitions

For example, Chopins Etude Op. 10 No. 3 in E Maj has seventy-eight measures and is four pages long. So if I come across a problem phrase, I would practice it 20 times.

Now, it looks simple enough on the surface, but there’s something special about this number.

When learning a new piece of music, or working on a phrase, there’s something I notice. There’s an anxiety. A fear. An intuitive feeling that I’m going to make a mistake or that it will be harder than I expect. A feeling that I don’t know the music and should take care when learning it. And only when I reach perfection in knowing(actually knowing the notes I’m suppose to be playing, and can tell if I make a mistake), does this feeling completely dissipate. So the purpose of the number of repitions is to give yourself allowance to reach that perfection. To be able to be at peace with the music, even when learning it. Also, you witness first hand just how fast you learn and remember.

Sometimes the first time I learn a phrase it could take an entire minute. That may not sound like a lot, but remember, that phrase may be only 1-3 seconds in real time. Not to mention when learning new music, there could be several dozen more phrases to go. So that minute seems like forever and I start feeling like I should be better than this. But after that 1st, 2nd, and 3rd time, the music starts to open a little, then a lot. I always reach my “point of knowing” before the number of repitions I set, and I continue the repitions to that number for the sake a good measure.

Now, don’t misunderstand. When it comes to learning an entire piece of music, especially a difficult one, it could take dozens and dozens of tries before you learn it to a point that you can appreciate. I originally came up with the formula to learn entire pieces rather than problem phrases, but learned that it wasn’t necessary to do this with every phrase. This is about building confidence with problem phrases so that they are no longer a problem.

So if there’s a phrase or two in a piece of music that you’re learning that you just can’t get, stop and find the magic number. Use that number every time you practice it. Slow down, and be perfect. The speed will come. Don’t ignore the problem, stop and fix it. Don’t be a lazy bum like I use to be.

As for my experience with this new method, it’s shown me that it works with even the hardest music I know. Of course you don’t have to use my fun little equation. Just be reasonable with youself.

Since tunes are so short, I use a different equation for tunes under a page long.

M + N + 7
M = number of measures
N = number of notes in the phrase

Thanks for reading! Have fun playing!

Your comment about very much slowing down a challenging phrase is a very useful practice. But let me take that one step further. The trick is to get into the feeling of a phrase, more than just playing it. So as I’m practicing through a tune, I’m checking for the problem phrases. Phrases I don’t know as well as the others, or that for whatever reason are giving me trouble. Chances are if you’ve been working on a tune for a while, or it’s at a performable level even, you know what phrases will give you trouble. I will stop at that phrase, do my repitions, and then continue through the music.

For new music, this is just a lot of work, but it is a great method. But as I stated above, I don’t find it necessary for every single measure. Music being of proportion and pattern, often repeating itself and giving clues of what to expect, makes anything easier to learn as a whole. So now I only use it for those problem phrases. Also, keep in mind this is specifically for getting the notes right.

For the phrase:
1) Play it with different bowings, tempos and emphasis - this makes it interesting

2) Play it to hear how musical it can be - this makes it sweet

3) Play it ’til it comes naturally - this makes it fun

4) Play it until that little bit of tension in your neck goes away - this gets it ready for playing out.

Re: A practice method for problem phrases.

Be careful how you use the word “phrase.” Its meaning is very specific: A phrase consists of an understandable segment of a piece. This can be best illustrated with a song lyric:

Oh, say can you see,
By the dawn’s early light,
What so proudly we hailed,
At the twilight’s last gleaming?

To learn those phrases, one would never break it up as:

Oh, say can you,
see by the dawn’s early light

To do so would be to learn in fragments rather than phrases. I suspect one would only break up a tune that way if there were a technically difficult fragment of the tune that required special attention and repetition to master. However, learning a tune in phrases is, IMO, the best way to be able to master difficult portions. Doing so in fragments runs counter to how we think our way through a tune. We remember tunes in phrases, so when a person is interrupted before the end of a phrase, the impulse is to complete it, whether or not there is any actual reason to do so. It’s simply how we think.

Re: A practice method for problem phrases.

Jerome, I certainly recognize you as a skilled, talented musician so I know I’m preaching to the choir here. I think you’ve got a handle on the learning process. Some have mentioned their thoughts, here’s mine.

Point One as to speed. It is most useful to slow down and learn a difficult phrase, the “groove” it lays in, and it’s relationship to the rest of the tune. Still, if you only practice slow, slow is the only way you’ll play. Speed doesn’t magically come all by itself. Maybe the best thing to do is practice in increasingly longer segments and increasingly faster speeds. Small increments please until you’ve worked up to the place you want to be.

Also, we know two things…if we’re not focused on what we’re learning, well, we’re not learning, and our brains can only focus on one thing for a very short time…about 90 seconds. Isolate the difficult part, identify the thing that makes it difficult, focus practice on that part (without forgetting it’s place in the whole tune), then move on. Personally I use a 2 minute timer. Come back often until you get it right and I’ll bet you get it faster than if you just beat yourself up. I often find it useful to learn a tune on more than one instrument, especially if the problem isn’t technical. When it is technical (on one instrument or another), it helps me to work out the technique in multiple keys and positions. I “get it” sooner and the same difficulty will show up again somewhere.

Re: A practice method for problem phrases.

Well this turned out to be more active than I expected. I guess then it wouldn’t be a bad idea to explain how I came up with this, to put it more into context. Since we’ve gotten on the subjects of playing at speed and rote repetition.

The number was designed to “be enough”, but also not be “too much”. A reasonable goal you could reach without worrying about getting bored or distracted, while still getting the job done. This is why I chose the number of measures divided by the number of page; the number of measures representing how many phrases you’ll have to deal with and the number of pages representing the duration of the music. With those in mind, the number represents getting the job done right, keeping yourself interested, and moving forward.

The most beautiful thing about this experience for me, is realizing that doing an exercise like this only takes about 5 minutes. That’s why it’s part of my practice session and not it’s own session. A 5 minutes that is well worth the end result.

Re: A practice method for problem phrases.

I read Make It Stick on the strength of that post. It’s primary message is that retrieval is one of the key processes for building long-term memory. For academic learning that means testing yourself rather then reading and re-reading the source material. I suppose for musical learning the equivalent would be learning to play the phrase from memory rather than sight-reading it.

Another key process is consolidation, by which short-term memories get stored or reinforced as long-term memories. This mostly happens whilst you sleep. For me this suggests that five repetitions played every day are better than 20 repetitions played every four days.

Other factors that it cites that promote learning are the variety of what you practice and how you practice and adversity, meaning that you actuallly learn more when it is hard-going than when you are breezing through it.

Sorry, Na Eisc for not replying here, I intended to PM you. These problem phrases were isolated incidents, meaning even after I learned the rest of the music, they would still give me trouble.

These phrases tend to have a sequence that deviates away from the flow and pattern of the music, making them harder to remember. Looking deeper, if there is a pattern throughout the music, these phrases within that pattern are easier to learn and require less energy to remember, as they naturally get the attention they need(no one skips playing through a passage because it’s repeated, they simply play through it as it’s repeated). Whereas these problem phrases only appear once. So naturally overtime they get less attention. By the time the rest of the music is learned, I’m left over with something I missed, overlooked or neglected. Now they need to be isolated and focused on to get up to par with the rest of the music.

This also may be due to my learning style and methods. Because I play by ear and sight, I feel I have very complicated and “sophisticated”(for lack of a better word) ways of memorizing and learning music. But that doesn’t mean they’re the best. Though I like the way I learn, there are many imperfections that cause things like “neglecting an isolated phrase” to happen.

The downside, is sometimes these phrases are hard to notice in the beginning because sometimes their differences and deviations are very subtle, which is why I don’t really notice them until much of the music is already learned.

As for how well this technique is working for me, I’ve been working with it for several weeks with all of the hardest music I know and so far I’m liking my progress. Looks like I’m gonna finally complete that Chopin Etude after all!

Etude Op. 10 No. 3 has a 6 measure passage on the third page that’s extremely difficult to sight read and even harder to learn by ear. It is officially the most intimidating passage of music I’ve ever tried to learn. And I never perfected it. But now I’m breezing through it. Not only are problem phrases ever as long as a 6 measure passage, but none of the other ones I’ve dealt with have given me such a hard time. If this method works for this personally infamous passage, I can use it for anything.
