Tune Title Police

Don’t you just hate it when you’ve grown up with the name of a tune you can play only to find out it’s called something else. There must be hundreds of tunes out there that go under different names but it’s a real pain in the backside when we don’t know who’s right. So i am thinking that there should be a sort of Tune name bible with all the tunes going into this so called “Book” and every one has to abide by these names. If anyone is caught using other names for tunes then they should be made to learn the Guitar for a week. You could even have undercover “Tune Police” mingling in on sessions to catch people out and if they still persist they could have their instruments towed away and crushed.

Re: Tune Title Police

Here’s my theory: The names of tunes are just embellishments, ornaments (unlike the more vital and essential articulations such as rolls, triplets, cuts, etc., too often mistakenly called ornaments). As such, they add a little to a tune’s charm, and it’s more interesting to have a range of variations, once you know them.

So I think it’s fun that we have at least three tunes called Toss the Feathers, two reels known as the Ashplant (one of which also goes by A Night in Ennis), and that some tunes have sprouted dozens of names.

Even new tunes become instantly susceptible to this embellishment--see: https://thesession.org/tunes/7159

If you grow up learning tunes and their names from people immersed in the tradition, and it later turns out that those names are not widely associated with those tunes, it doesn’t mean that “your” names are any less valid. It just means that the same tunes are known by different names by different players within the tradition. The longer you play this music, the less this matters. And you end up either knowing many names for each tune, or not thinking about tune names much at all. Whatever.

Bear in mind too that certain names may rise to prominence based on popular players and recordings, but these are as open to error as any--witness the Roaring Barmaid/Butlers of Glen Avenue debacle.

Re: Tune Title Police

Normally it makes little difference so long as a tune has some name (there’s nothing much worse than Gan Ainm!). Examples are the Hunter’s House which most people know as Reavy’s or The Tinker’s Daughter which is more often called Vincent Broderick’s. In both these cases the name is either the one intended by the composer or the composer’s own name. However, this may not always be the case and a wrong name may distance the tune from its composer which to me would be a bad thing - see https://thesession.org/tunes/2698 for a good example of this with Maurice Lennon’s Road to Garrison reel.

Re: Tune Title Police

The tune titles are the icing on the cake. They are a poetic insight into the lives of the people who created this music. They celebrate the humour and pathos of life for people who must mostly have been illiterate and poor. People who cannot read and write have to have incredible memories and wouldn’t have had any problems remembering all these witty titles. Why stop with one title per tune? They’re such fun, so you can have a jig called Short Grass, Put on Your Clothes, The Kinnegad Slashers, and Hitler’s Downfall and it’s all the same basic tune. You can quickly change the title of a tune if you’re a composer (especially if you’re a Scottish one) to toady up to a different bigwig. No, the titles don’t prompt the tune if you’ve forgotten how it starts, but they are part of the fun.

Re: Tune Title Police

Andyras, I do think that a lot of people, myself included, attach a tune to its title as a sort of mnemonic device. I do that very subconsciously, and it can come in handy. Because in the middle of a set, someone will say “can you get into Hare’s Paw”, or something, and invariably I can.

However, I found that I was relying on that way too much in the first several years of my playing, and I would have difficulty playing a tune if I couldn’t remember the name. I think that was mostly because I would be exerting too much brain power to try to figure out what the tune was, instead of just playing the tune.

It took a couple of years, but I was able to rid myself of that issue, so now I just let it go, and play the tune. Generally, the tune name will slide into my consciousness at some point during the tune anyway, but there are still times where we will finish a set and I’ll have to ask what it was that we just played.

I haven’t yet gotten it to the point where I can let it slide and *never* know a name for something that I play, though!

But this still doesn’t mean that I don’t enjoy the fact that tunes are known by different names. It is kind of like a game to collect tune titles for a given tune, and it gives you something to talk about when meeting new players. “What do you know that tune as? Oh, I know it as ‘the new policeman’. So where did you get your setting?” I find that all part of the appeal of this music!

Re: Tune Title Police

So I think it’s been established that knowing the titles for tunes isn’t essential for playing them. We also can see that tune titles serve a particular function for the dialectics and tune lineage. They also serve as a handy way to catalog the tunes either on paper or in one’s own memory, and they function as a way to hold on to the tune so it will be less likely to slip into obscurity.

But the thing that I still enjoy most about tune titles is how entertaining so many of them can be.

The Cow That Ate the Blanket
Good Morning to your Nightcap
I Buried My Wife and Danced on Top of Her
Will You Come Home With Me?
Tell Her I Am

Etc.

And then there are many that evoke images.

The Glens of Atherlow
Cottage in the Grove

Etc.

Others seem to have a story.

Etc.
