Honest encouragement

This can be as serious or light hearted as you wish.

Basically, based on your own experiences, what do you feel is the best way to encourage someone to continue playing and/or learning “the music”? Also, how do you feel about some of the advice, encouragement you’ve received over the years?

A few years ago, a well known and respected mandolin player asked me if I was still playing music. I responded with typical coyness to the effect that I still enjoyed playing but, of course, I would never be as good as he was.
He immediately responded “Don’t worry about that. Nobody is as good as me…” :-P

I’m sure it was mostly said in jest as he’s not naturally big headed(Confident, yes) but I thought that’s actually the best comment you can get. Far better than all this patronising nonsense such as “I’m sure you’re much better than you think” or “Stick in, you’ll get there…”.
However, this was still the actual message which I took away. I thought that was rather nice.
:-)

THE TWO ORIGINS OF MUSIC

1) Playing music as self-expression, as creativity, as spontaneous living of LIFE, of communicating with others using the language of music: find the easiest and most immediate ways to make the sound. Learn about music from that starting point, and with that process in control. That should start with something like whistling just using your mouth, humming, and instruments that allow the fastest and easiest expression in your own case. Creativity in the instant is important. Don’t take it “seriously”, and while your skill at it can be developed by regular usage, it can’t be taught, it’s already there, you have to find the natural way for it to flow out of you along with the experiences of the moment, like breathing is natural but can be consciously controlled, like so can walking, running, thinking and using emotions, they are in part richly embedded swirls of energy and can also be consciously accessed and expressed. So you have to learn to R E L A X in every way, take off the “student” or “big talent” masks and personas and like the birds, BE the music, BE the pathway it flows on. If you don’t find a way to discover, get immersed in and develop this aspect of music, you’ll be stuck with the second Origin of music, which may eventually resonate with the first Origin, but the second always goes far better if you have regular access to the first one too. Someone please show me where all the great music is that was created by the hordes of people with “Music” degrees, who got their degree and did nothing else with it but teach rudimentary piano or dropped music altogether after they moved away from their parents, relative to career or personal activities. University, music theory books, radio music formatting, your parents, the 15-yr old moron with attitude down the street who takes instrument lessons and is a conceited and ice-cold fool, are not where ORIGIN #1 comes from.

2) Playing someone else’s music. There are both snakes and ladders here. You found you like an instrument or to sing or some song, artist or music style, and you want to play it yourself, so you sing or select one or a few instruments to learn and you have a go. Music instrument stores are ALWAYS populated with people of various musical intentions. Some find that music is a natural language for them and they seek to enjoy what that is all about. Some are spurred by hearing some artist or instrument played in some way, and want to try making that sound, because it stirred them. Some of them get amazed by some artist playing very well, and suddenly see themselves AS that artist and risk attempting to step into a HoloDeck on a spaceship, to make it so. A lot of electric guitars are sold that way. Some are pushed by their parents or some subculture to “learn proper music properly” and are driven like cattle to a corral where classical style training commences, the student has little natural concern for that type of ancient, cold, divisive, left-brain instruction, and instead of expressive music being created, or a musician being created, the student is feeling obedient to external forces they know nothing about, alienated from themselves, falsely arrogant perhaps if they think they are getting “accredited training in social status music”, they might lust over various composers musical approach, and they think of music as a sort of LEGO RUBIK’S CUBE MATHEMATICS that can be calculated and repeated like an equation, using standardized theories of construction, blueprints anyone can read, consistently repetitive toolkits and the result will be perfect duplication that gets you brownie points from people who scare you and that you can’t shake free of. So without the inner ORIGIN driving the process, people become a real mix of intentions and their prospects for persisting with music follow along that cause and effect.

3) ITM. What ITM is to you, and how you approach being a musician playing it, is going to be largely affected by how you consider yourself and function as a musician, and how you are letting your own experience of music originate. Are you all or mostly the first type, or the second type, or some blend of the two?

Re: Honest encouragement

I started fiddling (with zero musical background) at 42 right at the start of Covid. At my first session in Boston at the IHC in Canton. I was recovering from a distraction (guitar) and injury (guitar) so was really just getting back in. I was encouraged to start a tune and so did the only one I could play well (Britches Full of Stiches). Someone else continued it with Kerry Polka (which I also knew). It was a huge rush just to be around the music for the first time.

An older fella came up to me when it was all done, said he was glad I was there. Told me to keep at it. It’s a tough instrument and hard to learn as an adult but if I just keep at it, it’ll all come together.

At my third of fourth session (also recovering from an injury so a bit rusty still) a different older fella came up to me and also said to keep at it.

Honestly, just that encouragement meant so much to me during my musical lows, or when I get frustrated at weaknesses in my playing. The reality is one of the most important things is to keep at it, and the great musicians that I’ve played with all expressed a deep desire for me to do that.

Really means a lot to even share this. So for all you fellow beginners (especially on fiddle where the beginner phase is like 10 years), keep at it! :-)

Really nice to hear Vecheys comment (and more evidence that you can learn fiddle as an adult, which is a future goal of mine!)

My only comment here is that “encouragement” is really hard to do well!

I’ve “only” been playing for 5 ish years and remember as a beginner it being and odd combination of taking any thing negative to heart
(I stoped playing the flute when after I played a tune someone randomly ONCE said she struggled with my B being so flat, as, whilst doubtless an offhand comment, it really stuck with me as a beginner and I remember it 5 years later)

And people being so supportive that you never actually got any tips on where you were going wrong/how to improve (as you just don’t know as a beginner).
I remember this being a big issue as people wanted to be friendly but just wouldn’t tell me how I was doing when I asked = had no idea if I was doing well or not.

If I had to make any “rules” I’d suggest:
- Don’t give negative feedback unless asked. Assuming the beginner isn’t ruining the session, they’ll eventually learn most things just by keeping on attending sessions/playing.

- Phrase comments as “thoughts/suggestions/advice” rather than things they’re doing wrong. Eg not “you should work on your rhythm/your rhythm is not in time” but “I find nailing the rhythm gives extra drive, try playing with a metronome/against a YouTube video etc”

Re: Honest encouragement

Around 20 years ago, I started a discussion thread here about this topic (https://thesession.org/discussions/7161). I had been playing the music for about 5 years, and only about 2 years into playing banjo, and I felt that a lot of the encouragement that I was getting was somewhat hollow. If you’re telling people how well they’re doing, and they can see how bad they are compared to the person giving them the compliment, it seems patronizing.

I teach regularly, and I try to be honest and encouraging when I’m teaching, but I try to keep teaching and sessions separate. The most encouraging thing for people is often just including them in the session. If you encourage (but don’t pressure) a new player to start a set or two, even if they don’t feel “ready”, it makes them feel like part of the group. If they feel welcome and like you are glad that they are there, it speaks volumes! Sessions are about more than music, and if a player feels welcome and part of the social aspects of the session, then they are more likely to come back. In my experience, it doesn’t take that long for that kind of inclusion to become addictive.

I try not to be overly complimentary to new players, because of the fact that it can feel patronizing. But I will at least acknowledge them if they did well. For instance, one of my newer students was recently at one of my sessions, and I played a set of a few tunes that I had taught him, but up to session speed. He played the whole set, and I could hear him, which it impressed me. I didn’t tell him how much he was improving or say he sounded great, but I did give him a nod and said “nice job keeping up at that tempo!”, and left it at that…

Yes - directly commenting upon or “reviewing” somebody’s playing is just awkward, because who am I? Not some Trad guru, to be sure!

The only way I know how to encourage is musical…like, if you only know one jig, I will happily play your one jig with you at any speed you like…And include you in chitchat, share the tune names with you…I think that’s how most beginners are received, or at least I hope so.

I try to remember what tunes beginner players asked for, and start them…
Because better players have done that for me, and that feels like being recognized/accepted, it feels great!
Communicating musically in this way feels better than verbal encouragement to me.
