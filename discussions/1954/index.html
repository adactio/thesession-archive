Interesting subject. Wow, you should hear just how loaded that statement sounded in my own mind… ;-) (My last two partners were musicians and that about covers the last decade).

I think that fully responsible people never give themselves entirely over to the obsession and the hedonism which are both part of the package when you allow music to rule your life. They also think about things like having a proper job, paying the mortgage, and so on. In order to set out in life as a professional musician, you have to be incautious enough not to care about the lack of stability or money. You also have not to mind the constant travelling, lack of healthy food or adequate sleep. You don’t care about those things, because nothing is important compared to the music. You probably also have a novelty-seeking personality, so you really don’t mind the travel and the constant change of scene and meeting all those hundreds of new people all the time.

So by default, if you become a musician full-time, you’re likely to be an irresponsible hedonist. And I think the same theory extends, in a lesser degree, to non-professionals who centre their lives around music. The more that music matters to you, the less other things matter to you.

And to turn it around; if you don’t have that burning need for music, and the compulsion to go out and do it even when good sense might dictate otherwise, and to do it alot, at the expense of other things - then you will never be as good a musician as someone who does. Which seems only fair, given how much people sacrifice when they devote their lives to music!

I never became a professional musician, although I hovered on the boundary for a long time. Essentially I just don’t love performing, in the way that you should if you’re going to spend your life doing it. All the same, I did let music sidetrack me so consistently that I’ve only had a proper job for about 3 years now, and I’m.. ahem… well into my thirties.

Oh, and the stuff about drink and debauchery - that’s a whole nother question about musicians, which is also an interesting subject. But I really must go home now, I’ll get into that one later…

A tricky question.

In my opinion, your timing, intonation and all of that are their peak if you’re stone cold sober. But I just can’t do it. Well, sometimes I can, but it’s hard and it hurts.

On the other hand, once you’re one Ol’ Bush over the line, you play like crap. I know I do. The only difference is you’re too drunk to know it, at least until you start falling down. Good music transports you to another place, and a whiskey or some beer can help move you off toward that ecstatic state. But unlike sex and pizza, more isn’t necessarily better when it comes to mind-altering elixers.

Re: Wild Oats

’Bout the drink… I don’t think it’s so much that musicians are necessarily heavy drinkers to start out with, although I’ve already extended the theory that they are, by nature, somewhat irresponsible and hedonistic, and so probably vulnerable to it. And by becoming professional musicians, they put themselves in a situation where they are constantly surrounded by drink. Not only are they spending all those days and nights in bars, clubs, festivals and so on - but if they get remotely successful, then people constantly want to buy them drinks and party with them. Being a party animal starts to be obligatory rather than optional, unless you really have a tremendous amount of self-control and are willing to offend people by leaving promptly after the gig, when they want to be your new best friend and party with you till 4am. Also, unless you’re staying in hotels, you may be at the mercy of your hosts and there’s quite likely a big party on at their house the night you’re staying there. And if you do stay at a hotel, you’re quite likely sharing a room with bandmates, and so you’re at *their* mercy. And so on.

Plus some musicians drink to dull their nerves before they perform; or feel too ‘flat’ to put on a good show sober. So that’s further enticement to drink, and to start early. And there’s all that hanging around to do, before, during and after the soundcheck. Oh, and free beer at sessions.

And added to those factors is the romanticising of the wild, creative drunken genius, as Andee was saying. A lot of people absorb aspects of that into their self-image and the world at large tends to encourage it, especially when it comes to Irishmen. I think some people feel almost duty-bound to act a bit that way - the drink is such a way of life and so intrinsic to the music, that to be a sober musician and a sober Irishman is almost bad form. People talk about the exploits of Shane McGowan, or Paddy Keenan in his wild days, or certain others, with a combination of amusement, respect and awe…

Personally, I find it quite grisly to see talented young players heading off down this road - in their early twenties and already with a big drink problem or heading right towards one. It’s a great big trap that people have to either get around, or go through and come out the other side of. And some people don’t come out the other side, which is tragic. I’d like to see what Brendan Behan could have done with another couple of decades - never mind all that Live Fast Die Young crap…

Hope I’m not offending anyone here, (hey, I’ve already called half of you irresponsible hedonists) but those are my observations. You can probably tell I’m not mad on the whole heavy-drinking, macho-pisshead-mayhem scenario; even though I do like the Pogues, in spite of myself… and Motorhead, and the Sex Pistols as well, come to that - yes, I’m one mixed up kid…
