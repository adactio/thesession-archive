My First Session

I sat in on my first session the other night. I had a blast!!!, and I am still all pumped up! I was very nervous about sitting in, I did not think I knew enough tunes well enough to play with experienced players. The group was very nice and most welcoming. I played along with many more tunes than I thought I would, and I was pleasantly surprised that I could play them at the correct speed. I usually play at home alone, and as you can imagine, I have developed some bad playing habits. However, I was not that bad. I kept up with the pace, and made fewer mistakes than I thought I would. To be honest with you, I have to thank the Session for that. The tunes I played were all practiced here on The Session, and I was able to muster the courage to join in by reading the encouraging words in the discussion topics. I was prepared to “fit right in” by reading some of the do’s and don’t’s listed in a discussion of session ettiquette. So I just wanted to thank everyone (and especially Jeremy for his invaluable work on The Session) who had a hand in my successful debut…THANK YOU.

Re: My First Session

Chalk up another one for the powers of Irish Trad music…sessions 15,637, stuffy nose-in-the-air musical snobbery 0!

Tom, in my experience, one of the beauties of a good session is that a few mistakes here and there are usually well covered up by the rest of the group. In fact, even the best players falter or lose the thread of a tune now and then, and a session is as good a place as any to have it happen. With enough musicians in the circle, someone else is bound to keep the flow going. Frankly, it’s nice to play without the pressure of a performance’s requirement that every note be spot on.

In our local session, the other main fiddler and I often joke about our relay race style of playing, where when one of us drops the tune, the other keeps it going. We’ve had this devolve into each of us just playing snippets of a tune, but alternating “perfectly”--she plays two bars and fumbles while I play the next two bars, and then I fumble but she’s right back in there, and so on. It ends up sounding planned, like the “call and answer” technique common to jazz and blues, but we’ve never done it intentionally.

As Barry Foy writes in his Field Guide to the Irish Music Session, “The session is where the music lives and breathes, where it does its homework, where it flexes its muscles and idly picks its nose.” A good session also allows us to stretch beyond our abilities once in a while, to test the possibilities, and to learn from “mistakes” (I prefer to think of them as experiments).

Thanks too for your comments on how helpful The Session is. I think it’s important for ALL of us to be periodically reminded of how our contributions to this site influence the people who visit here. The wide-ranging discussions, friendly and enthusiastic tone, and the wealth of playable tunes all add up to a powerful tool for helping people JOIN IN. Irish music is a participatory sport. Every person I’ve ever seen come to more than two sessions eventually gets dragged into it…if they play an instrument, they learn the tunes. If they don’t play an instrument, they soon learn (even if it’s bodhran :-) ), or they end up singing, telling stories and jokes, etc.

Bottom line: have fun.

Re: My First Session

I agree with Zina that it’s better to bring a little humility to a session than to announce yourself with over-confident playing. Reminds me of the old therapist joke--“The good news, Mr. Smith, is that you have a healthy ego. The bad news is that there’s no basis in reality for it.”

In my mind, sessions are better if they’re NOT about performing or showing off or impressing anyone. If you’re just there to play some tunes and enjoy a few pints among friends, it’s much easier to relax about your playing and enjoy everyone else’s contribution however humble that may be.

This isn’t always as easy as it should be, however. Whenever the “audience” outnumbers the musicians, it’s easy to slip into performing mode, to rely on pre-arranged sets and play only the tunes you can really nail. Or if a “famous” player shows up, everyone tends to tighten up, worrying about not making mistakes instead of enjoying the tunes. Or if just one person starts showing off--flaunting technical prowess instead of fitting in--watch the contest begin!

I run into these problems a lot in my own playing because I’m the de facto leader of the local session, but I am not the “best” player in the circle. If you’re expecting a virtuosic display of fiddle pyrotechnics, you’ve got Leahy on the brain. I think of myself more as “solidly inconsistent,” capable of a workman-like trad sound that will get people’s feet tapping, but that’s as far as it goes (and that only about 70 percent of the time on a good night). I worry that it’s too easy to lose myself in enjoying my own playing, when everyone else in the room is wondering why I don’t sit out a few sets. So sometimes I do just put the fiddle down and nurse my beer. It’s always gratifying to be asked to join back in. I’d rather hear that than a request (or plea or demand) that I stop.

After last week’s session, a recent addition apologized for starting several tunes that he couldn’t finish. I told him not to worry--if we all hesitated because we might falter half way through, no one would ever start a tune at all. And a guy on the small pipes winced over losing the melody in a new reel he’s working on. Someone else told him they liked the “variation”--it was all in tune to his drones and on the beat anyway. To me, it’s all part of the ebb and flow of a good night’s musical conversation.

Even here in Montana, where the only Irish trad music most people ever hear is on perfect cds or once a year when Riverdance is featured on PBS during fundraising week, even here people are learning to appreciate live, unpolished, amateur music from the heart. I’m still learning to trust that myself--that a listener won’t begrudge my bow noise (that wasn’t mastered out in the studio), my occasional forays into unintended sharps and flats (that will stay with me till I can dedicate 20 hours a week to practicing like the pros do), or my sometimes lackluster tone (because I refuse to spend $15,000 on a good fiddle). The people who keep coming back week after week appear to be enjoying it almost as much as I do. Either that, or they’ve ganged up to pull off one of the most elaborate and prolonged practical jokes I’ve ever been the brunt of…… ;-)

Will

Re: My First Session

This is a great thread (and I’ll barge in, hoping not to ruin it), but no one’s yet answered TC’s question. So here goes.

I can only speak for myself, but as a lead melody player on fiddle, I really enjoy sitting next to a bodhran player--as long as the beat is steady. Not much else matters. Yes, it’s fun when a tremendous player pulls out all the stops and the drum becomes another lead instrument, weaving around the melody rhythm as much as any other instrument in the circle. But this is far from necessary for a drummer (or guitar player, etc.) to be a welcome asset to the music.

Maybe I don’t get out enough, but I’ve never been “bored” by simple or even “plain” accompaniment. On the other hand, I have struggled to stay with a tune when the accompaniment gets too flamboyant. I’m not sure where the line gets drawn, and I know it’s subjective, so when in doubt, just ask the people you’re playing with. Ask them what they like. Ask them for open-ended feedback on your playing. Ask them to offer suggestions if something occurs to them (some melody players are surprisingly good at suggesting interesting chords or rhythmic variations). In a word, communicate. It doesn’t all have to happen through the music. Some players prefer to sit away from accompaniment, others don’t mind, and some actually like being next to a drum or guitar. I’m the latter, and I’ve extended an open invite to the bodhran players in our group to sit next to me whenever they have the chance.

As for overwhelming lead players by banging in their ears, I’ve only had this happen when someone holds their drum at ear level, or stands up to play while everyone else is sitting down. That has almost always been annoying, but it’s easy to avoid. Don’t stand up unless everyone else is. Don’t hold the drum at head level. Learn to play with your hand or a brush for songs or quieter tunes.

Kerri, I’d suggest that your first experience playing in your new-found session offered a (relatively gentle) lesson in what can happen when you go in with guns ablazing. Even if the group doesn’t mind, it can set you up for a bad case of the nerves. Why not get to know a few folks first--by listening to them play for half the night, or by chatting them up. This usually helps everyone feel more comfortable.

Also, in most cirlces you’ll be welcome (assuming you can play), but some sessions are more exclusive. Sort of “by invite only.” I think it’s worth reminding Session readers that some pubs have Irish music that looks and smells like a session, but in fact it’s more of a performance, and newcomers aren’t instantly welcome to join in. I think it’s better to find this out before playing three rounds of Whiskey Before Breakfast and being shown the door without your case or coat.

Finally, (gawd, won’t he ever shut up?) if you want to eventually play at a session but don’t feel ready yet, just go to listen. Leave your instrument at home if you must, but go and listen. Visit with the other people there. Listen. Ask questions. Listen. Find out what tunes are played week after week. LISTEN. If you show up often enough, sooner or later someone will ask if you play, and like as not you’ll get an invite to sit in. If you’re still not ready, ask if there’s a slow session in your area, or start one yourself. Playing with other people, especially people who’s music you enjoy and aspire to, is the most fun and most effective way to learn. You can spend years in your living room and not learn what you might pick up in one three-hour session.

I remember when you first moved East and posted gloomy laments about having no one to play with in Toronto….now you’ve got all us westerners jealous….:-)

Re: bodhran players: My bodhran playing / making friend goes to Ireland quite a lot and says the bodhran players there, especially the old ones, sit quietly in a corner and pump out a regular rhythm as reliably as a metronome. Nothing fancy at all. The fancy drumming is a quite recent development, I think. More in the interest of “showmanship” than adherance to tradition. If your rhythms are simple and you’re on the beat and have your left hand on the skin (as opposed to gripping the cross-braces) you have met all the requirements of traditional bodhran playing, and no-one is likely to be irritated. Keeping in mind, of course, it seems really easy to end up with too many bodhran players. One more tip from bodhran Dave, if you see two other bodhrans out at a session, leave it in the bag.

When I’ve got it on the fiddle, I go back to Dora’s, start it off, and when better players than me join in I listen, watch and tweak and pick up ideas on technique.

I’d feel a bit bold recording a session, especially a session full of pros. Even if you ask first if anyone minds, who’s going to be the snob with the “problem” and say “Actually, I’d rather you didn’t”? I just had an idea of one more element I intend to add to my un-necessarily complex tune learning method: I intend to take a lesson or two from a player there. I will go to the lesson prepared with my little list of tunes I *almost* know and a tape recorder and ask him to play them for me nice and slow. That way I don’t have to sift through 4 hours of really quick tunes and bar noise to find the three I really want to learn.
