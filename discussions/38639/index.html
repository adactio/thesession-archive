What notes to leave out!

I just can’t seem to get up to quality session speed on my tenor banjo….at fast speed I lose tone, musicality, rhythm..the works.
Recently someone helpfully advised…sure you can always drop a few notes or leave out one or two here and there….but I hadn’t the cop on to ask him which notes or if there was a trade secret or a way of dropping the right notes!
Would appreciate any help in this area…btw…I,m not being lazy…but would like to be able to add positively to the music rather than stumble and bumble along. Thanks

Re: What notes to leave out!

You can certainly drop a note (or rather, replace two or more short notes with one longer one) here and there - this is one way of introducing melodic variation into a tune. The only ‘trade secret’ I know of is to keep playing a few more years and keep listening to good players to see how they interpret tunes. If there is any kind of system that can be applied to dropping notes, it would be to avoid dropping the same note (or other note in a similar context) every time. If you treat a certain phrase in a certain way every time, it can change the overall rhythmic character of the tune - or just sound like you don’t know the tune properly.

I am probably not as up-to-speed on plectrum instruments now as I once was (i. I have been playing more fiddle in recent years; ii. most of the sessions I get to now are non-Irish and the tunes tend to be slower or less ‘notey’), so I am very much aware of how dropping a note can be a useful ‘recovery’ opportunity from the relentlesness of a reel at session speed. But whilst I often do drop notes, I also add extra notes in other places and alter a note or two elsewhere; sometimes, dropping a note might mean making some changes to the notes that follow it.

I’m sorry I don’t have any easy answers for you. For some ideas on how to leave ‘space’ in the music, I strongly recommend listening to Tommy Peoples.

Re: What notes to leave out!

I agree with previous posters, dropping notes shouldn’t be used as a crutch to get you to session speed. Better to practice slowly and get all of the notes under your fingers, rather than steamrolling into a session by playing a reel entirely with quavers/quarter notes.

To be clear, though, are you looking at a tune that is notated out with specific ornamentation in it, like say setting #2 of The Five Mile Chase (https://thesession.org/tunes/458#setting13336)? If there are notated ornaments, those can be stripped out or replaced with your own choice of ornaments. But that is more of an artistic decision to get the melody to fit your instrument and how you want the tune to sound, not a trick to play faster.

You’ve been playing about a year? It takes 10,000 focused hours to get to a level where dropping notes happens without conscious effort, i.e., naturally, phrasing the tune as you conceive of it. Just keep at it and in a few years this won’t be a problem. Meanwhile, slow down and play every beautiful note. Just leave out the ugly ones.

Natural Talent: Not Important

One fascinating point of the study: No “naturally gifted” performers emerged. If natural talent had played a role, we would expect some of the “naturals” to float to the top of the elite level with fewer practice hours than everyone else. But the data showed otherwise. The psychologists found a direct statistical relationship between hours of practice and achievement. No shortcuts. No naturals.

I’m surprised no one has mentioned this, so I will. As a flute player, I use a fair amount of ornamentation when I play tunes alone that I sometimes pull back on to play at session speed. Sometimes it’s good to do that just so the sound is cleaner and more homogenous, which a session needs to be to avoid cacophony. If you can’t play the tune stripped down a little (without actually dropping notes), then you need to work on the tune more or your playing, in general.

I will admit that I am not the fastest player, so on some tunes that our session lot tend to play at lightning speed, I will come in and out in order to keep up. In general, though, that is not necessary, and if it were, I’d find another session.

Re: What notes to leave out!

As others have stated, I think it’s important to not drop notes in the name of speed. You also shouldn’t dumb down the “hard parts” of tunes if you can’t play them as fast as the rest of the tune. You should slow down, relax, and work on getting everything up to the speed you want.

I remember when I was struggling to hit session speed on banjo, and there were a few things that really helped me (and much disagreement on thisboard about the right and wrong ways to do things… So only take these as suggestions as things that helped me…)

1. Picking patterns (lots of disagreement on this one). I remember having a lesson with a good player when I had only been playing for a year or so. I was struggling with a particular part of a reel, and telling him that I always struggled with that part every time, and it was messing up my rhythm. He asked to see me play it, and immediately noticed that I was “cheating” in my pick motion. Since my hand was moving from a note on the A string to a note on the D string, I automatically picked that note in the up direction, even though it was a down beat. But for most of the rest of the tune, I was picking DUDU DUDU. So the change in direction created a jerking motion in my hand that messed up my rhythm. So from that point, I started making sure that I was picking DUDU DUDU consistently on reels, which requires some work, because you’re often “cross picking”, in the sense that your hand crosses the string without hitting it, and then strikes it in the opposite direction.

So that brings us to jigs. A few months later, after feeling like my playing had been torn apart trying to pay attention to my pick direction, I was working on a jig with another great player. And there’s a funky part where the notes seem to fall off the bar lines strangely, and I couldn’t get my brain to do it. And the player asked me if I was picking my jigs DUD DUD. Argh! Even though I used to accompany jigs with DUD DUD a lot of times, I had gotten so fixated on picking reels DUDU DUDU, I had translated that to my jigs, and I was having a hard time with the rhythm. So now I pick jigs DUD DUD pretty much exclusively. The hard part about that for jigs is getting ornamentation in without changing your pick pattern, but that’s a topic for another thread… (And has been discussed many times over the years here).

Anyway, implementing specific picking patterns in my playing helped with a lot of things. First, it helped me play with good rhythm. It feels like your hand is dancing to the beat. Secondly, it really helped me gain some speed with my playing. My hand always knows which direction it goes next. And lastly, it helped open the door to variation for me. My hand is always going to pick in the same direction, no matter what note, or ornament, etc. that I might play next. So the picking pattern helped me get rid of relying too much on “muscle memory”, where I would lose it if I tried to add or leave out an ornament, or play a harmony note or double stop…

2. Economy of motion. Try taking a video of your right hand when you’re playing fast, and see if it looks like it is moving efficiently, or whether it is flailing about. Your pick only needs to move just past the string in the “follow through”, and if it’s moving way past the string before stopping and changing direction, you need to tighten up the motion a bit. And I used to have my fingers on my right hand spread out, kind of brushing the head, and even just the momentum of my fingers was pulling my hand motion further than I needed. So curling my fingers under helped me work on the compact motion that is needed.

3. Relaxation. Watch any great player, and they’re not exerting much energy when they play. Our natural instinct with playing faster is to tighten up and play harder. Think about running - if you want to run faster, you run harder, so that’s a natural instinct. But the opposite is true with playing banjo. If you want to play faster, you need to relax, and keep everything compact and under control. If you tense up, and strike the string harder, of course your follow through is going to be longer, and take more time… I play fast, but in the occasional situation where the speed is pushing beyond my comfort level, I have to force myself to relax and become part of the flow.

4. Practice Practice Practice. When you’re playing at home, try practicing at different speeds, different amounts of swing, and different amounts of ornamentation. I hesitate to have people practice with a metronome, because they’re too rigid, and teach you some potentially bad habits. But they can be useful as a tool. I actually have a mobile app on my iPhone called BPM Studio, which listens to what you’re playing and graphs the BPM, and that’s a great way to do it (except that it isn’t particularly good at picking up the tempo of a single instrument… But toss it out in a session and it will do a pretty good job.) But the main thing is to work on tunes slowly. Find the speed that you can play a tune with very few mistakes. Then push it up faster and work for a bit, then bring it back down. Doing that, you’ll find that after playing beyond your comfort level in speed for a while, when you go back down, your comfort level tempo has probably increased a bit…

5. Play with people that are better than you. Surround yourself with the kind of music that you want to play, and let them push you. It helps if they’re not the hot shots that are going to go off and play at speeds that are impossible for you. It’s better if they play just beyond your comfort threshold. But the nice thing about playing with other people is that they can be responsible for a lot of the heavy lifting. They’ll keep it going even when you mess up, and they’ll be playing with nice rhythm. So you can relax and focus on your playing and not worry about what happens if you make mistakes, etc.

Re: What notes to leave out!

Greatest advice from David Levine and Reverend…and best of all it applies to just about all walks of life! I’ll only add that if all you do is play tunes, you’ll likely learn a lot, I mean a lot, of tunes. What will come much more slowly, if at all, is the ability to play really well. That takes focused practice on technique by that I mean scales in all their combinations (major, minor,chromatic, 3rds, octaves, long tones, etc.), left hand exercises that focus on individual finger movement with no regard to melody, and right hand exercises. (Note: the pick is not to just “stab” at a string, but should include preparation, execution, and follow through. It takes time, that’s why the 10,000 hours is so important.) I find it best to work on these “chops” in short bursts between tunes to avoid boredom when I do them. If you’re mind isn’t focused you’re not learning anything. Speed follows when technique is solid. You can drop so much from a tune that it’s almost unrecognizable in an effort to play fast and still never become anything but mediocre. I’ll bet that’s not where you want to be. I wish very much that I was not so overcome with enthusiasm that I forgot how to learn to play well. Playing catch-up is a bummer.

Oh and for reasons I’ve voiced before, get a metronome. There’s a time to use one and a time to set it aside.

Re: What notes to leave out!

Good question, Timmyrebel. It’s common advice, with the “details” often left out. Sometimes it’s, “You don’t need to play all the notes.” Or, “triplets & ornaments are tricky in reels; leave ’em out.”

To answer your question it’s not so much which bits to leave out, rather when is it o.k. to have a rest. I think the answer depends on which rhythm is being played. Is it played straight (reel) or swung (hornpipe)? Strong downbeat emphasis vs. giving a lift. Pay attention to your phrasing, when playing fast tempo it’s easy to forget natural places to rest (leave something out). Speaking of phrasing, I play flute, I agree that you may benefit from listening to fluters with good phrasing. To play flute well is to learn where to take your rests, to take a breath, to leave out a note. But absolutely do not mimic their phrasing. Tenor banjo has it’s own way of finding the notes, which are very different from flute.

To sum up, there is some good advice posted above. I’ll leave you with some instructional material from banjo player Chris Smith about tempo, which I think is at ‘the heart’ of your question;
