Re: Circular Breathing.

Didge players can use circular breathing, for the same reason that reed players (oboe) and brass (trumpets) can - they’re forcing air through a narrow aperture (reed or lips) and so they’re breathing against a fair old back pressure. Doing it on the flute is much more difficult because the player is blowing into free atmosphere at the lip of an aperture and so the back pressure is much lower.

Re: Circular Breathing--throat tuning/embouchure

As for this…I never knew it had a name. Anyways…when I was taught to play my Boehm as a child, I was told to press my lips together and smile…then keep them pressed together and make as small a hole as possible between them and blow. What this did was thin my lips and pull them back from the flute in a curve to where the only contact was right in the center. Playing took a lot of air, my tone was airy, my lips would get tired, and I had trouble playing in tune. I was told to counter that by pushing my headjoint in and out and to constant roll to correct as I played. I played all through my school years that way…and always felt it shouldn’t be so hard.

My Sr. year, I was told by a visiting prof. classical flautist that my embouchure was all wrong. I didn’t know what she meant, but she played the exact same flute as I did and her tone was sooooooo warm and round. After college, I ended up not playing seriously for eighteen years, and when I picked it up again, I decided to take proper lessons at the local university to work on my embouchure problem.

It was there I learned about “throat tuning”, but it was referred to as the correct embouchure for the flute. I don’t remember the method name and the name of the famous player who published the books we used which stressed using this technique…he was an old frenchman. I want to say Moyer? I’d have to dig my old stuff out to see.

He believed in proper development of the embouchure in the beginning above all else, and in not rushing that process. Everything else could wait. I was taught to relax my mouth toward the flute, make it flat so that this flattened shape matched the flattened shape of the lip plate and rested on it, pillow my lower lip, but blow down over it with my upper lip, make an elongated oval air stream, and blow somewhat downward. I had to keep an open throat as usual with a dropped jaw. I could only get the lower midrange out in this position. When I would tighten my lips to reach a higher note, I was told not to. I was forbidden to even try to play those yet anyway…and would learn a different way to reach them. He stressed playing only the notes in that lower range until a student had this embouchure “set” in his/her brain. I could play longer, had more breath control, played with less air, my tone was full and warm, and I was consistently in tune…no more rolling! I was amazed. We played baby pieces in that range for a while. The next step was the next range of notes up the scale…reached by moving the jaw forward to raise the airstream--not by tightening my lips and making a smaller hole to blow through as before. More pieces followed including the new range of notes. Then another progression to reach the next group of notes up the scale and additional pieces including them. I was able to set my headjoint with a mark…and keep it there…and always play in tune for the first time in my life without all the constant rolling. I had to practice the jaw movements by holding a thin drink stirrer in my lips, pretend it was my airstream, and move my jaw/lips forward and back to raise and lower the “airstream” while keeping my lips and mouth relaxed, my jaw dropped, and my throat open. Then do this while blowing on my palm…moving the airstream to the three positions. I guess this technique can be used as well to tune when needed rather than roll…or to bend notes?

My question is this…what embouchure were you taught? Any mention of moving the jaw?

Re: Circular Breathing.

My first flute teacher described the embouchure as “pretend you just ate a sour lemon and then try to kiss someone”. Rolling the headjoint in and out was never discussed or thought of except if you deliberately wanted to play with a note. I used to do it with my flute students to demonstrate control and to help them to find the position that produced the fullest sound. The movement of the lower jaw shouldn’t change your basic embouchure the hole should be the same. It’s more like directing the airstream higher or lower. For low notes on the flute…you want more of your airstream going into the flute for high notes you want less. I don’t recall however being told deliberately to move my jaw. I know I do it ever so slightly and when I’m playing with those out of tune pianos you have to. I’ve always called it lipping.

Throat tuning.

I’m reading and remembering…and laughing. When I was told to try this new embouchure, I was asked if I’d seen pictures of this pair of Mardi Gras masks (Mardi Gras is big down here and the masks are well-known)…one’s laughing and one is frowning…or the way a sad clown draws that upside-down exaggerated frown…or the way someone looks when they do some really UGLY crying like Lucille Ball when she wales…lol I was made to practice pushing the corners of my mouth forward to touch the lip plate and pulling them down as hard as I could into that upside down frown. But my teacher believed in over-compensating and exagerating to learn technique. She acted quite serious and told me this was to be my new embouchure. I thought I would die right there. It was hilarious, and I cracked up.

However, that is basically the embouchure, although in an extreme form…the corners pushed toward the flute with the maximum amount of lower lip pillowed on the plate, a flat mouth as though pressing your lips to a flat surface. It’s almost impossible to push the corners to the flute with maximum lower lip contact unless you pull down slightly. But the goal was to find the way to do this with that dropped jaw, open throat, and relaxed mouth. Quite a task. And it feels ridiculous. I got the giggles at many a lesson. At home alone practicing the “grimace embouchure” with a drink stirrer in my mouth laughing hilariously all by my lonesome made me look quite mad indeed. ;-)

Circular breathing and throat tuning

My personal opinion about circular breathing is that it’s a neat trick, but one i can live without. Flute playing to me is like singing, and you gotta breath sometime, breathing is ok. One more note, in the old times circular breathing was called “glass blowing”, because flute players often would go learn how to blow glass in order to learn the technique.

Throat tuning is another thing altogether, and i recommend any flute player to pick up Robert Dick’s book about advanced techniques and practice it. Throat tuning means “shaping” your throat and vocal chords to resonate with the note you’re playing (like singing, but without actually vibrating the vocal chords). Try it, especially for slow pieces. One way to practice is to hum at the same time you play (usually a couple of octaves lower than the flute note). But be *very* careful. Singing requires a lot less air than playing the flute, so you have to go soft, so you don’t hurt yourself.

g

Throat tuning

Throat tuning, by the way, doesn’t aim at getting a different sound from the flute, just to improve the resonance and richness of the standard flute sound, by optimally shaping the airways for each note. It’s not something you can see, unless you have x-ray vision.

Embochure

No problem, milesnagopaleen. What’s the origin of *your* name? :-)

One more thing i thought of while my dog was walking me this morning: be aware that the embochure for an “Irish” (conical) flute is different from a modern (cylindrical) flute. The conical body provides a lot more “resistance”, and you have to give it more air pressure. One ends up using a tighter, thinner embochure than one would use for a modern flute. Also, one ends up having the embochure hole turned more towards oneself than one would have with the modern flute (try it). Irish players often talk of “covering” the hole, because when you’re doing it “right”, one’s lower lip will cover from 30% to 50% of the embochure hole. Here’s someone else’s attempt at describing this:
http://www.flutesite.com/learning.htm
