Trad Bands

Between my ipod, CD player, 1970s record player and youtube I always seem to revert back to those amazing albums, videos and recordings of “the big three”. My CD player has heard the Kesh Jig set(The Bothies), Tom Billy’s jig(Dé Danann) and The Blacksmith(Planxty) more times than I would care to guess.

I have Slide, Danú and Dervish perched on the CD shelf but I always end up reaching for the “oldies”. The question I put to the table is: is this type of raw trad music gone or is the more flowery stuff here to stay????

Will there ever be another Bothy Band???

Re: Trad Bands

They did this stuff when they were young - that’s what gives it that passion and edge. Altan’s first album (and I dare say others of theirs that I have not heard) had the same hackle-raising quality, for me anyway. I first heard this one in 1988 - I don’t know when it was first released.

I suspect that most teenagers / twenty-somethings latch onto, in particular, music made by bands and individuals round about a decade older than they are. These are, well, young enough to be young, and at the same time old and successful enough to be looked up to. Their younger listeners and devotees will not see them as vulnerable, fragile, pathos-shrouded young things fluttering into life’s storms: they will see them as young lions kicking butt - even if some of these performers are actually dead by drugs before thirty.

I imagine these factors will have influenced people who have started following Irish music when young - that they’ll have latched onto some particular bands that’ll be *their* bands, in a way and at a time of life that won’t be repeated. And in the case of those (like me) who didn’t grow up with Irish music, there’ll be memories of particular music we found in youth that actually directed us towards Irish music.

Re: Trad Bands

“But what is there that’s not already been done? ” (ian stock)

People have been looking around them since the dawn of history, and asked that question. A Greek of ? the c6 B.C. opined that every ‘techne’ (art, craft) had reached its pinnacle, and that was it. Solomon mused that everything around him had happened before, and would happen again. (I believe the haunting song Clannad composed for the series “Harry’s Game” is based on this latter, familiar from a Gaelic Bible.)

But of course, new worlds in the arts and in technology continued to be found. People who made really dramatic breakthroughs into the unknown have, I get the impression, been people who were very well versed in what they were doing up to then - i.e., masters and mistresses of what they’d grown up to do in existing traditions. Newton, Beethoven, Turner (the artist)…any number more. Newton was a theologian before discovering modern physics, and as far as I know continued to value his theological studies, not cast them off as irrelevant.

This is a sunnier way of saying, “Who controls the past, controls the future…” - or rather, “Who understands and respects what has been done, is in the best position to receive unexpected new insights that have real mileage to them…”

We’re not all going to be Beethovens but we can all be open to better and different ways of doing whatever it is we do.

My core feeling about art - art of whatever kind, music included - is that it is about discovery.

It’s hard to believe that the same names still get trotted out whenever there is a discussion around the big trad bands of the 70’s. For starters Planxty, for all of their wonderful songs, were not a trad band in any real sense, instrumentals formed a relatively small part of their repertoire. Secondly, there were other bands of equal quality around at the same time, Stockton’s Wing, Inchiquin and General Humbert to name but three. In more recent times bands like At the Racket and Lunasa have made innovative and high quality CD’s proving that ITM is a continuum constantly changing and developing.

Irish music has always had excellent individual performers, what made the difference in the 70’s and early 80’s was the influence of people like Donal Lunny, Alec Finn and Steve Cooney brought to the backing and arrangements.

The other forgotten hero of that era is PJ Curtis, the producer of bands like the Bothy’s and Stockton’s Wing. He is widely acknowledged as being a key player in the development of these bands.

Re: Trad Bands

While I understand where ashington83 is coming from the sheer number of errors in his/her posting must be countered.

1) ‘For starters Planxty, for all of their wonderful songs, were not a trad band in any real sense, instrumentals formed a relatively small part of their repertoire.’

Well, on a very superficial level it all depends upon which version of Planxty we’re talking about, but ashington83 seems to be adopting some hitherto unencountered definition of a ‘trad band’, one that only plays instrumental music. Planxty’s precursors, such as Sweeney’s Men or The Johnstons or the band which played on Moore’s ‘Prosperous’ album, certainly didn’t fit that limited definition either.

2) ‘Secondly, there were other bands of equal quality around at the same time, Stockton’s Wing, Inchiquin and General Humbert to name but three. The fact that the latter two only made one studio album each should not detract from their contribution, after all the Bothy’s only made three studio albums.’

Stockton’s Wing were not formed until 1977, six years after the release of Planxty’s first album. Inchiquin released one album in 1976. General Humbert released two albums, not one, in 1975 and 1978. None of these bands were around during Planxty’s initial and peak period of influence and General Humbert could hardly be considered a ‘trad band’ in the terms employed by ashington83.

3) ‘In more recent times bands like At the Racket and Lunasa have made innovative and high quality CD’s proving that ITM is a continuum constantly changing and developing.’

Jayz, that reads like a terrible press release.

4) ‘Irish music has always had excellent individual performers, what made the difference in the 70’s and early 80’s was the influence of people like Donal Lunny, Alec Finn and Steve Cooney brought to the backing and arrangements.’

This is simply not the case. What made the difference was, without question, The Bothy Band’s first album, the trio playing of Tommy Peoples, Paddy Keenan and Matt Molloy and, as Dónal put it to me, the need for himself, Mícheál Ó Domhnaill and Tríona to catch up with the team of horses that was heading off into the distance.

You’d really be hard put, ashington83, to identify any long-lasting influence supplied by Messrs. Finn or Cooney.

5) ‘The other forgotten hero of that era is PJ Curtis, the producer of bands like the Bothy’s and Stockton’s Wing. He is widely acknowledged as being a key player in the development of these bands.’

PJ is indeed someone whose influence upon the development of Ireland’s music has been largely ignored. However, he did not produce any of The Bothy Band’s albums (he was the band’s sound manager, roadie and all round factotum until its demise in 1979). That’s not to diminish his record producing career (some sixty albums, including artistes such as Altan, Stockton’s Wing, Pumpkinhead and many more), but the most influential album he recorded would have to be Davy Spillane’s ‘Atlantic Bridge’.
