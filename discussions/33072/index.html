Re: Turlough O’Carolan

To answer the question about Carolan’s place in Irish music: Irish traditional music in general is a reference to dance music, airs and old songs. Carolan did not write any of these. His tunes were heavily influenced by Baroque music and were written to be performed on harp, an instrument rarely used in ITM as compared to fiddles, concertinas, flutes, whistles, and pipes. This is not to say that harp is not popular. It can play ITM as can guitar, but it is not the mainstay. Many harpers accompany themselves singing, but one is not likely to see a harp in a pub. For me and most folks I’ve played with, a little Carolan goes a long way. There are certain tunes that everyone seems to know - Planxty Irwin, Fanny Powers, George Brabazon, Farewell to Music, Carolan’s Concerto and a few others - but these are generally played as a break from the usual diddly-diddly as opposed to being a focal point. That’s my experience, anyway.

Re: Turlough O’Carolan

O‘Carolan is often mistakenly described as the “last of the Irish bards” of course that’s wrong because the bards were murdered in the middle ages, almost 300 years before O’Carolan. He was a blind, itinerant harper who didn’t learn to play until he was in his late teens, and although he was good at composing melodies, sucked at putting words to them - which is why we have over 270 of his melodies and very few of the original texts.

What was music like before the 1700s in Ireland? Nobody really knows as it wasn’t written down. The first written music was published in Dublin in 1724, and that was mainly a compendium of “pop” tunes transcribed for pianoforte that were heavily influenced by English and European music, hardly “Irish” at all. We have nothing prior to that.

One can hypothesize about the music based on the instrumentation, of course. That certainly sets limitations on what could be played at the time. But we just don’t know for sure.

Re: Turlough O’Carolan

Bunting rambles at length about the way instruments were tuned and played (according to him - et al). Semitones didn’t require levers, as any accidentals could be taken care of by a pre-tuned string (the harp might not have been tuned in a straight diatonic sequence).
Presumably, tunes were not compiled into sets, but played as set pieces, the harp being “prepared” beforehand. I had a conversation with Wendy Stewart long ago (when she gave me a demonstration of how my own harp could be played - yowch), and a suggestion was made that, on these old wire strung instruments, a fingernail might have been used to stop a string to produce an accidental.

Re: Turlough O’Carolan

I would like to respond to a couple of my points that have been challenged. Regarding harp, I fully realize the harp is arguably the most traditional of Irish instruments; it is, after all, a national symbol of Ireland. However, I don’t think it can be denied that most Irish harp music played today is either an adaptation of traditional music unsuitable for a pub or dance, or one of Carolan’s works.

Regarding Carolan, I tried to answer what I thought the OP wanted to know. It may be some of his work did indeed start as songs (I seriously doubt that, but concede the possibility), but his works are not trad as we generally think of it. Certainly there is nothing quintessentially Irish about his compositions. The reason why I said he was influenced by Baroque music (someone said he was a part of it) is because he was not a composer in the same sense as Vivaldi, Handel, Telemann, or Bach. I don’t know if any of his music ever made it past the shores of Ireland in his lifetime, but the larger point is that he was just trying to make a living, not write for the concert hall or royal courts. My impression is that he was not schooled in composition, so his contribution is a matter of general feel, rather than formal compositional style.

Carolan is unique in that we have a body of work from an Irishman of yesteryear. So far as I know, he’s the only one - but to call his work traditional strains the term as we know it today, IMO.

Re: Turlough O’Carolan

Some things:

1) Bunting’s transcriptions, however accurate they may be, either didn’t reflect the actual key for the tune or reflected the key to which the particular harp was tuned that he heard when making the transcription. Since the transcriptions were of different harpers and there appears to have been little attention to key center in that dray beyond getting the instrument well in tune with itself it is very likely that when transcribed and applied to whatever was the pitch level of the time (A-440? A=425? A=452? etc.) they would appear on the page in different keys. Did Bunting have perfect pitch? Does anyone know? And if so what did he “hear” as A??

2) As far as I know none of the harps of Carolan’s era would have had sharping levers. If you go read Ann Heymans huge book on wire strung harp playing you can probably get the answer about how accidentals were played if they were. http://www.clairseach.com/shop/books.htm She’s the greatest living authority on wire strung harp playing I believe.

3) The business of sharped leading tones is often an addition by folks whose ears have grown up around the “modern” major and minor scales. Often those who are used to the various modes may be content without the accidentals. I do not know how much impact this has on the Bunting transcriptions of Carolan or on any of the other notated versions.

4) It is well to remember that Carolan had been dead for a good long time before anything was written down. He died in 1738 and the famous Belfast harp festival where Bunting wrote things down was in 1792 (most of the players were very old) http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_Harp_Festival We have no way of knowing how accurately the players represented the tunes nor how much they changed through the folk process.

5) There are 135 pages in volume 2 of the Donal O’Sullivan books on Carolan devoted to notes on the tunes and including words to many of them. There is no question that many of the tunes were also songs. A quick check of a couple of well known tunes, Fanny Power and Bridget Cruise (airs 1 and 4) shows words for all of these. I have no Irish so can’t translate the words, but I know translations can be found on the web. There is also mention of quite a number of pieces for which only the words survive. That is further indication that many of his works were songs, or at least could be sung or played. Despite this I too have never heard any of his things sung.

6) To me (opinion here) Carolan is musically “in the cracks” between whatever was Irish music of his time and the then current Baroque styles which were well known in Ireland then (as others have mentioned). The old Irish land owners at that time were, I believe, being pushed out by one of the many influxes of British “lords.” Carolan was doubtless canny enough to adapt what he played to his audience and hence the mixture of things we currently know.

7) There is discussion in the O’Sullivan book of his relation to the Italian Baroque masters, and to the supposed famous “competition” with Geminani. Read it and draw your own conclusions.

Whew…pretty long winded. I hope it is useful..

Re: Turlough O’Carolan

“Can you point me at some of these references Weejie? I’d like to follow up a bit. Thanks.”

O‘Sullivan mentions an incomplete book in chapter XXI. This is apparently in the Joly Gift in the National Library of Ireland, and contains 23 tunes, all by Carolan. O’Sullivan says this is the “earliest book” of Carolan tunes. (Vol. 1, p. 125). Through Petrie’s notes he identifies this book as being by the Neal(e) brothers. He also describes the other Neal(e) book in the same chapter, and the Daniel Wright “Aria Di Camera”.
O’Sullivan dates these books 1721, 1724 and 1730. Others differ, but they all date them well within Carolan’s lifetime.

In the “Conspectus of Authorities” on page 3 of Volume 2, he assigns the letters H, I and J to these books for reference to sources.

There are Scorch transcriptions of two of these books on the NPU website (the Neal 1724 “Most Celebrated Irish Tunes” and Wright’s “Aria Di Camera”, which they date as 1727):

http://www.pipers.ie/imco/

Re: harp

As has been mentioned, levers (for nylon/gut) and blades are a matter of convenience rather than necessity: harps--especially wire--require constant tweaking to keep in tune, so sharpening a few notes for a particular tune is not problematic. Additionally, as blades and levers modify the length of vibrating string--they must be precise in order to function efficiently; many are not, and necessitate further tweaking to attain proper tuning.

Diatonic harps can have full levers/blades, but they are still diatonic--employing various sharps and flats as needed, but once set, preclude that string from its natural pitch. Chromatic harps are (cross-strung) double and triple, or pedal. But I don’t believe there were any cross-strung wire harps--for a variety of reasons. I could be wrong about that, but I don’t think I’ve seen any record of this. At any rate, O’Carolan’s harp was diatonic

Re: Turlough O’Carolan

“At any rate, O’Carolan’s harp was diatonic”

I think the point is that, in the days of the “old harpers” the harp might not have been tuned in a straight diatonic scale for a particular piece.
There was no detailed record of how Carolan tuned his instrument. From what Bunting et al have said, harps were tuned according to the piece to be played. Carolan lived in that interim period where the “old harpers” were dying out. He wasn’t a “Baroque composer” in the true sense, because he wasn’t from the Baroque school of composition - if he were, we’d have, with little doubt, much in the way of his own manuscripts. He didn’t write down his compositions, nor did he have anyone transcribe them for him ( he is said to have composed on the buttons of his coat, but no reliable explanation seems to exist on how exactly he did this). He was a composer of tunes who lived during the Baroque era, and some of his tunes, but by no means all, reflect that era.

Re: Turlough O’Carolan

“And are, still”

Indeed, but not to the same extent - yes, for those who play wire strung harps without levers.
In Carolan’s day, cross-strung harps were relatively new, and the double action harp hadn’t been invented. I don’t think he would have thought of his instrument as a “diatonic harp”. The nomenclature is there to distinguish between the types of stringing and method of modulation. To Carolan, and his contemporaries, his harp would probably have been considered a “harp” (or clàrsach, futiver).
Today, with gut strings to the fore, and various methods of playing chromatic pieces at hand (or foot), modulation isn’t just achievable by re-tuning via the tuning pins. Other than for those who stick to the traditional wire strung models, the prospect of pieces with accidentals or modulation isn’t as daunting.
Yet, as discussion on here is seeming to establish, there were pieces in Carolan’s day that modulated or contained accidentals. The instrument wasn’t confined within diatonic scales. To call Carolan’s instrument a “diatonic” instrument under those circumstances would be misleading. Within the current system and variety of instruments, it has some relevance.
