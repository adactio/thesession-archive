I enjoy the challenge and motivation that comes with “playing up” (nice term!). For me, it’s mainly a reality check for session tempos, so I don’t practice too slowly at home, and for learning new repertoire.

Unfortunately, playing with my betters doesn’t help with specific instrument technique, because I’m almost always the only one playing a mandolin in a local session. Or a flute, oddly enough, considering what a core instrument it is in this music. I’m in an area thick with Irish fiddlers and Scottish pipers, but I very seldom see another flute player around here. There are a few things in phrasing and rhythm pulse I can learn from fiddlers, but there just isn’t that much overlap in technique on mandolin or flute.

I might be a more proficient player if I was more disciplined about that, but life’s too short, I play this music to have fun, and all the other usual excuses.

Re: Playing Irish traditional music

I’m probably an outlier here. It has always been my personal belief that the most disheartening words we’ve ever invented are “more” and “better”. I fully get that they have some value and aren’t by themselves evil. Still when the concepts become to far to the front they guarantee feelings of disappointment and inadequacies. Better to happily grasp the concept of “enough” and then see what comes. Working at being “better” is both the path to our success and the source of much of our frustration. There will always, say it with me, always, be somebody, something, “better”. I believe that if you can’t find joy in “enough” you will never be “better”. That’s me anyway.

Oh and I agree that the path to playing well isn’t by playing more tunes. That mostly results in knowing more tunes. To play those tunes better you have to develop “chops” (ya’ll have heard this from me before). I’m usually sad to hear someone say they’re “working on a tune”. They will learn that tune almost every time. If they have the chops I’ll want to hear it. If they don’t it will just be a minute of my life I’ll not get back. Yeah it’s not as much fun unless you defer the excitement to the time you find you really are playing well. That’s when someone else includes you among the better players they want to play with.

Chops aren’t that hard to get. Don’t waste time with an hour a day on scales like your Mom said. Try three minutes an hour…on one scale…up and down…F# Dorian for example. The next hour maybe the first inversion of a C chord. Keep it moving. That’s six minutes more than almost everybody else does and now when you hear it (or read it) you can play it…and sooner or later you will hear it. Or you can make an exercise of a difficult figure from a tune (and in more than one key, it’ll pop up there too sometime). Or, if you can stand it, make it five or ten minutes a day. You decide what your limit is before you find the point od diminishing returns. The point is do something with discipline and a purpose. After a while you’ll be “playing up” with those you once discouraged you with their talent. Getting better isn’t a second job, not a horse-race. Don’t forget to enjoy the moment. Don’t be the runner who keeps on running after he’s already won. That’s what this is all about, understanding, and being, “enough”.

Hi GITN,

Notwithstanding my previous comment, I do understand your point of view and broadly agree. It’s just that your opening post came over as a little simplistic but, of course, I do realise what you mean.

We are talking about “quality” sessions whether they be Irish and/or Scottish etc with “respected” and/or “respectful” players. So, yes, attending these is surely beneficial.

However, not infrequently, we can sometimes find ourselves in a situation where there are players who are seemingly “technically” better on their instruments (If not always musically) who will just “rattle” out tunes without any great feeling or just try to show off. Or just do their own thing and exclude the bulk of the company there. They are not necessarily being unfriendly and may even be very welcoming in their own way but you are still not going to learn an awful lot from them.

Also, there are the situations which Trish has described where the music is actually excellent but it’s probably best to “sit it out” and listen.

Having said that, I’ve been fortunate enough to end up in the same sessions as some of the very best Irish and Scottish players at folk festivals etc or if they have just happened to “sit in” at a regular session. In such situations, these musicians will generally choose to “go with the flow” but their playing will still be at a much higher level. So, the rest of us can’t really help but “raise our game” which is all to the good.

Re: Playing Irish traditional music

In some ways, this is starting to mirror the recent discussion about how happy you are with your own playing… (https://thesession.org/discussions/43453). I still submit that you can show a strong desire to keep improving while still enjoying the moment and taking satisfaction from the progress you’ve already made. They don’t have to be mutually exclusive. Playing music is one of the highlights of my life. I look forward to every time I get to play. Some nights disappoint, and some don’t, but it is *always* worth it! In fact, it’s so much fun that I keep pushing to get better, because the better I get, the more potential I have for fun!

Unfortunately, God’s cruel joke on us is that the better you get, the more difficult it can be for that potential fun to fully manifest. When you’re first starting out, it’s easy to play with people that are better than you, and every session has the potential to be the best you’ve ever played. The more experience you have, the harder it becomes to “play up”. For me, this led to several years of struggling a bit, because I felt like I only played well when I was playing with people better than myself. So it became a struggle to “find the inspiration from within” (https://thesession.org/discussions/18151). But working at making myself better helped change that. I can now certainly be inspired by my own playing, but it doesn’t mean that I want to stagnate in my progress, nor does it mean that I don’t still love playing with people better than me…
