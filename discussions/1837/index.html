Teachers teaching and being taught.

Teachers of music - are they necessary? One extreme viewpoint I’ve heard is, if you need to be taught how to do this stuff, there’s no point in doing it. Then again, a teacher, I imagine, can show you some short cuts and get you doing technical stuff quicker than if you tried to figure it out on your own.
I’ve only had some silver flute lessons many years ago, just to get started. None on the wooden flute whistle or box. Have got Hammy Hamilton’s Irish flute book, but that’s about it…it probably shows!

As for teaching, I’ve given a couple of simple lessons. I’ve been asked to teach a fair few times, but never got round to it - my sight reading is pretty dire so I think that’s why I’ve shied away from it. Do you have to sight read to teach this stuff? And how would you construct a profitable lesson?

Danny.

Re: Teachers teaching and being taught.

Just a quick funny story about learning by ear vs sightreading or what have you…

Well first let me say that Michael Tubridy’s teaching style was that he’d start off the class playing tunes until no one in the class could play along with the one he was playing, in our case, not a lengthy process. Then he’d patiently play the first phrase alone, then the class with him, then again, alone, ensemble, until we could string together the whole tune. Amazing stuff to hear how peeps can really learn this stuff quickly, & also solidly locked in the old mental vault.

But the funny story is from Kevin C’s class… he would post the ABC’s on the board, a style most of the (American) students were entirely unfamiliar with. (Also he taught the same tunes to both his beginner & advanced classes.) At one point, this precocious little 4th grader playing the silver flute (there were a handful of them), a dancer named Katie & KC’s “Biggest Fan” haha, turns to one of our friends, & looking at a phrase on the board that reads BEGE, says, ‘What’s beige?’

It was a classic moment of how dot readers trying to make the leap to sight reading another ‘language’ fail miserably. Some of us were able to transcribe to staff paper & made photocopies which made learning the tunes for the school girls etc much easier, & the transcription process does make the tune stick a bit better, but overall it felt like cheating, & Kevin couldn’t understand it anyway. We did it mostly to keep from totally sucking on the group student recital, & Katie ended up putting her flute down dancing to the second slip jig anyway, heh. Learning by ear, that’s the ticket. Amen.

Since every person brings different experience/questions/way of understanding or expressing things, everyone needs to be taught a different way. Teachers who don’t know this should think again. Otherwise, stay away from them.

Re: Teachers teaching and being taught.

I think it depends what is being taught. For a playing classical music ie reading the dots, then I think it is essential.
I played flute for years without a teacher, then decided to attend a musical summer school. I was under the illusion that I was a good player…until I had an audition to find out my level. I played Bach (not easy even if your good) The teacher a Mr Trevor Wye eased me back to reality with a few chosen words…they being “forget everything you have learnt and we will start again”
It was a shock at the time but after a week at the summer school and then getting a good teacher I progressed in leaps and bounds.
In classical playing, the emphasis is usually on technique, tone and phrasing within the written score, without much room for variation and ornamentation.

We all know that ITM is a different animal altogether, being that it is full of spontaneous ornamentation and variation and is really best learnt by ear from players steeped in the traditon. That is not always possible, so we do the best we can by any methods available. In my case I learn some tunes by the dots some by ear and very rarely by going to Ireland and just listening. I did have a some lessons with Carmel Gunning (who also uses ABC) that was a useful experience but it didn’t really change my style… I have been an Englishman too long but I am happy with that. The music still grows whoever plays it. Maybe not in the way some people would like but it still moves on nevertheless.

Re: Teachers teaching and being taught.

Chris, I’d agree with you on the points you made. The technical discipline imposed by classical violin training is never wasted, and it gives you a solid technical foundation for any type of fiddling. Fair enough, if you go from classical to Irish trad then there are going to be fingering and bowing techniques you’re going to have to learn which you’re not likely to have come across in classical, and you’ll have to put aside or at least control some things like the pervasive classical vibrato, but you’ll have the technical expertise to absorb the trad techniques without too much trouble. For this reason I believe it is a lot more straightforward going from classical to Irish trad, blue grass, or whatever, than the other way round.

The last time I was at workshops in Ireland I noticed that most of the fiddle tutors, the younger ones especially, had been to music college and would have had a broad professional musical training including classical. Their technique I would describe as having had a firm foundation in classical training somewhere along the line.

As regards the fiddle hold by trad players it is worth noting that no less a classical virtuoso than the late Yehudi Menuhin was of the opinion that the details of how you hold the instrument are only important insofar as they enable you to play easily and efficiently - he was a great advocate of folk music. The modern “standard” classical hold you’ll see used by most professionals in concert halls all over the world is certainly by no means the only effective hold. Personally, I believe the older hold without a shoulder rest (a fairly recent innovation and invention of the Devil himself, btw) is more relaxed and enables you to move the instrument around to accommodate the music you’re playing. I’ve noticed that some professional classical players are now dispensing with the shoulder rest, presumably for this reason.

I wouldn’t go quite as far as dispensing with the chin rest and reverting to the even older pre-19c tradition, although it is useful to be able to play without a chin rest in the unlikely event of your chin rest falling off at a critical juncture in a gig (!). The Irish trad player, strictly speaking, doesn’t need a chin rest because the music almost never goes out of the first position (although there are ways of coping with this if it does), but the chin rest does protect the belly of the instrument from wear and perspiration, and, unless you know what you’re doing, holding the instrument with the chin in contact with the wood is likely to deaden the tone slightly.
Trevor
