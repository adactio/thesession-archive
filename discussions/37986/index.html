Re: Musicians hear songs when they read music

Great observation R D Cook!

I’m not sure what the issue is. Of course musicians hear the notes they read. Yet we all know musicians who can’t. How’s that? Some of us see a note and hear the sound that note makes on the instrument we play. Maybe some of us hear that note as a voice first and have to translate that voice to the instrument. It’s a longer way, but down the same path. The next note we see lets us hear the next note we play and so on. We see, we hear, and we play. Some of us have the ability to skip the “seeing” part. (That’s what opens the door for the tired old learning by ear vs. sight-reading arguments. Another topic entirely.) The process is the same at all levels of ability. The action comes from the sound.

So how about the other side. There are some of us who take a different path. They see a note and it doesn’t translate into a sound, rather to the place their fingers have to go and the motion they need to perform to make a sound. The sound results from the mechanics. I suspect that for most of us it’s some combination of both skills and likely happens with no conscious effort. This in no way limits the quality of the sounds that come together to make a tune. It’s a matter of see, hear, do vs. see, do, hear, in the end all the parts come together (or not, depending on the skill of the player not the process).

We all know people who seemingly have no idea what the dot they see sounds like and others who can’t find the note without seeing it first and others who go either way. Funny how our brains work. And now I’ll pour another whisky and wait to enjoy remarks about how we don’t need to read music anyway.

Re: Musicians hear songs when they read music

I think one of the things to remember is that you don’t have to be a trained musician to play folk music of any kind. Trained musicians are trained to be able to make a living at music, and so they get trained in things that are expected of professional musicians. A lot of people who play folk styles do it for a hobby and so they don’t need to develop those skills.

Sight reading is a great example. you don’t have to even read the written word, much less music, to play any folk style. If you can read enough to use sheet music, it helps, but even still you don’t need to read at the level as somebody who makes their living doing studio work.

so if you can’t “hear” what’s on the sheet, don’t worry about it. It doesn’t mean you aren’t any good. It probably just means you play an instrument that has frets or buttons. I found that the best sight singers in the classes I took were always from the brass and string sections of the orchestra, with low brass players generally being the best.

This may be a side note to the real discussion.

I agree with Gobby. Personally I’ve, seen the need for, or any use for, ABC’s….except that it’s easier to send a tune on line. As far as a usable method to play a tune…nothing’s better than standard musical notation. It’s universal across genre lines and it’s the easiest way to “see” the rhythm and intervals. A tune can be written “bare bones” and allow the player rely on his familiarity with the genre, the “feel” of the tune, to apply ornaments, variation, and articulation to interpret the tune. Or, those things can be very specifically written in. (Note: even though ornaments and articulation can be quite specific there is always room for interpretation.) I suspect that some of the resistance to learning from sheet music comes from trying to learn from ABC’s. Hey guys, it’s just not that hard to learn, at least not to a workable level. Even a long-time, skilled, learn-by-ear players can find a lot of value in sight-reading…just as the dedicated sight-reader can get a lot from developing the ability to play by ear.

So yeah, Gobby, when I get a tune in ABC’s, I have to translate it into notation, and that’s where the flaw’s in ABC’s really stand out, before I can start to get it. (Oh, and yes it always helps to have a good recording from a solid player to learn from. I’d almost call it necessary.)

I can do the same with ABC notation. However, in my experience it’s utterly wrong to regard one’s own experience as universal; just because one person found musical notation easy to learn doesn’t mean everyone does. There are many different approaches to music: for many a visual representation is the way they initially connect with it, for others it’s aural. Just as dyslexics do not understand text or numbers the same way I do, some learners are perplexed by staff notation. Most people use a combination of visual, aural and kinaesthetic.

No offence intended to you, ross - but I read many posts here on thesession from people who are firm in their belief that there is only one right way of learning/playing/talking about music, and I am very firm in my belief that this is not the case.

Re: Musicians hear songs when they read music

Whether relative pitch or perfect pitch is more important is open for discussion; it’s tough to have a definitive answer. I think relative pitch would be more useful, but it’s hard to say for sure.

What I can say definitively is that just because you have perfect pitch does NOT mean that your relative pitch is in working order. Relative pitch is about hearing intervals, where perfect pitch is about distinguishing pitches. I can say this definitively because I do have perfect pitch and have always had perfect pitch, but my relative pitch was very bad until I was made to work on it in music school. That’s maybe another thing worth commenting on, that perfect pitch is an ability that is pretty much (though not entirely) either/or, like being able to distinguish colors, but relative pitch is a skill set that can be developed.

Also, perfect pitch cannot be turned off. For example, I can only play a D whistle, because if I play any other key, the music I hear myself making and the positions my fingers are in don’t line up, and it throws me off. And back to the original “is relative or perfect pitch better” question, I have no idea what it would be like to not have perfect pitch. You don’t learn you have perfect pitch, you learn that other people don’t, and it comes as a shock. I had learned not to bring it up by high school, because if you do, people will assume you think you’re better than them.

That’s not to say I don’t find it a fascinating topic, and I’d be happy to discuss it as far as anybody would like.

Would that sung note then be verifiable by playing a C5 note on the piano? I guess that latter example is just the perfect pitch test done in a slightly different way.

I don’t have perfect pitch (I’m about 3 semitones out either way when randomly tested), but I do have good relative pitch (I think).

I can identify many guitar chords by their sound. Eg, the basic ones : C-F- G, D-G-A, E-A-B, plus 7ths, augmented, diminished - which means that my sense of relative pitch is good (I think). What I can’t do with absolute certainty is identify their actual pitch (ie difference between a D shape open vs a D shape with capo on I). I’d expect any guitarist to be able to identify those previously mentioned chord shapes as a matter of basics (ie, normal). I’d be surprised if any could not.

On fiddle, it goes without saying that I can immediately identify any intervals within an octave range, and anything greater that that I’d likely have to pause momentarily to work it out aurally. I’d imagine that that would be a minimum requirement for any fiddle player (and of course there are many who are better than I).

I think that if someone had difficulty in differentiating intervals, then it would be really difficult to pick up a tune by ear. Does that follow, in your way of thinking?

So - (not to put anyone down) I think that relative pitch is very important indeed, whereas perfect pitch is simply a ‘nice to have’. I honestly can’t think of any scenario where it would be of real importance.
