Re: Copyrights of some nontrad tunes

Hey Marin,

I’ve gone through the copyright circus for three different albums. I see you’ve already looked up the authors of the tunes you want to record, which is a good start. There’s European law dictating how much you owe them in royalties when you record their stuff. The amount is calculated from your (theoretical) revenue (amount of CD’s printed times the price for which you intend to sell them), factored by the length of time their work is featured on the CD relative to its total duration. So say you have a CD with 60 minutes of music on it, and there’s a copyrighted tune on there which you play for 1 minute. In that case you owe them, for example, 1 % of the expected revenue of your CD (I don’t know the exact numbers by heart but it’s somewhere in that order of magnitude). If you print 500 CD’s and sell them for 10 euro each that would mean you owe that particular author 50 euros in royalties. If there’s another copyrighted tune on the CD that takes 2 minutes, you’d owe the author of that one another 100 euros.

Now, theoretically, the copyright-holder can choose to waive the royalties - for example, if he thinks you’re really nice and doesn’t really care about the money, as is the case with many trad musicians. I’ve had this happen a couple of times with authors I contacted and to whom I’m still grateful for that.

Unfortunately, many “big” musicians are under contract with this label or that one, meaning they no longer own the rights to their own compositions - the label does. In that case, you’d have to ask the label (a for-profit company) to waive their right to the royalties - which, as a good friend of mine would put it, has a snowball’s chance in hell of happening. In that case, you’ll either have to suck it up and pay them, or decide not to record that particular tune.

As for crediting people on the album cover, I’m not aware of any laws regarding that - it’s just common decency to do so. Giving credit where it’s due. But it doesn’t have anything to do with copyright or royalties.

Re: Copyrights of some nontrad tunes

The royalties you pay are worked out as Tijn outlines above but only on a percentage of the “Dealer Price”. Here in the UK it used to be 8.5% but may be different now. You can also decide to have some of your CDs as promo copies (they are supposed to be marked as such) for sending to magazines, radio stations and venues/festivals. You don’t pay any royalties on those.
Even if a copyright exists, you won’t pay any royalties unless the copyright holder is a member of a collection agency (MCPS in UK).
But if you are getting CDs pressed professionally, usually you need to get as licence before the company will do the pressing. You don’t need to know yourself which of your recorded works are copyrighted. The relevant copyright society to which you apply for a licence will have that information.

Re: Copyrights of some nontrad tunes

This is the nub of the matter: “Even if a copyright exists, you won’t pay any royalties unless the copyright holder is a member of a collection agency (MCPS in UK).”

My understanding is - and I’m sure someone more lawyerly will correct me if I’m wrong - that ‘copyright’ is automatic upon creation of a work. However, ‘copyright’ is almost meaningless without the will or the means to enforce it. In other words, if someone profits from your work without your permission, you can sue them, but it will be up to a judge to acknowledge - or not - your copyright, and compensation owed - or not. If you, as a creator, are concerned about your work being used without your receiving compensation, the sensible thing to do is to register it with a ‘collection agency’ (as above), which handles all the legal/financial bother. As a performer, you need to find out (do a search) if the work you want to record is registered with such an agency, and then do the paperwork and pay the (modest) fee for use of the work. In the case where a work is registered, direct permission from the creator is meaningless, other than as a courtesy. The creator may give you permission to record the work free of charge - but the agency will still bill you.

Re: Copyrights of some nontrad tunes

The legal background to copyright is very complicated, but the practicalities are usually pretty straight forward. As someone has mentioned things differ a bit from country to country, but as far as I can see they are all broadly similar. Providing the songs/tunes have previously been recorded then you are recording a ‘cover version’ and don’t need to seek permission from the rights holder, just pay the appropriate fee to the collection agency (MCPS in the UK), who will issue a licence. To get the licence you have to tell MCPS who is manufacturing the CDs, and MCPS will send a copy of the licence directly to them authorizing the pressing. In the UK (and this is where things are probably different in different countries) there are two types of licence that you could use - a Limited Manufacture licence, which gives blanket cover to record anything, but restricts the number of copies you can press, and how you can sell them - direct sales only, not through retailers; and an AP2 licence, which is what most small record labels use, where you have to detail each track individually but it allows 3rd party retail sales of physical CDs (you still need a separate licence for download sites). Normally the only time you need to approach the rights holder directly for permission is if the music has never been previously recorded, or if you are making significant changes to the melody or lyrics, so that it is no longer a ‘cover version’.

I think the best advice if you are new to this is to find out who is the collection agency in your country and contact them by phone. Trying to work it out for yourself from their website is a nightmare, because everything is in obscure legal language, but if you talk to them they’ll happily guide you through the maze.

Re: Copyrights of some nontrad tunes

“Regardless, in my personal view - copyright, royalties and Irish trad tunes are anathema to each other. Nothing could be further from the spirit of the music.”

Why? I take it you’re not a professional musician? The lion’s share of any musician’s income is generated by copyright and the royalties system. Without it there would be no professional Irish musicians to inspire you and make recordings for you to learn tunes from. Without copyright and people having the opportunity to make money from their music the tradition would have died out years ago. Do you think Michael Coleman’s recordings would ever have been made if there was no financial incentive?

I do think grass roots musicians need to wise up about copyright. People don’t copyright their work to stop others using it, they do it to ensure the right people get the money. Even if you just play a bit in the local pub, that pub has to pay a licence to allow live music, and that licence money is up for grabs. In the UK a couple of hours live music generates about £8 in composer royalties. If you say “all my tunes are trad” the pub still has to pay the licence, but the money goes into the ‘general pot’, which is distributed amongst PRS members in proportion to what they are earning from attributable use. So Sony, Warner Brothers and Michael Jackson’s estate get the vast bulk of the money. If you’re not a PRS member you won’t get any of it. But if you join PRS, register all your tunes as “Trad, arranged by Kilcash” and submit playlists then the money comes back to you. Registering an arrangement doesn’t stop anyone else using the tune - they can register their own arrangement of the tune even if it is note for note identical to yours, but then when they play it they get the money, when you play it you get the money. Eight quid for an evening in a pub might be small beer, but that’s the bottom end of the scale, if you play a ticketed concert with 200 people paying £20 per head there is about £190 up for grabs, and for playing at a festival it can run into the thousands. Personally, I’d rather register my tunes and take that money rather than just saying “it’s all trad” and letting the money go to the fat cat corporations.

Re: Copyrights of some nontrad tunes

To the original poster:
Typically, there is a licence fee which you pay to a copyright collecting society when you make a recording, and I assume (?) that it (more or less?) covers the mechanical royalties which the composers get. The fee depends on things like medium (CD, LP etc.), duration, number of tracks, number of copies, price etc. Information which is required on the recording include composers, lyricists, arrangers, publishers - for each track title.

Who gets what money from where can be confusing.

In theory, you could record an album with nothing but new compositions, and that’s it. The composers won’t get anything more unless the song/tune is played live, on radio, streamed etc. - assuming everything is reported. Such coverage can also generate money to the musicians on the actual recording, but it’s different money (which come from the licence fees paid by the venue/radio station/hotel/gym/etc.).

If all this should work, the correct information on the album has to be included (and correct), and everyone has to be a member of the various societies order to get the right money.

In theory, if you are a solo musician and also the composer of everything on the album, you get mechanical royalties from the number of recordings printed, payment if you do gigs, and finally performance royalties both as a composer and a musician whenever the material is played.

The copyright system is just there to ensure that you buy it from them rather than stealing it.
