Re: Playing ITM from sheet music is like…

Don’t be deceived. Those “great” classical musicians are no more reading the dots than a good session player. Sophie Mutter probably memorized that violin concerto by her 3rd go-round playing it. Nobody gives a solo performance for an audience while actually reading the dots, they had the full piece committed to memory long before then.

There may be some tiny percentage of the human population whose brains are wired such that reading is more efficient than listening, but for the vast majority of the human race, it just ain’t so. Music is an auditory experience, and you process it most efficiently using the part of your brain designed for it - your auditory nervous system. To read dots requires a conversion step - from a visual abstraction to an auditory abstraction, and then to music. Some instrumentalists bypass this step - they go straight from a visual abstraction to a physical motion - “3rd course, 5th fret, 3rd finger, got it” but that’s not musicianship, that’s being a robot or a player-piano.

You can of course get good enough at reading music and instantly hearing it in your head. But the point is, to really know a piece of music you *must* hear it in your head. If your mental abstraction for a piece of music is to see a staff with dots scrolling by, you shouldn’t be a musician at all, you should have been a visual artist instead (painter, etc.) because your brain has too strong a visual center and it’s wasted on audio.

If you can sight-read a tune and be actively listening to yourself at the same time, you can learn OK from sheet music. That requires being such a good sight-reader that reading is as natural as breathing. Few trad players are ever drilled so well in sight-reading to get there (and it’s not necessarily worth the effort either). The best compromise (which I learned from Cait Reed) is to record yourself playing from sheet music, and then put the sheet music away and learn the tune from your recording.

I learned how to sight-read in public elementary school, and then being in choirs and orchestras kept me in extremely good practice. Sight-reading is particularly crucial for a classical singer, because you don’t have a physical instrument to give you reference points; you have to train that “perfect pitch” and know precisely what “A below middle C” means on your own vocal chords. I fought tooth‘n’nail when Cait told me I had to quit sight-reading tunes, but in the end she was right.

Re: Playing ITM from sheet music is like…

Well, you have to keep a clear goal in mind. The point is just to memorize the shape of the tune without having the sequence of notes dancing in your mind’s eye at the same time, and you *will* memorize faster if you don’t have that visual step in the way. After that, style is still a separate issue altogether.

There are plenty of reasons to disagree with this approach, sure. The idea that you can learn a basic melody and embellish it later isn’t necessarily sound. Alasdair Fraser might not approve; he hates the word “ornament” because it implies that something is an optional accessory, when in fact it may be essential to the tune.

WJ, I know the leader of a band that works a lot with set dancers. She keeps a little notebook with the first few bars of the tunes, and, most importantly, the number of bars in each tune. When playing for dancers it’s very important to make sure that the tunes are the right length and type, and run well together. You can’t afford big mistakes with dancers. A band leader will often liaise with the dance leader to make sure everything goes smoothly.
A fiddle teacher I know keeps a note of the tunes she deals with in a lesson or workshop, together with the first bar or two in ABC. This is to avoid future confusion, especially with tunes like “The Silver Spire” and “The Silver Spear”, and the numerous unnamed Paddy Fahy tunes.
I’ve never seen an “aide memoire” notebook used in a session.
Trevor
