Hi Eia--

How far you get depends on how hard you work at it. A good teacher--violin or fiddle--can help a beginner get a good start.

There are violin mutes available. Some are quite heavy, designed to make the violin very quiet. The local violin shop can help you get one. Or you can try clothespins (the wood-and-spring type) on the bridge for a cheap (but less effective) substitute.

Or, get an electric violin that makes almost no sound when it isn’t plugged in.

Re: Beginning Fiddle lessons soon

Ditto on the progress thing. It’s all up to you: your abilities, talent, and productive practices. Teacher a big +. Prior musical experience a big +.

My advice is to forget the mute. A mute is counterproductive to learning bow control. Which is everything with fiddle. Talk to your neighbors and tell them what you are doing. Be friendly. Find out what their bedtimes are and try to work around their sleep schedule if at all possible. Only use the mute as a last resort. Two years down the road you might be able to handle the mute with no ill effects, but as a newb it’ll slow your progress down.

Good luck!

Aaron

Hey there,

so having only been playing for just over a year, i still remember the very early days pretty well. First regarding the mute: there are several options. What you want is a practice mute made out of either rubber, metal, or ebony. it’s a big hunking thing that slips over the bridge and muffles the noise very effectively. For whatever reason though, when I started playing I found that hte mute quieted all my good sounds but still eft the horrible noises at full volume. This was an observation shared by many in my class as well. Somethign about my playing was also driving my cat crazy ( he was agressively pulling out his hair under his chin and was just losing hair at his temples, somehow).

I ended up making my first fiddle purchase an electric silent. It did the trick, though now I may end up selling it as after a year I feel ok imposing my practicing on my neighbors and my cat has adjusted. The sound on the electirc is just too…“clean”. If you can afford it though, the silent can be a real nice toy. You can practice with it pretty much anywhere and unless it is dead quiet or somone is only two feet a way they will not hear you.

As for practice…as folks have said, it can vary a lot. By the end of my first three months my repertoire included: Hot Cross Buns; Old Plank Road; A very simple version of Morrisons; and maybe one or two other simple tunes. None of them were anywhere near session speed or anything, but I definately felt like I’d learned something.

A year later, I’ve got about ten tunes that I am working on, a few of which I am working on litle details (getting particular ornamentations exactly how I want them) others I’m stuck on bigger detials (getting particular phrases to come out consistently at a speed above “funeral march”). The biggest thing I’ve noticed though is that my ear is really starting to take over: If I listen to a a tune obsessively for a couple of days, when I sit down to learn the left hand, i can sometimes get it figured out in the space of 10 minutes (depending on the complexity) and hten it’s jsut a matter of getting it into muscle memory and making it sound awesome…which never ends.

Ok this essay is long enough. I hope it’s been a little helpful.

Re: Beginning Fiddle lessons soon

I agree with Aaron. A practice mute will hurt you when learning bowing, since it changes everything about how the bow affects the sound of the instrument. If you learn on an instrument with a heavy practice mute, then you’ll have some re-learning to do when you take the mute off.

Three months isn’t much if you’ve never used a bow before. Don’t expect too much, it takes a while for most people to draw a good tone with a bow and cross strings smoothly etc. It’s easy to get discouraged with this instument in the beginning, so don’t worry too much if your progress seems slow at first. It’s like that for most people.

Re: Beginning Fiddle lessons soon

I’ll have been fiddling a year next month. Three months into this adventure, I could play ten tunes, and was playing at a session. Badly. As others have said, progress can be slow and frustrating, but I’d finding that learning as an adult has an advantage that I never experienced as a child playing piano: I notice every single milestone. Three months in, my intonation was still cr@p, my bowing was sloppy, and I could barely get through a 32 bar reel in ninety seconds…but I distinctly remember that it was around that time that I finally got the hang of bowing each string separately, without inadvertently taking an adjacent string along for the ride. And that kept me going for the next three months.

I also live in an apartment, and I own two mutes: a heavy mute, and a little orchestral mute. With respect to the anti-mute posters, I’d ignore them: you’re the one who’ll otherwise have to contend with your neighbours coming after you with pitchforks; they’re not. But you’ll need to practice unmuted, too, as the mute does affect the tone. That said, you can still judge your tone with the mute on: the mute will muffle your fiddle, but you’ll still be able to hear if your tone is even, if it’s scratchy, etc. (My mute has actually helped me in this regard: my fiddle teacher has to keep reminding me to apply more pressure while bowing, and with the mute, I have no choice BUT to apply a lot of pressure: I can barely hear myself otherwise.) Organizing your practice around your neighbours schedules rather than yours, IMO, will be more detrimental to your progress than practicing whenever you want, albeit with a muted instrument.

Re: Beginning Fiddle lessons soon

Re the strong fantasy world - yes, absolutely. When I told people I was taking up the fiddle, I found that nearly everyone had a very strong opinion about the Single Most Important Factor in becoming proficient in playing the fiddle. In order to be successful, someone told me, you needed a good ear; absent that, you’d never get anywhere. Another told me that it was coordination that was the most important thing. A particularly ignorant acquaintance told me authoritatively that my long, thin fingers guaranteed me a bright musical career, and that the reason HE’D never gotten anywhere on the violin was because his fingers are shorter than average.

Rubbish, all of it. The Single Most Important Factor in learning to play fiddle is *tenacity*. You have to be able to practice and practice for hours, sound absolutely horrid, and then go back and practice some more. Ears can be trained, coordination can be developed, and anatomical deficiencies can be worked around. I record myself weekly, and the other day pulled out a tape I’d made of myself last August, two months after I took home my cheap student fiddle. My playing back then was positively wretched, and I knew it at the time, but I still remember the day I made that recording: excited about how far I’d come in the past two months, but even more excited about how far I knew I’d be going in the next many years.

Heh, I’ve survived people telling me that I’d started fiddle too late to ever be good at it (heard that in my teens and again in my 20s), that if I couldn’t sing in perfect pitch I’d never be able to play fiddle, and that I might as well give up if I wasn’t going to practice a minimum of four hours every day for the rest of my life. My point is that the “truth” for most of us lies somewhere in between--a very happy middle ground of music joyfully played among friends and neighbors, and a lifelong passion for an instrument’s particular voice and the tunes it brings to life.

So -- no matter how awful it sounds today or tomorrow or a year from now, no matter how untalented you feel, no matter how many times you land flat on the high 1st string B or smudge a bowed triplet like a moth under your thumb or feel like your ears are full of peanut butter, ***just keep playing.*** You will improve, and you will enjoy making music. And when it all starts to click, you won’t be able to stop smiling.

Yeah, and it’s worth highlighting the “you will improve” aspect of learning to play fiddle, since the “it’s slow going” part so often tends to dominate. The main reason I started playing last summer, instead of twenty summers ago, is that I’d been frightened away from the instrument by people who’d told me that it was too hard to learn to play decently. (My mother among them - which was a big part of why after eight or nine years of lessons, I was playing piano with only slightly more skill than I’ve acquired on fiddle in a tenth that amount of time. Play the instrument that calls to you, and the rest will come. I had to be forced to practice piano, but now I suffer withdrawal when I go more than a few hours without picking up my fiddle.)

Anyway, as a result, a year ago, I had this notion that “eventually” I’d become a halfway decent fiddler, but that it would be ages and ages before I would be able to do *anything*. Honestly, I must have been a hell of a masochist to have taken up an instrument full well *expecting* that a year in, I’d barely be able to play an open string without dropping both fiddle and bow. Of course, I can do far more than that at this stage, and it’s the little improvements that keep me hurrying to take my instrument out of its case first thing in the morning, and first thing when I get home from work.

[Cheshire, your comment about the silliness you get these days made me smile: a few months ago, I somehow got roped into performing (actual performing, not playing at a session) at an art show. Afterwards, someone told me, unironically, that I was really good, and had I been playing since I was a kid? I replied that I’d been playing for less than five months, and although I’ll grant that I was quite good for someone who’d been playing for five months, absolutely *no one* is an objectively good fiddler after that amount of time. Incidentally, I get these sorts of outlandish compliments from the very sorts of people who’ve never played because “the violin is so hard”. Ignorance about what’s involved in learning to play spans the whole spectrum, I’ve found; folks who think that fiddling is rocket science are, for the most part, so disconnected from music that they’re also completely unable to discriminate middling playing from a virtuoso performance.]

Re: Beginning Fiddle lessons soon

I suspect that an important reason why some beginners (who may be young children, or perhaps adults) can make startling progress on the fiddle or other musical instrument is that no one told them it was supposed to be “difficult”. To my mind, the use of that “d” word to a beginner immediately sets up a mental barrier or block which impedes progress. I think this applies to any discipline that is being learned, whether it is a musical instrument, a language, art, writing, or a science, for example. This is not to say that acquiring proficiency is going to be quick - it won’t be, for there is necessary time, work and application, and the right sort of teaching involved in setting up motor skills and learning things, and this can’t be hurried, but it is not inherently “difficult”.

Further, I think part of the problem is that there is today a pervasive culture in which a desire to acquire a skill or qualification must be satisfied immediately, otherwise time and effort must be expended and the skill or qualification therefore becomes too “difficult” to acquire. One consequence of this attitude is the current official expectation that 50% of UK school-leavers should get an university degree - just like that - but this isn’t really the right forum to explore that particular topic.

Re: Beginning Fiddle lessons soon

I agree, lazyhound, but I think the problem with the fiddle is not just the myth that it’s “hard” and requires “talent,” but that it’s so much harder to see progress in the beginning versus other instruments.

For example, the piano is certainly no easier to master than the fiddle, but anyone can slam a finger onto the key of a piano and hear a satisfying note. With the fiddle, you have to struggle with a lot of unpleasant noise before nice notes come out, and I think that’s what discourages most people.

I think that’s also where the myth of the fiddle being a “difficult” instument comes from. Anyone starting out--of any age--is going to sound absolutely horrible on the fiddle, and not enough realize that that’s normal.

Re: Beginning Fiddle lessons soon

Well, the Wohlfarht book may offer boring exercises, but it gets full marks for a name that’s just fun to say in front of anyone who blushes easily….

I’ve taught lots of kids and adults to play music over the years, and I’ve noticed two things. (1) Both are likely to know what instrument calls to them, but kids are less likely to be dissuaded by (or even consider) wholly rational, practical reasons for ignoring their gut and learning some other instrument instead. Adults too often date a series of instruments before finally settling down with the one they wanted in the first place. And (2) most kids are less intimidated by the unfamiliar. Many adults tend to label anything unfamiliar as “difficult.” That’s only natural--as kids, we all run into new things, concepts, etc. every day. But as we age and gain experience, we grow familiar with many, many things, concepts, etc. So by the time we’re wise old 30 somethings (*snicker*), we’ve already bumped into all the low hanging fruit. Anything strange or new must therefore be “difficult” or we would’ve mastered it long ago.

My answer to Eia’s question in the opening post is that s/he will be exactly three months ahead of where s/he would be if s/he doesn’t start playing the fiddle now. Or, more accurately, however many hours of actual playing ahead of wherever s/he’s starting from. Which is all any of us can expect, given that we learn by doing, and we all learn at different rates, coming from different prvious experiences and abilities.

The good news is that it’s a lot of fun.
