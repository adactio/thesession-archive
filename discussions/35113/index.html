Re: Just-tempered instruments in ITM?

Just intonation isn’t really to be regarded as a “temperament”. That word is usually applied to fine- tunings away from JI. The big advantage of JI is that chords sound sweet, without “beats”. there are disadvantages to the modern ear, however. Playing in different keys/modes on a JI-tuned instrument means that different notes may sound out of tune, according to the key. Also, playing with JI with instruments tuned differently (which may be fixed-pitch, so the poor devils can’t do anything about it) can grate. Lots of attempts to obtain a compromise tuning have been made down the centuries. 12-tone equal temperament is just one such, though it’s the one that’s caught on most. Diatonic harmonicas come in several different fine-tunings; some are tuned to 12tET, a very few come in JI and quite a few are tuned to something (not all the same something) in between. As my penchant down the years has been to play melodies rather than chords I decided a long time ago to fine-tune all my harps to equal temperament. This is because I usually find myself playing with people using fixed-pitch instruments in that tuning. If I use a non-ET harp in that setting I can hear my out-of-tuneness with them all too clearly. Oddly, it doesn’t seem to show up anywhere near as much on recordings I’ve made. Another point is that cheapie tuners are generally only set up to tell you you’re “in tune” with equal temperament.

Re: Just-tempered instruments in ITM?

Yes as Steve points out “just tempered” isn’t a term used by anybody because it’s self-contradictory.

“Just” and “tempered” are two opposing approaches to tuning.

The term you’re thinking of is “Just Intonation” (often JI for short). It’s an ancient traditional way of tuning found in many folk musics around the world. Pythagoras wrote about it thousands of years ago, and people speak of the Pythagorean scale. It’s the way acappella choirs and brass ensembles tune themselves today. It’s the way Scottish bagpipes and uilleann pipes and Bulgarian pipes and Spanish pipes and pretty much any bagpipes are tuned, because every note of the bagpipe scale is heard over a fixed drone. (The idea that Scottish pipes have a unique scale, or that they play a “Middle Eastern” scale, is simply not true.)

All it means is that every note is “in tune”, that is, a beatless consonance, with the fundamental. It’s not opinion but physics: the pitches of the scale are dictated by the laws of acoustics which nobody can change. If you can hear beats when two pitches are sounded simultaneously then it’s not “in tune” because the interval is deviating from the intervals dictated by natural physical laws.

The problem with JI is that it only produces an in-tune scale for ONE fundamental note. And in Bach’s time it started becoming the fashion to modulate to different keys, to use the same keyboard to play in multiple keys without re-tuning it. Therefore various “tempered” scales came into being. Bach’s “Well-Tempered Clavier” was written to demonstrate the flexibility of an instrument tuned to a “tempered” (that is, altered from JI) scale. Eventually “Equal Temperament” arrived, which sounded horribly out of tune to 18th century ears. It was attacked saying “it’s Equal only in that every key is equally out of tune” which is true, and the very point of it. Most intervals in ET are fairly close to JI and our modern ears usually accept its out-of-tuneness, but the Major 3rd, Major 6th, and minor 7th are particularly bad.

I stand by what I said regarding Highland Pipes’ scale being unique (although the subject is complicated in that over recent decades the tuning appears to be changing). When I started out fifty-odd years ago, the low G and High G were not in tune with each other, the D was quite sharp, and the high A flat. “…in 1954, J. Lenihan and Seumas MacNeill published a study of the tuning of 18 pipe chanters in which they concluded that Ds were tuned to 27:20, and the high G to 9:5. These values are sharper than the simple Just values, although since they are whole-number ratios they are, strictly speaking, just. A scale formed in this way has unique and interesting melodic properties, particularly when one considers the various pentatonic scales which can be derived from it (Piping Times, October 1997).”

@David50 -- It is indeed common practise to have the high ‘A’ slightly flat, often with what is called a ‘groggle’ or croak. Personally I have never liked doing this; perhaps because I play other instruments. I find it more satisfying for the high A to ‘disappear’ into the drones, which, presumably, is the reason for having it flat in the first place, ie to keep it audible.
One of the most desirable attributes of a reed, either drone or chanter, is stability. In the Highland pipes, the pressure remains constant; but the humidity and temperature affect pitch. As the pipes warm up, they get sharper; then when they reach their optimum conditions after a few minutes, the best reeds (and players) will stay in pitch. It is possible to change pitch slightly by varying the pressure; but it is greatly undesirable, not least because the different reeds change pitch by different amounts.
In the Uilleann and other octave-jumping pipes, stability is needed to counteract the tendency to increase pitch as the pressure increases. The drones should stay constant throughout both octaves, which can cover quite a large range of pressure differential from low to high. Changes in pressure produce undesirable effects, such as dropping the octave, wavering drones, and regulators going out of tune with drones and chanter.

Compensating guitar bridges are not the same kind of compromise that ET is. The guitar frets are still logarithmically equal, which makes them ET. Of course the bridge position is important. The fret positions are based on the scale length. If you alter the scale length, the ratio is altered with it. The effect of pressing a string (regarding thickness, tension etc) differs depending on which fret you are holding it down on, thus bridge compensation can only go so far. The musician might make further compromises in tuning to achieve the balance suited to the music being played/personal taste.

You haven’t really got away from ET - each fret still shortens the string or increases the frequency by exactly the same ratio. But what you have done is to stretch (or compress, depending on which way you move the bridge) the octave - as you move up the fret board you still have twelve equal sized semitones, but the twelfth fret is no longer exactly an octave above the open string.

The reason that bridges on steel string instruments have to be compensated, and the string length made slightly longer than is theoretically correct is only partially due to string stretch, but mainly due to inharmonicity of the string - because the string isn’t completely flexible, the vibrations actually start a little way from the saddle, giving a vibrating length slightly shorter than the actual string length. The same thing causes problems in piano tuning (and piano strings are never fretted/stretched): because of inharmonicity of the strings the second harmonic is slighly sharp of an octave, and to make the piano sound in tune the octave has to be stretched - on a small piano C3 and C4 are typically about an octave +35 cents apart. With nylon or gut strung instruments, because the strings are much more flexible there is no inharmonicity, and no need for the angled, compensated bridge.

>>“For those who insist that the guitar, once compensated, is brought into line exactly with ET, there is a fairly straightforward question to ask yourself:”

Both of them do. If you look closely at the Martin saddle you will see that although it is a one piece saddle, it is filed so that the second string comes off the back of the saddle, the third comes off the front. The end result is string lengths that match those of the Lowden.

Compensation has absolutely nothing to do with temperament. It is nothing to do with the ‘out of tune-ness’ between the second and third strings, it is because of the change from plain to overwound strings. The core of the wound 3rd string is thinner than the plain 2nd, so the string is more flexible, has less inharmonicity, and therefore needs less compensation. If you use a plain 3rd string the step in the saddle moves across to between the 3rd and 4th strings, but your tuning problem remains between the 2nd and 3rd.

The tuning problem isn’t anything to do with compensation, it is down to the deficiencies of equal temperament. Assuming the compensation is correct, if you tune across the frets (or using an electronic tuner), the guitar will be perfectly tuned in ET, the 1st string will be exactly 2 octaves above the 6th, and every note on every string will be perfectly in tune with a piano or other ET instrument. Between all the other strings you have 4th, and the ET 4th is almost exactly the pythagorean 4th - if it is perfectly tuned in ET the two strings will beat at less than 1/2Hz. But between the 3rd and 2nd strings you have a major 3rd, and the ET major third is a long way from pythogorean - correctly tuned it will beat at about 3 Hz, which sounds out of tune. Play the same notes on any other ET instrument (an electronic keyboard with a flute voice is easiest to hear) and you’ll hear the same out of tune beating. If you fret the 2nd string at the 1st or 3rd fret to produce a perfect 4th or 5th with the 3rd string, it will sound in tune.

So if you are picking rather than strumming, and particularly if you play with keyboards or other ET instruments, then sticking to exact ET will give the best tuning over the whole fretboard, even though the open strings sound slightly off.

If you are mostly strumming and only playing with other guitars or alone, then you might want to ‘sweeten’ the tuning and get rid of the beat between the open 2nd and 3rd strings. In this case the guitar is still equal tempered - you haven’t moved the frets or re-tempered the scale in any way, its just that the strings are no longer in tune, and if you play octaves or 5ths across strings you will hear them beat as they are no longer perfect.

Re: Just-tempered instruments in ITM?

That’s exactly right khasab. ET is a set of frequencies, not a set of stringlengths as weejie seems to think. And compensation is used to allow for inharmonicity (an imperfection in the relationship between string length and frequency) to give you exactly the ET frequencies when the string is fretted.

The effect of string stretch is negligible, a bit of simple trigonometry will tell you that on a 625mm string length with a 3.5mm action height the stretch when you fret at the 12th is less than 1/25mm - less than the tolerances guitars are made to, and less than a guitar expands and contracts with changes in the weather, and giving rise to a maximum tuning error of 0.25%. However, since the stretch increases linearly as you move up the fingerboard (providing the nut height is correct) it has the same effect as inharmonicity, and will be compensated when you adjust the saddle. If the saddle is adjusted so that the string plays a perfect octave at the 12th fret, then every other fret will be perfectly tuned to ET. If the compensation is wrong, then the 12th fret won’t be a perfect octave, but the octave will still be divided into 12 equal semitones, which is the definition of ET.

Sweetened tunings are more problematic. If the strings are tuned across the frets or to a tuner, then every note will be exactly the same pitch regardless of where on the fretboard you play it. Intervals will also sound exactly the same regardless of where you play them - 8ves, 5ths and 4ths will sound harmonious anywhere on the fretboard, major 3rds will sound ‘off’ everywhere. If you sweeten the tuning by tuning to harmonics or whatever, then a particular note will be at a slightly diifferent frequency, depending on which string you play it on. Intervals will be more or less in tune depending on where on the fretboard you play them. That can be used to advantage to make specific chords and keys sound better, at the expense of making others (that you maybe don’t use much) sound worse. But it doesn’t take you out of equal temperament. If you have six pianos, each perfectly in tune with itself but all at slightlt different pitches, they don’t suddenly stop being tuned to ET if you play them together, and that is the situation will sweetened guitar tunings - you effectively have six different perfect ET scales.

Even Brian Capleton (I don’t think he’s online any more), who places a great emphasis on inharmonicity, (him being primarily a piano man) recognised that the inharmonicity varied between various stopped positions on a string, and that simply adjusting by compensating at the bridge saddle was not sufficient. He devised a tuning method in which all the “anomalies” were taken into consideration and a very even tuning was achieved- BUT - he also acknowledged that this was a compromise, and absolute even tuning was not achieved.

“You can calculate string stretch using simple trigonometry, and convert to tension using Hooke’s law. From that you can calculate the compensation needed. You’ll find it comes to about 0.0248 mm on a 625mm scale length (regardless of string guage). ”

You can take the trouble to look at compensation in real life terms. Trig doesn’t cut it. Read up on research made long ago by Mercadier and Alessi etc.
Inharmonicity is affected by string length, thickness and other factors, including the very materials and structure of the instrument - even the method of anchoring the strings. Moreover, it varies at different stopped portions of a string. Simply lengthening the string length at the bridge saddle is not going to completely eradicate problems “across the board”.

“If you don’t want to do the maths, you can prove it to yourself very easily just by looking at a Stratocaster or something with absolutely minimal action height, and therefore minimal stretch - according to your theory it should need minimal compensation. And yet, if you measure it, if it is set up correctly you’ll find the compensation is almost exactly the same as it is on an acoustic with high action (given the same string gauges).”

Perhaps you should try that yourself - and try raising the action on a Strat. See how it fares.

It don’t correspond mate.

“BTW Equal temperament (and even the more specific 12TET ) is defined by the relationship of the frequency of one note to it’s predecessor - providing that ratio remains constant it is ET, regardless of whether the octave exactly coincides with a pythagorean octave or not.”

Believe that if you want to, but the octaves remaining perfect is a fundamental part of the equation - for a harp player, at least. I had to learn how to tune one of those things by ear - and then unlearn it to play along with others.

For others, there have been deviations from the preservation of perfect octaves. See Mieczyslaw Kolinski : A New Equidistant 12-Tone Temperament ; Journal of the American Musicological Society
Vol. 12, No. 2/3 (Summer - Autumn, 1959), pp. 210-214. You’ll find it online, and you’ll be able to read it if you want to pay for it - or you are able to log in as someone who studies such things (and acknowledges the value of peer reviewed papers instead of relying on static, and often flawed formulae). Google can be your friend.

However, Kolinski’s 12 note temperament is not the one that the standard fret spacing of guitars is based on. It’s one that is adopted by some - many, in fact, piano tuners.
It’s all a compromise, buddy. Get used to it, and don’t pretend it’s anything different.
