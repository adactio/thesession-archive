Three scales, what order ? (Whistle and Recorder)

https://i.ibb.co/BcNNjCx/20230710-192452.jpg

All the three in the above picture play the same scale as marked (as well as with all those chosen toneholes open, as well as with only top marked tonehole open ), and without using blanked out toneholes (closed top, open lower) . They play second octave as so also by overblowing (helped on recorder by lifting thumbhole but not completely necessary)

I am trying to figure out the order they appeared in. For example whistles were around before recorders, but the scale now used might have come from recorders, or not.

Order 1, 2, 3 is whistle extended to include a further note using little finger for early recorder (other blanked holes don’t matter here) , and finally a folk (German key) recorder shifts the scale down by changing an interval by making a tonehole smaller.

Order 1, 3, 2, 3, would be where a whistle scale gives the idea to an intemediary unknown recorder scale looking like 3 (and now ‘reproduced’ in this modern one) , which was then taken up by the early recorder in the format shown (with a key shift by moving the scale and widening the smaller tonehole) , which then had the tonehole in question returned to original size to be a “folky” number 3.

Order 2, 1, 3 is where early recorders provide a scale for the whistles and the modern folk recorder decides it is better using all the lower toneholes for that scale and so tweaks one tonehole size of early recorders.

There are other possibilities, including what music, scales or keys were being used, that might have affected the choice. The English or later baroque fingerings used by most recorders nowadays don’t really come into this, they are their own path.

Anyway, maybe someone knows the answer, or for those who don’t mind getting utterly confused about something.

At the least, it helps explain without explaining, apparent similarities between whistles and some recorders.

Re: Three scales, what order ? (Whistle)

Rather than thinking about them as some sort of logical progression, it’s better to think of them as a family of instruments that are all designed to do different things. You can find a decent list of well-known fipple flutes up on wiki:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fipple

What you’ll notice if you click through to them is that all operate under similar principles, and some even have similar or the same fingerings, but they are not some kind of movement from one to the other. Instead, different musics take the basic concept of the fipple flute and adapt them to what that particular music needs.

I was happy to use the continental term as a young teenager, I thought telling people I was doing my flageoletion practice was hilarious. (Sadly it never convinced anybody else I was a comic genius.)

But the 15th century was the period where English had only recently become established as the official court language. Obviously ordinary people spoke English, but the history of French being the language of the court and the law may have meant it was regarded differently then, or had a different presence in society. And anyway, several centuries on, none of us bats an eyelid at using all the words and phrases we’ve borrowed from other languages. It seems unlikely that flageolet being a French-derived word jarred with anyone back then, any more than violin being an Italian one does now.

I’m sure you’re right about whistles always being present. One of my questions, if I could ask it of a 15th century whistler, would be about people’s attitude to accurate (or otherwise) pitch. They could hear it as acutely as we can, but where they as concerned about it? Would playing along in a group on a poorly-tuned instrument make you a bit of a pariah, or get grumbled about behind your back, or was it normal for there to be variation? If wind instruments weren’t made to a standard pitch in the way they are now, did people compensate by playing differently?

Seperatio: etymology would suggest that “chwyth” is onomatopoeic (based on PIE and Ancient Greek), whereas “anadl” is simply the act of breathing: compare, e.g., Swedish “andas” (to breathe).

Like Creadur says, the latter leads to concepts like “soul”. This is analogous to Russian “душа” [dushá], which actually means both “soul” and “breath” today.

Slightly more on topic, playing in a session yesterday, a tourist [from a Spanish-speaking country] asked me about the instrument I was playing (the pipes), and specified “not the flute” (I also play the whistle). I suppose most languages [in Europe] would call a similar type of instrument by a “flute” derived name, with “whistle” being a marked version only present in English. “Recorder” is even more marked, and a bit weird. Being bilingual between English and a North European language, I find I alternate between calling the [flageolet, tin whistle, penny whistle, Irish whistle] a “whistle” and a “flute”-derived object, depending on mood and knowledge of the receiver.
