Re: Horrible bow shakes

I agree that more exposure to audiences is probably the only way to feel more confident in front of them - simply because it makes their presence seem less imposing, less like a big event where all eyes (and ears) are on you! I’m still one for stagefright and feeling frustrated that I rarely achieve my best playing when listeners are present, but it certainly seems more likely that confronting audiences, rather than avoiding them, is the logical solution.

Another thing I’ve realised is that so often when I know that people can hear my playing - not an audience as such, but for example someone in another room of my house - I often play in a non-committal way because at least then when I make mistakes etc. I can excuse myself on account of the fact that I ‘wasn’t really trying’ anyway. And I’ve realised that this isn’t a good attitude, because all it achieves is sloppy playing and musicianship, and anyone who is listening is given a false impression of your skills, and indeed, the music! No one wins, it’s a complete lose-lose situation. So nowadays I try to play my best, even when simply within earshot of a few family members and I’m just working on a familiar tune or set, and that simple change of attitude - ‘I’m not going to pretend to be casually noodling around, I want to make real music’ - can be enough to give me the focus to actually play more confidently, and everybody wins :) I feel better about my playing, and anyone listening is going to hear something more satisfying.

Getting back to the original issue, I’ve done quite a bit of solo busking in the past few summers, and I’d recommend it. You could start with a friend if that sounds absolutely awful nerves-wise, since having someone familiar there can really take the edge off the nerves, but it’s good to wean yourself off that and take the plunge by going solo. I’m not the ‘look at me, look at me!’ type at all, but paradoxically I do enjoy performing and want to get better at playing for listening audiences. Busking can be a good bit of training in that regard because although it may seem far scarier in theory, the fact is that most people walk by, with only a few stopping to listen, and so it doesn’t quite have that sense of pressure that you get from a hushed, expectant audience. In that way, once I’d get past the initial nerves - and I’d have a bit of that every time I went out there - I’d end up feeling very comfortable playing in the street, and every little helps create confidence! In time I’m hoping it’ll just feel natural to play, and play my best, in front of anyone in any situation.

Playing the fiddle has to become an integral part of you - also in the situations you describe above. When playing in front of people is as natural as breathing in front of them then you certainly know you are on your way… the key question is how to get there without too many detours.

If you have the curiosity and patience I suggest having a go at reading “Zen in the art of Archery”. The wisdom and keys to useful habits found in this book can easily be adapted to the art of violin playing. I had a drop-in class with a violin teacher who recommended this book to me - and it changed my perspective to many things in addition helping me out to get rid of unwanted self-consciousness while playing.

One last point though: If you can positively confirm to yourself that it is you who should be playing this piece in the given situation, then you have nothing to be afraid of. In some situations 8 years may come short of the cited 21 years to mastery…

Re: Horrible bow shakes

I went through a journey like this one. Eventually, somehow, I figured out that the audience’s experience seemed to have nothing to do with how well I thought I played. I could think I played better than I’ve played in my life, and no one would notice, or I could have thought I screwed it up beyond recognition and the audience loved it.

Once I internalized that, I got over stage fright. That is one of the things that comes to do with experience.

I would also recommend seeking out ways to play in low-pressure settings. With friends, etc. Jazz or bluegrass are both great for this… everyone takes a turn at a solo or a break. Keep it light and fun, and don’t worry if you mess up. It’s what it’s for. You can even mess up on purpose, for laughs. But whatever you do play, play it with confidence. Like you own it.

The other thing that helps with bow control is lay off the caffeine before the show. If I drink too much caffeine my bow is still a little jumpy, because the caffeine jitters transmits itself to the bow hand and the effect is magnified.

You might also try playing with a bit more bow pressure throughout, though the material your playing may put a limit on that. Playing with a touch more forefinger over the bow may help. You don’t necessarily have to go Heifetz with the Russian bow-hold, but experiment and see where that takes you.

Lastly, the time to be a perfectionist is in the practice room. Not onstage. When you’re onstage, you should already be confident in your preparation. Go up there knowing you’re absolutely ready, and you are the best player in the world for what you are about to do. Walk up there like you own it. Like you were born to be on that stage. Be supremely arrogant about it Like you’re queen of the world. Which you are, for your solo part of it, anyway!

Re: Horrible bow shakes

The audience don’t care about you, particularly -- you are merely a vehicle for the music.
Before you go on, imagine what you would be thinking if you were in the audience watching someone else walk on stage -- one of many who come and go during the course of the evening.
You are ‘special’ only from your own internal view-point . To everyone else, you are exactly like all the other people that you see around you: just another person.
In other words, the audience is not sitting there watching *you*, waiting for *you* to make a mistake, or poke fun at or ridicule *you*. As far as everyone else is concerned, you are just another human being.
Don’t think about your *self*, the individual. Instead, bear in mind that the people have come for the music. You are merely the one holding the tray. You are the glass case in which the exhibit is being presented. It is the contents the audience is interested in, not you.

After over 50 years playing in public I still get nervous. Tommy Peoples still gets the shakes. Almost all musicians get nervous before performing. If they don’t the music can be flat and uninspiring. I find that loosening the bow hair helps to calm a bouncing bow, and also helps me to be less tense. I try to channel the nervousness into energy that I can release through the music.
Keep in mind that the audience is on your side and wants you to do well. Listeners are forgiving, especially so when they see that you are nervous.
Good luck with it all. I’m sure you’ll be fine.

Phoeb, this sort of problem is difficult to analyze and resolve on an internet forum and really needs face-to-face with a teacher to resolve. I expect the teacher would first look at the basic mechanics of posture, bow hold and general relaxation. One method which works for some : the hand usually shakes most when it’s not doing much, so we get it to do a lot of work in long full bows, on a fairly quick stroke both ways. Then, gradually slow down the bow speed, until it’s really slow.

Re: Horrible bow shakes

Hello Phoebe,
Thank you for being brave enough to ask. It takes courage to play.

Stand comfortably, with relaxed legs, relaxed stomach, relaxed shoulders, especially relaxed arms. No tensions.
When you are comfortable and relaxed, you will play musically. If not, not.
Play by feel, not by looks.

Arm tensions can come from holding the arms too high. Too many muscles involved.
I recommend “A New Approach to Violin Playing” by Kato’ Havas. KatoHavas.com

Shaking comes from the body wanting to relax so you can play your best, but your mind keeps insisting on holding the body tensely.
Holding yourself tensely comes from misdirected teaching and/or need for personal safety.
Be sure you always practice and play as relaxed as possible.
Then there will be no tensions, and no disagreement between “relax; hold on; relax; hold on” which results in shaking.

You cannot both give and take at the same time. So always give the music.
It’s not about you, it’s about the music.
Fortunately, the audience doesn’t care about you. They care about the music.
Your audience is rooting for you, so they can hear the music.
So get yourself out of the way of the music.

Practice playing. You must practice playing the whole as much as you practice bits.
Play for people as much as possible.
Play on the street, for friends, family, strangers, schoolmates.
When you don’t have someone to play for, record yourself (preferably video), and be sure to listen/watch the results. You will notice where you are tense, so you can relax those areas. You will notice where in the music you need to practice.

Play and play, having fun all the time. Then you will really know the music, so you will not be nervous because of not ‘being prepared’. And you will not be nervous because of ‘having an audience’.

Have fun! Send us a video link!
