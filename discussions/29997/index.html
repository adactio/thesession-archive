In a session, one of the things that registers on the aural radar is the ongoing overall sense of pitch. When there are fixed-pitch instruments playing, people gravitate toward that tuning. When no fixed-pitch instruments are playing, it tends to be a more fluid, dynamic affair.

Intonation is just one part of the overall sound wave, though. Making good music usually hinges on surfing the whole wave, not just one ripple within it.

I had a piano instructor who played a piano piece but he moved one of his hands to play everything a step off of the other. He played confidently as if he was just playing the tune normally. It was amazing how consonant it sounded when the player doesn’t flinch or fall out of rhythm.

Anyway, I got to a point in my fiddle playing where I stopped really worrying about intonation unless I was playing fourth finger notes or double stops. Even then there really is a point when you have to just do your best and not sweat the small stuff. It’s not really useful to stop in the middle of a tune and line up a note everytime it sounds slightly off. But when you’re done with the tune, check your strings, check your finger positions. If it’s all good, then try the phrase from the tune and try to find out why it sounded off. It’s likely a stretching issue, or perhaps over stretching if you percieve the finger position to be a farther stretch than it really is.

The intonation of a mixed group needs to be a bit flexible (obviously) where there are fixed and variable pitched instruments.

Sometimes a simple rolling 5th on the fiddle, in D or G, works fine. Other times, a loud D or A from the melodeon is enough to get everyone in tune… assuming the tremolo is switched off :)

I guess you don’t really think about intonation that much .. till you turn up at a session where they all tune to each other as they go round the circle, and everyone’s incrementally a few microtones out from the previous one :)

When I’m practicing fiddle at home, I sometime play along with a digital tanpura drone machine. The drones can be set to a tonic note, or tonic+4th below, like some pipe drones.

Regardless of what you play, playing along with those low octaves and 4ths really highlights any intonation errors and gives you a kick up the arse when you need it.

Not the kind of accuracy you need for session work, but occasional discipline does no harm :)

Re: Intonation

The most difficult intonation problem for a violinist is to play exactly in tune with another violinist. Not quite so important perhaps for folk music in general but it certainly is for classical. It’s slightly easier for three violins, and easier still for four, when a bum note from one player isn’t quite so obvious. Which is one reason why orchestras like to have a line-up of at least 8 violins in a section. The difference in overall tone quality of 8 violins in unison (a very slight ‘fuzziness’ for want of a better word) compared with that of a solo violin playing a separate line enables the soloist to stand out more easily.

Re: Intonation

Heh, I was just going to post something in reply to AQ’s “casual” comment, but Ben beat me to it, and with my own words to boot.

AQ, if you listen to Willie Clancy or Paddy Canny, you may notice notes that sound “off pitch,” particularly sharped c naturals not sharped enough to be c sharps. That can bother ears accustomed to conventional Western music intonation. When you realize that Clancy and Canny (and others) are completely consistent with them, and are spot on nailing other “standard” pitches, it’s worth asking why some notes are consistently, intentionally “sweetened.” What qualities does that give to the music? A brightness? A bit of lift?

Sean Smyth once spent a fair amount of time in a workshop going over the distinction between c natural, sweet c, and c sharp, saying repeatedly that us fiddlers should be clear about which one we mean to play--use them intentionally, not as an excuse for sloppy (or “casual”) intonation. I agree.

↳ Posted by Will Harmon on June 16th, 2012.

Re: Intonation

“The simplest way I can think of saying this is: The sweetened minor third and other tones common to this music may sound (at worst) ”off“ or (at best) ”beguilingly discordant“ within the standard western system of scales and modes. But these same tones are neither ”off“ nor ”beguiling“ within the scales and modes of Irish music. They just are.”

In case you hadn’t noticed, there have been *hundreds* of different intonation systems used for Western art music, which embrace just about every imaginable possibility. (Listen to Elgar’s own orchestral recordings for a pretty far-out example). And if you think there is just *one* definitive tuning system used by *all* Irish players which could justify such a categorical statement, I’d like to see your evidence.

The only study I know of where anybody tried to measure objectively what the tuning systems used by traditional fiddlers actually were is Peter Cooke’s studies of Shetland fiddling. (He used a Stroboconn tuner, which is still the most accurate tool for the job). He found that there simply wasn’t any system to it, and what players said they were doing bore almost no resemblance to what they were actually playing. No precisely definable modal intonation scheme existed.

FWIW, you get the same sort of result in other genres: classical violinists don’t do much better, and Signell’s measurements of Turkish classical musicians (also using a Stroboconn) showed they were nowhere near their theoretical pitches a lot of the time either.

The idea that there is is some kind of secret lore of traditional intonation that classically trained musicians can’t hope to penetrate is just pseudo-religious crap.
