Re: Tin Whistle - hole size?

I’ve been whistling for about a year now. In my experience (I now have 10 whistles), hole size is fairly standard across a given key. Different material tubes might require different sized holes (eg wood), but usually hole size isn’t something I concern myself with as far as comfort goes.

You said you are playing a Low D? Be careful not to fall into the trap of only playing low whistles! I started out on one of Nick Metcalf’s ~$100 range low whistles and it played beautifully, but it stunted my growth. I finally picked up a cheap Oak high D on Amazon - best decision ever! I saw an instant improvement and it made me want to play more.

Remember, plenty of pros use $9 whistles and sound great and they’re a lot easier to get your hands on. I don’t know about New England, but Huntsville Alabama does not have a brick-and-mortar “Whistles-R-Us” with custom Overton’s in stock, so shopping around online for cheaper whistles is the safest bet for a beginner.

Re: Tin Whistle - hole size?

More expensive does not mean better. Anyone can make a whistle, most of which will at first sound terrible, if they sound at all, until you get all the proportions and the processes right. From time to time some marketing guru in a big company decides there is money to be made, comes up with a new design, material, process and or marketing ploy, and muscles in on the rapidly-expanding market.
If you want to pay three hundred dollars for a penny whistle, go ahead, but pay no attention to anything the company says, and use your ears and your fingers.
Most of the cheap mass-produced whistles are perfectly good given the little finishing touches that the manufacturing process skips over. You just need to read the comments here to realise that it’s largely a matter of personal taste, not price that defines what is ‘the best’.
Of course there are certain dedicated makers who produce beautiful whistles at a price; but there are some expensive as well as some cheap howlers out there.

Re: Tin Whistle - hole size?

No, you’re not mistaken. The hole size makes a great deal of difference, which is why the holes on flutes and whistles are not uniform. The larger the hole, the louder the note and the higher the pitch. Different makers compensate in different ways, so, you can make, say the F# closer to the E for ease of playing, but you have to make the hole bigger to compensate.
There is also the matter of matching the volume to the other notes, and matching different octaves, by varying the internal dimensions, wall thickness, overall shape (conical, wide-bore, parallel and contoured bore) as well as windway, fipple, tone-holes etc. There is hardly one thing you can change without affecting everything else.
Some makers offer different hole sizes, but they compensate by doing something else, like moving the holes.

Re: Tin Whistle - hole size?

Re: tone hole size, and bore size, yes if all other variables are identical then larger should mean louder. However all other variables are rarely identical and for example the loudest Low D I’ve ever owned, one of five MKs I’ve owned, had smallish/medium toneholes for a Low D, and an ordinary bore, while the two Burke Low Ds I’ve owned, with larger toneholes and bores, weren’t as loud.

Re: inexpensive mass-produced High D whistles v expensive “boutique” High D whistles, I’m a fan of the old-school Generation tone and performance, and for me a really good Generation is what an “Irish whistle” is supposed to sound like and play like.

When I started, back in the 70s, Generations were pretty much the only High Ds available and all the professional players and top traditional players played them. So whistledom was a simple matter of finding the best Generation you could.

The problem is quality control, which nowadays with Generations seems practically nonexistent.

I’ve posted before how I had a crack at a new shipment of 24 Generation Ds around ten years ago. I played them all. Two were superb players, whistles which I would play in preference to any high-end boutique High D out there. What do I mean by superb? Light, easy, sweet high notes; round full solid low notes; extremely nimble “action” meaning that octaves and other wide leaps are effortlessly negotiated without relying on tonguing; and good tuning between the octaves.

In that batch of 24 there were, besides those two magic ones, a few that were good players and a few which were unplayable (the 2nd octave wouldn’t sound at all). The rest were mediocre.

It’s an example of quality control, or lack thereof, making or breaking a reputation. What I mean is that if Generation had only shipped the two best whistles of that 24, and charged $200 per whistle, they would have a top-shelf reputation as one of the best whistlemakers on earth. Had they shipped the best 8 whistles and charged $100 per whistle, they would have the reputation of a solid dependable medium-price whistlemaker (along the lines of Tony Dixon). But no, Generation shipped them all, the superb and the unplayable alike, and have gained a commensurate reputation.

Which isn’t to say you can’t get lucky! A student went down to a local shop and bought the first Generation D he laid hands to and it is wonderful.

What you get, when you spend $200 for a Burke or what have you, is extreme consistency.

Re: Tin Whistle - hole size?

I should mention that there’s an extremely effective modification you can do to Generations, to make a mediocre-playing one play like a good one.

The problem is often that the windway isn’t properly oriented to the blade, and that can be corrected by (yikes!) sawing the head in two right through the middle of the window, so the window looks like this [ ].

This does two things: the material that’s lost in the sawing moves the end of the windway closer to the blade, which helps sharpen the 2nd octave, usually flat on Generations.

More importantly you now can move the windway about to find just the right position vis-à-vis the blade, the “sweet spot” where you get full low notes and sweet high notes and good “action”.

Cement the two halves of the top together with old-school styrene cement, which is hard to find here in the USA now. Put cement on both surfaces, which sort of melts the plastic, and put the two halves together, and you have several minutes before the cement hardens to experiment with various alignments to find the ideal one.

I’ve turned a few good Generations into great Generations with this mod.
