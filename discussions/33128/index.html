Re: Similarities and differences in Irish and Scottish Traditional music.

I find Scottish music to be richer in terms of quality and variety of melody. Irish tends to be more driving rhythmically. Scottish ballads are also richer and have better lyrics than Irish. Scottish bands tend to do more arranging when they record, whereas Irish bands tend to play as they do in sessions. This has changed somewhat in recent years as Irish bands have moved to a more contemporary sound.

The similarities are greater than the differences, but the above characteristics are, to me, easy to hear once your ear becomes accustomed to the sound and style. Just like trying to describe the difference between Italian and German opera, the contrast is easier to hear than it is to explain, so I hope I did the two countries justice, although I’m sure some will disagree.

Re: Similarities and differences in Irish and Scottish Traditional music.

Usually if a tune is limited to single diatonic octave and the leading note below, it will be of Scottish origin, especially if it is in Amix or Bflat. Irish tunes tend to have more semi-tones (but still not many) and changing accidentals, and a range of one-and-a half to two octaves, especially in the keys of D and G and their equivalents.

The trouble is that a lot of people on the internet have no idea what they are talking about. Taking comments from this website and citing them is about as useful as citing the comments that appear underneath news stories. If you said in a paper, “ The Irish tend to rework their melodies over and over and their lyrics are, to me, rather boring in comparison to the Scot’s,” (random anonymous poster, thesession.org), it wouldn’t help your paper and if your prof knows anything about Scottish and/or Irish music, it wouldn’t look very good.

And no, you cannot use the key signature to figure out what country the tune is from, nor do the tunes follow the language, except for the ones that explicitly do, like Puirt a Beul ones. The obvious things, as stated above, like tunes with only nine notes on them intended to fit on the GHB chanter and types of tunes like 2/4 marches and strathspeys. There are Scottish regional styles, as there are like Irish regional styles. So not all Scottish music sounds like other Scottish music. There’s the fiddle and accordion country dance style, which sounds like fiddle and accordion country dance music and you will just have to find some and listen to it. There’s the kind of Northeast playing associated with Scott Skinner. There’s West Highland style playing, which doesn’t sound like Northeast playing. There is a Borders style. There is the military regimented style of piping but there are also much freer styles, associated from the West Highlands, some re-imported from Cape Breton, which are increasingly popular. The cities would have had influences from all over, especially Ireland, and there has been a lot of Irish music in Glasgow for a while. What there isn’t are simple answers where you can say “Irish music has X” and Scottish music has Y."

↳ Posted by Someone at The Session on January 13th, 2014.

Re: Similarities and differences in Irish and Scottish Traditional music.

This is a complex, and vast, topic. But a couple of things come to mind:

1. Scotland’s deep and broad tradition of marches is not anywhere close to matched by the march tradition in Ireland. Countless 2/4s, 4/4s, 6/8s, 3/4s, etc. Retreats, i.e. 3/4 marches, to my knowledge are not found at all in ITM, and represent a beautiful form in their own right. Each march form is a research paper in and of itself.

2. My pet peeve is for folks to paint a broad brush of STM as “pointy” or “snappy”. Depends entirely upon the tune form. Yes, strathspeys and certain marches can fall into this category. But reels and jigs, played in a driving traditional style, are not “pointy”. Listen to the piping of Gordon Duncan, Hamish and Fin Moore, et. al., and you’ll hear driving straight-out jigs and reels. Nothing “pointy” about them.

3. There are many different styles of STM, and many different styles and forms of tunes. Hamish Moore has identified at least 12 different kinds of strathspeys alone. And approaches to tunes can vary widely. The Scottish Fiddle Orchestra style, for example, unfortunately too often associated with STM in general, should not at all be taken as anywhere close to an accurate portrayal of STM. Another example would be Highland piping, which should not be taken as representative of Scottish traditional music in general. Highland piping is heavily influenced by the competition culture, and jigs and reels are played differently as to tempo, emphasis, etc. than a more traditional driving style.

4. Piobaireachd (“pibroch”), the ancient pipe music of the Highlands, is a vast topic in and of itself. Airs, i.e. “grounds”, with layers of variations added, the variations focused on the unique ornamentation of the Highland pipes. Some pibrochs can last as long as 45 minutes. I imagine ITM has some traditions involving tunes and variations, but nothing coming close to pibroch to my knowledge.

This topic is interesting, and the potential for illustration is great. But generalizations should for the most part be avoided. It’s all about nuance. Especially as to specific tune forms.

Re: Similarities and differences in Irish and Scottish Traditional music.

I’ve playing Highland pipes for 40 years now, and uilleann pipes and Irish flute nearly as long.

One thing I have noticed over these many years is that people who only know about Highland piping often are amazingly ignorant about Irish traditional music, and visa versa.

I have heard Irish trad musicians make countless false claims about the nature of Scottish music on this site.

To balance this trolling for ideas on what is, primarly, an Irish trad music site you ought to start a similar thread on a Highland piping site. Perhaps you already have! I’ll check the main ones and see.

One thing is that you will have to try to define WHICH “Scottish traditional music.” There’s the Highland piping scene, a very rich tradition which happens also to have kept alive perhaps the oldest Celtic music of all, the music pipers call Ceol Mor. It is an ancient music tradition, an Indo-European music tradition, which has surprising parallels with the classical music of Northern India.

The bulk of the current Highland repertoire was composed over the last 120 years or so. However the strathspeys and reels that Highland pipers play in competition, the big tunes, are nearly all traditional.

There’s also a large number of traditional Gaelic songs which are played as instrumental pieces on the Highland pipes.

There are many other types of “Scottish traditional music” though! A rich fiddle tradition, with all sorts of old regional variety. And modern fiddle music such as the Scottish Fiddle Orchestra. Ceilidh dance music. A vast folksong tradition, many in English and part of the shared Child Ballad tradition, but also a vibrant Gaelic song tradition including all the wonderful pentatonic Waulking Songs and other old work songs.

I will say that as far as reels and jigs go, Scottish Highland pipe reels and jigs often have fewer notes per bar, progressing with a combination of quarternotes and eighthnotes, while Irish reels and jigs (double jigs, the sort one usually hears nowadays) mostly are a flow of eighthnotes. Also Scottish Highland jigs and reels will often group eighthnotes in pairs while Irish jigs and reels tend to group them in threes (‘long rolls’). A comparison of a few dozen jigs in any modern collection of Irish session tunes and a Scottish Highland pipe collection will show you that.

This difference is highlighted when a tune crosses over nowadays from one tradition to the other. The Jig Of Slurs, a composed Highland pipe tune (George MacLennan in the early 20th century), mostly progresses by pairs of eighthnotes but when it got taken into the Irish session scene this typically jagged Scottish outline got smoothed out and notes got grouped in threes. Ironic because a ‘slur’ is the Highland pipe term for a lower gracenote which separates a pair of notes, and The Jig Of Slurs when played in an Irish session has no slurs.

I have in front of me two classic collections of Scottish fiddle music (though there are a few tunes specifically noted as pipe tunes) the Simon Fraser collection (1815) and the Skye collection (1887).

In the Simon Fraser collection I can see some reels that are entirely eighthnotes. The most common pattern is to have one quarternote per bar, usually the first beat in each bar having eight eighthnotes and the second beat go quarter two eighths or two eighths quarter.

There are also a number of jigs which are entirely eighthnotes.

The Skye collection shows similar reel patterns but also a large number of reels having the single quarternote on the first beat of each bar… as I look through it my impression is that this is a very common pattern. There are also quite a few reels with two quarternotes per bar, usually falling on each of the two major beats.

As far as the jigs go, these seem mostly to be Irish borrowings. While the strathspeys and reels nearly always have Scottish-sounding names, the jigs include The Irish Washerwoman, Paddy O Carroll, The Highway to Dublin, etc. There’s one jig specifically called “Highland”, Gillan an Drover. The first part has the quarternote pauses typical of Highland pipe jigs while the 3rd and 4th parts have more flow.
