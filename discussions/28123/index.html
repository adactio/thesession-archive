Re: Pros and Cons of the Mustard

Here are some of my thoughts about your questions.

(1) I think the educational benefits of the session.org are pretty limited by the fact that it is the internet and as such, the accuracy of the information is unverifiable. You can get a lot of bad advice here. You can also get a lot of good advice. How do you separate the good from the bad? If you already have fairly thorough knowledge of the music, it’s not that hard to sift through it all and you can work out who has a clue and who doesn’t, but if you’re a newbie to it, you don’t know what you don’t know. I think good and interesting tidbits of information emerges out of the rows, but as I said, it takes a bit of a clue to differentiate the good bits from the sh*te. ANY internet forum, not just this one, suffers that. The very small advantage this one is that compared to some other Irish music message boards I occasionally peek into, the moderator lets rows go a bit more, so the people who are talking rubbish generally end up getting a shellacking for it. It’s up to the reader, of course, to decide which participants in a fight know what they’re talking about, and which ones don’t. :)

That all said, there are interesting nuggets in the tune section’s comments -- stuff about who wrote the tune, who played it, how various players have interpreted it, that sort of thing. If you’re into that. Not everyone is. It also contains loads of YouTube clips which people have posted, so you can hear people playing the tunes.

I think this, or any internet forum, is a really terrible way to learn the music if it’s your primary source. Real life, real interaction, real people, is always better.

(2) I never tried to learn Irish music online. I pretty much use this, and have always used this, as a source for procrastination, not information, more so since I started a PhD. Don’t you? That’s what thesession.org is really good for.

(3) The best is the sense of community (two fingers to all you who say it isn’t) I have from the posters here and the banter you get. There are some bright, knowledgeable, and funny people who post here. Really funny. And some of them are even good players! ;-) The worst is when rows get out of control and people get fecked off with the message board and leave forever. We’ve lost some really good posters that way, which is a bit sh*t. People just end up taking it all too seriously and would enjoy what the Mustard Board *can* offer if they lightened up a wee bit.

Re: Pros and Cons of the Mustard

1) The opportunity to discuss the music with people who know more than you do (anyone who thinks that they know more than everyone else here on every subject is a fool). The debates aren’t always pleasant, but over the years I’ve learned a great deal this way.

2) Dividing the good info from the BS. Again, not always pleasant, but it usually gets sorted around here one way or another. Often there is blood on the floor before it’s all said and done. Some people will refuse to admit that they’re wrong about anything no matter the evidence, and this can be maddening.

3) Best feature: some great people here willing to help out anyone with a question. Worst feature: some people (some who are also in the first category) who at times get too absorbed in playing “I’m right and you’re wrong” and bury good info with pedantic one-upmanship.

And BTW, I’m not claiming innocence on any of these things I’m pointing out, I’ve made my share of posts when I should have kept my mouth shut.

Also, when you say “learn Irish traditional music online” I take it to mean learn *about* Irish traditional music online. Good advice aside, you can’t actually learn to play from a discussion board.

“I find it enlightening to learn that there are people who strongly prefer their tunes without guitar and bodhran accompaniment.”

Just to help this thread jump the rails, I’m in this camp but not because I think the music sounds better this way.

I like listening to solo playing to get inside of how a player really plays, when they aren’t making adjustments to play with other instruments and just play in their natural way. As an example, a solo fiddle or flute is usually played with different intonation solo versus with a guitar backer.

That’s not to say that I don’t think that backing makes the music sound better or that I don’t enjoy listening to bands. I just prefer my trad straight up. That’s purely a matter of personal taste and not an opinion on how the music should be played.

“Over the years here, I’ve noticed that some people seem to assume that no one here could possibly be an experienced, knowledgeable player of this music”

And there are some who assume that no one who disagrees with their views could be an experienced, knowledgeable player of the music.
I can think of one or two of those who have stormed off in their huffs. This brings me back around to the idea, raised above, of “dividing the good from the BS”. I don’t think there’s much BS, at least not in the Harry Frankfurt sense. Instead, there are people who hold divergent views, or seemingly divergent views. There is no reason why those views can’t all be useful, and no reason at all why one of them must be correct. For example, the view that “bodhrans are a desirable part of the Irish session” is an interesting viewpoint. My view is quite the contrary, and we don’t need to resolve the two - what, would we take the average and decide that bodhrans are only half desirable? Or that they should play fiercely, exactly half of the time? Neither should win out.
It’s the argument that gets you somewhere, not the resolution.

Re: Pros and Cons of the Mustard

“You simply cannot, in my limited experience, learn to play a fiddle or a flute well in any style without guiding hands in the same room. That is, not in any practical way - it is possible, but so is learning fire-eating by mail”

Well, I’m more or less entirely self-taught. I had a few lessons on the fiddle when I was very young, but they didn’t take. Later on, I started figuring stuff out on a mandolin and then on a tin whistle, and just worked it out from there. When I was doing that, any guidance, however misguided, was useful.
If someone suggested something, I’d give it a try, and if it helped, I’d pursue it further. What I didn’t have was someone standing over me saying, no, do it that way, but I did have a a pretty good idea of what sound I was looking for, and so when someone described how to do a roll on the whistle, I was able to apply it, and figured out how it fit into my mental picture of the sound, and then I started playing rolls and that particular piece of the sound started making sense.
This was in the late 80s/early 90s, and at that time some people had the bizarre idea that you could play a roll on the mandolin the way you do on the fiddle. They described how they tried to do this, and I tried to do it, and it didn’t work, and so I didn’t do it any more, relying on a pick (which I worked out for myself) to get that effect. Years later I figured out (by some combination of observation and logic and listening) that there were these “bowed triplets” that fiddlers would play, and what I was doing was basically that, and that was neat.

What I love most about abc notation is that it meets every requirement you could ask:
1) it’s machine readable, so it can be transformed into any representation you choose and can represent. You could transform a reel into a piece of abstract art with not too much effort, or you could of course generate sound files from it, or search it for patterns.
2) it’s not only human readable but it’s actually derived from a standard human notation, so it’s human-readable with almost no effort
3) it’s as compact as you could reasonable ask for. Further compression could be achieved, but a typical tune can be represented as fully as was managed by O’Neill or Breathnach, with significant metadata, in the space if a few hundred bytes.
Taken all together, that’s pretty good.
