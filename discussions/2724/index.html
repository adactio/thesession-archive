Re: history of the harmonica in traditional Irish music

Early models were reeds stuck in small tubes or pipes, like the tuners you blew on to get a note ofr each string of your folk guitar back in the 60s (dating myself). The Germans developed this into the encased wood and metal version common today. Mass-produced harmonicas swarmed out of Germany in the early 1800s, and Hohner (he was a clock maker originally) started cranking them out in 1857. Relatively inexpensive, portable, and at least as musical as the penny whistle in the right hands, it no doubt was well applied to Irish music in the late 1800s. Hohner was actively pushing harmonicas (“psst! Hey kid, give this a blow”) in Irish schools back in the 1950s.

Re: history of the harmonica in traditional Irish music

This isn’t strictly on topic but there are quite a few good “moothie” players in Scotland whom you can hear in sessions playing predominantly Scottish tunes. Some of these were also pipers and they frequently feature pipe marches in their repertoire. Some of the better known players include the late Iain Grant (Great source of tunes and encouragement to younger musicians-all instruments), Bryce Johnstone, Norman Stewart, Donald Black, George Current along with a few others. Many of the “moothie” tunes are in C or related keys but Iain and Bryce had these multiple contraptions with something like half a dozen harmonicas in each set so they could play “just aboot aw thing” as Iain used to say.

Getting back to Irish music. I’ve heard Brendan Power a few times too and thoroughly enjoyed his playing.

So, yes, I think “moothies” can sound great in sessions in the hands of good players. Unfortunately, it’s one of these instruments which anyone can “get a tune out of” and its reputation has suffered as a result.

John

I’m new to the session so hesitate to chime in here, but I recently did a lot of research to try and decided which style of mouthorgan to play ITM on. It seems to me that the tremolo is the most “traditional” harmonica for ITM. In my opinioniIt blends more into the setting of a session and because it’s solo tuned all the notes are there without bending.
New proponents of Irish harmonica are going with altered richter tuned 10 hole diatonic harmonicas (paddy richter tuning) and slide diatonics. I think these latter styles are more for solo and stage work. Different instruments, differnt styles, different patterns, different sounds. I guess it depends on what you want.
I’m trying to learn both. So, I play my Tombo 3121 tremelos at sessions, and on the rare occasion that I am on stage and playing into a microphone I play my paddy richter tuned Hohner Meisterklasse harmonicas.
I’ve also heard some older gents say that the tremolo’s are called mouthorgans or moothies and everything else is called a harmonica.

Re: history of the harmonica in traditional Irish music

Welcome to the mustard board (aka, “the squabble”).

I favor straight D diatonics when I play tunes on the harmonica, but I’ve heard great playing on all of the instruments you’ve mentioned. Other people prefer D or G chromatics, and I’ve found some tunes play nicely in E flat on a C chromatic with reversed slide - this is the easiest chromatic configuration for me, since I play a lot of B/C button box. A reversed C# harmonica would be the analog to the C#/D box - reversing the slide puts the D row on the “out” position and now the slide cuts a half step instead of raising, which makes more sense to me for this music.

There’s a lot of variety in harmonicas, it seems to me that as long as you like the sound and can play in time and in tune, they’re all fair game.
