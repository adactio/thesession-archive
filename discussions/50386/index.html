Reasonable prices for “pre-loved” instruments - your views gratefully received

I have a few more quality artisan -made bouzoukis than I can keep satisfied, so in the words of the estimable Seanie McGrath, I should probably “look to move some on.” However, how much would be a fair price to put upon these?

I am not looking to make money and will cheerfully sell for the same price that I paid, on the grounds that I don’t particularly want to take a financial hit. The few potential purchasers I have found so far seem to want a very substantial discount from the maker’s price. In one way this is fair, on the grounds that the instrument is undoubtedly “secondhand”, I’ve had some use from it and it will likely have a couple of minor dings.

On the other hand, any purchaser will be able to jump a (potentially long) maker’s waiting list and would also get a good instrument that has (at least partly) been played-in.

So - do you think I am being reasonable in seeking to get back what I paid, please?

Before anybody says “condition is everything“ these are all quality instruments (Foley, Crump, Fylde, Taylor et cetera) with nothing amiss bar a couple of minor dings from the odd over-lively session.

Thanks.

“do you think I am being reasonable in seeking to get back what I paid, please?”

Honest answer? No.

If there are “a couple of minor dings from the odd over-lively session”, or there are fret marks or pick scratches or whatever, the instrument is no longer in the condition it left the maker. Unless you’ve got something really rare, and/or sought after by collectors or enthusiasts, or the very fact that it was *you* who has played it might in certain rare cases add value, you can’t be surprised that a used instrument should sell for less than a brand new one. Same with just about any new versus used item in the marketplace.

How much of a reduction in price will depend on a whole host of factors.

Re: Reasonable prices for “pre-loved” instruments - your views gratefully received

I have to disagree with sfmans position. Superficial damage - dings that do not penetrate the finish and the like - do not affect the quality of the instrument or its value as an instrument, so unless you’re thinking of the instrument as a wall hanging, those should not be considered in setting the price. What matters is the playability of the instrument, its acoustic properties, the quality and condition of any onboard electronics, and the cost and difficulty of acquiring a similar instrument. If you have an instrument to sell, and the only way to get a similar instrument is to wait for several years for the maker to make one for you, then you should not sell that instrument for a cut-rate price unless you need cash in hand immediately.

One option that occurs to me - and I don’t know if this is at all reasonable - would be to see if the original maker would be interested in offering the instrument to their waiting list. This would have advantages all around: the maker would be able to satisfy their client base, the buyer would get the bouzouki they want without delay, and you’re able to move the instrument along easily for about the price you’re looking for.

↳ Posted by Bill Ross on July 28th, 2025.

Re: Reasonable prices for “pre-loved” instruments - your views gratefully received

It’s difficult to put a price on a used instrument for many of the reasons listed above. But the calculation of “value” is a slightly different equation.

You have to start with what the value is to you. Part of how you would come up with that number is, as you already mentioned, the original amount that you paid for the instrument. But there is more to the personal value equation, like whether you have an emotional attachment to it, what it would cost you to replace it if you decided later that selling it was a mistake, etc.

The value in the marketplace is a simpler (but harder to calculate) equation. Its value is simply whatever someone is willing to pay for it. There’s a reason that you sometimes see people list items at ridiculous prices. If they’re not in a hurry, they score a win if someone wants it bad enough to pay the high price for it. But there are other factors, like you mentioned, with general availability of the instrument. Does the maker have a multi-year backlog of orders, or has the maker stopped making them, or passed away? Those things can greatly affect how much people are willing to pay for something. I’ve seen Olwell flutes that are basically brand new sell for significantly more than people paid for it originally, simply because of the extensive wait time to get a new instrument made.

So your goal should be to try to reconcile your personal valuation of the instrument against your best guess of what actual market value is. And then decide your price from there… It has been a few years, but I have sold many an expensive instrument on eBay by starting the price really low (like $1) and having no minimum sale price. It’s risky, because you might end up selling it for less than you want, but in reality, it’s a great way to get actual “market value” for it. The low price and no reserve tends to attract a lot of attention, and you have people adding it to their watch lists, and getting into bidding wars, which just drive the interest and the prices up. But it’s always a scary process because it will look bad until about 5 minutes before the auction is going to end.

Another thing I should note is that it’s a great suggestion to look around and see what people are selling things for, but you can get a bit more information by searching for completed listings on eBay. https://www.ebay.co.uk/sch/i.html?_nkw=bouzouki&_sacat=0&_from=R40&rt=nc&LH_Sold=1&LH_Complete=1 That will show you both things that were actually purchased, and what people paid for them, as well as listings that timed out and didn’t sell. (Sometimes sellers will list the same product multiple times, with one listing cheaper than the others, often times a lot cheaper, so that it makes the cheaper one look more enticing… so it’s good to look at what things actually sold for!)

Can I add one thing I haven’t seen mentioned…

In the whistle world (cheaper instruments) there’s a joke “no one sells a good whistle”

To some extent I find this is actually true.

In my area you can occasionally actually see the same “premium maker” whistles turn up again and again on auction sites as people (presumably) buy them, thinking they’re getting a bargain, then try to sell it as they realises it’s not a great version of that makers whistle.

This (personally) makes me a little cagey about ever buying second hand instruments online (unless I’ve tested them a fair bit), and no longer do it.

I think it’s relatively rare (unfortunate financial issue, age etc) a musician ever parts with a truly good whistle/instrument, and instead tends to sell the ones they’re not that impressed with.

I’m not sure if this carries across to more expensive instruments, but (on top of the other reasons (“new car economics “ etc)) could definitely see it as a reason why you wouldn’t expect to achieve the new price for a second hand instrument in normal circumstances.

Belayatron makes an interesting point, and there’s also a potential issue with selling more than one at a time. If I saw a private seller listing a dozen premium bouzoukis, I’d wonder if they’d been stolen from a shop or were cheaply made copies.

You can get round that in the listings, dodgy sellers usually offer very little detail. You can say when you commissioned it, how you worked with the maker, and what you like most about it. Uploading a clip of you playing a couple of different tune types on it would also help – most people selling instruments don’t offer buyers a chance to hear them, so the listing would stand out.

It’s time-consuming, but it shows you’re a reputable seller offering genuine instruments and would help justify the price tag.

Yes, the final price is whatever the buyer and seller negotiate, but I think this thread is more about general rules of thumb for the “right” price for a used instrument - namely, how do we account for factors like scarcity, wear and tear, and such. Basically, it’s climate versus weather - the final price is the weather, this thread is about the climate. It’s what you can reasonably expect versus what you actually get. There are good reasons to want to understand the climate, for example if you’re not sure whether you want to sell, the expected price might make a difference on whether the instrument ever gets to market in the first place.

I’d be amazed if you’ll get what you paid. I say that as someone who has bought and sold musical instruments across decades. I expect to pay less money for a secondhand instrument when buying; I expect to receive less money for a secondhand instrument when selling.
