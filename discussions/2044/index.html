deconstructing technique

OK, so you’ve been playing an instrument long and hard, you have a decent repertoire but you have been putting in any old kind of ornament whenever and however you please, only to find out, say from a workshop, that it should be more disciplined than that. What do you do next? Do you systematically relearn every tune, deconstructing your technique and replacing everything with your new-found knowledge, or do you keep what you’ve got and vow that everything henceforth shall be done properly?
It seems that doing the former could wreak havoc on your repertoire -- especially since additional workshops could keep you continually chasing your tail -- but doing the latter perpetuates mediocrity. What think’st thou?
Paul S.

Re: deconstructing technique

Um, quit taking workshops?
Heh, seriously, I can overdose on two workshops a year--too many fresh ideas to incorporate.

I’ve completely overhauled my playing many times over the years (which is why it growls like a old 356 Chevy rather than purrs like a brand new Toyota), usually based on hearing a player do something I haven’t heard before. Early on, it was hearing Tommy Peoples put triplets into places I hadn’t heard triplets, or Sean Smyth do long rolls on the pick up notes, or Kevin Burke’s little hammer-on slides, or Ciaran Tourish’s amazing drones and doublestops and octave-down doubling against Mairead’s driving melody line. Each time I re-evaluated what I was doing and those ideas crept into my tunes. Sometimes it was a conscious decision, and other times stuff just popped up as we played a tune in a session or gig. But either way, what I added to my playing was informed by years of listening to the music, so it fit (most of the time, and when it didn’t--to my ear--I thought twice about doing it again).

Also, it helps to listen to a wide range of regional and personal styles--where to place ornaments and which ornaments to use varies over those ranges, and it helps to be able to recognize what your session mates are doing so you can adapt your playing (either to blend in, or occasionally, to add a bit of pizzazz). In other words, it’s not so much a matter of playing “properly,” as “appropriate to the situation.” For instance, if everyone’s cranking along on a set of Donegal tunes and you’re off in the corner slurring notes and rolling away in a nice legato style, you’ll likely get some funny looks.

Does that make sense? Regardless of the instrument you play, realize that (1) there are a handful of traditional ornaments (cuts and taps, triplets, long and short rolls, crans, slides, vibrato, dynamic variation), and (2) phrasing is highly variable and personal, and (3) this music is about playing with the timing and phrasing and sound, like fingerpainting only with sound waves. Then listen to how players you admire use these things to get the sound they do, and go from there.

One last thought: Sean Smyth says this is a “busy” music. As players, it’s important to create space, breathing room, inside the tunes. Sometimes what you leave out is more important than what you put in.

Re: deconstructing technique

Em’s got that right, too. You want the basic bits to work on autopilot so they can pop in on the fly. Then, as the tune unrolls in the air over your head just before it comes out on the instrument in your hands, you can imagine those bits and they happen.

Usually it helps to isolate the bit and try it out in all its permutations before slapping it into all your tunes. For example, a common form of moving scratch triplet involves repeating one note, as in: (3BBA. This comes in handy when you want to put a triplet in the place of two different eighth notes (rather than waiting for a plain old quarter note space to stick it). Say you’ve got a reel in D with the phrase: |cdef g…| Now you can play it |(3ccd def g…|

So now you go home and play that triplet up and down the instrument. But don’t ignore the other possibilities. You can also play the repeating note *after* the first note of the triplet: (3BAA. This opens up new places to fit it into tunes. And try it out in all the different tune forms--reels, jigs, slip jigs, slides, hornpipes, polkas, etc. (And bear in mind this doesn’t even scratch the potential of 3-note scratch triplets, e.g., (3Bcd…)

Re: deconstructing technique

I think there are two aspects to this question. One.- be so familiar with the tune you are playing that you don’t have to think about how to play it and two - be able to play all the ornaments well on all notes. This requires a lot of woodshedding.

Now, having reached this stage, it should be possible to play any of your tunes ornamenting them in any way you feel like at the moment. Like a jazz musician, the ITM player should be able to express himself. Therefore the inspiration of the moment should prevail. This does’n mean that a tune should have every possible ornament at every possible spot. Part of playing well involves playing with taste at all times.

Re: deconstructing technique

Dave’s got it. But I think part of Paul’s question is how you develop that “taste.” And for me, it’s personal expression based on years of listening to the music. There is a structure to the tunes, and how our technique fits them, which is mostly based on the fact that these tunes are dreamed up on our instruments in the first place. In other words, there are pipe tunes, and fiddle tunes, and flute tunes, etc. In part, the tunes are shaped by the trad techniques used on the particular instruments. Take the second half of Mulqueen’s for an example.

The basic structure is something like:
M: 4/4
K: D
cd|eA A2 efed|cdef g2 fg|ae e2 efed|cABc d2 cd|
|eA A2 efed|cdef g2 fg|afff gece|dfec d2…|

Now, most string players will see obvious places for triplets right off the bat:

cd|eA(3AAA efed|cdef g2 fg|ae(3eee efed|cABc d…| and so on.

Wind players would more likely stick rolls in those same spots.

Then when you run into the same phrase again as the second half continues, you might want to try something different….(I’ll stick with string-friendly stuff)

|[e4e4] efed|cdef (3ggg fg|a~f3 gfec|dfec d2…|
or
|eAce AceA|(3ccd ef g2 fg|afdf gece|dfec d2…|
or
|e2 A2 (3eef ed|cdef gefg|abaf gecA|(3ddd ec d2…|

For me, these variations come from tinkering with where the accent falls relative to the beat. Even the Texas swing (I can hear the purists howling now :o) style run of |eAce AceA| is more about syncopation than the pitch of the notes involved. To pull them off on the fly, you need to have all those techniques and “technical ideas” down pat. I don’t think, “Oh, I could put a shuffle bow in here,” or “Let’s do a moving scratch triplet there.” It’s more a matter of having the sound in my head and my hands do whatever they’ve done before to produce that sound.

And then you pick up something new from another player and you start the process of adding it to the store of available sounds in your head. And then one day, that sound inserts itself somehwere in a tune, and if you’ve woodshedded enough, your magic fingers do their thing….

I’m going out on a bit of a limb here, making assumptions based on listening to a lot of music and a bit of dialectical reasoning chucked in, but I reckon the important thing is that all really good players use ornamentation according to an instinctive feel for when that type of emphasis is required, rather than according to any strict ‘rule’. I’d say that the bigger the onamentation, the more sparingly it should used, so use cran-type ornaments sparingly - and that the most important rule should be that the ornament should add to the tune, and not obscure it - something that some very well thought of flute players might stop and consider. I think that players like the ones Will mentions above do use ornaments with precision and insight, but that is because they know precisely what effect they are wanting to acheive, and not because they know when that ornament is SUPPOSED to be used in that particular tune (is that stating the obvious).
In respect of learning things like this in workshops, what sorefingers says above is obviously true - ‘Everything you learn is a plus’. However I always have this blues analogy go through my head when I think about the precise learning of traditional music….
It’s 1937 and Lame Lemon Pie has just been recorded by a field scout down from Chicago. It is the record which we always associate with him, “Gone Off In My Kitchen”, and it perfectly illustrates his inimitable guitar style. He learned to play guitar after being laid of from the plantation due to a horrific accident with a cotton baler, which left him lame. The fingers of his left hand were extricated from the baler with the greatest difficulty, and had been deformed by the machinery, and were now two inches longer than before the accident. He played on a guitar of his own making, with a biscuit tin resonator. His style and technique were also of his own devising, worked out after he once saw Charlie Patton at a fish-fry. His fingers didn’t move too good, but he could stretch to make some pretty impressive chords of his own devising. All of his small recorded output was characterised by this distinctive Guitar Style.
Jump forward to the 1970s. A young man, besotted with the blues, sits in his bedsit, beloved Martin in hand, and a book open before him. It is the just out ‘Lame Lemon Pie Complete’ with accurate tablature worked out from the original recordings by Stephan Gropesman. It is a pig. He just can’t get his fingers around those chords. That Lame Lemon Pie must have been one hell of a skilled guitar player to be able to do that. The young man decides to devote however long it takes to learn to play like Lame Lemon Pie…..

It may seem artificial to those raised in the tradition, but it’s about the only way some of us in the hinterlands can manage an “authentic” sound (I don’t know what that is, but I know it when I hear it). Just bear in mind that this all falls under the rubric of woodshedding, and that the actual playing out comes to life best if it’s spontaneous and reaching a bit.

If a player gets into a rut and finds it impossible to progress perhaps that is the time to go to a really good teacher, preferably one who doesn’t know the player, who can look at all aspects of the player’s technique with a critical and unbiassed eye, and suggest remedial measures to get things on course and going again.
This may very well mean drastic measures like (with a fiddle player) deconstructing the bow hold and technique, the fiddle hold, left hand position and technique, and last but not least, posture and breathing. And then rebuilding everything from scratch. It need not necessarily be a long process with an intelligent player who is prepared to work.
