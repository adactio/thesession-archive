Re: Whistle woes

It has been a controversial subject over the years on this site, but I personally cannot think of any more appropriate moment to bring in THE METRONOME. You clearly have a good general awareness of what is going wrong in your playing – playing to a click will show you exactly which notes are early and which are late. My guess is, since you can hear the lack of rhythm but can seemingly do nothing about it, it is more a problem of fine motor control than an ‘innate’ lack or rhythm (if such a thing really exists). Each time you try and adjust a note to co-incide with a click, you are exercising whatever neurons, muscles and joints are involved in gaining the necessary control. (Bear in mind that timing on the whistle does not depend only on the fingers but on the tongue and the breath also.)

Slowing down per se may not be the magic solution, but it is essential. Playing slowly is not necessarily easier, but it does give you time to hear in more detail what is going on and make adjustments to it. Before trying the tune itself to a metronome, it might be useful to try some very basic exercises – this frees you from the ‘burden’ of concentrating on playing the right sequence of notes and allows you to focus purely on technique*.
1. With the metronome set to, say, 60 bpm, play one note repeatedly in time with the click, tonguing each time
2. As above, but without tonguing, using just the breath to stop and start the note
3. Alternate between two notes, one note per click, slurring the notes (as long as breath allows)

Once you can do these, you can start increasing the tempo, then halving the metronome setting and playing two notes to a click, then three notes to a click. You can eventually try out different combinations of tonguing and slurring, and introduce fingered articulations. I don’t want to be too prescriptive, as this is only a hypothetical method, but it’s surely worth a try.

*I am paraphrasing fiddler James Kelly here.

If you did have rhythm that wandered all over the place, I too would recommend a metronome.

I know on these boards metronomes are often demonised, but all a metronome does is allow a person to play along with a rhythm that’s outside of his/her own body.

Your playing sounds pretty good to me, but I understand being your own worst critic. You’ve gotten some great advice here, but I wanted to touch on the bit about not being able to tap your foot while you play. I’ve been working on this myself. On the mandolin it’s second nature and I probably couldn’t stop if I wanted to, but the fiddle is another matter. I suppose it’s because the fiddle and bow press more braincells into service.

What I’ve been doing at the beginning of my practice time is taking a nice slow tempo and starting of by tapping my foot a couple of measures before I begin playing, and then when I do start playing, I keep as much focus and awareness on my foot as I can and as little as possible on the fiddle. I began to notice how good it felt when the beats and taps coincided, and the entirety of the exercise is to keep my foot happy. When I hit a snag, I just loop that phrase until my foot is happy and move on. It’s also a great method to pinpoint and work out those trouble areas, the kind where you can play the phrase, but it takes a little more effort to navigate.

Coincidently, the practice has helped out with my ‘fiddle face’ as well. As I repeated those phrases it occurred to me how I made the exact same involuntary body jerk or mouth movement every damn time. So I’m also weeding those out one by one. A happy foot and a still face. Then of course I go on to play tunes not worrying about that stuff and concentrating on the sound coming out of the fiddle. The foot tapping is coming along, and every time I notice a pause or some erratic tapping happens, sure enough it’s another one of those “Oh no, here comes that phrase” moments.

Re: Whistle woes

I don’t think that you should put more emphasis on the first note of a group or phrase. Over-emphasis on the main beats of a jig (or any other tune for that matter) tends to produce unfortunate results - a pedestrian, clumping feel - that spell out to the listener that the player is trying to apply a rule they have been taught rather than playing the tune in a musical manner.

In fact I find that you are already emphasising the first notes of the groups pretty strongly, especially with the frequent double-tonguing separating that first note into two. I wonder where you picked that up from :-)

Gudrun talked about the differing length of notes. I agree with this point completely but it is much easier to produce on other instruments, especially fiddle, than on whistle. If you want to follow up this idea, the wrong way to do it, IMO, would be to make the first note louder rather than longer, which would give the unfortunate results I alluded to above. The best way I know of doing it on whistle is to tongue, not the first note, but the second two. You can use the tongue to shorten the middle note by isolating it. You’ll hear this technique prominently in the playing of Mary Bergin, Sean Potts, and many others. Not to be used constantly though, and very effective when the 2nd and 3rd notes are repeated.

I wouldn’t have said any of this had others not given you advice that I disagree with. I think you’re doing fine. Just relax and keep at it, play with as many people as you can, preferably people better or at least more experienced than yourself.

Here’s a mad thing I’ve never told people I do but might help. When I’m trying to get a different swing or bite to my playing - I will play the whistle just by blowing over the fipple to get the rough sound of the notes (i’m sure you know what I mean).
I have the idea of how the new rhythm of the tune should sound. I then literally just blow over the fipple and play the tune - imagining I’m playing it exactly as I want to - and just letting my fingers move about along to the idea of the tune in my head. I think that I start to get the new lilt to the tune now and again - and I don’t care so much about when my fingers or tonguing etc is slightly out of sync - it feels like I will start to get a phrase *exactly* as I want it maybe 80% or so - then I’ll start to work on that phrase ‘over the fipple’ until it sounds like the intended rhythm. Then I’ll start playing it ‘as God intended’ through the full whistle.

I hope that makes sense!

Look - I might be sending you on a heading to nowhere with that one - but a change is as good as a rest and you sound like you could benefit from a wee rest too ;-)
