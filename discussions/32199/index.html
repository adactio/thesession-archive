Re: Improvisation in Irish music

Saying that ‘improvisation’ is inappropriate while ‘variation’ is, is perhaps infusing these words with meanings that they might not carry with other people.

The words are not mutually exclusive. You can play variations which are mapped out in advance, or you can play variations which are improvised.

Seems that Irish musicians do a bit of both. For example the variations are often created in advance and stored away in memory; but WHEN they’re used is improvised, the variations being thrown in, in one spot in the tune or another, on the fly.

This aspect struck me when I sat down with four different versions of the same Paddy Keenan ‘party piece’, a hornpipe that he uses to show off all sorts of uilleann technique and amazing variations. (Two were from concert CDs, two were from bootleg tapes.) I transcribed all four versions in full, and the nature of the different versions was this: they all comprised of the same bits, just rearranged in varying orders. So, he has an arsenal or vocabulary of bits, of melodic fragments, a half-dozen different ways of playing certain phrases (while other phrases don’t vary). And he has an overall map of the arc of the piece, starting by introducing the tune in a straightforward way and putting in more and more complex variations as the tune goes along, ending up with spectacular staccato “backstitching”.

At the same time, no two presentations of the piece were the same.

This is the way I was originally taught Irish music, my teacher telling me “it’s better to learn twenty ways to play one tune, than to learn twenty tunes.” He would teach me a basic framework, then say “you have the tune, now let’s f**k with it!” and he would take me through a number of different ways of playing each phrase.

Different Irish players use different approaches. Some vary so much of the tune so often, each of their three playings of a tune being so fundamentally different from each other, that the listener never can get a notion of what the “core” tune might be. Other players will play a tune more or less the same way the first two playings, so that the listener has a clear idea of how the melody goes, and on the third playing put in a clear big variation, sort of serving it up on a platter for the listener.

Re: Improvisation in Irish music

It always amuses me how ITM players think they are unique. There is only one meaning to “improvisation,” and that is to play spontaneous melodic or harmonic lines based on the tune being played. You cannot separate “variation” from this beyond the fact that you may already know what variations you are going to play. However, much improvisation is also planned or at least anticipated from playing the same tune night after night if you are a performer.

Stylistically, improvisation in ITM is not going to sound like jazz any more than the improvisation in a classical cadenza does. Still, to the extent that you stray in any manner from the set melodic line (as you know it) of the tune, you are improvising. It seems to me that improvisation is part of the foundation of ITM, whether or not its practitioners think of it that way.

So improvise or harmonize to your heart’s content. If done well, the worst you’ll do is offend the closed-minded. When I have done it, it has met with much favor, for it can give new life to well-known tunes, provided you don’t throw off the other players or stray into territory that makes the tune sound less than Irish. If you love the music, you’ll know better than to do that.

Re: Improvisation in Irish music

I think it was Nate above that said that improvisation (in this music) should never be a substitute for knowing the tune, to which I can only reply, amen. And I would argue that is true in every type of music--those who think improvisation excuses them from learning the music are only fooling themselves--their playing is like a house built on sand. I have seen people show up (I remember one drunk whistle player in painful detail) who claim they can improvise during a session even if they don’t know the tunes, but I have never seen it work (see also, threads on the topic of ‘noodling’).
Sessions are a collective endeavor, with everyone playing at once. A gathering designed so that even folks who have never rehearsed together, and whose musical ability levels may vary from person to person, can make good music together. And, while some ornaments or embellishments or whatever can fit into the overall sound, generally, the more people stray from the melody, the more they detract from that collective sound. Even accompanists, when there are more than one or two of them, learn to consult with each other or take turns so as not to detract from the collective sound.
Moments of pure improvisation, even in jazz, are generally a solo endeavor. The other instruments shift to a support role to give the soloist room. And the soloist is not free to go anywhere, as there are changes that they are sticking to, a structure they must understand and hew to. And even in Dixieland, where there is polyphony going on, the different instruments have their roles that they stick to (cornet takes the middle, clarinet descant, trombone oom-pah, then maybe on the next go-round they trade roles). They stay in loosely defined lanes, so to speak.
So in a performance, the performer can do what they want. But in a session, improvisation to the point where the melody becomes a new melody is not desirable.

Re: Improvisation in Irish music

The distinction that I make between variation and improvisation is this:

Variation (while maybe being improvised) is a different expression of the same “musical idea”. You listen to the variation that John Carty or Kevin Burke (or whoever) may play, and it is unmistakable what tune they’re playing even though they’re using different notes, ornaments, and timing to make you think and feel differently when they play that piece of the melody. As has been mentioned, there is a big difference between playing solo and playing in a session. When you’re playing solo, you may reinterpret a phrase of a tune by actually switching modes, or pushing the boundaries in some other way that would sound horrible if you were playing with other people. When you’re playing in a session, the variation needs to be limited to what works well with what everyone else is playing. But you can still do it in an interesting, “eloquent” way. I would also say that the length of the variation is usually very short. Very rarely would you go further than two bars at a time. (In his research of this stuff, Eliot Grasso shows that players will often “apologize” for a wacky variation by returning to the core melody quickly, and staying there for a bit before throwing something else in…)

Improvisation, on the other hand, is what I think of as making up a whole new “musical idea” within the confines of a particular key and chord progression. This is more like what you would find in jazz, or a rock guitar solo… The length of the improvisation is generally much longer in time, and is never played with other people that are playing melody.

For variation to be successful in Irish trad, the player must be intimate with the tune, instead of “fluffing” over parts that they don’t know. I will also say that variation is really for the enjoyment of musicians who are familiar with the tune. Someone who doesn’t know the tune won’t even notice the variation a lot of the times. But there’s nothing more fun for me than sitting next to someone who is expressing the music eloquently! Some of the best times I’ve ever had playing music have been sitting next to someone I know and respect, throwing musical ideas back and forth and finding new ways to think about melodies that we’ve played for years.

Most discussions on this board would end if people grappled with the fact that the “tradition” of irish music encompasses a number of varying practices and geographies including now players outside of Ireland. Thankfully we haven’t as it is more fun.
This was originally dance music and still is for some people. I like the music to be played in certain way when i’m dancing a set and in a different way when i’m playing or listening. That’s my preference.

However most Irish music today is probably played as a group exercise, participatory or as a performance. This has different needs and may welcome more variation than I would like as a dancer, as a listener or a participant. Also as an intermediate or beginner player you may prefer the tunes to be fairly consistent. After 30 odd years playing you may welcome some variation or a lot of variation. .

The tradition does evolve as a response to what people need. If we take the long view, pub sessions are a fairly recent phenomenon. low whistles, banjo, etc are incorporated into the tradition if they work. bassoon has not (yet) been incorporated into the tradition. The “tradition” is arguably a set of different traditions.

Sean Nós is so called because something came to replace it. In short the tradition does evolve but not necessarily to incorporate the things that you or I may want it to. And that’s, I feel what makes it exciting.

Re: Improvisation in Irish music

As I see it (after being immersed in Irish music for around 25 years and also having studied musicology) is that improvisation does occur and should occur as an integral part of Irish traditional music. Listen to e.g. Johnny Doran.
It’s just that the rules and boundaries for improvisation in ITM are different than the rules and boundaries for
improvisation in the music forms that are the most widespread in the world today, i.e. blues, rock and jazz. Every musical genre has its own set of rules and boundaries for improvisation.
In blues, rock and jazz the rules are that you have a chord sequence over which you improvise. How much you
can deviate from the notes actually in the chords and what melodic sequences are ok differs between blues, rock and jazz and also between their sub-genres. If you are in a blues session and start to deviate into too much modern jazz solo style, it will probably be considered inappropriate by the other players.
In classical Indian music improvisation has nothing to do with any chord sequence, but instead the melodic rules that make up a certain raga together with classical Indian ornamentation practices set the boundaries for improvisation.
Improvisation in Irish music has more in common with how improvisation works in baroque music, where the lead musician is expected to ornament and vary the basic tune. One important difference is of course that baroque music has a definite (and explicitly composed) chord structure which also affects the improvisation, whereas Irish music only has a melody line (which leads to more or less implicit chords).
One rule for improvisation in Irish music are that you must “stick to the tune” - what that means more exactly is probably harder to define, but I know it when I hear it, and most of you probably do too. Playing harmony lines or improvising freely over the implicit chord structure of the tune is NOT part of “pure drop” ITM. However, bits of harmony lines might occur occasionally when two player play different variations.
Improvisation in ITM includes:
- ornamentation: rolls, cuts, crans, etc. Exactly what ornaments the player uses will vary every time around the tune (if the player is good).
- “long” notes. In the context of a tune mostly consisting of an endless line of quavers, a dotted crotchet or a minim feels like an ornament, so this should actually be included under ornamentation (or perhaps variation)
- melodic variation. E.g. ABAF, ~A3F, FAAF are often interchangeable, depending on their context in the melody. Where the limits of melodic variation lie is harder to define, but as I said I know it when I hear it.

Re: Improvisation in Irish music

A very informed and wise post there! Hear, hear! Hopefully it will put some of the nonsense to rest.

A good point about the variety of approaches to improvisation in the various genres of jazz, blues, and rock. I hear live Dixieland every day (at work) and the Dixieland players have often said how hard it is to find players who have a style suited to that genre. You can’t play Bebop in a Dixieland band! Or rock!

Also, each instrument (trombone, sax, trumpet for example) has its own distinctive form of improvisation, so much so that it would sound strange indeed if a sax player were to play a trumpet-style solo or visa versa. People who think that jazz is “anything goes” need to do a lot more listening! Each genre and each instrument within each genre has stylistic boundaries which are expected to be maintained.

Irish music likewise has clear stylistic boundaries, within which one is quite free to roam.

In my opinion Irish reels and jigs are built from a library of stock phrases, the traditional building blocks. Variations naturally arise because these phrases don’t exist in isolation but in families, each member of a family fitting equally well into a specific rhythmic and harmonic context. As the player goes along he (consciously or unconsciously) makes selections from these families. The result is that a tune can be played several times in a row, with no two playings being the same, yet all playings being equally valid presentations of “the tune”, all in fact being “the tune”. (Note that this is different from the “theme and variations” approach of Northumbrian piping.)

I’ve commented about this misunderstanding before here, but Henrik’s post is inaccurate in several ways.

Henrik wrote - ‘In blues, rock and jazz the rules are that you have a chord sequence over which you improvise.’

This is utterly incorrect as far as jazz goes, mildly incorrect concerning rock and alarmingly incorrect concerning the blues.

Yep, there are elements of the genre described as jazz which do involve improvisation over an agreed chord sequence, but, ever since Miles Davis discovered modes in the mid-1950s and released ‘Kind of Blue’ in 1959, jazz has moved a heck of a long distance from the rigidity of Dixieland, swing or bebop. Re: Improvisation in Irish music

Just because every tune in a particular genre doesn’t use exactly the same chords, that doesn’t mean they (and particulary any improvisations they contain) aren’t chord based.

What everyone here seems to have overlooked is that genres like rock and blues are song based, where ITM is tune based. In a song, the strummers and drummers repeat the same sequence of chords through several verses and choruses. When it comes to the break they just keep going with the same sequence while the soloist does his stuff over the top of it. So the improvisation is inevitably based on a chord sequence.

Traditional music, played in a traditional way isn’t like that - you play a tune a couple of times, then go on to a different tune with a different chord sequence. But what seems to be happening more and more these days is that recording artists like McGoldrick treat a tune as a song - they play the tune, then the strummers and drummers keep the rhythm and chord sequence going while the hero noodles for 16 bars, before they all launch into the next tune.

The nature of variation in ITM

Those of you still reading this Titanic thread may be interested in a academic treatment of this topic by piper Eliot Grasso in his Ph.D. dissertation, “Melodic variation in the instrumental dance music tradition of Ireland”:

https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/xmlui/handle/1794/11557

Very scholarly but also quite approachable. It also cuts right to the heart of many of the points being made here using a thorough analysis of a broad set of tunes drawn from recordings from the last 100 years.

Readers of this thread might be particularly interested in “Chapter 3: AESTHETIC CONSERVATISM IN IRISH TRADITIONAL MUSIC AND THE LIMITATIONS OF MELODIC VARIATION” (sound familiar?) and “Chapter 5: A METHOD FOR THE SYSTEMATIC ANALYSIS OF MELODIC VARIATION IN THE INSTRUMENTAL DANCE MUSIC TRADITION OF IRELAND” (which has the tune analysis and general conclusions).

EXTEMPORIZATION in Irish music

What about EXTEMPORIZATION in Trad Irish Music?

There are two sorts of backer:
1. The bloke that probably plays an acoustic guitar and knows all the chords to dozens or hundreds of tunes. No spontaneous composition, arranging at all!
2. The person who busks or extemporizes an accompaniment on the spot; a bit different each time a tune is played; winging it as if it’s the first time you ever heard the tune albeit with huge store of background knowledge to call on. Utterly spontaneous composition and arranging!!

A lot of very good backers are in category 2 so there must be a lot of improvization/ extemporization going on at lots of sessions.

BUT is improvization different from extemporization? ‘Improvization’ implies some sort of improvment, n’est-ce pas?

Latin Improuisus, im, not; pro, before; uidere, to see. i.e. you didn’t see it coming.

gam, the two terms are often used as synonyms by lazy journos, but the key difference lies in the unexpected ‘tempo’ of ‘extemporization’.

For instance, I could turn up at a session and improvise guitar accompaniment to whatever tunes were being played (not that I’d ever do such a thing).
