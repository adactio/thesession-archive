1) A roll and a turn consist of the same note patterns in most cases. The difference here is that a roll can have different notes in the case of quick rolls which are often used in jigs, or sometimes in reels where you really don’t need or want to hit the note below for whatever reason.

↳ Posted by Banjo Joe on March 12th, 2012.

Re: ornament name?

Jim’s spin on rolls is something I’ve heard a lot from violinists, and it’s wrongheaded. Irish fiddlers have an assortment of rolls at their fingertips, among good fiddlers, all of them done intentionally, not fluffed or “miscoordinated” at all.

A roll in this music is a note, followed by a series of cuts and returns to the note being rolled. Take a roll on E (1st finger, D string). You play the E, your middle or ring finger hits the string above the E, you hear the E again, and your 1st finger rises for a nanosecond, and then you hear the E again.

Some fiddlers (Brian Conway and Seamus Connolly come to mind) tend to play all of the notes clearly and more evenly spaced. You’ll hear them sound the ring finger G and get a glimpse of the open D in the E roll outlined above.

Other fiddlers, notably Kevin Burke, favor more percussive rolls, hanging on that first E, and the cuts (and even the middle E) are just flicks interrupting the E note. They aren’t “fluffed” at all, any more than the crisp cuts that Irish fiddlers use to distinctly sound two E notes in a row without changing bow direction. Which is exactly the same thing as the “G” cut in the E roll we’re describing.

And lots of fiddlers, James Kelly included, intentionally do a whole range of rolls, from open and notey to crisp and percussive, depending on what sound they’re after in the moment. I’ve heard James do them, and talk about them at length. It’s a standard part of the tradition, and unlike anything I’ve ever heard a violinist do.

Same goes for bowed triplets (“trebles”). Yes, violinists do triplets, but not cat-sneeze percussive triplets. I can’t tell you how many violinists (including concertmasters in big symphonies) have come up to ask about trebles because they love the punch and have no idea of the mechanics involved. Again, standard idiom in Irish traditional music.

David, in a big noisy session it doesn’t matter much, but in other cases I do tend to match my rolls to the timing and notey vs. percussiveness of other players’ rolls. That’s one advantage of learning to do rolls more than one way--to have an expressive range, and to be able to gibe well with other musicians.

On flute, the cuts and taps aren’t pitches, but you can choose whether to play them evenly spaced over the whole roll, or linger on the first note and bunch the cuts and taps into the latter half of the space of the roll.

dah-blah-blah
or
daaaaaah-bl-bl

and lots of timing options in between.
