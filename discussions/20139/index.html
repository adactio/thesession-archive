Of Woodpeckers, Life, and Traditional Music

Monday the lights went off. It was a little past noon when the un-interruptible power supply at my feet began to beep its warning. I finished and shut down the computer. I went outside with my dog. It was fifteen degrees Fahrenheit outside. Ice storms had coated most of the landscape and the weather had not changed for days.

The trees, solid hickories, oaks, and elms, glistened in the sunlight. I heard them groaning under the weight of the ice, a forest moaning and creaking all around me. Each woody sound was a warning not to be taken lightly. Trees had fallen in a number of places, and large branches had come crashing down from above and were now strewn everywhere. One could seek no solace in the illusion that this place was a still life as one walked beneath the overhanging threat.

The dog stopped and sniffed each article on the ground that seemed of some special importance to him, taking care to pull us into proximity of the shaggy, ice coated trees. Even without his urgings I had no choice but to walk below the ice weighted boughs hundreds of feet above me to reach the road, where one might look up and see clear sky and perhaps find a safer path. It was a long walk. Without power, I had no reason to bring my terrier to heel for a quick trip back inside, which the cold had made me do too often the past few days. When we’d finished we returned amid the chorus of groaning timber to a house still rendered lifeless. The utility assured me that the power would be back on by Friday. It was time to find a place with power, light, and heat, so I put the dog in my still warm bedroom, closed the door, and made my way to the car. (I’d return long before the bedroom temperature became uncomfortable for my canine friend.)

I should have filled the gas tank the day before. I knew it was bitter cold. The engine didn’t want to start. Condensation in the gas, I wondered. The engine started on the third try and seemed willing to run. I drove to the end of the little country road, to the place where the tee intersection joins it with the connecting county road. There is a little hill before one reaches that intersection at which one is required to stop, and my car, with the engine suddenly relieved of its burden at the crest, abruptly shut off. I keyed the starter. It ground away, but the engine remained lifeless. Cars were whirring past on the road in front of me. It was after noon, even after one, but the traffic went rushing by in a steady stream. It seemed strange for that time of day.

I saw something on the road in front of me out of the corner of my eye as I gazed down at the gauges set in the dash. It was small, and black and white and red ..a dead bird. It seemed still. I ignored it, then I saw, as I focused on the gasoline gauge and the Voltmeter, both lower than I would have liked, that the creature’s wings begin to flutter weakly, trying to lift it from the frozen street. It repeated the effort a second and a third time, so it was not the wind. The bird was mortally wounded, struck by a car, but not yet dead. I was sure the next car would finish it off. It was a woman in a sport utility vehicle with a cellular telephone glued to her ear. She missed the terrified woodpecker. The next car was an over-sized passenger truck, and once again, though the driver came very close, he missed. The bird tried again to escape from that mad rush of traffic as it lay broken and dying. That was when I saw the van behind me in the rear view mirror. It had stopped, waiting. I could not move. The van began to slide backward down the icy hill. It soon stopped as I held my breath. The bird remained on my mind. I could do nothing to start my car. I was not going to watch the bird crushed before me while it lay terrified and helpless.

I stepped out of the van amid a brief interlude in the traffic and picked up the woodpecker that lay in the street. I held it in one hand and gently stroked its back. It may have died in my hand, or moments before I reached it, but I would not not permit the crazed and oblivious stream of humanity zooming past to add insult to deadly injury by crushing this animal and rending its flesh. This was as close as I’d come to a woodpecker. Its cap was the usual crimson, and its wings and back the checked pattern of white and black that marks its kind. Its tongue stuck out of its mouth, still and unmoving, tipped with what appeared to be a rapier like appendage, no doubt useful for harpooning insects in the wood of a tree. I laid it on a snowbank beside the road. It lay there, still, unmoving as the frozen landscape.

In that gesture I did nothing for it. It had already been left to suffer and die by the representative of the human species that had careened down the country road and smashed it, probably as it sought to fly to that remnant of what had once been a larger wood from which I had come, where it might have better luck finding food. I merely sought to relieve its terror before it died. The onrush of humanity had felled most of the trees in the area long before I had arrived, and with it most of the creatures that had lived among them. How could the increased population in the area, comprised of motorists already too busy operating a car, a cellular telephone, and potentially a DVD player and satellite navigation system, be expected to act to avoid transforming creatures already stressed by the rages of winter into carrion? I walked back to the car and motioned for the driver behind me to go around, shouting that I could not get the vehicle to start. I did not know what had inspired him to wait as so long when he saw me exit the car on the driver’s side. Perhaps my behavior had offered some mild entertainment. He began to back up to go around me. There was no one behind him. I got in and shut the door. I laid on the starter. The engine came grudgingly to life. With the road before me still clear, more according to the norm for that time of day, I floored the gas pedal and amid the steady whine of tires upon ice crept forward onto the other road and down the hill.

What does this have to do with traditional music? Nothing, unless, like my gesture on behalf of a dying bird, it is to some extent a means of seeking to shape a semblance of sanity, of humanity, amid the uncaring madness that has become the rule in too many places. Perhaps there was a time when Christmas meant the same.

How about this one, instead? We should all be so clever as Martin Stillion, who wrote this:

On the circumference of a cistern
A Cistercian sister sits.
She’s cheerfully chewing chokecherries
And spitting out the pits.
On a stringed Celtic cittern
She assiduously strums
Whilst wistfully she wishes
That the chokecherries were plums.
She’s schlepped out to the cistern
From her Cistercian cloister,
Where the sisters step in silence,
Not a whisper nor a roister.
Taciturnity disgusts her,
So she’s clambered through the brambles--
Now she looks a tattered slattern,
Her skirts a shameful shambles.
Fifteen ferrous frets she fingers
And so stridently she sings
That the bitterns flee her cittern,
Wrens and warblers take their wings.
Any humans within hearing
Would certainly have hissed her,
This Cistercian cistern-sitting
Celtic cittern-strumming sister.
Suddenly she ceases singing
And she gasps in consternation:
A chokecherry she was chewing
Has fulfilled its appellation.
Were the cloister somewhat closer,
Then those nuns who won’t palaver
Might hear two successive splashes:
Chordophone and then cadaver.
This unfortunate, stentorian,
Chokecherry-chewing sister
Sinks with her cittern in the cistern
With no one to assist her.
So mind the maestro’s maxim:
It’s unbearably uncouth
To sing, in choir or solo,
With something in your mouth.
---Martin Stillion
