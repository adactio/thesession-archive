Here’s my own input:

Our session happens weekly (Saturday afternoons) in a pub. It’s an open session lasting about three hours, and the standard of play is a mixture of “advanced beginners” to “advanced.”

There can be as few as five players or as many as fifteen.

On average, it’s about 70% Irish tunes, the rest made up of Scottish, Canadian/Quebecois, and a few odds and sods from other places. Normally there’s no songs, but sometimes a nice singer will show up and we’ll get him or her to sing four or five.

Anyway: I’m trying to become a regular at a Friday night pub session about 10 minutes from where I live. It’s anchored by (from what I gather) a trio comprising fiddle, accordion and guitar, although one or two may have a substitute sit in for them in a given week.
The repertoire is almost solidly Irish, but the regular guitar player likes to throw in country-flavored songs every so often.
Technically, I’m told, it is an open session, but they would seem to prefer experienced -- there’s another session at the same pub earlier in the week which is more inclusive of all levels.
I’m acquainted with one of the session “anchors,” who had invited me to sit in in the first place, and he said they haven’t publicized it very widely. The primary reason is the venue itself: To be put it mildly, it has to be one of the noisiest places I’ve ever played in. They stick the musicians around a table in the middle of a room with very high ceilings, and as a result it can be very difficult to hear one another even in such close quarters. There are mikes and pick-ups for the musicians to use, but unfortunately the speakers are in other parts of the room, so it’s as if you’re playing with a one or two-second delay.

But the musicians, who are regulars at other sessions, are top-drawer, so if one is willing to put up with less-than-ideal surroundings it’s worthwhile.

Re: Tell us a bit about your session

I’m glad someone asked this question, because I don’t currently intend to list my regular session here, but don’t mind describing it “confidentially” :-)

WHERE: Pub.

PLAYERS: 2-15ish, averaging half a dozen. Most often this would be guitar, pipes, fiddles, flutes.

OPEN/CLOSED: Hmmm. A difficult question, often discussed here. Some of us have played together for a long time, but everyone was new once. We have accommodated a lot of visiting musicians of various styles and abilities. But I think it would only be honest to say most of the regulars would not want to see any bodhrans in this session, or boom-chuck guitarists etc. We have sufficient regular backers who know how we tend to play and don’t f**k it up. And that’s not to say there isn’t a place for bodhrans or loud, driving guitarists. But it’s not here :-)

FREQUENCY: Weekly.

IRISH TUNES: 95%+

SONGS: Depends on the number of singers, duration of the session, general mood etc. We normally have at least one singer, who will sing a couple of songs. We have some excellent regular and semi-regular singers, but it certainly isn’t a free-for-all and the main focus is tunes.

LEVEL: I’m not sure what you mean by “advanced”, but by my definition there will always be at least a couple of advanced players there. But there’s often a mixture of skill levels. For example, we had a blow-in fiddle player from the USA who seemed to know about 30 tunes, it was a quiet night, so he got to play all of them :-)

-----

Here are some notes I wrote when we were considering listing it here, for what they’re worth (including some repetition of what I’ve already said):

SIZE/FEEL: The session varies in size from a couple of people to a dozen or more. How things go on any particular night depends on who’s there and what humour we’re in. Some nights are busy, some are quiet, sometimes there’s more talk than tunes. If things get really quiet we might put some Billie Holiday or Saw Doctors on, or discuss the relative merits of John Coltrane and Miles Davis, as you do.

SPEED: We don’t play at warp speed, but neither is it a “slow session”. The pace is steady-to-moderately quick, with a fair bit of swing. We can play fast, and may do elsewhere, but here it’s about melody, rhythm and phrasing before speed. Well, most nights anyway; we’ve been known to play faster when we’ve had a few drinks.

BACKERS: It’s our opinion that “backing” instruments can make or break a session; all too often it’s the latter. We have a house-trained guitarist, and we don’t need another backer.

BODHRANS: They say there’s a time and a place for everything. But this session is not the time or the place for a bodhran, sorry. And that means YOU, whoever you are, and however good you may be. The best of players seem to be playing a different rhythm to the way we play here, and we’ve had our fair share of bashers too.

TUNES: Don’t worry if you don’t know many tunes; we were all in that situation once, and most of us were indulged by other sessions. You’re much more likely to get slagged for playing a flash but boring tune rather than a “simple” one. If you play lots fewer tunes than us and it’s a quiet night you’ll probably get to call the tune anyway … if you want to, no pressure, we don’t sit in a circle or anything. If you play lots more, how about playing one or two a week so we can learn them? If we all put down our instruments and gather round the bar you may have played a few too many, or infringed session etiquette in some other way.

ETIQUETTE: Those of us lucky enough to grow up playing in sessions weren’t aware there was such a thing, or wondered what all the fuss was about. If that doesn’t describe you, do yourself a favour and search this site for some clues. In a way it’s a subset of “pub etiquette”, and is about not “taking the p*ss”.

Re: Tell us a bit about your session

Well, I’m a bit new to mine, but I’ll tell you my perceptions:

WHERE: It depends. There are officially two, but the same people seem to go to both. One meets in a pub on a Sunday evening once a month, the other meets in a meeting room at the NOAA facility at UCSC once a month. The latter is officially a “slow session,” though, as I’ve said, the same people seem to go to both.

OPEN OR CLOSED? Open. Very nice, friendly, welcoming atmosphere for newbies. Session regulars seem very friendly and helpful.

SIZE: Again, I’ve only gone to two (one in each location), but it seems to be a small enough group. At both I’ve gone to, there have been about 10-15 people, give or take, including myself. Generally a nice (and often eclectic) mix of instruments. At the last one, we had two bodhrans, 4 soprano whistles, 1 low whistler who also played the uillean pipes, a fiddle, two guitars, a recorder and a couple of vocalists. The previous one also boasted a harper and a flutist.

ETIQUETTE: We take turns introducing ourselves and starting tunes. Whoever starts the tune sets the pace, though we try to keep the first run-through fairly slow. We play at least three times through a tune, unless it’s all falling to pieces, and after the third run-through, those who want to can pick up the pace. More experienced players tend to hang around later than newer players and play faster/more complicated stuff. Some performance pieces are OK, but the general trend is to play things all (or at least many) can join in on. Several people do use printed music, and there is no stigma against this.

At the pub session, it’s considered good form to at least order a drink, even if it’s only a soda. Many people come early and have a bit of supper. It’s a family-friendly pub, so spouses and children seem to enjoy coming along, having a bite and maybe playing a bit of billiards or darts, even if they’re not into the music. At the NOAA “slow session,” it’s good form to bring something to share, as people tend to break for munchies and socialization part-way through.

All-in-all, I figure I really lucked out. As a beginner to sessions, and a person with a fairly low sense of self-esteem, I couldn’t have designed a better situation if I’d custom-ordered it. I’d heard so many complaints on message boards about “session newbies,” I was very nervous about the idea of participating, but these people seem much more interested in passing on and enjoying the music than in being exclusive, and they’re helpful rather than scornful if you make a faux pas.

Re: Tell us a bit about your session

I always thought the primary rhythm instrument of Irish music was whichever one happened to be playing the tune at the time.

In fact, at all the ceilis and sessions I’ve been to, the rhythm always comes from one person (or maybe two, if they’ve played together a long time), a melody player who sets the pace and amount of lift. Everyone else keys off that.

Of course, with several strong players in the room, each of them will likely take turns, albeit subliminally, being the alpha rhythm machine, and I’ve heard bodhrans take their turn in this way, either to pump the energy up a notch, or when some other noise (e.g., a stampede of hardshoes on a raised stage in a cavernous hall) drowns out the fiddles, whistles, concertinas, etc.

In short, who carries the rhythm, and whether a drum leads or follows, can fluctuate. The “rules” for how this works are unspoken and perhaps ineffable. Reminds me of the poor guy in Not Another Teen Movie whose role is to start the “slow clap” scene, which he does at precisely the wrong times, repeatedly, throughout the movie. Until the end, when he’s so gun shy, someone else does it at the one *right* time.

Re: Tell us a bit about your session

Heh, grego, I was getting around to it, but life conspired against me this evening. Till now.

My local sesh (the only one in town, in fact the only one for 100 miles around) happens every Tuesday night in the tap room of a micro-brewery. Free beer for the players, no smoking, willowy tapstresses, publicans who bury us in kindness and generosity, and loyal, friendly punters.

There’s a core of about 6-8 players who can be counted on to carry the tunes every week. Another dozen filter in and out. Once in a blue moon it’s just four of us. On holidays we balloon up to as many as 20 players.

Our session is fairly open, although 8 years into it, word has gotten around that we’re serious about not getting derailed by snare drums, jazz piano, or weekly renditions of Orange Blossom Special. That said, we encourage interested beginners, anyone’s welcome to start a tune, and we do veer into the odd Johnny Cash song or Cajun two step about once a month. Ages range from teenagers to 50s (our eldest player passed away two years ago at 61.) Most of us are 40s and 50s. Out of town visitors are not uncommon--some people sit in fairly freqeuntly from other parts of Montana, and we’ve had guests from Canada, the east and west coasts, and a surprising number from Ireland. We’re also “open” in the sense of including a hammered dulcimer player, sometimes having up to four bodhrans going at once (but in unison), pausing for the occasional song, etc. Last night someone showed up with a silver flute and not much knowledge of the tunes. He was welcomed and invited back. We also do a monthly tune learning session to help people learn about the music and sessions, and to build a common repertoire of tunes.

On most nights, we play 95% Irish trad tunes. The rest consists of a few Scottish and Cape Breton tunes, a waltz from New Mexico, and the rare but always entertaining Czech or Lebanese song from one of our more well-travelled members. When our mayor showed up last night, two of us played a limping version of Hail to the Chief. The bulk of our tunes are reels, jigs, and slip jigs, with forays into hornpipes, polkas, barndances, highlands, flings, and the occasional waltz, mazurka, strathspey, march, and air.

In the course of 3 hours, we typically hear 2 to 4 songs. When the punters are really boisterous, or the tunes are just flowing, we might get only 1 or 2 songs.

We welcome all levels and abilities. A handful of us have been playing this stuff for 20 years or more and can hang with the big boys when we get the chance. We also have some really talented musicians who’ve migrated into Irish trad from other genres and quickly (in four years or so) became top notch players. And some of us are less experienced but no less enthusiastic. Everyone is good craic--beyond the session we are all neighbors, co-workers, friends.

Our usual instrumentation includes:
fiddles
whistles
wooden flute
bodhran
guitar
concertina
mandolin
hammered dulcimer

Some nights we also have:
uilleann pipes
bouzouki
tenor banjo

Just noticed the bit from Orson Carte about bodhran players playing a different style from the players at the session. I’ve noticed that and discovered why. Many people now attend bodhran classes, I didn’t believe it either, and I think they are taught rhythms without music. I don’t know anything about theory of rhythms, 2-4 and all that means nothing to me. You play to the music that is there. Unfortunately many new players, quite good players, seem to have the one basic style and rhythm, and that’s the one that fits with Gerry O’Connor type tunes, or Appalachian/Irish as I refer to them. When they come across “old fashioned” session players, they cannot adjust. Well what do you expect if you learn without music, that teaches you not to listen to what is being played.

Now when you have bodhrans playing a rhythm that doesn’t fit, you are in trouble, and would be best saying something to the bodhran brigade.
I also believe that the bodhran does not travel well. I imagine the good old US of A is full of “wail away” players. Maybe I’m wrong, first time for everything, eh.
