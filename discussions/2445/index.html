Improvisation

Recently, one of our session members, an accomplished fiddle player and teacher, attended a workshop on improvisation at Bristol University. She reported that the workshop was apparently intended for jazz and blues, so didn’t think it was particularly applicable to Irish trad, or at any rate in that format.
This set me thinking. Passing on interesting philosophical speculations whether improvisation can indeed be “taught”, or whether it can be a substitute for the hard work of learning tunes, has there been, is there, or could there be any place in Irish music for improvisation, and if, so what form would it take?
Any views?
Trevor

Re: Improvisation

If you mean improvisation against the existing structure of the tune, I’d say it happens all the time in Irish music. That’s what all the variations--melodic, rhythmic, placement and style of ornamentation, etc.--are all about. It’s usually more nuanced than you’ll find in jazz, more closely bound to the melody line than the underlying chord progression (though this is not always the case), but no less improvisational.

Another way to think of it is that the trad musician’s bag of chops is maybe smaller than a full-blown jazz player’s. On fiddle, for instance, we use rolls, triplets, cuts, doublestops, slides, and slurs, and a minimum of vibrato, off-the-string bow work, pizzicato, or up-the-neck playing. A jazz fiddler is much more likely to move around the neck, explore the insturment’s dynamic range, and use different bow attacks, a range of vibrato, trills, etc, resulting in a wider variety of sounds to improvise with.

Also, since we’re playing dance music, us tradheads tend to stick within a fairly narrow range of rhythmic variations compared to jazz. Get too syncopated or far from the beat, and it no longer sounds Irish or traditional.

But I’d still say we improvise when we play. It’s that old ideal of changing the tune a bit every time around, so you’re never playing it quite the same way twice. As your bag of chops grows, you find an almost infinite variety of ways to string the bits together to fit the tune.

Re: Improvisation

The straight forward improvising of the placement of rolls etc in diddly music is a well established and essential part of it. But some people I play with like to take this further. Improvising harmonies on the hoof is great fun, espacially when there’s a few of you doing it. This can sound really bad, but the harmonic variation within most diddly tunes is very limited and predictable anyway, so its not as hard as it might appear. The viola is particularly suited to diddly harmonies, espacially when played with two or three fiddles. Its register sits perfectly in between the usual strummers and the melody, a space normally left unoccupied.

Re: Improvisation

In Irish music, I think you have to work the other way round, Rog. You don’t start with the improvisation or the chord structure, you start with the tune itself, and work your way round from there. To me, variation is playing with the tune, finding different ways of saying the same thing, or perhaps I mean saying the same things and telling the same story in different ways. (A different “setting”, now, usually changes the inherent chord structure, but that a whole ’nother ball of wax -- and argument, because then you have to start arguing about “inherent” chord structures, which a purist will tell you doesn’t exist in this stuff -- than the subject of variation.)

Usually, improvisation (using how I *think* Trev is defining it, though I could be way off the mark) is using the structure of the tune as a sort of framework to hang your chops (god I hate that word, almost as much as “tasty” where it refers to music) around. So for session playing, it’s not much good, because the goal there is to be playing with people, more or less in unison given different variations and settings. Solo work, I’d find it more acceptable.

What I really really REALLY can’t deal with is Tweetys, who don’t know the tune at all and sort of just riff and noodle around the chords in order to be “playing” with everyone. Yeargh.

I think Zina’s waryness of the tweeters as she so wonderfully puts is the crux of the waryness we all should have about improvising over diddly music. And its the same problem as taking the baroque or the jazz approach to improvising. In baroque and jazz music, the structure and harmony is the stuff at which to base your improvs. Diddly music however is about pure melody and zina is right when she sggests that if you improvise over the melody to the chords that the melody suggest, your just gonna get a major/minor third an fith based harmony that all its gonna do is sound pretty and not offend the strummers.

A completly different approach is needed that bases itself squarly on the melody alone and the first prerequisit is to know the melody inside out (this is why we hate the tweeters.)

For example: if a phrase in a tune moves up in fourths (which happens often in diddly tunes) then improvise in forths. And I don’t meen arround the forth note of the key your in, but arround the interval of fourths. Open your ears to it, it’s new.
