Timbral manipulation in Irish music

I don’t wish to start a war, but Tommy Potts’s tone on the violin isn’t all that great. It’s very scratchy. I don’t know why my ears are suddenly hearing this. It feels like I hear the bow more than the notes. I listened to people like Paddy Glackin. Much better, but still a bit scratchy. I don’t know if He just doesn’t keep the bow straight or his technique is off. Maybe fiddle technique is just different. I don’t know. I’m curious to hear everyone else’s thoughts maybe someone can change my mind. I think I finally understand what makes Irish music complex, and people have certainly done new things with it. Like Beoga. I’m just not sure these old recordings hold up. Perhaps the scratchiness is to create a rhythm and my theory is that’s the case. Perhaps it’s necessary for the complexity of the music. Timbral manipulation. Of course this isn’t what they were thinking about, but I think it’s important to bring up because if you listen for it, It definitely adds to the music sadly if you listen to the old Masters there definitely is a scratchiness to their timbre. Perhaps that’s the purpose of the pedal note at the full speed. Perhaps that’s what lift is. Has nothing to do with Melody everything to do with the rhythm of the scratch on the fiddle. Of course I read some of The book that was recommended to me, and it seems that most of the tunes are the result of composition and what is idiomatic to either The pipes or the fiddle. The fiddle creates rhythm better in my eyes. I’m not sure how this applies to other instruments though. Like the flute, but if I was to theorize it’s manipulation of the diaphragm and The embouchure. The poppiness for wind and reed instruments perhaps. The manipulation of the air flow into the concertina and bosca cheoil. According to the book I read, Irish music is all about finding a note that the music returns to, not the tonic, but The note that is most common in any given tune. It’s all about ornamentation of that note and the variation and emphasis that The musicians place on that note.

Re: Timbral manipulation in Irish music

I heard Tommy Potts did listen to classical music a lot (which is not unrelated to his distinctive style). But even in this case: you’re absolutely right Pól. Classical music sound is not an absolute yardstick. There’s no reason why Potts should have tried to imitate the clean sound of classical violinists more than the sound of his peers in traditional music. His timbre made sense in his context, his era, the musicians he played with. It is inextricably linked to his style and he was respected for it. Personally, I find vulnerability, directness and sincerity in it.

Maybe this sound was a choice. Maybe it’s also the reflect of a social relationship to music where a clean sound was just not the priority. Maybe the priority was the dance. Maybe it was contemplation. Maybe it was just playing the tune for its own sake after a long work day, like you would hum a tune in your kitchen. Or just sharing the melody with others and finding a common ground for swing and rhythm. I don’t think players had as much time to practice as we tend to have nowadays. But that doesn’t make their sound lesser than, say, Martin Hayes’s.

Also, technique is just a way to achieve a given musical standard. You can’t ask people who play according to standards other than yours to have the technique *you* consider to be good technique. Expecting another sound from Bobby Casey or Paddy Canny seems a strange way of approaching music from the past. Their sound reflects who they were in their era, and it is what makes their style beautiful. Music is so much more than a clear timbre or perfectly tempered notes. You can find a tremendous joy in ambiguity, scratchiness, obscurity. Modern trad music has its own standards and they’re not the only interesting and acceptable ones.

I was helped in this slow opening-up by listening to other players that are deeply familiar with their predecessors but remained more accessible to my modern ears: Aidan Connolly, MacDara and Mícheál Ó Raghallaigh, Caitlín Nic Gabhann, Mary Bergin, Noel Hill, etc. During that journey I spoke with players more experienced than me, who had a deep love for older recordings and found treasures of style and creativity in them, and I tried to understand their way of listening to these recordings. Then one day you find yourself learning a tune from Con Cassidy or Julia Clifford and you just don’t have the sense of oddness you had before while listening to them, and you begin to appreciate their uniqueness and looking for details in their playing to which you hadn’t paid attention before. But I feel it’s because I listened to other contemporary musicians before that I’m able to understand better the playing of the elders, because it gave me some sense of context and a better understanding of the multiplicity of Irish music. Learning to listen outside of modern CD music standards and to hear past music for its own intrinsic qualities is such a rich experience.

Re: Timbral manipulation in Irish music

I tried to read everything above but I might have missed it: there’s a particular fiddle ornament that, with many fiddlers, is intentionally “scratchy” even when their playing otherwise isn’t.

Cleaned up and Classicised it’s a “bowed triplet” though with many fiddlers it’s not a triplet per se.

As best I understand it, under extra pressure the bow changes directions a number of times in a very short span of time, the distance the bow travels back and forth being extremely short. In other words it’s completely different from a Violinist performing a bowed triplet where ordinary bow pressure is used and the bow is travelling back and forth long enough to give all three notes a pure clean tone.

Back in the 1970s a friend picked up his first Irish album, which featured Tommy Peoples. He didn’t know what to make of the intermittent scratchy noise. He thought the album was defective.

Re: Timbral manipulation in Irish music

I find this topic, for some reason, makes me a bit sad.

Looking past the clunky title and the clunky prose, the OP posits that Tommie Potts’ violin tone could be better. Tommie Potts doesn’t need me or anyone else to defend his tone, his playing, or his interpretation, but let’s accept the OP’s own personal preferences at face value. I personally find the tone wild and beautiful, but that’s me. That is fine and normal and wouldn’t it be terrible if we all liked the same things equally.

The OP generously posits that there may have been some rhythmic or other intention to “the scratchiness”, as they put it. Separate criticisms of Tommie Potts from the OP include his rhythm and tempo. Again, this is all fine – even the legendary Tommie Potts can be put to scrupulous acoustic analysis.

And yet… to me to do so misses the point so widely, so totally, and so completely as to cause sadness. Tommie Potts, one of the most sensitive and unique musicians ever to spring up in our tradition, by all accounts was deliberate in his rubato timing, deliberate in his improvisation, deliberate in his choice of tunes. He produced a record that was so completely, utterly different from anything else in the tradition, yet so completely, utterly part of the tradition, there was nothing and no one like it before, and nothing and no one like it since.

Focusing on Tommie Potts’ tone, while I guess is legitimate in some academic sense, misses the point of what Tommie Potts has to say. Tommie Potts used Irish music as a means for self-expression and to me was so successful at this, that the record – for all the limitations of the recording technology of the time and place – has undiminished power, even today. More today. It’s the first record I put on for musicians from other musical traditions when I want to demonstrate the depth of this music, and I still delight in their reactions.

I don’t want to make the OP feel bad at all. But if they can’t enjoy the Tommie Potts record, of all of them, because of some shortsighted focus on “tone”, the my overwhelming feeling is one of sympathy, genuine sympathy and deep, deep regret for a missing gift.

Re: Timbral manipulation in Irish music

Having read a few of his posts, Kellie strikes me as that dangerous thing: a free thinker. Dangerous because, inadvertently or otherwise, they challenge norms that some would rather not have challenged. That’s why such people frequently attract opprobrium that they probably don’t deserve; in fact, they may well be doing everyone a service by raising issues that benefit from being raised.

Kellie seems to come from outside the tradition, or at least to operate well beyond it in his analysis of music. It may be why he doesn’t ‘get’ some things that one is supposed to get within the tradition. But that may not make his analysis wrong.

I sympathise. I did not grow up in the tradition either, though I’ve been playing ITM for decades. Most of that was pre-internet when I did not have the access to the culture that we take for granted today. My family’s music was (and is) almost all classical. Being subjected to that was where my ears and expectations were trained.

In recent years, the need to explain the mindset of the tradition to newcomers has made me realise just how much of it I have totally absorbed, but I never manage to quite rid myself of that outside critic.

I too struggle with some of those old masters. It seems to me too easy (uncritically?) to hail them as geniuses when their genius may, in the bigger scheme of things, only be visible to relatively few individuals with very specific, esoteric - ifexperienced - tastes. “Genius” is an over-used phrase – but it is also a human-defined one, and I think it is risky when applied to people whose genius is only apparent to a few members of a relatively small pool of admirers; the potential for group-think is too great. Of course, this is a widespread phenomenon that goes far beyond trad. And I think it’s an endearing feature of the Irish mindset to hail almost everyone as “legendary” 😊.

To a wider audience, I suggest many of those recordings do sound as Kellie describes, and while I can understand the idea that specialisation can refine one’s appreciation in unexpected ways, in more general terms, I think it is not unreasonable to conclude that the sound some of those players made would define them, in more widely accepted terms, as “not very good”. After all, the roots of this music are largely untutored, and the difference between untutored rubbish and untutored genius may not be as big as is sometimes thought. I have enough personal experience of fiddle playing to know what an untutored player can *very easily* sound like without even trying!

I don’t think it is reasonable just to move the goalposts and define their genius as being solely their ability to realise their own intent. By that metric, all of us may be geniuses!

It’s of course possible that the opposite argument applies, and those players did know precisely what they were doing and wanting to achieve – but the fact is, this is probably unknowable. In which case, either argument still applies. To a fair extent, genius is an externally applied evaluation, and just claiming that everyone else simply does not know what they are talking about cannot be valid.

With particular respect to the fiddle/violin, we have an instrument with an immense capacity for varied sound – and a huge challenge required to realise it. While defining the sound of that instrument as any one thing is probably wrong, the fact is, only some people are skilled enough to extract its full potential. For all the reservations one might have about “western art music”, a highly skilled classical player is more likely to have the refinement and breadth of technique to extract more of the potential of the instrument than anyone else. I only need play alongside my classically trained sister to hear the difference!

Accepting that is not somehow to deny the validity of other types of music or playing, nor to agree that classical music should have a monopoly on defining what “good” is, even if it thinks it should. It is simply to acknowledge their relative position in the bigger picture of musical potential.

Perhaps a useful question to ask is,” Would those players have *chosen* to sound scratchy even if they had had the ability not to?”.

Re: Timbral manipulation in Irish music

My guess is that, fiddle tone aside, Austin’s problem with Tommie Potts’ playing of the Butterfly is that he wrote it as a hop jig instead of as a slip jig, and played it as such. The Butterfly is the most “popular” tune on this website (meaning, I believe, that it has been added to more tune books than any other tune), and it has been popularized as a slip jig, rather than a hop jig…

Slip jigs are played smoother than a hop jig. As good examples, here are a couple of videos of the tune played in the different styles:

Slip Jig: https://youtu.be/wA0Ja5g_e6Q?si=ApTaxBOx73Uiq0xH&t=89
Hop Jig: https://youtu.be/mTEWtrmC0TM?si=BYfp3iS-ed5HAbTP

You may find that you like slip jigs more than hop jigs, but as with all the other tunes in this tradition, hop jigs are meant to be played (and danced to) with a fairly specific rhythm. Besides the fact that he wrote the tune, Tommie’s rhythm is intentional and correct.

Re: Timbral manipulation in Irish music

Kellie - not sure which recordings of Tommy Potts you listened to (some VERY good videos shared in this discussion) - but please, always take into consideration that the recording equipment for the time of many older recordings may not have been good, or the material it was recorded on may have deteriorated. The choice of strings was nothing like we have today - AND, consider the condition of older folks’ hearing… after playing many years in loud venues.
‘Scratchiness’ has nothing to do with rhythm - now-a-days any ‘noise’ picked up by an improperly placed mic (or wrong kind) would be edited in post processing. Everyone’s hearing is flooded (today) by recordings & music videos that have be edited & processed to death - but DAW didn’t come into the picture until the 90’s.
Timbre for fiddles depends not only on bowing pressure, speed, and where the bow lies between the bridge & fingerboard - also what position the fingers are on the fingerboard, type of rosin, bow hair quality/condition, type & brand of STRINGS, where the bridge/soundpost is position, AND material/workmanship of the instrument. Timbre is color/texture & folks have personal preferences.
‘Lift’ is energy in a groove, helping dancers anticipate what they need to do - so if they need to hop & skip, a fiddler should drive a little to get them there (bow speed, louder dynamics, more smaller subdivisions of notes feels faster, etc..) . ‘Lilt’ is probably a bad choice of terms, because there is an Irish Dance called the ‘Lilt’ and a type of singing, called ‘Lilting’. I think it’s better to think in terms of GROOVE, but here’s an article you might gleam something from, just realize the source states ‘Lift’ & ‘Lilt’ are different - but when described, I believe they both refer to ‘groove’.
https://fiddle-online.com/blog/listening-skills/lift-vs-lilt/
THE note you want to feel returning to in a tune is supposed to be the tonic - if you don’t, then you may have mis-identified the key - it may be a mode & you are feeling the tonic of a mode.
