Concertina - Why C/G?

I have been learning the D/G melodeon and, as a matter of curiosity have borrowed a 30 Key Anglo concertina from a friend. Following Caitlin Nic Gabhann’s fine online tutorial, I have managed to learn the basics of the Primrose lasses and have a bit of an idea about how the keyboard is laid out.

My Question: Why are C/G Anglo Concertinas the norm in Irish music, especially considering that D and G are much more common keys in which to play. Especially (considering only the two main rows) the fact that there is only a single f# on the pull and none on the push, and c# requires use of the Accidentals row. Neither being exactly rare notes in ITM. In fact, should it not be possible to play a lot of this music on a 20 key, two row D/G?

Practically, I will be doing effectively that when I try to play tunes from this tradition on the D/G Box. The only drawback there is the greater weight of a 14 bass box which makes frequent, rapid direction changes more difficult, though I am building up my arm strength and coordination on this bit by bit. It seems to be a very specific form of strength and control which is required.

2. Everyone playing C/G is just too invested in the system to consider changing everything…

As far as I can see, there has been no argument which has persuaded me of an objective advantage to the C/G. In fact, more the opposite…

There is however a valid subjective advantage of the modern 30 key C/G due to the fact that if you want to fit in and learn from the body of developed wisdom and skill in the community then it is best to be playing the same instrument as them; i.e. the C/G 30 button.

So as someone already buying into the D/G (Melodeon) system, I probably will not go too far down the road of the C/G at this time. I am, though, finding it enlightening to understand how and why concertina players play like they do.

(I have not included the third school of thought which is that there is a secret cartel of C/G Anglo dealers agitating to keep the prices of C/G concertinas high…)

Re: Concertina - Why C/G?

I went through the same state of confusion when I decided on my own first concertina. Having played ITM on the harmonica (essentially a one-row bellowless concertina) for several years already, I’d always used harmonicas in G and D, so a G/D concertina seemed the obvious choice. Imagine my surprise when I found out that practically all players use a C/G. After a bit of research, I came to pretty much the same conclusion as you do: there’s no fundamental reason, it’s just a lock-in effect. Everyone plays C/G, therefore all the makers only make C/G, all the teachers only teach C/G, all the second-hand instruments are C/G…

Being the rebel that I am, I went and got a G/D. I’ve been playing that for about 7 years now and never regretted it. I miss a few notes on the high end, but those are hardly ever used anyway. I have a few more on the bass side, which can be fun for backing songs and the likes. Since the G/D is a fourth lower, playing the same tune means playing in a (relatively) higher register, so I do more work with the right hand. That means you have fewer options for two-hand ornamentation, though as a box player you’ll be fine with that. It also frees up your left hand more for the occasional bass note or chord - which you have a lot more options for on a G/D.

So anyway, both are perfectly fine. Learning the C/G gives you the advantage of being able to buy a second-hand instrument, and a wider choice of teachers. Learning the G/D means you can be smug about it, like I am. I’d say the choice is obvious!

Re: Concertina - Here’s Why C/G….

On a C/G Anglo it is possible to play long runs of notes with no change of bellows direction, should one want to. The fingering is neither obvious not intuitive but once a few basic patterns are learned, the instrument is capable of more expression and subtlety of phrasing than either the English concertina or a D/G Anglo. I don’t know about other systems (Hayden, Duet, etc).

The few D/G players that I know tend to play up and down on one row -- similar to playing in the key of C on the C/G, but without shifts to the G row -- rather than the cross-row playing that makes the C/G so interesting. There are more bellows changes on the D/G, which necessitates a more staccato, “bumpy” sound. Perfect for English music (Morris) but perhaps not ideal for ITM.

On a slightly modified C/G concertina it is possible (though not necessarily desirable) to play the scales in D, G, and A in one direction. I have a 33 button Anglo, which has, among other notes, an added middle E draw and an F# push. There are a few arcane changes to the standard 30 button keyboard on my C/G, but that’s another discussion. I suspect that many people who prefer a D/G Anglo, and who think that it makes no sense to learn the C/G, haven’t ever mastered the initial illogic of the C/G. They have been put off by the initial learning process and have accepted the limitations of the more accessible, though more limited, D/G concertina.

You can of course play Irish music on a D/G Anglo, or a piano accordion, or saxophone. But the pure drop seems to work best on fiddle, timber flute, C/G Anglo, or Uillean pipes. There are outliers (great players of ITM) on other instruments, but we define something from the center, not from the edges.

Re: Concertina - Why C/G?

There’s one point that may have no real bearing on the intent of the thoughts people have expressed, but I think should be addressed for technical clarity. That point is how the tuning of an instrument is named. I see several references to D/G instruments here that I believe to be unintentionally misstatements but fear use of the term will confuse others following the discussion. I think those using that tuning reference actually mean G/D.

The common anglo concertina may have two rows (as in the basic 20-button model) or three rows (30-button model). Although less common, I’ve seen 21, 22 and 26 button versions as well as 31, 33, 38 and 40 button versions. Regardless, they all have the same basic 20-button two-row core layout and it is the tuning of those two rows that determine the names we refer to them by. The lower pitched row is the furthest from the palm bar and hand strap of a 20-button (middle row of a 30), and the higher pitched row is the one closest to the palm bar and hand strap on both models.

These two rows are always pitched a fifth apart, and when the tuning is referenced, it is expressed with the lower pitch first, and the higher pitch second. If the lower row is tuned in C then the higher row is pitched a fifth higher (in G) and we call it a C/G instrument. If the lower row is tuned to G then the higher row is pitched a fifth higher (in D) and we call it a G/D instrument. You will also encounter less common instruments tuned to Bb/F, A/E, C#/G# and likely a few others I haven’t as yet run across. C/G is the most common tuning available, and after that I believe G/D to be the next most common with Bb/F in third place for popularity. For those seeking even lower pitches there are baritone models pitched a full octave lower.

Coming to the point, people have been making reference here to D/G concertinas when I believe they mean G/D. There are a lot of G/D models in existence and they are pitched a fourth below the more popular C/G. If you were to make a conventional layout anglo with the lower row pitched in D, then the higher row would be pitched in A (a fifth higher), not G, and it would called a D/A instrument.

Again, this is a minor point in some respects, but I thought it should be addressed for clarity as there is a big difference between G/D and D/G.

Re: Concertina - Why C/G?

In Irish music the other pitches, most commonly G/D, C#/G#, Bf/F, are played with the same fingering patterns as a C/G. They are often referred to in reference to fingering for the key of D on a C/G. Consequently a C/G is a D instrument, C#/G# is an Eflat instrument, Bf/F is a C instrument etc. Noel Hill often plays an E instrument. A and A# instruments also occur.

I don’t think this occurs much outside Ireland unless a singer has selected an instrument to suit their voice?

My observations are that the C/G system places the middle point of most of the music on your index and middle fingers of both hands playing on the C and G rows. You can play many tunes orbiting around the center of the instrument mostly using those four fingers this way. Re: Concertina - Why C/G?

The anglo keyboard layout was German in origin, and the instrument was known as the “Anglo-German concertina” until WW1. It wasn’t designed with Irish music in mind. The majority were made in C/G, possibly to suit German music or perhaps because C is the simplest key with no sharps and flats. A large number were also made in flat keys for playing brass band music. G/D instruments were not all that common, although they are now becoming increasingly popular, especially with English musicians as it is better suited to playing the full chordal style in those keys,

Concertina players in Ireland, like players elsewhere, played C/G instruments because those were what was available. Early styles of playing seem to have been mainly up-and-down the rows, with some playing in octaves. They were often playing solo or with only one or two others, so the need to play in universal keys was less important. What we now think of as the Irish style of playing emerged relatively recently and is usually attributed to Paddy Murphy, and seems to have largely replaced the old styles, certainly amongst younger players. This is the style that is now taught to new players.

If you want to play Irish music in what is now the recognised and accepted style then C/G is the right instrument, because that is what this style is based around. Playing a G/D might raise eyebrows (rather like playing ITM on an English concertina does) because it is not the “correct” style . You would be playing up and down the rows, which was the old style, but in the right session keys. That might be seen an unusual choice though.

Incidentally, the D/G melodeon is also a relatively recent introduction, from the 1950s. It was brought in to play alongside fiddlers and has now become the standard. Many melodeon players feel that the lower-pitched instruments sound better, but we can’t persuade fiddlers to move out of their comfort zone!
