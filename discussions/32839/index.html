Hello GreyMorph.

I would guess that anybody interested in playing music has at least had a go at several different instruments. Most fluters and pipers play the whistle as well. Fiddlers mess about with mandolins, guitarists with bouzouki and or banjo. Different styles, as well, will cross the path of anyone who listens, and most people can knock out a 12-bar blues, ‘The Saints Go Marching In’, or ‘Summertime’ as the need arises.

My opinion on ‘being a multi-instrumentalist’ is that very few people manage it with a high degree of accomplishment, unless they put in the requisite hours. However, I would recommend learning a second instrument at least, possibly a third, if only for the insight it gives into the music, as well as the convenience of being able to swap as the need arises.

By all means incorporate Irish music into your other genres -- just don’t do it the other way round.

I wouldn’t recommend that you find some guys and gals to do some Irish jam with. First, I suggest you learn a bit about Irish music, and a few tunes.

Good luck.

GreyMorph, as to your question on being a multi-instrumentalist, I think that can only be a good thing - provided you learn to play each instrument reasonably well. By that I mean (melody) - timing, rhythm and intonation need to be faultless … in fact those first two would apply to any instrument. You would need to be able to play a few tunes solo, no hiccs, no mistakes, no bummers.

As for multiple genres, I have nothing against borrowing from one to use in another, provided that is done tastefully - on that point, there’s a huge amount of subjectivity, ranging from a purist to progressive attitudes. Only you can find that out from your listeners.

As for finding people to play with, newsboards, local press, music forums etc.

Re: Empty Glass

Here’s my take on it all.

On being a multi-instrumentalist: If you’ve got the time and the drive to put in lots of practice on several instruments, go for it! Otherwise it will depend on your objectives. There’s no harm in progressing slowly on several instruments or being alright but not great at several instruments, so long as you are sensitive to the people you play with. You can play Irish or old time music with quite a limited technique so long as your rhythm is spot on and you listen to others and try to blend with them rather than pushing yourself into the central attention-grabbing spot.

On mixing styles: again senitivity is the key word here. It could be great fun and musically interesting, provided you find people who are into the concept. I think going down to a session or meet-up where people are definitely into the pure drop and trying to force some kind of fusion wouldn’t make you many friends, but there are plenty of people out there with in interest in lots of different styles- find them and play with them!

I would second Michelle sims’ comment as a good way to get going on this. If there’s a rich musical culture in your area, go and listen to the people who are involved in it. Meet them, talk to them, see if any of them are teaching. Once you’ve got some tunes and some chops under your belt, join in the fun! (Sensitively mind you.) In no time you’ll be playing at a serious standard.

Good luck!

Alright amigo, I’ve been reading from the sidelines, but I’ll offer some more encouragement and add to all the other great advice that’s been given here. Slow is the new cool, and humility the path young grasshopper. ;-)
In truth, I can relate to you as I started out in the serious pursuit of learning this music when I was about 20, and went to Ireland a few times in the subsequent years. I had a tin whistle and played a bit the year before that. And I had a bunch of accomplishments performing solo on the guitar (first instrument) and singing from the age of 15, as well as forming a few bands in different genres. That means little to me now, and nothing in any form of bragging or ego flouting, as scads of great, humble players started earlier, accomplished more, etc.
One thing I’ve seen in all the players I’ve come across over the years is that attitude makes so much difference. We’re all tempted, especially as young bucks like ourselves, to see a quick path to becoming a “multi-instrumentalist” in our own eyes, the eyes of our friends, and those you mentioned previously who were so complementary of your playing. I’ve heard thousands of flatterers over the years, and none of that means shite to me today. In fact I remember none of those comments, because I knew they were all empty. I know my playing. My ear doesn’t lie to me like my ego and flattering ‘friends’ do. ;-) One of only a few compliments that mattered to me in my life came from a big piping hero of mine, Paddy Keenan. It was mostly just the encouragement to “go on now, you got this.”

I’ve encountered many people who enjoyed bestowing upon themselves the hefty title of “multi-instrumentalist” or “uilleann piper” only to demonstrate how barely mediocre or completely inept they were on any instruments or things musical. True story. It happens so often.
In hindsight I would have done a few things differently. Like start earlier, focus on the 1-3 instruments I had the chance to attain a level of mastery on, go slower. And here’s the shocking one: I would have been more choosy about who I played with, when, and if at all.

You said earlier: “I really thought playing with other people was the best way to get better…”
And, “Throw yourself right in, forgive yourself for making yourself look like a fool and then you’re doing it.”

I’ll counter these statements with a respectful, “negative, my man.” Playing with others, particularly those who have believed every lie their ego every told them about how great they were, can take you backwards years, miles. Beware my friend. An old bandmate, friend, hermit, savant guitarist and songwriter who is now in his 60’s once told me something that always stuck with me. It was a warning similar to what I’m giving you now, except he was a bit more harsh. He said “never play with people beneath you.” He went on to explain and expound, and the conversation went on for hours in his “cave” downtown where he writes and records his own music. Long story really, but his message hit home to me as I’d just given 5 yrs away playing with guys I nurtured and weaned from total infancy in this music and who never really “got it.” There attitudes were never right, most importantly. Hi GreyMorph, fair play to you. ;-) Sounds like you have a good perspective on it all.
I’ve used several tunings, but mostly DADGAD for trad backing. I like the openness of the chords, and the drone effect. Goes well with pipes, whistles, and most other instruments IMO. Also good for flat picking the tunes amidst backing segments. And, I prefer alternate tunings much of the time for accompanying my own singing. It just adds an interesting touch to familiar tunes.

As for backing in sessions on guitar, watch what the great players are doing on YouTube and other recordings. Subtlety and blend, with appropriate chording and rhythm is the name of the game. It won’t take you long to work out chords for some of your favorite tunes, but the execution is where the art lies. You have to know the tunes, so take a handful and get to know them, write out some chords that fit well and give it a go with a friend. After doing a lot of listening and watching good players, you’ll catch on quickly. Have fun!

Well, to chime in again after reading this thread-- Slow down, stop, and get some good recordings to listen to. Put them on your phone, pop them in the car when driving about, listen to the music and get a feel for it. If you can whistle a tune or have it in your head, then You can play it easier (finding notes can be tricky, but, hey, there are ways to help with that, ABC notation is what I use). “What do you guys recommend as a first Irish tune to learn on a standard tuned guitar ”…

I had intended to give suggestions to this question, primarily suggesting you try out some of the many two chord tunes so that you can focus mainly on the rhythm. I guess that would still stand, but my hesitation comes from the knowledge that it’s far more complicated than that. Firstly, I couldn’t answer your question unless I knew what style you played, and in that, I guess you must to work out the answer to your own questions. With Irish music I tend to play rhythm without a pick, i.e., with my fingers, almost like flamenco. Most people wouldn’t do that. My focus is on the percussion, though I would vary my style depending on the tune. An alternative is, as most seem to do, to use a pick to get a clear a melodic alternating bass note/chords pattern. This adds a bit of interest, even when you are playing a two chord backing. But as a person who has mostly played rhythm backing and bass lines, here is my honest caution:-

First off, I consider that over the 30 odd years that I played in various pub bands that I was an adequate to good musician. We played many pubs and big jobs, and we played a variety of music:- both country AND western (ala Blues Bros.) pub rock, heavy rock, punk, weddings. parties, anything, and eventually, as I got too old for that stuff we got into blues and folk. I was also a multi instrumentalist, even though I sometimes only bothered mastering a tune at a time on a new instrument (the punters couldn’t tell). Me and my old muso mates went back a long way together, and so we were pretty tight and comfortable and we got to the stage where we didn’t even practice. It all became really easy. Then when I was 57 I discovered the fiddle and started playing ITM.

I will put my fiddling abilities to the test in coming months. I am a very happy fiddler but have little desire to prove myself. I just feel I owe it to people on this site (and one should have the guts recently displayed by The Blind Bard). But my caution to you is this:- I was considered by my peers to be a very good rhthm gutarist. I find it bewildering when you talk of people not knowing minor chords, as if they are different and somehow harder from major chords. A chord is a chord, and I know my way all around the patterns on my guitar fingerboard withought much thought. Also, open tunings, dropped tunings, and DDADGAD, are all just part of the package. I can pick (say) Leo Kottke tunes in whatever tuning they come in, or hammer out some blues rhythms or flamenc, as required. But my intention here is not to show off, it is to caution you that even given my time and experience of playing all of that music, I have to start again when it comes to playing good backing to my own Irish fiddle playing. I’ve stated my opinion on this before on this site, in that I believe even given my previous experience, that it’s easier for the melody players in ITM than it is for the backers (that is if you want to be good). I honestly belive that if you want to be a multi-instrumentalist and you want to play ITM you should first take up a melody instrument (fiddle, mandolin, whistle, etc). If not you just have to do lots and lots (and lots) of listening. Knowing the chords is the easy bit, but it doesn’t get you there.
And then… just read BB’s comment and I agree with it. Also, please don’t belive I’m trying to discourage you. Your enthusiasm is great. If your focus was on any other kid of music than this, I would have nothing to say.
