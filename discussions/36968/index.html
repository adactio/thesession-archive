Hi Jerone,

For an audio course and book, based mostly on Ulster Gaelic, definitely get: “Now You’re Talking”: Multi-Media Course in Irish for Beginners, by Éamonn Ó Dónaill and Deirbhile Ní Churraighín (this book was reprinted as “Irish on Your Own”). It’s out of print now but still available on Amazon (try amazon.co.uk as it’s cheaper than in America). This is written by two native speakers from Donegal and is by far the best audio course going. (And don’t worry, Ulster Irish is only inflected in the first person. And modern Irish has only eleven irregular verbs!)

Ireland may still the best place to learn Irish Gaelic, but unsurprisingly one of the best places to learn languages in general is Harvard University! Barbara Hillers is a lecturer in Modern Irish language and folklore etc. at Harvard and University College Dublin and has a good introductory course (in two volumes) in Irish Gaelic focusing on Ulster dialect (and is FREE, from her web page). here’s the link:

http://people.fas.harvard.edu/~hillers/

Barbara Hillers explains why she uses Ulster Irish (meaning in effect Donegal Irish) as the basic dialect in her classes. She writes:

‘For the learner, an introduction to a consistent regional dialect was thought to be
desirable. Ulster Irish has never presented a more viable didactic choice than today. Its
prominence on both sides of the border is on the increase and is reflected in the media and
educational apparatus. There is much literature written in the dialect, most notably by the Mac
Grianna brothers from Rannafast, and a wealth of folklore publications collected in Donegal…’

The Summer Course “Buntús na Gaeilge: Cúrsa Samhraidh” (The Fundamentals of Irish: Summer Course) is a shortened version, but has the advantage of having extensive audio files accompanying the book.
You’ll find them all under: Course Materials / Textbook

Pádraig Ó Loingsigh’s book (though William Neilson is credited as the author) “Introduction to the Irish Language” in this case the Irish of Co. Down (1845) is available on google books (but will probably be more value to you after quite a few months of study than as a complete beginner), but it’s free and nice to have a look at for how Ulster Irish was taught in the 19th century.

http://books.google.ie/books?id=1icVAAAAYAAJ&pg=PA1&source=gbs_selected_pages&cad=3#v=onepage&q&f=false

Unfortunitely the best books on the Ulster dialect are all written in Gaelic, for those who already speak and read Gaelic (which is a big oversight and a pity). But after a year of study this book will be certainly of great use to you: Dónall P. Ó Baoill’s An Teanga Bheo Gaeilge Uladh (i.e., the living language: Ulster Irish), which is only 10 euros: http://www.litriocht.com/shop/product_info.php?products_id=1441#.VScEjvg6jVs
(don’t get it from Amazon where it’s $250, even though it’s still in print; talk about a rip off!)

Happy learning, I’m sure once you start there’s nothing that can stop you. (I’ve sent you a PM too.)

Ádh Mór! (Good Luck!)

Re: Books on the Ulster dialect?

The easyirish podcasts are indeed of Ulster Irish (as distinct from the other dialects), however the pronunciation is not really that of the native speakers of Ulster Irish but rather uses the pronunciation of Northern Ireland English. This is not to say that they don’t have excellent Irish, but if you are a foreigner coming to the language for the first time (and not knowing what is Gaelic pronunciation and what comes from Belfast English) I think this might be very greatly confusing and counterproductive. I’m sure this podcast can be helpful to many people (it looks very well laid out etc.), especially if you’re from Ireland, but just adds a complication if you’re a complete beginner. And if, as you said, you want to get Irish phonetically right then what you really want is the pronunciation of the Gaeltacht areas (that of the native speakers). “Now You’re Talking”/ “Irish on Your Own”, by Éamonn Ó Dónaill and Deirbhile Ní Churraighín, will definitely get you that authentic Gaeltacht pronunciation of Irish.

Re: Books on the Ulster dialect?

I must say that describing Gaelic as “very much a ‘hobby’ language” is quite insulting and quite incorrect. This is much the same attitude the London government has towards Scots Gaelic: “You all speak English, so why not just all speak English”. (white-bread Tory comedian David Mitchell said this on his ‘soap box’, stop funding Scottish and let it die, all of you have English anyway. This is a manifesto for killing languages.) The real nature of Gaelic as a living language has nothing to do with its official status as a national language (the biggest enemies of Irish at present are the Fine Gael government, many of whom can speak the language!)

Remember what the vile Elizabethan Edmund Spenser said , in his (A View of the State of Ireland, 1596) “For it hath ever been the use of the conqueror to despise the language of the conquered and to force him by all means to learn his.”

Sometimes languages die out, but sometimes they are deliberately exterminated. Gaelic still is the first spoken language, and the everyday language of children in the Donegal Gaeltacht. And many outsiders who come to live there simply learn the Gaelic language (often Irish culture is their reason for moving there in the first place). Strangely, foreigners who move to the Gaeltacht (in my experience) are far more likely to learn Gaelic than people from English-speaking places in Ireland. Re: Books on the Ulster dialect?

Sure. For example from page 46-47:
“Bhí sé mar sin gan bhrón ganbhuaidhreadh air feadh móráin bhlaidhain i sláinte mhaith agus gan tinneas ná aicíd air féin ná a chloinn,”

is translated as: “He was that way, without mishap or misfortune, for many years, in good health and without sickness or sorrow on himself or his children,”

The natural English translation “without either himself or his family HAVING illness or sorrow” is instead rendered “without sickness or sorrow ON himself or his children”, to mimic the structure of the Gaelic sentence and using ‘on’ to show how ‘ar’ is used in Irish. (“on” here is used as a translation of “ar”. & “On him” for the Gaelic word “air”). This method produced very stage-Irish English (& bad English prose), but is an excellent learning tool for language (Hyde says this is purely as a aid to learning Irish language).

Here’s a longer bit from page 1-2, (it’s a version of the story of Jack the Giant Slaver) where you can see the same awkward English to show the Gaelic syntax:
“Dubhairt sé ann sin go gcaithfeadh sé dul go B’l’acliath go cúirt an rígh, go bhfeicfeadh sé an dtiucfadh leis a deunamh. Bhi an chúirt sin ‘gá dheunamh le fada, acht an méad dí do gníthidhe ann san lá do leagaidhe ann san oidhche é, agus níor fheud duine air bith a chur suas mar gheall air sin. ‘S iad trí fháthach a thigeadh’san oidhche a bhidheadh ‘ gá leagadh. D’imithigh an táiliúr an lá air na mhárach agus do thug se leis an uirlis, an spád agus an tsluasad.”

“Then he said that he must go to Blackleea (Dublin), to the king’s court, to see would he be able to build
it. That court was a’ building for a long time but as much of it as would be made during the day used to be thrown down again during the night, and for that reason nobody could build it up. It was three giants who used to come in the night and throw it. The day on the morrow the tailor went off, and brought with him his tools, the spade and the shovel.”

This makes very awkward English. But it’s not meant to be read as English prose, it’s meant to show the structure of Gaelic syntax. There are still places in Ireland where people will say things speaking English like ‘I have a great hunger on me’. This syntax is taken directly from Gaelic. Likewise, the awkward English in this translation is meant to mirror Irish syntax completely, in order to allow readers to learn the Irish and to get used to a syntax quite different from English. I uploaded this text someone a while ago; it’s not so easy for a beginner but once you’re getting on with your learning you might find it fun to read. This book is in the old script (where ‘r’ and ‘s’ look a little different, and the dot over a letter is equivilant to putting a ‘h’ after the letter in modern Romanized script), and it is in pre-standardization spelling (which is actually quite phonetic once you learn the pronunciation rules, (e.g., ‘dh, gh, mh, etc. after a vowel has the function of lengthening the vowel without changing its value.) I met an American student in Donegal a few years ago who even wrote all his Irish notes in the old script, so never be discouraged.

A lot of these old Irish Gaelic books are available on http://www.archive.org (uploaded mostly by American and Canadian libraries rather than from Ireland) so it’s worth your while learning the old script so you can avail of this wonderful (and excellently free) resource.

There are those who would be happy to see Gaelic die out, after all if it’s just a hobby language like the native American languages then it can just as easily be replaced by another hobby without any real detriment to our civilization. This is very much the attitude of this government, who said that Irish language is not central to Irish culture, and that other aspects should be emphasised such as Jedward and Riverdance. It’s no surprise that many people in Ireland hold Irish language in low esteem, bad teaching (beginning with the brutal, standardized Dublin Irish) and post-colonial ‘cultural cringe’ seem to be prominent, but monetary greed and a total disdain for the artistic life also play a role. Irish music is tolerated simply as a means of socialising, as a hobby no different than basketball. Clearly an ancient language and its culture (which is still alive, despite protests to the contrary and opposition from the revisionary history of the political ruling class) will struggle to thrive when such a low mentality exists among so many of the population. I doubt many of these people will change their minds, all we can hope for is that they simply leave us alone and try not to discourage those who believe that a living language is important enough that they want to learn it. In East Belfast there are classes in Irish language and a teacher told me that 80% of the students are Protestant (harking back to the enthusiasm for Gaelic in the 19th century there) and we see that many foreigners are eager to learn the language (and Scots Gaelic too). But it is a pity that some musicians who play Irish music hold it in such low esteem, as unimportant to Irish culture.
