Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

I’ve put together maybe half a dozen tunes that I’ve been satisfied with. Composing is a surprisingly complex mix of simple and hard elements. My thought is that if I can write one little phrase that I like--two measures long, perhaps--I can then use variations on that to get another phrase, and so on. Most of the ITM style tunes are only 16 measures long, and divided into 8 measure long sections.

I find that first I have to choose a tune type (jig, reel, etc,), key and mode before I start. I then just start noodling around with an instrument in hand, and write down a couple measures at a time. Looking at some music theory about cadences can be helpful in determining how to move a melody around, but often just going by feel (where the melody wants to go) is just as effective. Immersion in the kind of music you want to compose is also important.

I suspect the most important part about composing tunes is to practice composing tunes. Just try it, and then try it again. If it doesn’t turn out, just let it go and try another. Just as it takes hundreds of hours of practice to learn to play an instrument, I suspect that it takes hundreds of attempts to compose tunes before anyone becomes a really good composer.

Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

I compose tunes occasionally. Most of them are crap. There are a few that get played around by people though.

Only once have I sat down specifically to compose a tune, and that was a waltz that I wrote for my wife for our wedding. The rest of the time, they tend to come when I’m playing a lot, and always start out as noodling. I will often sit down to play, and just play a few notes to see if it brings any old tunes that I had forgotten about back to mind (which it often does). Most of my tunes are ones that started out with me noodling, and when I hit something cool, it will stick in my mind and ultimately form itself into the A part of a tune. And then I often have to work a bit at finding a B part for it. So sometimes the B parts of my tunes seem a bit more contrived to me. In my case, the tune exists as a whole before I ever write it down. I will sometimes record it so I don’t lose it, and come back to it for a few days and let it sink in before I try to write it, because some of them will morph a bit over the first few days. (And some will eternally morph, as tunes do).

A couple of times, I have sat down and had a tune just appear sort of fully formed. This is what Will Harmon (the most prolific tune composer I know, and an old friend to many of us here in mustardom) calls “channeling a tune”. He says that his tunes already exist, and he is just the conduit that brings it into being in this world. (And if you like cool, but often difficult tunes, looking up Will’s tunes will find you some good ones!)

If you care, most of my tunes are written in ABC in my profile. One of these days I need to refine my written version of the waltz and put it in my profile, because one of my regular sessions tends to play it now and again.

As to how I compose, I will often be just playing around on the fiddle, and I might accidently play something that sounds good, and I’ll try to turn it into a tune. I will rarely ever sit down with a cool chord pattern that I had come up with and be able to write a good fiddle melody. I have a natural feel for “where a tune should go” as far as the melody is concerned. Most of the time I will have come up with one cool bar of music, and because I have an idea of where it should go, I’ll be able to come up with something that works. I am one of those people who always has music playing inymy head, and sometimes something I’ve never heard before will pop into my head and I’ll hum it or play it, and I’ll write it out. Something might pop into my head in the middle of science class, or while I’m out in a corn field (which happens to be where I composed an excellent G mixolydian crooked reel). I find these tunes that just pop into my head are the most natural feeling, and I love when a whole tune, both parts, will flow from my mind, fiddle and fingers, as if it is the work of God himself. If I none of these sources bring up anything too close to it, then I will decide it is in fact my own tune. I do believe somewhat that composing is almost something you are born with. I am trying to teach my dad music and the bass guitar and he simply has little natural understanding of music as a whole. He never hears music in his head, and has a hard time wrapping his head around new concepts, making for a frustrating jam session. I also have friends with grade 7+ classical piano, or are just naturally really good at music, but they just don’t have that innate idea of chord progression and where a tune goes to. It could probably learned, but the raw natural talrnt for such musical ideals must be present somewhere in a person. Sorry for this being so long, but I hope it is helpful.

Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

I’ve composed two pibroch and about six tunes that I would consider decent over a period of about fifty years. I have a book containing little scraps of phrases and tune ideas that is gathering cobwebs somewhere.
Two things come to mind -- one is about chord progressions, which in my opinion is not the way to compose traditional tunes. You only need to look at some of the discussions about backing to realise that the chords come after, and are not obvious to anyone trying to make them fit.
The other is about ‘cliché’ lines and sounding contrived. I mentioned once that a good tune will go where you are not expecting, by which time it is obvious that it couldn’t have gone anywhere else. In this way you avoid both the boring predictability of tunes that are built around little phrases or chord progressions, and the ‘contrived’ effect of trying to hard to be novel or radical.
The problem, obviously, is that it is very difficult -- or should I say, it takes a lot of work. Like a good poem, a good tune seems as though it wrote itself, or (heaven help us) God did it. What you don’t see are all the crossings-out, amendments, discarded scraps, torn-out hair and snapped pencils, or these days, binned files.
Like most people here, I would think that the best way to compose a tune is by noodling, playing around, thinking of little phrases etc, and then building on what you like, rather than sitting down and starting from scratch.
But, as I say, I haven’t written much worth playing, so I’m prepared to admit that I am wrong. What I will say, is that it is a great feeling to have one of your tunes accepted and played, especially if nobody knows you wrote it.
Give it a go.

Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

I’ve composed a few tunes over the years, maybe 20 or 30. Only two or three are any good.

As people have said it’s not ‘composing’ like sitting down with some formula and hammering out something. A fragment, a melodic motif, will just pop into my head. Most of those have been lost because I was in a place with no instrument to play it on and no paper to write it down.

When I do scribble it down, later when I go to play it I find out that I’ve not written it right, because what had popped into my head was nice and what I wrote isn’t. I don’t have perfect pitch so there’s a good chance that, when I don’t have an instrument to hand, what I jot down is not what I heard in my head.

If I do have an instrument to hand I’ll usually be able to play what I heard in my head. I’ll finger it through several times to try to remember it (if don’t have anything to write with). Sometimes later I’ll have forgot the thing entirely, or misremember it.

An argument for a smart phone! But I only have a stupid phone.

The other way of inventing something is from noodling around on the pipes or whistle. Those melodic bits are never as nice as the ones that just pop into my head.

So I have a nice phrase! But it’s not an entire tune. Usually all I have is a two-bar phrase, or in some cases an entire 4-bar or 8-bar part. But this thing that magically appeared, and sounds nice, isn’t playable and needs to be fleshed out to create a complete part, and then at least one more part.

That part isn’t ‘inspiriation’ but ‘perspiration’, hard tedious work that often doesn’t come to much.

Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

Heres a really methodical approach you could try, that I found works.

Decide on the tune type, key, and feel.Maybe play some tunes first that you would like to compose similar to.
Just start playing around in the key and feel of your choice.
If you hit on a nice phrase keep it and play around with it… until you piece together a whole first part.

Second part is tricky….you want the tune to go somewhere but not so far that it doesnt sound like the same tune. Write a bunch of 2nd parts, pick your favourite, refine the whole thing.

That way you will come up with a tune, whether anyone else likes it is another thing, but if you like it… then some people will.

Keep at it, I’m guessing like anything …the more you do it the better you will get. Some days you will get about 5 great ideas for a tune and its all flowing nicey…. enjoy that…because that might not happen for another month…. such is life!

Try different approaches (compose on different instruments if possible) and see what works best for you.
Talk to people who have written tunes you like and ask them how they did it. Liz Carroll once told me she trys her best to ‘block from her mind’ any tune that sounds like the one shes writing.

Be your biggest critic, dont be afraid to go back to a tune and try to improve it. Trad tunes probably change all the time… maybe some of the best tunes kicking about are the best ‘versions’ of a tune which have survived.

All the best

Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

Answering the original question, I compose in a few ways. Sometimes I’ll just get the odd phrase going through my head, and next time I pick up the fiddle I’ll try it out. That usually leads to more phrases, and if it sounds good, I’ll record it for reference, and add to it as time goes on.

Other times I lay out a chord pattern from piano or guitar, and compose around that. (Don’t see why anyone could think this is bad?)

I’ve composed about 40 tunes in all. Most of them follow a general 4/4 or 6/8 time sig, usually with equal bar counts in each part (usually two or three parts).

That’s about the only ‘diddly’ element in them - the melodies tend to be quite removed from common trad patterns, often with a lot of swing / modulation going on too.

Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

Some extracts from an article I wrote about composing a few years ago. Hope it might help in some way!

For me, anyone can compose music, but to be a composer you must immerse yourself in your chosen genre of music. Of course for me, that’s traditional Irish. Even the oldest of tunes have to have had a composer. When I compose I always keep the tune very much in the traditional idiom. That’s what I was brought up on listening and learning from great musicians like John Joe Gannon and Davy Joe Fallon here in Co. Westmeath. People have said that my tunes don’t sound like ‘new tunes’ but that this is a good thing. I’m flattered by these comments but that’s what I want to achieve when composing: keep the tune traditional whilst having a nice flow and rhythm to the tune.

I would like to say that I have no problem with some of the more modern, contemporary compositions but are they traditional? I don’t think so. But’s its a sort of a grey area for another day’s discussion. All I will say is for me, our traditional Irish music is a dance music, meant to be danced to and listened to with our ears and feet.

Composers I like are people like Paddy O’Brien(Tipperary), Sean Ryan(Fiddle) Charlie Lennon, Vincent Broderick, Richard Dwyer etc… composers and musicians who concentrate(d) on the traditional flow and melody of the tune.

How, when, where do I compose? Well it can happen at anytime, anyplace, anywhere. Sometimes some notes or a particular melody will just come into my head and I will hum the melody and record it onto my phone or dictaphone. I never write down the notes first. I will compose the tune and later write it down for my own records and teaching purposes. Tunes have come into my head whilst driving, in bed, teaching a class, even at mass! The key is try and keep the melody and record it as quickly as possible. I compose alot at home where my instruments are close at hand. I also love walking around the beautiful countryside in Co. Westmeath where a particular melody of a jig, reel, hornpipe, slow air, polka etc… might come to me.

I agree with the late Ed Reavy who said he needed to be in a particular good mood to compose. Ultimately though, one needs to be listening to and playing music all the time.

Gam, the topic of the thread was ‘composing tunes’. Yes, of course this is an Irish trad site, but there’s no reason why something that’s truly original-sounding can’t come out of the exercise.

The fact that the OP said ‘How do you check to see if your tune is already out there and you aren’t just re-writing a common tune?’ plus other comments, imply that (even with the best of intentions) you could easily try to compose something original, but simply end up with nothing but a regurgitation of commonly played phrases and motifs, strung together to form a new ‘tune’, that sounds so much like one hundred others (and bears a very close resemblance to ten of those). That’s what I want to avoid, at all costs.

There are comps which sound like Irish trad tunes, but some are so ‘ordinary’ that you couldn’t tell if they were ‘new comps’, or simply real old trad tunes that you just happened to miss along the way.

Then there are the comps of someone like Liz Carroll - some of them are quite stunning, imo - you can tell they’re ‘different’. Now take a jump further to something like Bill Whelan’s ‘Nova Scotia Set’ from Riverdance - unmistakeable in their form (reel, polka etc) - Irish sounding, but not trad. Excellent all the same (although I’m sure many will disagree).

That’s composing, in my book.

When I was just beginning to compose, I came up with all these terribly CLEVER phrases in my tunes. The thought being, of course, that it would make the tune more original / unique / attractive. It was as if I was a foreigner trying to write the next Great American Novel while barely being able to speak, read or write in English. Like any other type of music, ITM is a LANGUAGE, and you’re going to have to do a lot of “See Spot run” before you produce a masterpiece of Western literature! For me, total immersion is key to learning the vocabulary and syntax of Irish tunes. I have done (and still do) a TON of listening, mostly to internalize as many phrases and regional styles as possible. These are the “cliches” that everyone seems to be worried about. These “cliches” are what make Irish music SOUND Irish! Think of the opening “rocking” phrase in the first bar of “Drowsy Maggie”. It shows up in a BOATLOAD of other tunes. But do all those tunes then sound exactly like Drowsy Maggie? No! If you’re trying to compose within the boundaries of traditional style, the cliches are your FRIENDS. Consciously or unconsciously, I tend to use these devices in just about every tune I write. Often, they’ve helped me write my way out of what I thought was a dead end. And many of these cliches are Rhythmic devices, which give this music the danciness that I love so much. Of course, these are the principles that I rely on to help create my Trad-style tunes. If you’re looking to write outside the traditional style, it’s still not a bad idea to have some of these phrases in your back pocket, so to speak. You never know when you might need them to get you out of a jam! If you want to get an idea of my style of composition, I’ve put up some of my favorites in my profile (finally! Also, TBB, as far as putting original compositions in the database here, Webmaster Jeremy has a simple rule: for each original composition you submit, you must first submit 5 actual traditional tunes. Some people ignore this, but I think it’s a good rule. If a tune you thought about posting is already here, it’ll force you to “dig a little deeper” to find a tune to submit, which will hopefully help by expanding both your repertoire AND your fellow mustardboarders. As far as a tune becoming “Traditional”, I remember some wisdom NJ Poet Joe Weil imparted on some of his students- “I’ve never believed that you can call yourself a Poet; someone else has to do it, and then you can agree or disagree.” Same goes for traditional music- the list of living composers of tunes whose music has been accepted & absorbed into the tradition is VERY short. Off the top of my head right now, Paddy Fahey, John Brady, Charlie Lennon, Maurice Lennon and until recently, Brendan Tonra (R.I.P.). In recent years, it seems that if a newly composed tune ends up on a popular Trad recording, that’s enough to get the ball rolling. But these things take a LONG time; a newly composed tune might gain huge popularity today, but if people only like it because it is a novelty (i.e. too different than the majority of Trad tunes), it will be forgotten shortly due to lack of staying power. Patience is the key, I think, as well as having a person or persons with DEEP roots in the tradition become a champion of your music. Ed Reavy, for example, had his close friend Louis Quinn:

http://irishtunecomposers.weebly.com/ed-reavy.html

It’s a long process, but technology is speeding it up these days, I think.

Re: Composing Tunes: how do you go about it?

I jot down promising first and second bars in ABC notation on scrap paper. Sometimes they simply take off, and become a tune. I usually don’t remember how they got there. Usually it’s off opening bars, but sometimes the bars can be ‘infill’, or an ending to a tune, or part.

Where the ‘ears’ come in is in bringing to mind the sound of standard session instruments in the hands of good players. I think: “What would this tune, or these bars, actually *sound* like on the fiddle / whistle / Irish / Highland / Northumbrian bagpipes / other?”

I don’t often compose on an instrument because I am too lazy to get one out, but I think it would actually be the best way.
