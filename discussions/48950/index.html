Will has recommended the Fiddler’s Fakebook in the past as a source of common tunes with basic chord accompaniment (i.e., nothing fancy or controversial). I don’t like the book, mainly because of the misattributions, but the chords in it do work.

Also, despite what Will says, substitution is allowed in trad, especially if done tastefully by the likes of me! But substitution shouldn’t be a starting point and less is more, in my opinion.
For many tunes the implied harmony is obvious. It’s a good idea to stick to that, to begin with anyway.

A little understanding of theory in practice helps, like the 1-4-5 thing, but, ultimately, chord choices should be influenced by your ears more than your eyes. I’ve said it many times before, but too many people think you work out chords for a tune by looking in detail at a score and the notes in each bar. What “works” on paper doesn’t always work in practice. It’s how it sounds (and not how it looks) that’s important.

Re: Chords for ITM tunes

Will: “iI was taught ,quote “ there is no substitution in traditional Irish music ””

Well, that’s just not true. Chord substitutions are all over the place. Subbing the minor vi for the I chord is an obvious example. But you can sub in entire turnarounds, as well.

What you don’t see much of is the flat-V substitution on dominant chords, and you don’t see very much in the way of altered fifths, unless you know what you’re looking for and you have melody players who play it up.

For example, in the 2nd bar of the B part of Paddy’s Trip to Scotland, there’s an opportunity for the melody player to walk up from A or A7th through A7#5 to get back to the D major chord. And if the melody player does that, there’s your opportunity to do an altered substitution.

Another example would be the Na Mooney’s recording of Morning Dew, which goes through one substitution after another. To glorious effect.

Re: Chords for ITM tunes

As for the OP, my best advice for a mandolin player is that less is more, and discretion is the better part of valor.

Most tradheads don’t want mandolin players banging out chords because it tends to devolve very fast into bluegrass. It’s just idiomatic to Irish trad.

That’s not to say don’t learn chords, and how chords work to support melody (and vice versa!). But in ITM on mandolin, where it comes in useful, is knowing when to let what strings and notes ring sympathetically while you’re picking the melody.

Case in point: There’s a wonderful mandolin recording of Lough Mountain on Eoin O’Neill’s “In Session” CD, which is a great example of sympathetic vibration using a mandolin in an ITM context, WITHOUT falling into the bluegrass trap.

That sound would be welcomed in any trad sesh anywhere, I think. And that mandolinist doesn’t bang out a “chord” once.

Approaching this topic from an entirely different persepctive —

Concern about indicating chords for traditional dance tunes can be traced back to Scotland in the middle of the 18th century. The piano entered into that discussion just before its end, establishing an approach to harmonization and performance that led more or less directly to current ITM practice. Re: Chords for ITM tunes

not sure I understand the “no substitution” argument. to begin with, there is several classes of chords that are pretty much interchangeable and that obviously all work just fine - one-note drone “chords”; two-note neutral 1+5, no 3rd “power chords”; normal 1+5+3rd triad chords; complex “jazz” chords; etc. if the big idea is to always start the Kesh jig with a Gmaj chord, never Em or C, one cannot help but notice that, on guitar there is a choice of at least 3 different G chords, on a GDAE cittern/mandolin there is a choice of 4 or 5 G chords, on a CGDA mandola/mandocello/banjo at least 4 different G chords, (on a DADGAD guitar, count them yourself). it would be silly to insist that the first chord of the Kesh jig must be the standard-tuning-guitar 3rd-fret-bar-chord Gmaj, no substitutions permitted.

What chords you use may well depend on your instrument, its capabilities - and your own! If you look at a written score which has chords marked you can sometimes guess what instrument the chord-maker plays - e.g. chords with 9ths, 11ths, 13ths (??) etc may well be from a guitarist, while a multitude of 7ths may suggest a piano accordion or piano at work. Me, on my button accordion, I have the choice of only 6 major chords, expanded to 8 by the fact that 2 have both pull and push variants, and for minors, the middle third stop can be pulled out to make them ambiguous. But I do what I can, and leave out any I can’t!
As for backing tunes, one assumes that each tune will be played at least 2 or 3 times, so it adds something if the chords can perhaps be varied on each run of the tune - e.g. stick to the very basic I IV V first time through, but add in some relative minors second time, possibly develop further with a different but relevant rhythm 3rd time. Of course the latter only works well with a single backer, or a group of them who all know the pattern: not suggesting that you have a pile of backers all doing different things which would be absolute chaos!

Dissonance is an instantaneous phenomenon, if the phrase you are playing a chord over doesn’t end up with the following intervals
• Minor Second
• Major Second
• Tritone
• Minor Seventh

it’s not dissonant. In all likely hood there will be some dissonances under most chords because of the passing notes or ornamentation in the phrase.

Of course, that’s a very good reason why more than one guitarist etc at a session seldom works very well. We all have different approaches and ideas which may not necessarily be right or wrong. Even if and when we refer to “books”, it’s highly probably that the “chord suggestions” will vary there too depending on the source.

The answer is yes. The 60s pop hit ‘’Lover’s Concerto‘’ by The Toys used the melody of Minuet in G and changed the time signature from 3/4 to 4/4. I’m pretty sure "Autumn Leaves‘’ was a piece of Bach before it became a jazz standard. There’s an explanation of it by George Shearing in this clip:https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lor4iEoMaG0 .The relevant part is at about 5:33.

I’ve been fortunate enough over the years to have been engaged to accompany some of Ireland’s greatest musicians, in part because their regular backers could’nt make the long trip to Australia. What typically happens is someone will send me a recording/CD/etc of the music and then I sit around in my music room and go through each tune/set, write out a rough set of chords, play it through, change this, change that, add a chord here or there, and just spend a lot of time getting to KNOW the tunes and the chords.

One of the oddest things that ever happened to me occured when I was to accompany a visiting fiddle player and I was given a copy of his latest recording at the time. I thought the guitar backing was just lovely and so I set about, as a first step, trying to duplicate the guitar parts, all backing. There were some unusual voicings but eventually I made a fairly good fist of it and the performer told me he was very happy with the job I had done. It was only sometime later that I found out his backer on the recording was playing in DADGAD and I had worked out all of these mystical sounding chords using standard tuning!!
