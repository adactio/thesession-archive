How do you go about memorizing a tune?

Added by fiddlerdan on April 23rd, 2010

How do you go about memorizing a tune?

What tricks do you use to make a particular melody stand out in your ear to the point you can internalize/memorize it?

Do you learn a tune by rote so that your fingers ‘know’ it (muscle memory) without having to *think* about it?

Do you have a photographic memory and ‘see’ the dots from the sheet music while you play?

Or do you learn it by ear off a recording, phrase by phrase until you’ve got it just right?

What’s your approach/method to learning/memorizing tunes?

Maybe some of your approaches to learning tunes might help others who find themselves just beginning the instrument, new to the tradition, hit a plateau, or just plain in a rut.

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

Listening isn’t learning, neither is reading from the dots or playing along with a recording. Learning is the thing that comes afterwards when you force yourself to remember by removing the source of the information, like when you’re swotting for an exam and you close the book and recite or write from memory. You have to remember it in your head, and then repeat it unaided. Then you leave it for a while and repeat it again without the source. You might have to take another look or listen, but then you look for the trigger that recalls it for you, and at this stage you just remember the trigger. Sometimes the trigger can just be the note the tune begins on, and then you’ve got it. Then you can go back to the phrase by phrase relearning to pick up the subtelties that you’ve missed. If I can force myself to repeat a tune in the morning that I was trying to learn the previous night, I’ve got it.

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

Or perhaps you haven’t listened to it correctly. You have to hear the whole tune, and it’s easy for the brain to listen partially, to pick up the most salient bits and let the rest just be “diddly diddly” in the head - in order to have the tune in your head, you have to hear whole thing.
I’d suggest you try “playing” the tune without your instrument. You can use the dots or the abc as a prompt, but force yourself to create the sound in your head. For me, this seems to make sure that all of the pieces are there, and not just the bits that stand out. If you can’t visualize the sound of the tune with the dots in front of you, you definitely don’t know the tune well enough to learn it in the sense that Rook and others mean. If you can, this act of visualization will help to cement the whole tune in place - at least, it does for me.

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

People who see the dots in their head or learn the tune by rote in a note sequence usually sound like a midi file when they play. Without the tune in you you’re not expressing a tune, you’re spitting out a sequence of notes.

Sing the tune to yourself, like everyone is saying above. Once you can remember all of it that way, then you can put it on your instrument.

The better you get at your instrument, the better you will get at putting what’s in your head on your instrument.

Basically we’ve got one barrier already: putting the music in our heads through our hands into the instrument.

Dots or sequences of notes are an extra barrier, an extra step in the process.

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

fiddlechick - that’s not strange at all. Perfectly normal, in fact. Typically, people learning tunes at home, from sheet music, will play along until they make a mistake, and then jump back and “correct” the mistake on the fly. Perfectly natural, you never even think about it, and a terrible habit that you have to consciously decide to break.
It’s a terrible habit, because when you get around to playing with people, you suddenly realize that you’ve taught yourself (for example) that the third part of the Musical Priest goes
Bc|d-dc, damn, Bc |d- dc damn, Bc|dBB- bafb ….

And you don’t even notice it until you come up to that part, and you seize up and you don’t know why.

This is just one typical source of the sort of freeze-up you describe - another would be the flexibility of tempo that you can allow yourself when it’s just you and the music stand. The flexibility doesn’t exist in company, and again, you don’t even realize that you’ve taught yourself to slow down at the hard parts and speed up at the good bits until you find yourself out of time and you don’t know why.

1st off, I listen to the entire tune as many times through as possible.
I try not to memorize a tune at all. Either it is in my head or it’s not. This may sound extreme so I’ll try to describe.
When the entire tune is in my head*, I don’t have to think about it. I am then able to start it off & play all the way through at least three times. The good thing about this is it frees me up to listen to all the other players (& myself) & also come up with possible variations.
If only the A part is in my head, or even just one complete phrase, I may be able to suss it out. But, if I am not retaining what I’m hearing (which is from memory ~ so I’m contradicting myself . . .) then I may not *know* the tune when it comes up next session**.

Regarding sheet music ~ it’s useful. As a primary source, not a good idea at all. Listen 1st, 2nd, & 3rd . . . look later (or not at all). I encourage everyone to look at some dots at some point. Please, get the tune in your head (sound not dots) 1st thing.

When learning one phrase at a time, listen to yourself & get it right. If you’re not doing this stop playing & listen. Don’t be a perfectionist, though. It should sound as good as possible, but *almost* everyone makes mistakes. Just try & not learn the mistakes.
One of the best ways to learn a new tune is one-on-one with someone who knows the tune, though a good recording works when you have heard something you like yet don’t know anyone who plays that tune.
So, with a recording listen to the entire tune at least 3 times through. After that I’ll usually turn off the recording & play whatever is in my head. Hopefully I have the 1st phrase. At this point if you can turn the recording on & play the entire tune, then you’re brilliant & wasting your time reading my comments. Cheers, have a grand day.
. . . For anyone still reading . . . I would suggest putting the A part on loop, though you may loop a phrase. Just don’t loop a so called tricky bit. That is when you begin deconstruct the tune (this isn’t an autopsy or brain surgery - yuck!), it is then no longer a tune, & if you get the trick bits correct you mess up the easy bits . . .

that’s the end of my ramble for now, you’ve all been spared for the moment. it’s work.

** for me there are 2 types of tune
~ the one’s I can play when they come up in session
~ the one’s which I want to play, in session, but need to do lots of woodshedding.

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

The best way to learn the shape and feel of a tune is among other musicians using your ear. But when I do that, I find I sometimes miss little details, so some reading of sheet music, and repetitive drilling of certain passages in the privacy of my home, fills in those little details. I improve a tune through repeating this process over the course of weeks, until I reach the point where I no longer feel like I am riding on the back of the other players when that tune comes up. And when I can start it off, play it alone, or even carry some other players along with me, then I feel like I really know a tune. Of course, then I learn to shade things a different way during different times through the tune, and extra twiddle here, a little simpler there. As has been said here many times, you are never done learning a tune.
And I find that, the more things you memorize, the better you get at memorizing. I think that this is something that is not appreciated, nor emphasized enough, in education today--the training of people to memorize information.

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

In my own purely personal, anecdotal, and unreliable experience, “memorizing” a tune is precisely what leads to difficulties in later retrieving and playing of the same tune. Better to immerse yourself in the tune, let it saturate you. Then all you have to do is let it out.

In the early going, this isn’t as straight forward as it later becomes. You need a fair number of tunes rattling around inside you before you can have a decent context for them--an understanding of their idioms and of their place in the music as a whole. When even strange new tunes feel familiar as soon as you hear them, you’re well inside the music, “living inside the tunes.” And then they tend to unfurl easily, whether or not you’re distracted.

I would advise letting go of the reliance of finger patterns, which I know is tempting, easy to do, and something I used to do. The trouble with it is that it will limit you in the end. If you have memorised a finger pattern, you will find that if you play a variation, you will trainwreck. If someone at a session has a slightly different version of the tune than you, it will be hard to adapt. And if they play the tune in a different key than the one you learned it in, you are really screwed. The trick -- and I am convinced this comes with time and Will and SWFL and a few others have already said it here -- is to absorb the tunes and cultivate a direct link between the melody rattling around in your head and you fingers on your instrument.

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

David, a great way to get more comfortable with a tune is to intentionally vary which beats you emphasize. For a reel, say, run through it several times with the emphasis all on the down beats, then again all on the backbeats, then yet again letting the tune itself tell you where to emphasize. Also play the tune yardstick straight several times, and then as swingy as you can, and then somewhere in between.

Lots of people tend to practice a tune repeatedly without varying anything--trying to get it “perfect” one way. But that’s not really *playing* the tune, and it doesn’t help in being able to blend with a different feel when playing with other people.

I also find that it helps a lot to play a tune 40 or 60 times in a row, noodling out variations and various feels. Some people seem to expect 3 or 4 times through a tune in a sitting is “enough.” That’s just getting warmed up. :-)

So, in other words, if you learn the tune on another instrument, and once it’s in memory, do you hear the tune in your head whilst playing it in real time on a box, and do your fingers ‘know where to go’ to hit the necessary buttons to make the melody?

I started out eons ago on the cello. We quickly learned due to the scale of the fingerboard being so large to shift positions when needed to bring the notes under the hand making the melody easier to play. After a while, the fingers just knew ‘where to go’--i.e. when and where to shift).
In watching cellists, no two players will shift the same ways or at the same times (unless they’re in an orchestra and the section leader annotates where to shift), much like watching fiddlers play--they all have different bowing patterns and bow strokes will seldom match while playing tunes. In this case the bowing arm ‘knows which way to go’ without having to think about it.
Is that the same for box players?
After a while on the instrument your fingers just ‘know where to go’?

Re: How do you go about memorizing a tune?

The function of how the mind works vis a vis memory/memorization is a fascinating one to me. Scientists have created experiments using electrodes on the skull to determine and map which areas of the brain are used (which synapses fire where) during such mental activities as speech, singing, abstract thinking, lustful thoughts, thoughts of love/joy, etc. Using this model, it would be interesting to map which areas of the brain get used while a person:
memorizes a tune by ear, reads a tune via the ‘dots’, works out fingering for same tune on various instruments (would thoughts germane to one instrument lie in one area of the brain, whilst thoughts about a different instrument use another part?), plays a tune by heart, teaches said tune to another…the possibilities seem endless; yet it approaches a core question, and one that seems a contradiction as much as a philosophical koan…
How can/do we ever actually ever really *KNOW* a thing (in this case a tune)?
The music contained in ITM seems ineffable; the tunes seem to exist prior to conditional reality. By playing them we bring them from a state of timelessness into our world of conditional 3 dimensional duality.
