The status of mandolin players in Irish traditional music today.

Are we fellow mandolin players looked upon as equal musicians amidst the ranks of fiddles, pipes, accordions etc? I think we should be. There should be more mandolins involved in the sessions. Never under estimate this beautiful instrument….it requires much more than other instruments….from the variance in picking, phrasing and harmony etc to the straight melodic playing. I rate the mandolin highly amongst the other instruments in traditional music and the reason is pure and simple…it is as beautiful as you will make it.

↳ Posted by Mz WhiteBread on March 24th, 2013.

Re: The status of mandolin players in Irish traditional music today.

The above clip is proof enough that the mandolin deserves to be seriously as a traditional instrument (read “instrument on which Irish Traditional Music can be played”). It also exemplifies how great a mandolin sounds in the company of its own kind - i.e. other double coursed plucked strings, or plucked strings in general.

The main problem the mandolin faces in trad is, I think, being heard in sessions. It is not just a case of volume - it’s more to do with timbre and ‘envelope’ (the attack and decay pattern) that make it easily drowned out by the likes of fiddle, accordion and pipes (A mandolin played tremolo style would cut through everything else in a session, since it involves lots of very strong ‘attacks’ without the time to decay - but jigs and reels played tremolo style? I don’t think so.). Perhaps the fact that mandolins are often seen but not heard in sessions results in a perception of mandolin players as being ‘hangers on’, or just ‘having a go’. Some people will, of course, take the opposite stance and make a special effort to listen to the mandolin player precisely because they can’t hear them above the other musicians. I have long since given up the battle to make myself heard in a big session - as long as I can hear myself, I’m happy. If an opportunity arises to play a tune with just one other instrument - especially another instument of the mandolin family - so much the better.

↳ Posted by Solidmahog on March 28th, 2013.

Re: The status of mandolin players in Irish traditional music today.

“the suggestion that there is a hierarchy of instruments in the first place is completely erroneous… one can hear of an informal hierarchy with flutes, pipes, and fiddles at the ”top“ and other things below them on the food chain, but any musician who lives in the real world, playing at real sessions, knows that’s BS. Is there anyone, ever, who goes along to sessions and says, ”Oh, no! There’s a banjo here. Clearly the lazy bastard couldn’t be bothered learning the pipes!"

I think you’re mixing two different things here 1) the notion of a hierarchy of instruments as regards their longevity in the tradition and/or their impact in shaping the tradition over the last few hundreds years and 2) the notion that certain instruments require more musicianship than others to master.

The two have nothing to do with each other. No one can seriously dispute that the uilleann pipes, fiddle, and flute have been playing dance music in Ireland longer than the banjo and box, and that that troika of older instruments had put their stamp on the music long before the banjo and box were introduced. On the other hand I don’t think anyone can seriously claim that the banjo, box, mandolin, bouzouki, harmonica, or what have you can’t be the vehicle of musicianship of the highest caliber. I’ve heard tell of a guy who brings a saxophone to sessions in London who is a brilliant player of Irish Traditional Music. But the saxophone has had little or no impact in shaping what we know of as Irish Traditional Music today.

Interestingly, Breandan Breathnach in Folk Music and Dances of Ireland, in his chapter on Musical Instruments, has the chapter divided thusly:
-harp
-pipes (he covers warpipes, then uilleann pipes)
-fiddle
-flute
-whistle
-free-reed instruments (he covers melodeon, concertina, and accordion)

And that’s it! No mention whatever of fretted instruments or percussion instruments.

Tomas O Canainn in Traditional Music In Ireland says

“A discussion of style must include a detailed analysis of those instruments which have been important in the tradition and on which the music is still played, for it is here that style is formed and reshaped by succeeding generations. In the Irish contest this means that probably the two most important instruments in the living tradition are the uilleann pipes and the fiddle. In confining our attention to pipes and fiddle we are neglecting whistle and flute which do, however, owe a lot of their stylistic devices to the pipes and may thus be considered to be under discussion. Some traditional musicians will rightly point out that in disregarding the accordion, concertina, and mouth organ we are leaving out the most popular instruments in the present tradition. This is undoubtedly true, but if one is attempting to define traditional stylistic aspect of Irish music it is clear that they must be sought primarily on instruments which have helped to shape the tradition.”

Note that fretted instruments aren’t mentioned.

From “Amazon” website

“Breandán Breathnach (1912 – 1985) was an Irish music collector and Uilleann piper. Breathnach worked as a civil servant with the Department of Education and was responsible for collecting music from around Ireland. By the time of his death he had collected over 7,000 tunes published from 1963. His efforts were responsible for saving numerous tunes and he is generally recognised as a major figure in the preservation and continuation of Irish Music. He is best known for his Ceol Rince na hÉireann series . Breathnach published three volumes, in 1963, 1976, and 1985. Two additional volumes based on his work were published in 1996 and 1999. His daughter Niamh Bhreathnach is a Labour Party politician and a former Minister for Education.”

[Breathnach began music, on the warpipes, at an early age, but a recurring split lip caused him to change over to his uncle Joe’s uilleann pipes, and brought him into contact with a piper in the nearby Coombe, John Potts from Wexford. Potts became his first window on the country piping traditions of the previous century and it was to Potts that he dedicated his first major music collection. Another early piping teacher of his was William Andrews, who had been making commercial piping records from about the period of the First World War. Breandán later went as a pupil to the person who would epitomise uilleann piping nationally for some three or four decades in the middle of the century – Leo Rowsome. Rowsome played extensively on radio and on record, and at concerts up and down the country, and after his classes in Dublin it was his habit to give an impromptu recital for his pupils, so his was piping that Breandán would frequently have heard at first hand. He also learned uilleann piping from Br Gildas O’Shea, a de La Salle Brother from Kerry, and through him was in touch with a line of pipers back to Fraher, a famous Munster piper of about 1800. In the second half of the 1920s when Breathnach himself was still a teenager in school, James Ennis, father of Séamus, was also an important figure in piping and a frequent performer on the new medium of radio.]
