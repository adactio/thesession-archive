Commercialism agus an Ghaeilge

Two interesting discussions recently, Commercialism and whether it is elitist to use the Irish language in relation to the music.

In relation to the first this is sadly a feature of modern life. Too prevalent is the view that because someone wants something, they have a right to have it now. There is no sense that some things cannot be bought. They are valuable precisely because they are hard to get and take work. To play our music, as with any other, takes work, practice, thought and obstinate dedication. You have to approach the music with humility. What you get however is a grasping, selfish acquisitiveness. This expresses itself in our ,music with the clowns who have three chords on the guitar, or a bodhrán who wreck sessions. When challenged they say: ‘you’re spoiling my fun’. They have no understanding or respect for the culture.

There is a collection of 16th Century poetry in Irish, ; An Duanaire, (The Dispossed) written at the time of the last great flowering of Irish Culture. A continuous theme within the poems is an awareness that Gaelic culture was dying, but the people responsible, the English, did not have the capacity to understand how beautiful was the thing they were destroying.

Which brings us to the role of Irish in relation to the music. When I joined this site I was touched that so many people around the world appreciated the contribution of our music to world culture. I am astonished then to find that people who love the music disrespect the language. They are different sides of the same coin.

At one level this is inescapable. Séamus Ennis said that before he learned to play an air he learned to sing it. For him, this was essential to convey the meaning of the piece. Speaking in Irish you do not ask someone to sing a song, which would be ;Cán amhrán. You say: ;Abair amhrán, ‘Say a song’. This conveys the sense that the melody is a vehicle to communicate the meaning of the words. How you do this if you do not speak Irish eludes me.

I have had the pleasure, one of the most exhilirating experiences of my life, of sitting in Cúl a Dhúin in An Teilinn playing the fiddle in an exculsively Irish speaking Bar, most of whom have Irish as thier first language. It was grand.

And the last point I will make is the English language for me is just a tool. I have nothing against it, no more than this computer. But speaking English is like talking in black and white. Speaking Irish is talking in technicolour

Re: Commercialism agus an Ghaeilge

there’s been disagreement on a particular point here before, and for the sake of clarity (I hold out no hope whatsoever of resolving the issue) I set out the two camps:

1. You’re ability to play Irish music (that is, the traditional instrumental music that was once dance music but is now primarily played in sessions across the globe) will be enhanced if you have a wider understanding and appreciation of the culture/cultures from whence it emanated.

2. Diddley tunes are mere abstract notions that have developed as a curious and tiny subculture of their own. They exist in a strange ethereal world that is completely aside from the norms of day-to-day politics, famine, emigration, inequality, craic and even language. To deny that some of these more mundane human preoccupations once had an influence on the original formations of the music, is obviously idiotic, but camp 2 maintains that the music has long since broken the shackles of such earth-bound content.

Unfortunately, these discussions of culture and language often take on an ugly edge-- there is a thin line between pride in being in a sub-group of the human race, and unhealthy arrogance--between esprit de corps and elitism. We should all take pride in what we are, but at the same time, recognize that others have differences that they take pride in.
Here is where the assertion of camp 2 comes in--the folks who visit this site are drawn here by the common love of a particular body of music, despite living all over the world, and in all different cultures. The music, in and of itself, has become a bond that draws us together.
