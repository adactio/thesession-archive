Re: Copyrighted Tunes on the Session?

I think most of the tunes that the composer is known have the composer mentioned in the comments on the tune page (like both the Fraser tunes you mentioned). A writing of ABC notes of a tune in and of itself is not a copyright violation. And if you decide to play that tune as a performance, then it’s up to the venue to handle the copyright (via ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, or other licensing agencies if you’re outside the U.S.). If you record a copyrighted tune, then you simply need to buy licensing for it, and list the credit on the recording. Some other useful sites with information about tune origins are: https://www.irishtune.info/ and https://irishtunecomposers.weebly.com/alphabetical-list.html

It’s surprisingly difficult to play nothing but traditional tunes that are old enough to be entirely in the public domain, but for most purposes it shouldn’t really matter…

Re: Copyrighted Tunes on the Session?

Being that this site is noncommercial, and could be considered educational, I think the posting of tunes in ABC format here should fall under Fair Use, as Conical bore has mentioned. Here’s some more information on Fair Use in U.S. law: https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/more-info.html

But on a more general note, I think that it’s good for all of us to try to be aware of the origins of the tunes we play. How old they are, who wrote them, why they’re named the way they are, who you learned it from (whether it be from another player, from a recording, or from dots, and where the transcription came from). That is a difficult task when you’ve grown your repertoire up to a size where it’s difficult to sometimes even remember the name of a tune… But it’s part of what keeps the tradition ingrained in its roots, as well as what “drafts” new compositions into the tradition…

Re: Copyrighted Tunes on the Session?

Tunes themselves are copyright, separately from printed editions which may have their own copyright, typically the tune copyright belongs to the composer and the typesetting copyright to the publisher. Any use of the tune, in any way (including setting it in ABC and then publishing that ABC on a website), without the composer’s permission is an infringement of his copyright. ‘Fair Dealing’ in the UK and ‘Fair Usage’ in the US are slightly different, but in both jurisdictions they would not be applicable because they only apply to the use of small sections or quotations, not the complete work. And in the UK (not sure about US) the private study and education clause would not apply because the criteria is there must be no possible future commercial use - in the case of an ABC on this site someone else could include it in a book of tunes, or play from your transcription to make a number one hit.

So although every time a copyright tune is posted there is a copyright infringement, I think it is fair to say that for the vast majority of the time no one is going to do anything about it - no one is making money, no one is losing money, so there is no point in paying lawyers £1,000s to pursue it. But increasingly these days musicians are publishing their own compositions in book form, and in those cases having their tunes freely available on a site like this reduces their revenue, because people can download tunes for free instead of buying the book. In that situation they will want their compositions removed from the site, but they will normally do it with a ‘Cease and Desist’ notice rather than going immediately for legal action.

But the biggest danger is that if tunes are posted without crediting the composer someone else may pick it up and record it without realizing that it is in copyright and they need a license, potentially losing the composer his share of the revenue.

WRT artists selling tune books, there is usually “value added” because you’re not just buying the sheet music, but usually the books include the history of the tune, or some neat story that goes along with it.

My S.O. and I have purchased tune books by Lunasa, Altan, Liz Carroll, and Andrea Beaton, all with supplementary info that can’t be found here in thesession.org database. Several were signed by the artists, either at concerts or workshops. Not that it matters that much, but it’s a fun thing to have in the library.

It should be noted as well, that many of these band tune books include primarily tunes that weren’t written by the band itself, like the vast majority of tunes in the Altan and Lunasa books, although both include a few originals. You’re basically buying arrangements, like the settings here in the database. Presumably they are paying fees to composers or estates that are still under copyright. So when you purchase a tune book by a famous artist or band, you’re at least making a small secondhand contribution to the original composer for tunes under copyright. And in some cases like the Andrea Beaton and Liz Carroll books, you’re contributing directly to the composer.

Re: Copyrighted Tunes on the Session?

So… This isn’t a gray area. This board does not fall under “educational fair use” by any stretch of the definition. This is full-on publishing, even if it is not collecting revenue. “Fair use” includes uses like reproducing small sections of music for purposes of student study in an academic setting, such as a composition or music criticism class. Emphasis on “small” and on “sections.”

Second, regarding ITM melodies “not fixed in stone:” any setting of a copyrighted work would be considered a “derivative” work, traceable to the copyrighted work and subject to the permission of the copyright holder.

If one wanted to reproduce a copyrighted work here, the copyright holder’s permission would have to be obtained. If the copyright is held by the author, that might be easy to secure. If by a publisher, not likely, unless sharing provides the copyright owner’s expected per-use revenue stream.

If an author or music publishing houses ever felt that an unauthorized, free source of sheet music (or ABC) posed a threat to their ability to monetize their music, they could demand removal and sue for financial restitution and damage, at least under US law.

Don’t post copyrighted music here without the author’s express permission. Just don’t.

A practical example.
Speaking from experience of an amateur band in the UK. We’ve recorded CDs which we sell at concerts etc. The money pays the costs of the CD and goes into group funds, it’s never distributed to players.
When we finished a CD I had to research all the tunes and find who had copyrights and what was genuinely copyright free. All that info went on a PRS/MCPS form, they calculated the royalties due. We paid the amount due and they gave us a licence, which was required by the duplicators before they would process the CDs.
Where tunes had been written by friends who are not PRS/MCPS registered we paid them the same amounts that we were paying those who were.

The relevance of all this to the current discussion is that this site was one of the valuable resources in finding out who was owed for the copyright of tunes. If I write a fantastic tune, but it’s not positively identified to me online then the chances of me ever receiving credit or royalties are about nil.

Re: Copyrighted Tunes on the Session?

Susan K: Yes, you can sell copyright, you can also give it away, which is what the vast majority of composers do if they want to make money from their music. When you register a composition with MCPS or other rights organizations within the contract you assign the copyright over to them, which is what enables them to collect royalties. The contract stipulates that in exchange any royalties collected (less a percentage) will then be passed on to the composer.

As to buying copyright to make money out of it, yes it happens. Famously Michael Jackson bought the copy right to all the old Lennon & McCartney songs, so now whenever anyone performs an old Beatles song the Jackson family get the money. More worryingly, at the moment the Sony Corporation is busy buying up the rights to as much back catalogue as they can get their hands on. The assumption is that they are going to use it to try to strangle the likes of Spotify and iTunes at some point when they launch their own platform.

Obviously people buy copyright to make money from it, but once the song is published they can’t stop anyone else from recording it, or charge an exorbitant fee. There is a standard rate for royalties set down in law. If the work is registered with a rights organisation you just send them a cheque, if it isn’t then you send a ‘notice of intent’ with a cheque direct to the rights holder - you are telling them that you are going to record the song, not asking their permission. (That does only apply to recording. Printing sheet music, making arrangements or using the music as part of a film or stage show aren’t covered, in those situations you do have to negotiate a deal.)

Re: Copyrighted Tunes on the Session?

Barry: As the article you linked to states, it was the publishing rights that Jackson bought. Publishing rights are the songwriter’s rights.

https://www.biography.com/news/michael-jackson-paul-mccartney-beatles-music-catalog

Calum, not exactly. There is only one copyright on a tune, and only one copyright holder, but different licenses for different uses (performance, mechanical, derivative etc.) When you register the tune you assign the copyright in its entirety to MCPS, they then issue mechanical licenses, and PRS collect royalties on performances. (MCPS and PRS now work together under the umbrella ‘PRS for Music’, so there is no longer a need to register the work separately with PRS). It gets more complicated when you record a tune, because several people will have had creative input (writer, musicians, producer, cat’s grandmother etc.) and therefore have a stake in the copyright, but the recording itself only has one copyright, which is normally held by the production company. Again, if the recording is registered with a rights organization (in this case it will be PPL) the copyright is signed over and held by them, and they distribute royalties amongst all the interested parties.
