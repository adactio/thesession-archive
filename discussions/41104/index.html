Re: Can whistle be as emotionally powerful as the most moving of singers?

Singers don’t impress me, or rather, it’s difficult to impress me with singing. With shows like American Idol, X-Factor, etc. you see how common and accessible singing is, in my opinion.

I agree that songs are easier to connect with, but a song is a song with or without the words. Because when people hear the song instrumentally, they’ll still think about the words.

In this regard, bringing people to tears, I feel that it is more about the command and expressive ability someone has, over any given instrument, rather than the instrument itself. Piano, for example, isn’t a very “singy”, or modulatable instrument. But in the right hands, it can summon memories from the grave, and impale hearts of steel. And the funny thing about the piano, this can be done with or without an actual song.

The human voice is powerful, but so is a well-played instrument. And it’s not only about the power. It’s about the expression. Being able to use your dynamics, rhythm and timing, breaks, ornaments… Some songs don’t call for power, and I will personally always be more impressed by control and skill than by power itself. One of the most powerful singers I’ve heard, Beyoncé, has never, ever brought me to tears. But people singing her songs have. Adele also hasn’t made me cry, but again, people singing her songs have.

Street performing, I meet singers sometimes on the daily basis, who’ll sit in with me for a couple of sets. I’ve heard sweet voices, strong voices, unskilled voices, theatrical voices, and even mediocre voices. And though I respect singing as an art form, I don’t feel that it has a particular monopoly on evoking emotion.

↳ Posted by Michelle Mc on July 16th, 2017.

Re: Can whistle be as emotionally powerful as the most moving of singers?

The question is unanswerable, because it’s entirely subjective.

To move the question more to the objective/measureable side, the human voice and various instruments have different capabilities. In addition to giving the listener words, the voice is probably the most changeable instrument. You have a great range of dynamics and a vast range of timbres available. Even a violin can’t whisper as softly nor shout as loudly.

I will say that a fluteplayer can sing words while playing (the articulation of the words isn’t all that clear). The flute and clarinet are both capable of a pretty wide range of timbres, especially when you get away from “classical” playing.

There’s no embouchure on the pipes so the player can’t manipulate the tone nearly as much as an oboe, bassoon, clarinet, or sax player can. Whistles have a fixed tone-production mechanism too, and are more limited as to volume and timbre. That’s the reason why fipple flutes and bagpipes, used in Baroque ensembles, didn’t make the leap to the Classical orchestra.

These things are expressive potential. Obviously it’s up to the musician to make the most of the potential his instrument gives him, and great musicians will make great music on whatever instrument they play.

Re: Can whistle be as emotionally powerful as the most moving of singers?

“a movie has a lot longer to deliver a message whereas a tune is only a couple minutes. Of course there are long sets (of tunes)”

That’s one of the things about the piobaireachd played on the Highland pipes, the extended amount of time the listener exists in a single tune. It’s the music of another time, before cell phones and internet, before the industrial age, when people in general could give things time to develop. A piobaireachd can take 10 to 15 minutes, and its progression and development is deadly-slow to our current “we want it now!” mentality.

But for those listeners who devote the time and attention, the longer format delivers greater emotional power.

I would suggest that being moved by a whistle tune has much to do with knowing the history and meaning of the tune and being part of the culture which produced it. A ballad or a battle themed piece may be deeply moving to one who knows the significance of the tune but may be just a tune to one outside the culture.

The same may be said of voice too. A national anthem or a religious piece, no matter how well performed, may have to meaning to people outside that cultural group. Or such a piece, even modestly performed, may deeply move people within that cultural group.
