Re: First things

This is completely different to using training wheels when you first get a bike.

The problem is very specific and it appears as though this has already been identified. When you learn a style of music in conjunction with the dots you make that connection with what you read with what you hear. And if you learn to read in 4/4, for example, in conjunction with what a reel sounds like, you will always be able to reed reels notated in 4/4 as reels. But if you learn to read in conjunction with a different style of music and you see a reel notated in 4/4, for example, one of two things will happen:
a. You read the notation in the style you are familiar with and you make an arse of the reel and never realise.
b. You read read the notation in the style you are familiar with and you realise it’s nothing like the sound you have heard reels played so you ditch the notation and use your ears instead.

I’ve met more of the former than the latter, but I’m always overjoyed to meet the latter.

Re: First things

In my opinion the difference between learning by ear and learning by eye is that different circuitry is built in the brain. The process uses different wiring, if you like. That is why people stop playing when you take the dots away -- there is no way of switching to the other bit of circuitry.
It is possible to play a tune using the dots and at the same time learn it by listening to what you (and other people) are playing; but it needs experience and time. The tendency is usually to believe that what you are reading is the ‘right’ way.
At sessions, as long as you realize that what is written on the page might be erroneous, even misleading, the dots can help in finding the key (if there is one), provide visual cues to aid a novice’s developing memory, and help to internalize the basic structure. They can act as a prop for a nervous player, but should be avoided for that reason -- if you are propped up you aren’t going anywhere. As has been said, you will miss eye and ear cues from you fellow players, get sent the wrong way, stay tethered.
Imagine having a conversation, telling a few jokes, while reading everything from sheets of paper -- almost as bad as mobile phones for killing off a friendship.

“I’m playing tunes by ear. I play with people who learn tunes by ear. I’m looking for practical ideas some of you might have to offer which I can pass along about playing by ear.”

Getting back to that angle - it occurred to me that there are a couple of very different ways of learning by ear, not remotely the same process or leading to the same result. There is a local organization that teaches Scottish trad to adult learners, using a variety of methods depending on what the tutor is used to, but the commonest one is working through the tune phrase by phrase or even close to note by note, with your instrument. This quite often produces players with a limited repertoire and a wooden style; they haven’t learned the tune as an overall structure, they’ve learned to plod through it a phrase or a note at a time.

I tried learning things their way and it took some time before I realized I’d been doing it by a completely different and far more efficient method most of my life, and went back to that: getting the tune in my head, and then trying to get it out of there and onto an instrument. One way of ensuring you’ve got a tune in your head is to learn to sing it, but that isn’t always necessary.

A consequence is that it doesn’t matter very much whether the process that gets the tune into your head is from notation or listening. If you understand the idiom, your memory of the tune and your perception of its possibilities will get reshaped from the original anyway. (Otherwise transmission would always be deterioration - it isn’t; a good player can always create an inspired performance of something they’ve only heard or read crappy versions of).

The absolute pits in ear teaching was somebody I did some classes with who would teach tunes in chunks that *didn’t* follow phrase boundaries. He would play a phrase-sized chunk of tune just stopping anywhere, and expect his pupils to reproduce that - as if you were learning from a tape recorder with somebody randomly hitting the pause button.

Re: First things

A problem with teaching by phrases (which often equates to teaching a 2-bar “question” followed by teaching its 2-bar “answer”) is that if it’s done too slowly the rhythm goes to pot. I remember many years ago at a class where we being taught a jig, I was under the impression for several minutes that it was a reel.

Another aspect of the tune-teaching problem if there is a mix of abilities in the class; so there will always be some beginners and improvers who are genuinely struggling with basic technique, which doesn’t exactly help their chances of learning a tune by this method. And even if they get a tune into their head by listening then they won’t easily be getting it out on the instrument until technique has become automatic. That takes time.

One thing that can help slow beginners in a class is for the teacher to start off by playing the tune solo a few times at normal playing speed and then again slightly slower for the benefit of those with audio recorders. Then the class will be able to assimilate the rhythm of the music before they start. I know teachers who regularly do this.

Another thing is for the teacher to go easy on ornaments in the early stages. By all means introduce them when the class has learnt the tune well, but I have had experiences of tunes being taught from the start with so much ornamentation that the structure of the melody was genuinely obscured for most of the class.

Polkas are sometimes seen as an easy option for beginners, because there are fewer notes than in jigs and reels and they “look” and sound easy. I disagree; the opposite is in fact true. There are subtle rhythms in polkas that need a high standard of bowing technique to bring out at the proper playing speed. Teach polkas without those rhythms and at a slow speed and the result is likely to be stodgy mechanical playing. Which is why I am not in favour of polkas being taught to beginners until they have a reasonable command of technique. Incidentally, a good way to introduce to beginners (and others!) to what is on offer in polkas is to get them to watch a polka set being danced by good set dancers.

Specific Areas of Aural ~ Intervals by Sally Daunt

- The ‘classic’ approach of linking intervals to tunes: it’s much the best if the student finds examples him/herself.
- Songs to Help You Identify Intervals (which gives tunes at different pitches) on: http:// www.youtube.com/watch?v=rVyzzCijXSA&feature=related
- Put coloured Post-Its around a room with the names of tunes (or key words or/and pictures) that the student has associated with different
intervals - e.g. sheep for 5th (Baa Baa…), heart for minor 6th (Love Story) etc.
- Move around the room and touch these, singing the interval, saying the name of it and maybe also making the ‘shape’ of the interval in the air
with 2 hands (i.e. very close together for a 2nd, further apart for a 5th - much further apart for an octave etc or using Kodaly hand signs).
- Given an interval for recognition: sing up the scale, counting the notes as you go. The student needs to be able to sing scales accurately
though! • Particularly for non-keyboard players, show the intervals on a keyboard; student should play them.
- Make a keyboard (piano size) on card and use this ‘on its side’ - i.e. vertically. Show and touch the intervals this way, singing them, which may
make more sense as the sounds literally ‘go up and down’. Do this with an electronic keyboard on its side which can then play the notes.
- Prepare different illustrations of a keyboard each one with a different interval highlighted in a colour.
- For the student alone: use a keyboard app on an iPhone to practise if the student has nothing else.

One of the tests for dyslexia - short term memory & balance

This is a balance test, one that slightly upset me. First I was asked to walk a straight line, one foot in front of the other, with my eyes open. Yes, like a sobriety test, and there were others. Anyway, no problem there. Then I was asked to do the same thing with my eyes closed ~ it was like I was drunk, very poor balance and the wavering and miss steps to support that.

It seems that dyslexics use their eyes to compensate for a poor short term memory, to help keep balance where the brain isn’t quick enough to process it all with the eyes closed. Evidently the whole thing with balance is very complex, multi-tasking…

When carrying anything, like dinner, drinks, anything that might spill, I need to keep it in view, or, inevitably, I spill it. :-/
