Re: Is Classical A Good Way To Start Before Fiddle???

Welcome Blu,
Your three years of classical training won’t hurt you at all if you don’t let it, and some of it will no doubt help you a great deal--especially in terms of bow control, good finger technique and intonation, and generally just being familiar with your instrument. Either way, three years of even fairly intensive instruction isn’t enough to set you for life. Lots of trad players do have some classical training (seems to me that this is more common in the Scottish tradition than Irish, but look at Matt Cranitch and Winnie Horan).

The main thing is to realize that, compared to classical violin, fiddle music has very few rigid rules for you to follow, so how you bow a given tune, the melody notes you choose, the embellishments, etc., are much more *your choice* than they would be in classical. Also, no one will criticize you for your technique unless it really interferes with the quality of the sound you produce. (Fiddlers love to praise other fiddlers whose playing is “a little rough around the edges” but full of life. On the other hand, few of us would turn our noses up at a fiddler who has brilliant technique, as long as the music is still full of life.)

The best advice I can think of for you is to find fiddlers whose playing you enjoy and listen to them as much as possible. Find one (or more) to teach you. Stock up on cds. Just get the fiddle sounds and attitude into your heart and head.

Re: Is Classical A Good Way To Start Before Fiddle???

Bluefiddle
There’s no reason why you can’t live in both worlds, classical and trad. One benefits the other. If, in classical violin playing you’ve learned how to go up and down the fingerboard easily, you’ll have no trouble in coping with the occasional fiddle tune that uses a c or d on the E string, and to be able to easily play an e on the A string if necessary, and you should have a vibrato available for when it’s needed (not all that often in trad fiddle playing).
When you’ve been around trad a little while you’ll realise that the “classical” way of holding the fiddle and bow isn’t the only way, and isn’t necessarily always the best or most efficient. Personally, I think the way many trad fiddlers hold their instruments has a lot more to do with 18th century baroque style than with today’s somewhat regimented symphonic style. It’s not for nothing that classical baroque musicians sometimes go to the trad/folk musicians for advice on how to play early music.
Another thing, you’re still young enough to have little trouble with learning by ear (if you can’t do it already). For those of us who have been playing classical music in orchestras for most of their lives and can sight-read like there’s no tomorrow, learning to play by ear in later life is a skill that’s just a little more difficult to acquire (but by no means impossible) - you’re young enough to be able to do both.
Go for it!

A slightly different route into irish trad fiddle from classical can be from an instrument other than classical violin. In my case it is the cello. Thinking about that, what it means is that I haven’t had to unlearn (or more accurately to modify or ignore) skills particular to the classical violin, but I have come to the fiddle with a left hand I know how to control and knowing how to use the bow, plus appreciation of intonation and tone production etc. Holding the fiddle bow is virtually identical to holding the cello bow. I sorted out holding the fiddle itself by observing and talking to others and reading around the subject, the objective being to identify the fiddle hold which for me felt natural, relaxed and efficient, with no sense of strain. A consequence of this was that I soon found that playing without a shoulder-rest for me felt far more relaxed and comfortable, so I haven’t used one since.

I believe that anyone who has trained their fingers for some time on another instrument (piano, guitar, woodwind) will find this training to their advantage when coming to the irish fiddle, a good proportion of the necessary muscular control will already be there as far as the left hand is concerned.

Re: Is Classical A Good Way To Start Before Fiddle???

Blu, as you can see, there’s any amount of ways to start fiddling (and to keep fiddling). Whether they’re “good” or not is a purely subjective decision. Each player comes to the tradition from a different angle from the player next to them in the circle. As Cait says, each person brings different experiences to their playing (which relates to the passion in playing thread), and that should always be valued even if you happen to think what a player brings isn’t particularly good.

To make a broad and hugely generalized statement, there are purists who we really need to keep us honest and to keep the tradition on track, and who give us passion for the pure drop and what drove the music in the first place. At the other end of the spectrum, we have those who are willing to mix everything they know from other traditions into the music, who we really need to keep the tradition alive and moving and breathing with controversy and passion of a different yet similar sort from the purists…

We need all these folks along the spectrum. It’s confusing and sometimes maddening to get caught in the middle, but it’s a huge part of what keeps the tradition alive.

Classicists tend to know their instruments in ways slightly different from the way the traditionalists know their instruments -- until a certain level. (To say it’s technical is misleading and patronizing, though -- they come at it a different way, but that doesn’t mean it’s a bad way, just another angle.) But at a certain very high level, it no longer matters where you got your knowledge of your instrument -- this has been iterated to me many times by many great players. The important thing is that you *get* that knowledge of your instrument, whichever way you chose to get it. You’ll have to work through the whole thing, though -- no shortcuts available to anyone here.

Many great players started classical, or started traditional and then got classical playing added in, or have never played anything but traditional. How you get what you need to get is relatively unimportant -- what one player will need is not what another player will need. The important thing is GETTING IT. If you don’t lose sight of that, then what your friends tell you becomes unimportant, because your journey to the music you hear in your head will lead you to wherever and whatever you need to get there.

Have fun doing it, too. When you come right down to it, we get born, and then we die. What we do in-between that is up to us, and my own feeling is that you might as well enjoy the trip. :)

Wow….I suspect Zina has a law degree she hasn’t told us about. Very nice summation and closing argument, barrister :-)

Trad music tends to emphasize rhythm and melody over tone, intonation, and technical prowess. It’s *dance* music, and dancers honestly don’t mind if your tone is a bit scratchy, as long as the beat is strong and you give it that “lift” that feet just can’t ignore.

Most classical music, on the other hand, is listening music, so as essential as rhythm and melody may still be in classical music (and some forms within the genre do intentionally sidestep these qualities), a singing tone, good intonation, and technical flair rise in importance.

When I was first learning fiddle, I thought about this dichotomy and decided to learn about rhythm and melody mostly from other trad players, and improve my tone production, intonation, and dynamic range by asking classical players for help. On and off over the course of the first 10 years, I took lessons from Kevin Burke and other trad players, but I also took three semesters of classical violin in college, and sought advice from classical violinists.

And it all helped. I don’t play classical music--at all-- but I’m a better trad player than I would have been otherwise. Partly because Irish music is becoming more and more a listening music, too. So people (even dancers :-) appreciate a sweeter tone and accurate intonation, etc., as long as it’s not at the expense of that undeniable pulse.

Somewhere along my learning curve I realized that classical music doesn’t have a monopoly on good tone, etc., and so I just decided to listen to lots of musicians in many genres and try to incorporate the best of what each had to offer. You want to hear the full range of tone a violin can produce? Listen to Grapelli, Vassar Clements, Paul Anastasio. Of course, no matter how far I roamed musically, Irish music always made up 98 percent of what I played and listened to, so that’s my strongest influence by far, and it shows.

In short, do whatever works to get the sound *you* want out of your fiddle. Try to be conscious about the musical influences you choose (some of them will still be subliminal, and that’s okay too). And, as everyone else is encouraging you to do, relax and enjoy the journey.

Oh, and realize that not all trad players are satisfied with their tone or intonation, so just because you hear music that’s a little rough around the edges doesn’t mean that the player isn’t hoping to improve. But it’s better to play the tunes a bit scratchy *now* than wait until you sound like Menuhin before you start learning the Kesh. I don’t know anyone who plays fiddle who isn’t constantly working on sounding “better” (whatever that means to them), in some way or another.

In fact, striving to get better is part of what I enjoy most about the music. If there was nowhere for me to get better, then I would actually be bored, which is not a state I usually find myself in -- a matter of choice.

As one gets older, that drive is usually modulated a bit as sometimes other things become just as, if not more, important. That’s natural. But still I strive contantly to be a better player, because that’s the way I’m built, and I don’t think there’s anything wrong with that, anymore than there’s anything wrong with wanting to simply relax and enjoy playing rather than actively striving to get much better.

Standards are a very dicey thing to talk about in public among people who might have a different standard, much like religion and politics.

Blu -- that’s it! All ways to the music are valid and good for the player that needs that way. Two people walking side by side never travel the same road. If your friends tell you that you’re taking the wrong side of the road, consider what they say and apply it if you feel it’s right for you, and then smile and thank them for their input and go your own way. Enjoy the trip on your own terms!

Re: Is Classical A Good Way To Start Before Fiddle???

As I’m sure Trevor will agree, to play in an orchestra you need ears. We all need to use them all the time we play. When we (classically trained or not) play from printed music, some of our focus is diverted into interpreting the dots, and we can’t focus so much on the sound. So the thing is to start focussing on the sound as soon as possible in the process of learning the tune.
We can’t turn back the clock even if we wanted to, so if we can read music we’ll always be music readers, for which we should be glad. I think it’s just a matter of using the method of learning we’re best at, and never forgetting that music is sound.
