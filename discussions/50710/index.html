How to learn to read sheet music effectively (NOT note letters / counting)

If you consider a simple melody like the Kesh jig:

X: 1
T: The Kesh
R: jig
M: 6/8
L: 1/8
K: Gmaj
|:G3 GAB|A3 ABd|edd gdd|edB dBA|
GAG GAB|ABA ABd|edd gdd|BAF G3:|
|:B2B d2d|ege dBA|B2B dBG|ABA AGA|
BAB d^cd|ege dBd|gfg aga|bgg g3:|

Looking at the tune there are many repeated visual patterns. Rhythmically, there are only 3 distinct patterns in the tune:

- G3
- GGG
- G2G

If you consider the melody in chunks of 3 8th notes, it likewise consists of a relatively small number of distinct patterns that are shifted up / down within the range (a few of them are listed below):

- GAB
- GAc
- AGG
- GAG
- cBG

These chunks are called ‘figures’, ‘melodic figures’ for pitches, and ‘rhythmic figures’ for rhythms. Tunes are build by sticking them together in various orders, and you’ll find the same figure patterns in countless other jigs (and a different set of figures for reels, etc).

When you read words in English, you do not sound them out one letter at a time, you look at a word and it automatically pronounces itself to you. What’s going on is that your subconscious mind has memorised the visual pattern of the entire word, and the sound / meaning of the word it corresponds to.

The goal of reading music is equivalent to this, to be able to look at some notation and immediately know what it sounds like (audiation), and what you need to do to perform it on an instrument (kinaesthetic responses).

And achieving this end goal is straightforward using figures:

- For rhythm figures, you listen to the equivalent rhythm performed (by computer software or a teacher), and clap it while looking at the notation. If you do this every day for a week or so, your subconscious will internalise the muscle memory and sound of the rhythm, connecting it to the visual representation in notation.

- Likewise for melodic figures, you can take a figure and perform the pattern starting from every single note in a given scale. For the figure DEF, this would be DEF, EFG, FGA, and so on. Performing the figure on your instrument in different places trains muscle memory for how the pattern is performed.

After a while you find that you can look at the notation for a figure, and it ‘plays back to you’ in your mind.

Continuing with the language analogy, a figure (either melodic or rhythmic) is like a word. The number of figures that you have internalised muscle memory for on your instrument is your ‘vocabulary’: the more of these patterns that you have internalised, the greater the number of tunes that you can sight read easily.

To get started, identify some common figure patterns, reproduce them in ABC notation, use a tool to play it back to you, and perform it repeatedly for several days, until doing so is effortless. Gradually build up your vocabulary of rhythmic and melodic figures.

The problem that I see witch the traditional process of teaching music reading (counting and note names) is that it puts a lot of effort on the conscious mind. Reading using note names leads to an internal process of:

- Recognise note position -> remember note letter -> remember fingering for this letter -> execute fingering -> play note.


This is SLOW, not just because of the number of individual steps, but also because it encourages reading music one note at at a time. For fluent reading we do not want to be doing any of the above, reading is FAR easier if you train the subconscious mind to directly connect the visual pattern formed by a large group of note symbols directly to their equivalent performance, without use of the conscious mind whatsoever.

If one reads enough music onto an instrument using this conscious ‘position, name, fingering’ process, the subconscious mind will eventually start associating visual patterns directly to fingerings, but not efficiently.

Not all melodies use the same figures in all positions, some will be practised more than others, and if you read a tune that uses a pattern you have not internalised, you will stall because your subconscious doesn’t know what to do with it.

Conscious awareness of how staff positions relate to note names can be useful for communicating with other musicians, but this should not factor in when sight reading onto an instrument.

Learning rhythms figures by imitating the equivalent rhythm by ear also has a clear advantage over counting rhythms: it allows you to easily imitate swing / lilt (things that can not be represented with simple mathematical subdivisions).

It’s not difficult to learn to read simple single-line tunes in keys near to D, which is all you need for trad music, all you need is to devote a bit of time to it. What matters is the time you devote: it doesn’t need to be a lot of time per day, but it needs to be every day, as with any new skill.

I’m not sure there needs to be much more said about it, honestly. It’s like touch-typing: there’s no reason not to know how to look at a tune written out in staff notation and have a good idea of how it sounds. If you want to be able to do that, it’s no harder than learning how to type, and a lot easier than learning how to play the tunes in the first place. If you don’t want to be able to do that, that’s also fine, but you need to be aware that the inability to read staff notation is an optional condition.

Re: How to learn to read sheet music effectively (NOT note letters / counting)

Heh. What follows is long and probably kind of boring, and can probably be skipped without any great loss, but Respect’s post provided me with a bit of a rant, so here goes.

“Reading” math is definitely a thing, and I would say the analogy to reading a natural language is a very tight one. “Reading” a number is very much like reading a word: it’s got a precise meaning (which can be characterized variously, depending whether we’re talking about a quantity or a measure or some other sort of thing, and depending on our philosophical commitments - see the new book “The Great Math War” for more on this) and it can - indeed, must - be interpreted in its context to be understood properly. So, if you’re talking about the radius of a circle, you’re talking about a distance between two points, probably in the real plane (so we’re dealing with a continuous quantity), its imaginary component is likely to be zero, and so forth.
We do exactly the same thing when we read a word in English - the word “strong” has a core central meaning, which is actually somewhat difficult to characterize fully, but it can only be properly interpreted in context. A strong man with a strong will drinking strong tea and declaiming with a strong accent has many strengths. If we say that tea is “strong”, that ultimately means something about the concentration of various chemicals suspended in the hot water, while “strong” in the context of an accent means something about the degree to which a speaker’s idiolect varies from some accepted norm in the direction of some reference dialect. (roughly). Totally different interpretations in context, but we understand exactly what all of them mean, and nobody thinks that “strong” is a particularly complicated word.

But when we’re talking about reading math, we’re not just talking about reading numbers. You mention the computation of the area of a circle - notice how much simpler it is to simply say “pi times r squared” (or, in LaTeX notation, $pi r^2$) than it is to try to explain that formula in English. That’s the point of mathematical notation: it allows people who work with mathematical concepts to efficiently and clearly communicate with other mathematicians. And mathematicians definitely “read” math in the same sense that they read English. By this I mean that a mathematician sees a line of symbols, determines what they mean in their context (for example, the Greek letter pi has at least three standard meanings in different mathematical contexts), and only then puzzles out what it was that led the writer to put those symbols in that order. In the same way, when we read, we determine what words the writer used, we decide how to interpret them in their context, and finally we decide what it is we think the writer was trying to convey by putting those symbols in that order. (this is roughly the standard ladder of linguistics: morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics: what words did you say, how do they fit together, what might they mean when put together that way, and what did you mean by saying that?)

By contrast, reading music is _much_ simpler than either reading math or reading English! There’s no interpretation at all to be done when I look at the dots for a tune, the notation is completely unambiguous. The meaning of a given symbol is completely determined* by its shape, its location, and in some cases by the presence or absence of some other symbols. (eg, the clef, the number of sharps or flats in the key signature, etc). Since those things are completely explicit, all of the work is done for me. In particular, I never have to do the final step of wondering what the composer _meant_ by those notes - i can think about that if I want, but that’s totally optional. Once I know what pitches are to be played in what order and with what emphasis, I’ve read the piece.

Of course, I might do some interpretation of the _tune_ once I’ve read and understood the notes, and it’s likely that you and I would play the tune somewhat differently because we’re different people with different tastes and different influences, but the information conveyed by staff notation is exactly what it says, and there is essentially no work at all involved in converting those symbols to their intended meanings - that is, in reading it.

* okay, fine, the “generic ornament” symbol commonly used in this music could be considered ambiguous and needing interpretation, but this really doesn’t affect the actual point I’m making

My opinion, as a guy who can’t read a score well enough to play from it at tempo. It’s just not that hard! Especially the simplified scores you get in the tune books and posting we get in various websites. They are a “shorthand” for the music, a system of 5 lines and four spaces ( and a few lies and spaces above and below)with simple note shapes to tell you something about the timing. From there it helps to know something about the language. A B C D E F G and whole, half, quarter, eight and rarely sixteenth. I don’t know what is taught in schools now but I learned that much in elementary school. More advanced scores offer much more information but are not that hard either. Those dots, elongated arrows, smooth arcs, squiggly lines, words in Italian, all mean something. ( An aside here, classical guitar music uses the letters p i m a and numbers 1 2 3 4 5 6 to indicate which right hand finger and which string to play, where to use harmonic notes).

Of course there can be, is, more to it. Take any string of 6 notes in a single bar, simply written as 6/8 time. There are very many ways to phrase those notes. Slur all 6, slur in groups of 3, staccato some, stress the 1 and 4 or the 3 and 6 … you get the idea. I liken it to a line from Macbeth “Tomorrow tomorrow and tomorrow creeps forth this petty pace from day to say until the last syllable of recorded time”. Re: How to learn to read sheet music effectively (NOT note letters / counting)

Richard Cook wrote: “It’s why ABC is so clunky for me to sightread: rather than shapes it’s just a row of symbols.”

If you come from standard notation, with its ability to show melodic contours, yes, at first, ABCs look like a nonsense stream of scrambled alphabet. But like any system, with practice, you’ll start to read whole clumps of ABCs, much like letters grouped into words. Tunes read like sentences—common “words” keep popping up, and structures (e.g., the 5th measure repeating the 1st) are readily sussed. You can even see contours and arpeggios once you internalize the symbols that distinguish lower, middle, and higher notes.

Having learned to sight read the dots as a young lad, and encountering ABCs about the same time, I now have decades of experience with both systems and can read both at tempo (~110 bpm). It’s mostly a matter of whether the time invested to become fluent is worth the return. In my case, fluency in ABCs happened only because it was the system of choice in several sources I was reading to find rare old tunes, and to share tunes with mates who didn’t read the dots. I didn’t do a crash course in them, more of an inadvertent acquisition.

All that said, I learn my tunes by ear. Any notation system is just a handy way to access and share tunes when an aural source isn’t available or would be cumbersome.
