“The Mason’s Apron”, - as far as I know - was only ever a 2-part reel until Sean McGuire added a load of variations to it as a musical showpiece. There’s a version associated with Co. Kerry, which was made popular by Kevin Burke who recorded it with Jacky Daly. There’s yet another version associated with Micho Russell, recorded and made popular by Gary Shannon, also “De Danann”.
My favourite version is a 4-part version in “D” by “The Boghall & Bathgate Pipe Band”, which they called “The Mason’s Ape”.
PS - Cathal McConnell’s whistle version is worth looking out for. It was recorded, along with Aly Bain’s Shetland fiddle version [ “Lowrie Tarrell” ], on “Boys Of The Lough - Second Album”.

Re: The Mason’s Apron

Yeah, learn as many parts as you like. There is no definitive version. Sean maguire set the standard for the flashy multi-part version so I’d go to his recordings if you want to do that, but honestly I’ve heard three and four and even five part versions at sessions and no-one ever seems to know exactly how it’s going to go this time round. I’d say just learn a version you like and join in with the parts you know if someone else starts it. If you’re feeling really on fire you can kind of faff through unfamiliar parts since they’re basically iterations of two chords and that little phrase at the end, but it’ll get very muddy and chaotic if you’re not careful.

Kellie, as a budding whistle player, I think it’s definitely worth learning “Micho Russell’s Mason’s Apron” (in G). It’s simpler, and in my opinion far nicer than the multi-part versions, and it often seems to be treated as a tune in its own right.

If you haven’t already got it, get your hands on a copy of his “Rarities and Old Favourites”. It might take time for your ears to adjust to his playing if you’re used to hearing more modern styles, but it should be essential listening for anyone interested in playing Irish music on the whistle.

I first came on the Mason’s Apron in the early 1950s. Often heard it played by Scottish Country Dance Bands on the radio. Two parts in Key A. Then I heard it played on the box, I think it was the late Paddy O‘Brien playing it. Four parts all in G. That’s when it became a must learn tune for budding box players and that was the early fifties. Through the following decades that was the usual way of the tune. Many musicians usually fiddle players kept adding variations. I used to play the four parts in G and then lift it Scottish style into A…….Gives the tune a good ’lift’….

Re: The Mason’s Apron

I got my version from an old recording by Sean Maguire - “Ulster’s Flower Vale” (1969).

To my knowledge that was the first version to be significantly different, and the tune given a “kick up the arse.”

It was lovely playing by Sean Maguire in his prime - and I heard he was loaned a Guarneri (or some such posh Italian) violin for the recording.

The tune had 4 parts in it - the first two original A and B parts, then a third part, all played in 1st postion.

The 4th part was in 3rd position, on the E string, using mainly the A, open E and C#.

He then repeated the B (2nd) part, an octave higher, on the E string, in 5th position.

I think that’s where the “Sean Maguire’s 5-part version” came from. I count it as 4 parts, but maybe that’s splitting hairs :)

As for the “cop out” comment earlier - if you can play all the parts, all good and well. If you can only play in 1st position, I don’t see why that should stop you playing the tune with just the standard A and B parts (plus the 3rd part that Sean Maguire invented).

It also means the tune would be more “session friendly”, and not thought of only as a solo.
