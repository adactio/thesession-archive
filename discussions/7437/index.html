your personal balancing act…

What I’d like to ask is how you, you personally, right now, balance the need to progress as a player, the need to get to where you want to be, to be as good, technically or whatever, as you picture yourself being, with the simple joy of just playing this stuff, playing with like-minded friends and acquaintances, etc. (Or, if you’ve already gotten where you want to be, what it feels like to be there and if it’s given you new goals, or what.)

I’m not looking for a thesis on what the balance *should* be, or judgements on anyone else’s snapshot of where they are right now, be warned, but for personal stories and explanations of how you balance the two needs out.

Also, if you care to, I’d also like to hear where you place this stuff in your life, how you balance it out with the needs of family, work, etc.

These subjects have been coming up between a lot of my friends recently, so I thought I’d see how you lot are doing with it all.

Re: your personal balancing act…

This is an interesting subject, and one which concerns me far more in recent years than when I started out 20-(mumble) years or so ago.
I never had much in the way of musical instruction, either for a specific instrument or as part of a school curriculum, so my grasp of theory was for a long-time quite lacking. I managed to figure out just enough things on my own to make it work; hey, people seemed to like to play music with me, or to hear me play it, so either a lot of them were trying to spare my feelings because I’m a nice guy, or there was _something_ there.
In any case, I didn’t really think much about how I was playing the music. I didn’t know what a I-IV-V chord progression was, but if something I played seemed to sound OK, then that was that. I played tunes almost exclusively by ear, and if I couldn’t get every note down, well, I got enough of them.
It’s not like I got out to every session in the Boston area, or went to one absolutely every week, but I think I had a pretty good lay of the land, and if I didn’t know everyone I at least had some idea who they were and what they did.
Then domesticity hove into view, I got married, had a couple of kids, bought a two-family, etc., and I stopped going to sessions, the band I was in gradually faded to black, and my musical activities were largely confined to the living room -- usually as entertainment for my kids.
Several years ago, the kids being older, I started to get back into playing music more regularly. And I discovered the landscape had changed dramatically: a lot of the people I’d known just weren’t around anymore, and there were plenty of new faces to take their place, along with new styles and new sounds. So I felt increasly compelled to find out where I “fit” in this altered landscape, i.e., could I derive the same kind of satisfaction from what I’d been doing?
Towards that end, I looked a lot closer at my style and approach, and this entailed learning more musical theory than I ever had -- yeah, including those I-IV-V progressions! -- and developing more of an ability to read music. I’ve also been fortunate enough to be around musical friends who have given me great pointers and suggestions for expanding on what I do.
So, beyond a doubt, I’m a much better musician than I was five, 10, 20 years ago -- although I suppose most anyone could claim that they’ve improved over time. But in my case, when I think of what I was capable of “back then” and what I can do now, I see a marked improvement. This has brought me a lot of personal satisfaction, and helped me better appreciate and enjoy the music.
At the same time, I’m aware now that I could still be even better, and here’s where the balancing comes in. Does the “better” mean just becoming more technically proficient, or deepening my understanding of music theory, and traditional music in general, or all of the above? But then I have to stop myself from over-thinking and over-analyzing, and remember that often the simpler way is as good as, if not better than, a concerted effort to come up with obscure, arcane chord structures.

I like jamming, but I also very much enjoy performing as part of a band, sharing ideas, putting together arrangements and so on. I’ve been in a couple of occasional ensembles these past couple of years and hope to have more regular experiences.

Well, I think the chief issue is simply realizing the issue exists. Practicing and playing are two different things. Once you seperate the two striking a balance simply becomes a matter of delegating some time to practice (you could call this by the nasty word “discipline”) and letting yourself go to simply play the rest of the time.

Technique isn’t the point of music, music is the point of technique and music is joy.

Re: your personal balancing act…

Thanks for starting this Zina, I’ll be interested to see the range of responses.

I’ve wrestled with these questions of balance my whole life, and I’m at a loss for clear answers, even ones that work just for me, personally.

I’ve recently taken a break from the steady diet of sessions and gigs and learning fresh tunes, and even playing much at all. I’m happy to suddenly have lots of time to spend with my family, and it’s far more relaxing to go backpacking or river running when you’re not annoyed by the opportunity costs (“Three days without my fiddle?! Nooooo! I’ll get rusty!”).

But after many months away from the tunes, it’s damn hard to join in at a session and keep the flow going. Tunes I’ve played for 25 years fail me, rhythms feel stilted, and my mind goes blank when it used to be full of music. In short, leaving the daily routine has happily lowered the sense of pressure in my life, but some of the thrill is gone, too.

So I’m blindly stumbling toward a different, more personal approach to the music. Case in point happened just last weekend at the annual Irish festival down in Butte, MT. Rose and I pulled into uptown about the same time as the cold front--rain, wind, and cold (we’re talking snow on the mountains just above town). So we skipped the jury-rigged indoor concerts and went out for a nice long meal at that Italian restaurant Paddy’s Angels descended upon two years ago. Mmmmm…lobster and crab ravioli, ceasar salad, and a zippy red wine. Then back to where the concerts were going--a crowded room, no where to sit, no way to see the dancers‘ feet, muffled sound system, etc. After 10 minutes of that I was ready to leave. So we ended up at the near-empty Finlen, where we ran into two friends. Sat down in the lobby and had a nice little “session”--me on fiddle, Guylaine on whistle, and Dave on bodhran. Some punters wandered out of the bar and sat down around us, and we just played tunes for two hours, able to hear each other, take time to visit between tunes, and sip our pints. Then, when Solas finished their set back at the festival, the whole crowd showed up. The usual session scene ensued. Nothing wrong with that, except my brain isn’t working at full bore any more, and my fingers weren’t up to the fluters’ hyper speed.

That experience reinforced my current leanings toward playing with just one or two other people, more impromptu and sociable. When our local sessions crank up to full speed again in September, I’ll be curious to see how I feel.

I guess this is what it feels like to come out the other end after being obsessed for years. Unfortunately, even if you get good, everything else does not necessarily fall into place.

I never have envisioned music as my livelihood, so that by itself limits the time and energy I devote to it in comparison to family and work.

Sometimes, that’s just impossible -- I need to be at home, or work is taking a lot of time and energy -- but I think it’s significant that I now would regard a month without a session as the exception, not the rule. I like Al’s reminder about perfection. Yo Yo Ma says music isn’t about perfection, but expression, and I’m finding that sessions tend to offer a relatively narrow window for expression, certainly compared to playing solo, or with just one or two friends. Over the last few years, it’s grown frustrating to work up a tune, find my connection to it, only to trot it out at a session and end up with just another horse race to the finish.

I don’t mind working hard to improve my playing, but day in, day out, I don’t want to feel *obligated* to do that. I don’t want an upcoming show or chronic reputation (even if it’s just the big-fish-in-the-little-bowl syndrome) to compel me to practice or rehearse, to meet some presupposed musical standard.

Given this, the smaller, impromptu sessions seem to suit me better.

“Balancing act” is a good definition of my life these days. I came home to work part time at bookkeeping by telecommute and write part time. I thought I would have more time for the music.

But the research for the book is snowballing, and I keep having to stop and work out technical details as I write. (It’s a book about handcrafting.) That takes time.

And my boss‘ clients are constitutionally incapable of figuring out three seconds before the deadline that they need to have a job done. They figure it out a week after the deadline goes by. “Ohmigodi’vegottahaveitYESTERDAY!!!!” is the common refrain, and it’s hard to get anything done when I’m always having stuff dropped on me out of a clear blue sky and being told, “Yes, I know you were supposed to have that one done by tomorrow, but put it back to Friday and do this one now instead.” *Grind of rapidly changing gears*

And then there’s spending time with my husband and friends.

So realistically, three or four days a week I don’t get to play at all. And my time here, and on the couple of email lists I belong to, gets pared down pretty hard in order to squeeze in time to play on a day when I otherwise wouldn’t have a chance. Which means that on the three or four days a week when I *do* get to play, I play for about 30 minutes.

What I’ve ended up doing is, on the one hand, just playing the stuff I love, that crowds its way to the front of my head begging to be played right now, and not worrying about anything else; and on the other hand, when I goof up on a passage in one of the tunes I love, taking a few minutes then and there to woodshed it back closer to where I want it to be.

Forget perfection, I’ll never get there; I want to play well enough to feel good when I put the fiddle away instead of fretting about the mistakes I made. And in spite of the time squeeze in recent weeks, I’m mostly achieving that. For which I am immeasurably grateful.
