Re: Transpose

If I’m understanding you correctly, you have a tune written in one flat, which would ordinarily play on an F or a C whistle, and you want to play the same note names but on a D whistle, without and cross-fingerings or half-holing?

That would not be transposing the tune to D, it would be playing a different tune. Might well sound fine - lots of things sound good on a whistle - but it won’t be the same tune. To transpose the tune to D, you’d have to shift the notes - one flat is the key of F or one of its modes, and obviously a D whistle is D or one of its modes, so you’d want to move the notes down two “names”.
For example, suppose you came across a familiar tune, written in an unfamiliar key, titled “The Funmore Lasses”, with the first part written as

|:GFGA BABc | dg g2 dg g2| GFGA B2 dB| c2 dc BG AF| …. :|

If you wanted to transpose this to the key of two sharps, you’d move everything down by two “names”:

|:EDEF GFGA| be e2 be e2 | EDEF G2 BG| A2 BA GE FD | … :|

(and restore the original title, “The Dunmore Lasses”)

On the other hand, if you have a tune written in the key of one flat which you play on an F whistle, you can effectively transpose that tune to the key of two sharps by playing the same fingerings on a D whistle. If you think about the note names on the two whistles, you’ll see that I’ve just said the same thing in different words :)


Transposing tunes manually is easy enough, and if you want to understand music, I’d strongly suggest you spend the trivial amount of time getting used to the process. If you transpose a few tunes from one key to another, it’ll become pretty automatic, and then you won’t have to depend on machines to do a basic task which doesn’t require them.

Re: Transpose

Let’s work on improving your understanding, rather than crippling it!

Consider the basic notes on an F whistle: from low to high, you have F G A B C D E (F), where the B is flat and the last F is an octave above the first.
Played from low to high, you get an F major scale. Probably you’re with me so far.

Now consider the same basic notes on a D whistle: D E F G A B C (D), where the F and C are both sharp. Again, playing from low to high you get a D major scale.

So we have the same “tune”, the major scale, which we’ve transposed from one key (one flat) to another (two sharp) by playing the same fingerings. If anything about this so far doesn’t make sense, stop now and ask the questions, because what comes next is the important bit but it doesn’t work if the setup didn’t work. (and if the setup failed, that’s my fault, not yours)

------------

So, if you’ve got the setup, here’s the punchline: We’ve transposed a tune from one key to another by playing the same fingerings on different whistles. This was a purely mechanical process, we just swapped out one part of the machine for another and didn’t have to think about it at all. Now let’s think about what we did by that swap. The first note in the original version, the F scale, was an F. The first note in the transposed version, the D scale, was a D. So by swapping one whistle for another, we moved the note down by two “names”, from F to D. The second note, same thing: we started by playing a note named G, and after the swap we played a note named E. Third note, we played a note named A in the original version, and in the transposed version we played a note named F sharp.

Stop here and notice what’s happened. The A natural became an F sharp. When we did the transposition “mechanically”, we didn’t have to think about this because the whistle did it for us. When we do the transposition manually, we still don’t have to think about because the key signature does it for us! Since we’re transposing the tune into the key of two sharps, when we write an F without any accidentals, it’s an F sharp. Things can get a little complicated with transposition when accidentals enter the picture, but we’ll save that consideration for another day. For now, when there are no accidentals in the picture, just trust the key signature to do that part of the work for you.

If you continue up the scale, you can see that we can replicate the mechanical transposition (swapping whistles) by (a) changing the key signature from one flat to two sharps and (b) moving each note down by two “names”.

I recommend that you sit down with a pencil and paper and write this out by hand - this stuff makes the most sense when you work it out for yourself.

Re: Transpose

When you say

> I have just been playing the written notes on a D whistle

are you half-holing any of them? If you have not been, then you may not be playing the tune the right way. As others have noted, if there are Fs and/or Bs in the tune, they will be one half-step lower than what your D whistle plays. The D whistle plays an F#, but you need an F natural, and the D whistle plays a Bb natural, but you’d need a Bb.

Now, it could be that these tunes are pentatonic and contain no Fs or Ds. In that case, you can indeed play them on a D whistle exactly as written.

If the tune does have Fs and Bs, the other way to go about things is to transpose the tune to fit on the D whistle. This is easy to do! When you see a D, play an E. When you see an E, play an F; when you see an F, play a G; etc. You’ll be playing in G major rather than F major, but the tune will sound right. It might take a small amount of practice to start but once you get the hang of it it should be a pretty easy conversion.

Re: Transpose

If you see one flat in the sheet music, that is either F or one of the modes of F, which have precise names but are often (usually?) named a bit informally in this music, based solely on the character of the third note of the scale. I’ll switch to a D whistle for this because it’s more familiar, with the parallel examples for the F whistle in [square brackets]: A tune in the key of two sharps tone flat] that’s centered on the note E [G] is in the key of E dorian [G dorian] but will often be spoken of as “an E minor [G minor] tune” because it resolves on an E minor [G minor] chord. A tune in two sharps that resolves to an A [C] is in A mixolydian [C mixolydian] but will often be called an A major [C major] tune because the resolution is to an A major chord.

That being said, if the sheet music shows one flat, the associated major scale is an F major scale and you’d play it on an F whistle, or sometimes on a C whistle, depending on what’s convenient. In this music, we usually think of most tunes as being played on a D whistle, if at all possible, so typically a tune in one sharp (G major or A dorian or E minor) will generally be most playable on a D whistle. It’s harder to say whether a one-flat tune will lay out better on an F whistle or on a C whistle, because we don’t know where the notes lie - one or the other will feel more natural, and the other will require more “folding” and other modifications, and it’ll depend mostly on the range of the tune.

@Respect - yes.

@Michael - I seldom hear someone shout “A dorian” when switching to a new tune, it’s always “A minor”. I don’t think that’s from any sort of disrespect for the guitarist’s theory knowledge, I think rather that the point is “you’re about to hear a tune in A, please don’t play an A major”, and it’s assumed that they’ll be able to work out the rest as they go. And more generally, I think when people are thinking about tunes and keys they’re thinking mostly about the root and the third, the seventh and the sixth are less salient. Which makes sense, since the seventh and the sixth are much more variable in tunes than the third - lots of tunes switch between major and minor seventh or sixth, or omit the sixth entirely, but relatively few tunes include both the major and minor third, so fine distinctions of mode are usually less interesting than whether it’s major or minor.

This doesn’t mean that players or backers in this tradition don’t understand the different modes, it’s just that they talk most about what matters most to them.

I don’t think backers should memorize the chords for specific tunes, they should learn learn to listen for the root key and mode of tunes and learn prototypical backing progressions for those keys/modes, at least as a reasonable starting point.

Even better if they learn to flatpick the tunes to better understand tunes that change root key and mode between parts, or even sections of parts.

Tell a backer to memorize the chords for a few common tunes and they’ll maybe do OK for one session, but will struggle and most likely fail outside of their limited set of tunes.

Teach a backer how to actively listen for the root key and mode, and if they are willing to do the work, they’ll eventually be able to back any tune.

Re: Transpose

Some of the answers here have made things way more complicated. A tune is made up of a series of pitches, each pitch separated by a certain interval. Think of playing two notes on a piano, then visualize the number of keys in between the two you are pressing; that is the size of the interval.

To transpose, you start on a different note but keep all the intervals the same size. Thinking of the piano again, the notes themselves would be different but the number of keys between each note would be the same as in the original tune. Put another way, the overall pattern of stepping between pitches is the same, only now the pitches are different.

If you use the same fingerings on a differently keyed whistle, the tune will be transposed. For example, imagine playing a tune written in D on a D whistle, then use those same fingerings on an F whistle. You are playing the same tune transposed to F, since all the intervals are the same.

Most whistles can play in two keys comfortably without half-holing. For example, you can play a tune written in G on a D whistle. If you use those fingerings on an F whistle, the tune will be transposed to Bb. This is because the interval between D and G is the same as the interval between F and Bb. Remember, it’s the patten of intervals in the tune that makes the key, not the key of the instrument itself.

If you play the exact same pitches on a whistle in a different key (i.e., the notes are the same, but the fingerings are different), then you are not transposing, just playing in the original key on a different instrument (which could be much more difficult or impossible, depending on the key!).

If you only change the sharps and flats but leave everything else the same, you will have changed the pattern of intervals. Therefore, you will be playing a different tune, though it may be similar in places.

Re: Transpose

It’s certainly “musically acceptable” (most things are). It’s just not the same tune!

One way to look at this is to look at the steps between the notes. These are called intervals and they should be identical in both the original and the transposed version of the song.

An example:

The first three notes of Three Blind Mice in D-major are:
F# -> E -> D (four fingers -> five fingers -> six fingers on the D-whistle)

Now look at the intervals between the notes:
F# to E is a whole tone and E to D is also a whole tone.

In order to play those first three notes in another key (any other key) those two intervals must stay the same.

So, if you wanted to play the song in “G” (for example), the notes would be:
B -> A -> G keeping the same interval of two whole tones. (D-whistle: one finger, two fingers, three fingers)

In A it would be:
C# -> B -> A (D-whistle: no fingers!, one finger, two fingers)

But in F the notes would be:
A -> G -> F and that F is not the same as F#! It requires a different finger position…
Three Blind Mice in F-major on a D-whistle would be two fingers (A), three fingers (G) and four fingers and a half for an F!
By that I mean covering the first (top) four holes and covering HALF the fifth one. That’s an F natural and (as you will hear if you switch between uncovering the fifth hole completely and half covering it), it’s a semi-tone (half a tone) lower than your four finger down F#!

Other notes are available as well, but it’s sometimes easier to get hold of a whistle which allows you to play a melody without having to do many half-hole fingerings,

I realise I’m not Jeff!

Transposing is simply playing a melody in a different key (different from “as written: or ”as played by . . . ").

Example: In the key of C, “Mary Had a Little Lamb” begins with the notes

e d c d / e e e

transposed to the key of D, that would be

f# e d e / f# f# f#

Transposing from one key to another is a perfectly ordinary and commonplace thing to do.

I wonder if it is worth a reminder that as you go up the notes on a whistle starting from the bottom the gaps between them are not all the same. The interval between (top)XXXXOO(bottom) and XXXOOO is smaller than that between XXXXXO and XXXXOO and that between XXXOOO and XXOOOO.

That can be hard to hear because the major scale (doh a deer etc) is so familiar. But play a familiar tune ‘one hole up’ from normal and it’s easy to hear that it’s ‘not right’.

Similarly as you go up through the lines and spaces on the sheet music the gaps are not all the same. And the gaps change if the key signature changes - that’s what the key signature is for.

When it comes to transposing the most useful thing I have memorised is where the tones and semitones are. Going up the whistle major scale it’s TTSTTTS. So on a C whistle the notes are C-D-E-F-G-A-B-c-d with the semitones at E-F and c-d. On the D whistle is D-D-F#-A-B-c#-d with the semitones as F#-G and c#-d.

That’s all you need to work out the notes on another whistle. It’s also all you need to transpose between any two major scales. Write out the notes of the starting scale on a piece of paper vertically as if it were the holes on a whistle, with T or S between them. Then alongside write out the notes of the required scale starting with the note is gets its name from, working out where to use sharps or flats to get TTSTTTS as you go along.

Writing the tune in ABC and using Michael Eskin’s tools is easier but less educational to start with (or when the fabric of civilisation fails and the power/Internet goes off). I haven’t found a use for the circle of fifths yet - but I only play tunes.

Because OP reads notation, here’s a simpler way to make certain the tune comes out right on a D whistle: Use a chart of the major scales to transpose the tune as written from F (the major scale with one flat) to D (the major scale with two sharps). Doesn’t matter whether the original tune is major, minor, or in some other mode - the note-for-note transposition will be the same.
