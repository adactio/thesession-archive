Re: ADVICE AND ANECDOTES FOR A BEGINNING WHISLER

Listen to music in the style you want to play--there is so much to whistle playing that you cannot get just from reading notes. Joannie Madden and Mary Bergen albums are particularly good starting points. I found that the Clarke whistle lesson book, and the accompanying tape (I am sure they have CDs now) to be a good resource when starting. There are a lot of good tune books to choose from, and they don’t have to be whistle books. And I bet there are other good resources out there that could help--good book and CD combinations in particular (there is a fellow named L E McCullough that has a nice one available which a friend of mine used to learn).
Don’t be afraid to play slowly, and even if it is hard at first, don’t scrimp on learning ornamentation.
My biggest challenge was “un-fifing” my playing. While I only dabbled in fife playing in my youth, as a New Englander, I grew up hearing it a lot, and so when I first started to play the whistle, I tended to use fife ornamentatio, which is more trilling, and using your tongue a lot, and I approached tunes like the fife marches I was used to. I forced myself to play without using my tongue at all for a while to help minimize that musical “accent.”
But most of all, enjoy. The whistle is a very straightforward instrument, which makes it a lot of fun to play right from the start. But even though it is straightforward, it can still afford you a lifetime of challenges, as you learn to do more and more with the instrument.
And finally, YOU ONLY NEED ONE OR TWO WHISTLES. A simple D Clarke or Generation whistle is sufficient, and can be played brilliantly in the right hands. You don’t need one in every key, you don’t need a tin one and a brass one, and a nickle plated one, and a fancy wood one, and a plastic one, etc. Whistles are relatively inexpensive, bought one at a time, but it is easy to spend way more than you should by buying too many. You will read more about this addictive behavior on Chiff and Fipple.
Best wishes,

Re: ADVICE AND ANECDOTES FOR A BEGINNING WHISLER

YES YES YES!! Listen to Al Brown, monkeyos, YOU ONLY NEED A COUPLE OF WHISTLES, ONE OF WHICH IS A D GENERATION (preferably a brass one) (the other one I suggest is a Bflat, for the lovely mellow tone). Forget low whistles.
Plenty of good advice on this thread, and 1 other thing you might try playing around with is the angle of your playing. Most people play “straight down” but I’ve fallen into the habit of playing in almost a flute position where the breath still goes directly into the fipple (as it must) but a bit is lost, so there is a slight breathy quality to the sound. I like this, but you must find your own preference.
Above all, enjoy playing and play lots!!

Re: ADVICE AND ANECDOTES FOR A BEGINNING WHISLER

Comparative lack of skill may not necessarily translate into bad advice, but a lot of time it does. And frequently it’s the the people who have learned a little bit who are the biggest problem, because they don’t know what they don’t know. (I mean, hey, four years ago I thought I knew it all when it came to whistling; today I’m a lot more humble, because I’m starting to see the outlines of all the stuff I don’t know.)

Even the four truisms you list have important caveats, or people tend to overstate them into iron-clad rules. Tonguing and sheet music become forbidden, no one mentions that listening to bad playing isn’t any better than learning from sheet music, and the common half-holed C-natural gets lost in a maze of cross-fingerings. (Or to name other recent examples that spring to mind: the only difference between hornpipes and reels is hornpipes are swung; all proper sources notate reels in 4/4; there’s no difference between slides and single jigs. Etc.)

Monkeyos

My two cents.

1) Get Grey Larsens “Essential Guide to Irish Flute and Tin Whistle” It’s the only tutorial you need. Shell out the cash, you won’t regret it.

2) As far as “Web Boards” go. Forget about “Chiff and Fipple”. IT’s way WAY to whistle geeky. I love the whistle and I am a geek so I can say that. This site will give you what you need; a) tunes b) craic and c) advice about the music in general.

3) Don’t add to the “whistle as a beginner instrument” by thinking just because you can articulate notes and blaze away at high speeds that your anything more than a hack without proper ornamnetation. It’s better to play more slowly and well.

4) Be preparred to practice your fanny off, in the car (at stop lights) on the can, in the attic or the basement or wherever and whenever for the next year (at least if you’re gifted and practice a lot more likely 2-3 years) before anyone who know’s what they are hearing will say “nice playing.”

It’s the start of a great adventure. Good Luck!
