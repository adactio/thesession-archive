Dealing with unfinished projects.

One thing that has baffled me for a long time, is how we can accumulate things over time that we never complete. Avid readers always talk about the 1st-world problem of always buying new books even though they have shelves of books they never finished. Finishing things in general seems to be an issue of the human condition, but that doesn’t make it any less frustrating. Maybe it even makes it more frustrating.

These last few seasons, I’ve completed 29 art projects. And though that sounds good on paper, there is an issue between the lines. Out of those 29 projects, 21 of them were freshly started projects, while only 8 of them were old projects that had previously been shelved. Those 8 projects were just a handful of an entire library of dozens of other projects that have gone unfinished, for whatever reason.

Whether it’s a feeling of incompetence, lack of skill, fear of failure, etc. there are very valid reasons why some of them just end up in the backlog longer than others. That’s especially true for tunes I want to learn on the fiddle that are above my level. I get it, and I hold grace for myself for that. However, what fascinates me is that the fresh projects are no different from the old projects, in terms of things like due-diligence/competence/skill.

This really stuck out to me with one of my earlier projects from late last summer. It ended up being a 30+hour project, because I had to learn several new auxiliary skills, and I had to develop my core skills because I wasn’t experienced enough to transcribe the music I was trying to compose. Though, through obsession and the pleasure of the creative process, the project got done. And then, I go through my library and see all of this unfinished music, some transcriptions literally over 95% complete. And then I open several of them up, one by one, trying to find a way back in to at least one of them. A solution for the block. Then, an hour or so later, closing my laptop, defeated by what I can only guess is decision paralysis.

Now, I’ve been on this site for what’s getting to be a long time now. I know some of you have done some great and amazing things for the sake of art and music. Things that took commitment, discipline, patience, perseverance, and maybe even a bit of obsession. My question for you is, how do you do it? How do you go back to something and fill it back up with the life of inspiration it once had?

Re: Dealing with unfinished projects.

Keep your daily goals simple and attainable.

If you feel overwhelmed by the amount of work ahead of you, keep a running list of goals, and just pick one or two items and get them done today, and then one or more tomorrow, and then the day after. Rinse and repeat.

For my ABC Transcription Tools, which I started work on in January of 2023, I’ve had over 2700 checked-in changes.

Achieving small daily goals over time can produce great results.

I set very small goals and then check them off as I complete them.

I’ll hear people say “I’m going to learn a new tune every day!”. Often that lasts about three days and then they experience regret. Far better to say “I’m going to learn a new tune every week!” and actually be successful.

Re: Dealing with unfinished projects.

I suppose I feel that not everything needs finishing, and not everything can be finished.

If I love something enough, I will usually keep going back to it. But there are things I enjoyed, that had promise, which I didn’t finish because I recognised they were part of a process and not an end in themselves. I might have learned something by doing them, or I needed to get an idea out my head, or maybe I needed them as a distraction. It’s nice to stumble across them every now and again, but something in my back brain knows I won’t take them any further, and I’m fine with that.

There are also things I’m really bugged about not being able to finish, like tunes that are too challenging for me to do well, or books I wanted to read but then couldn’t get into. But there might come a day when I’m ready, and that thought keeps me learning and trying to improve.

Maybe you’re just not ready to finish some of those transcriptions? They wouldn’t quite come to life, or you couldn’t find the secret beat that makes them fly. But the learning and the joy are the thing, not the crossing off an item on a list. It’s not like cleaning the bathroom; even if you finished transcribing that tune, you’d never finish playing it and changing it.

Re: Dealing with unfinished projects.

It *is* like cleaning the bathroom—you clean it spic and span, and then tomorrow there are stray hairs in the tub drain, toothpaste spatters on the faucet, and a spot on the floor that you missed. Nothing’s ever truly finished.

Sometimes it’s okay to stop pushing forward, or to reframe what you mean by progress. It’s a wellness fad now to walk 10,000 steps each day. If you walk up to a cliff edge at 5,000 steps, turning around and going back the way you came isn’t failure, it *is* the way forward.

I tend to agree with Mr. Eskin that it helps to break a big project into small, manageable steps. That’s my standard approach, and sometimes the project shows you where to draw the line between steps. When I write tunes, I often get the first half easily enough, but if the start to a second part doesn’t volunteer itself, I don’t stress over it. Better to let the first bit marinate overnight (or longer) until a second half grows out of the previous bit.

The power of the “small steps” approach is that it’s sustainable and habit-forming. You can keep plugging away, day after day. As the hours pile up, you’re bound to have successes now and then. It’s the old idea that “success is 1% inspiration and 99% perspiration.”

Sometimes, though, creating a new tune brings its own sense of urgency. I don’t know where that comes from, but the feeling can be intense. In those cases, I’ll ignore whatever else I should be doing (e.g., sleeping) and follow the inspiration as long as it takes. This isn’t about small steps, it’s about being swept up by inspiration and surfing it as long as the wave lasts.

It’s worth pointing out that both of these methods can produce sh!te as often as they produce gems. The older I get, the easier this is to accept. :-)

As for picking up an old project and breathing new life into it—well, that either happens or it doesn’t. If you go back to something and it doesn’t create a spark, maybe it’s a dead end. You can still hang onto it and try again later. Sometimes you need another year (or more) of experience to sort out where an idea is really headed. In short, you can’t will inspiration to hit. But you can be ready and receptive, and prepared.

Don’t forget to celebrate your gems, and also any incremental progress. Most important, enjoy simply doing creative play, no matter the results.

It just occurred to me that most of my creative output *isn’t* something I’ve gone “back to.” I never stopped doing it.

And it didn’t feel like “commitment, discipline, patience, perseverance” (but maybe a touch of obsession) because I was completely enthralled with it. I’d rather be doing that than anything else, so I did. For years on end.

It also occurs to me that I learned and honed this mindset simply by playing tunes. At last week’s session, someone careened into a tune I haven’t played in 30 years, but it was right there under my fingers, and dancing in my bowhand with the same rush of excitement and energy that it gave me when I was a youngster. And the same thing happens when I play tunes for the millionth time, tunes I’ve played for half a century.

You give it your energy and attention, but it doesn’t feel like effort or perseverance.

Clearly there’s magic in doing what you love, and keeping after it for a lifetime.

mandocello8, your posts made me chuckle, but they also reminded me of an idea I read somewhere years ago (that Jerone no doubt already understands). I suspect it will resonate with anyone who chooses to spend thousands of hours learning old trad tunes when they “should” be doing the dishes or “could” be sat in front of the telly:

“I don’t have time to do that!” actually means “I’d rather be doing something else.”

Day in, day out, we are what we do. Whatever we accomplish in life reflects our priorities. If it’s more important to you* to crunch corporate costs and revenues 60 hours a week and to keep a spotless house and a manicured yard, then you likely won’t compose a lifetime of brilliant tunes.

(*meaning the all-of-us “you”)

Also, we can either set and concentrate on our own priorities or spend our lives focused on what other people think we ought to be doing. Strong forces compel us to conform to outside expectations, but the choice is ours alone to make.

Of course, it’s also up to each of us to decide how best to manage our finite gift of time. Some of us do more if we’re organized and methodical, others thrive on creative chaos. Youth is a good time to explore the options and sort out what works best for you.

Re: Dealing with unfinished projects.

There’s an interesting little book I read years ago…”Art and Fear” I believe was the title. I don’t have a copy handy but I always remember the main point of the closing paragraphs which reminds one that you can give something your best shot and risk failing or not make any attempt at all and thereby guarantee failing.

Nothing ventured nothing gained.

Personally, I have had a lifelong wide variety of interests/hobbies/avocations. Music, art, photography, reading, woodworking—including furniture making, turning and musical instrument building, restoration and repair—travel, natural history study…

For all but the last 18 years I also worked a full-time job as a teacher. There are countless finished works I’ve loosed on the world but there also exists—as a result of this very full life of paying work jostling for attention with unpaid personal passions—a home, shop and studio filled with “unfinished” projects. My daughter eyes this collection with some trepidation fearing she may someday be responsible for all of it. I like to tell her I simply can’t die since there’s a lot of work left to do.

From time to time this unfinished business still troubles me but far less so now. When it’s nagging me that little quote from “Art and Fear” reminds me to simply forgive myself. What—if anything—has really been lost or wasted by the fact that I at least took a shot at something? Binge watch re-runs of Bonanza or work four hours on a shop project I may (or may not) “complete”?

I still have all the experience gained from those partially completed projects—none of which would I have without having started them in the first place.

Sometimes they are worth what they teach you simply by unpacking a bit about why they didn’t get finished. From one of them—a single, hand laced snowshoe—I eventually realized I only wanted to know how to do part of the work. Teaching myself to bend wood and then studying how to hand lace the complicated raw hide web were the two mysteries I really wanted to unravel. Once I’d gotten that far I didn’t feel a strong need to go any further. That lonely, partly finished single snow shoe hung around (and sometimes taunted me) for years. Eventually I re-acquainted myself with the friend who had inspired the project in the first place. I told him the story and gave him the partially finished shoe. He was delighted. I have no idea what he did with it but it doesn’t matter. It had served its purpose. It was finished.

What artist said: “A painting is never really finished. It just stops in an interesting place.”?

Re: Dealing with unfinished projects.

This tip may not fix your problem, but it may make you feel better.

I’m a guy with infinitely long lists of things to do - or rather, things that I might do. I try to tick off some of them every day, but I often feel frustrated that I’m not ticking off enough of them. I think your unfinished projects are a variation of that.

One day I was strongly feeling that “not doing enough” emotion about planned projects for our little house and yard - painting this thing, adding that thing, fixing that other thing, or much bigger house and yard projects. The list was so long!

Then I stopped myself and wrote a new list. I did a list of the projects I’d actually completed to improve our house. And it looked pretty impressive to me! It made me feel much more effective.

So maybe consider more time congratulating yourself on what you’ve accomplished. Maybe it will allow you some forgiveness for what you haven’t yet mastered.
