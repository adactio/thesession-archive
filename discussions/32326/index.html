A Sense of Humour in the music

In recent surfing and listening through a number of YouTubes I came across a few where for me the musician(s) in the ear hadn’t a clue about the tunes they were playing, the type of tunes, the ‘dance’ in them. But what immediately came to mind was that they lacked a ‘sense of humour’. There was no smile or chuckle about them. They were drab, dull, lifeless, danceless. But I’m not completely sure what I mean by that? :-/ And yet, this music can have that about it, a sense of humour, a playfulness, a smile, a chuckle, and, for me, that’s part of what makes it ‘dance’… Does anyone else understand what I mean?

Re: A Sense of Humour in the music

Absolutely ! Ask a dancer !

I’m convinced “dance music” is a genre unto itself.

Good dance music is not merely a letter-perfect rendering of the notes. Good dance music includes all sorts of variations: variations in rhythm, variations in melody, variations in backing, etc. Some call it “lift”, or “pulse”. The best dance musicians can do such things at will. For them, the “at will” part means it can be spontaneous, alive, interactive, in short . . . playful !

Re: A Sense of Humour in the music

On a wider sense of this, emotion, involvement, and not to an extreme, as that too can happen, to the point of melodrama and saccharine, the bathetic, or slap stick ~ not that one can’t have fun occasionally taking things that way, to the extremes. But, yes, I guess too much focus on the mechanics, or ‘speed’, or the push to impress, can detract from those more personal involvements in the music, which can be playfulness, cheer, compassion, empathy… Pushing things can disconnect us from them and force us to focus on one or a few aspects of the music while we miss others, missing a fuller involvement.

A term I let slip recently does kind of give some form to one result of missing the music in order to accomplish some narrow focus, like speed ~ the ‘session spoiled’ ~ those that push hard to catch up with session ways, which aren’t necessarily good habits, something that can so often ends up with not just a lack of humour, though a giddy adrenalin rush might be accomplished, but can also result in sloppy playing. However, that humour, or humours, they can be part of a group or session experience too, which is great kick, good fun, being connected in more than just the notes…

Understanding that humour, getting into the music, it can become an expression of emotions, but that requires more involvement than just pushing for speed or to win attention or accolades. For me, I’ve known and appreciated beginners that have managed that connection better than some folks that have been pushing notes for years. I love it when listening causes involuntary responses, like finding my feet tapping, or a smile, a chuckle, goosebumps, a sigh, a tear… I can be amazed, impressed initially, by dry acrobatics and flash tricks, but I quickly tire of it. I’d much rather simple good humour, personally. That’s what takes and holds my interest, that’s what moves me…

It’s completely subjective, but I think some tune types have more humour in ’em than other types: hornpipes and slip jigs seem more merry, and some polkas (except the brooding dark ones… when a polka goes dark, it goes very, very dark). Re: A Sense of Humour in the music

This, I think, is where many classically trained, if unfinished in that training, get lost, focusing on the more mechanical aspects of learning, the dots and the technique. Taking that path is minus that more important sense of what music is all about, listening, hearing, creating something for the ears, an expression of emotion in sound. Missing that, not putting their main focus into listening, and with the heart, it’s no surprise when they lose track of the emotion ~ in the melody and in the playing of it, that sense of humour(s)…

As with comedy, you can read a comic script and find some chuckles, but all comedians or actors of any salt will tell you it’s all in the delivery, the timing, that personal touch, when a comedian or actor breaths life and believability into the comic tale, the joke. Music or a joke, you really do have to be there, fully, to get the real gist of what is being played, said, and to understand it to the point you can deliver it with the same fullness of meaning, laughter or tears, with yourself fully involved and believable, able to touch others and spread that feeling like a sneeze… ;-)

Quoting Kevin, from the inside jacked of this recording:

"Although this album combines modern arrangements and recording techniques, I have tried to retain as much as possible the old traditional moods of Irish music as it used to be played long ago in rural areas by small groups of musicians. Many of these older musicians used to play by themselves and for themselves as an expression and a relaxation, just like the country bluesmen and musicians the world over. At other times it meant relief from more worldly troubles, a therapy. Today, impact and communication are regarded as essential and I feel that people forget that the musician often plays for his own enjoyment. Traditional music in Ireland sometimes suffers from an over dose of competitions in which so many young people in the last 20 years have been forced to play.

Something I’ve always remembered about the Irish musicians that I met when I was a child was that there was always an element of fun, pure fun, in their playing and their music. Sometimes this gave way to a plaintive wistful mood, but the fun was never far away.

Good humour is something I’ve always liked in music and I hope this record leaves you happy."

- Kevin Burke

~ and anyone who has had the pleasure will know that part of that is Kevin’s humour…
