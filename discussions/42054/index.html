Re: To Reel or to Hornpipe

A schottische (tune) is, I think, generally more heavily dotted than a hornpipe.

In some traditions (e.g., Cape Breton) hornpipes are played straight, as are reels.
In the GHB (competition) world reels are dotted and hornpipes are straight.
In some traditions some hornpipes are straight and some are swung and there doesn’t appear to be any logic to it.
Personally, I try to think of a tune and how it is played locally, and am not concerned whether it is classified as a reel or a hornpipe.

Re: To Reel or to Hornpipe

Yes, a 4 feel (hornpipe) vs. a 2 feel (reel) is part of it - and there is an ongoing argument between certain members on this site, which flares up now and again, over whether it is acceptable to notate a reel in common time (4/4) or whether they should always be in cut time (2/2).

In addition to the way the tunes are played, there are often differences in the way the tunes are structured. I am finding it very difficult to put all of these differences into words in a coherent fashion, but a couple of things that (in general) mark hornpipes out from reels are:

i. Hornpipes typically resolve onto their tonic chord at the beginning of the final bar of each part; reels typically resolve halfway through the final bar (or, for reels that are inherently cyclic, only resolve by adding an extra note on the end).

ii. Hornpipes typically end each section with a figure of two crotchets (1/4-notes) and a minim (1/2-note) or a crotchet, two quavers (1/8-notes) and a minim - and the same rhythmic figures often end phrases elsewhere in the tune; reels ‘come to rest’ in the 2nd half of the final bar, the 1st half usually being occupied by a group of 4 quavers and the 2nd half, by 2 quavers and a crotchet or just a crotchet, the remaining beat being made up by an anacrusis (or, for reels that are inherently cyclic, the final bar may comprise two groups of 4 quavers).

iii. Hornpipes always* have repeated sections of 8 bars each (a total of 32 bars for a 2-part hornpipe); reels can have repeated sections of either 4 or 8 bars each (a total of either 16 or 32 bars for a 2-part reel)†
some have one or more non-repeated part of 8 bars.

*There will always be the odd anomalous tune that is commonly known as a hornpipe but does not quite fit the mould; similarly, there are some hornpipe-like tunes that are commonly played as reels.

†A handful of 2-part reels have one part of 4 bars repeated and one of 8 bars repeated, or both bars with 8 bars, but one part repeated and the other not (making 24 bars in total); other variations exist.

Taking up DonaldK’s thread on regional variation: the boundaries between tune categories are not necessarily consistent across different traditions. For example, the Scottish and Shetland repertoires contain a lot of tunes known as reels that would pass more easily for hornpipes in an Irish context, whilst many English reels are more like Irish polkas.

Re: To Reel or to Hornpipe

Ssan, in the early maritime tradition at least hornpipes were not dances but working songs and tunes. They were sung mostly and played sometimes to coordinate group activities onboard. Group activities such as raising anchors, hauling mainsails and such, where considerable degrees of coordination and strength were required to complete an action. Each activity would have had it’s own chant/song/tune according to the size of the ship and the amount of crew. There would have been common tunes but very local ones too. Like the marine marches and such if you want a modern equivalent.

The recreational hornpipe is a different beast and these too cover a multitude of form and feature before the desire to catagorise, list and standardise, led to questions such as the op’s becoming necessary. As a form hornpipes go back a long way before your 3 hundred years, as a standardised thing a lot less so if indeed they ever really have been standardised.

Re: To Reel or to Hornpipe

Yes, if you wanted to write swung hornpipes out as they sounded you would write them in 12/8. Unfortunately, 12/8, with notes in the ratio 2:1, is much harder to read than 4/4 because you lose the beaming. So they are written in 4/4 (or 2/2) either straight, possibly with a metric modulation sign at the beginning, or as dotted quavers and semi-quavers with the conventional understanding that these notes are played in the ratio 2:1 rather than 3:1.

Re: To Reel or to Hornpipe

Yes to the above comments on tempo. In Pete Cooper’s Irish fiddle book he introduces reels by saying something really simply like reels are either played less dotted than hornpipes, or at such a tempo that the dotting is less noticeable. Which is always struck me as, to all intents and purposes, a good practical description of what actually happens.

In Irish music there’s usually a marked difference in tempo between a reel and hornpipe, and that tempo difference generally allows the player to give a hornpipe a kind of jerkiness (or a distinctive mixture of jerky-meets-smooth) and swagger that sets it apart from a reel. Perhaps a little more emphasis on the on-beats (beats 1 and 3) than you would have in a reel (depends on the player of course). On the fiddle you’re probably going to be playing a few more separate bows than if you were bowing a reel. All these are generalisations. Stereotypical hornpipes quite often have a lot of arpeggios and that distinctive two-note (or three note) phrase ending (“with his trou-sers on”).

’Course, I’ve heard Frankie Gavin playing hornpipes at the same pace others would play reels and the differences become a bit more moot, though you can still hear some of that jerkiness and assertiveness.

And then you have reels such as the ‘Dublin Porter’ which is very hornpipey and could definitely be played as one.
