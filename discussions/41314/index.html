Managing recording collections

I have an ever-growing traditional music recording collection on top of what was already an extensive cd collection of everything else. I have digital and physical copies and use both, but CD players are becoming obsolete and there are more and more hard-to-find recordings online. I’m loathe to give in and allow apple to control the music market though straight digital is appealing for everything except the quality of the sound.
I’ve considered going back and trying to find the records for the things that were produced at that time because there are plenty of players floating around and I enjoy the sound but that’s a lot of dedicated room.
Insights anyone? What does future-proofing look like for the home musician?

Re: Managing recording collections

I refuse to use iTunes, partly for the monopoly factor but also because it’s just terribly designed software. Here’s what I do, for what it’s worth:

Years ago I decided to digitize everything. All my vinyl and CD’s were digitized to high bitrate MP3 files (256 kbps), and I buy almost all new releases from Amazon in DRM-free high bitrate MP3 format. Once in a while, I still see a new CD release from a trad artist, I buy that and digitize it. I know there are good arguments for digitizing in lossless format like WAV or FLAC, but at my age I can’t hear the difference with a 256k bps MP3 anyway, and it transfers faster across home wifi from my media server.

So, all the files are stored on a media server hard drive on the home network. That’s backed up on another, separate server (and I should probably do cloud backup too, just haven’t got around to it). To search or listen to files, I use the free, open source Foobar2000 program (Windows only). I can search on a tune name like “Banish Misfortune” and it currently pulls up 8 different recordings in my library by different artists.

Foobar2000 also has a free mobile app version for Android or iPhones. With one Foobar2000 client running on my main home desktop computer as a front end to my home network network media server, I can go downstairs to my music room, and pull up any of those files on my Android phone (it does lack a search on the mobile version, which is annoying, I have to know the artist and album and dig down to a tune).

There are different ways to go about this, that’s just one way. Whatever you do, if you go all-digital, make sure you have a solid backup plan. I’ve donated away or sold all my old vinyl and CD’s so there’s no going back if I lose these files.

Getting back to the OP ‘future proofing’ may depend on how you think of the media on your computer. To most Apple and Android users they are accessed through an ‘app’ and you don’t have to know the details of how they are stored. Many Windows and most Linux users probably think of them as files containing an encoding of the audio and some tags and they will commonly use different software to access them.

The app-based user doesn’t require much technicial knowledge until hardware or software developments require a change. At that point they may well find they are in a hole that needs quite a lot of technical expertise to get out of.

The ‘music in files’ user needs to have an understanding of where they are and of the formats used, but probably won’t have much problem moving on, maybe changing format, as the world changes.

I’m firmly in the latter camp and doing much the same as ‘conical bore’. If something can be had in a lossless format I will squirrel that away for the future but the easy access versions are high bitrate mp3.

Re: Managing recording collections

True, the accompanying literature is the major loss when going all digital. You can get the front-cover artwork (usually) to display on your media player, but not all the great information on the CD inserts of many recordings. I do miss that, and I’ve kept a few CD’s where I wanted that information.

The big compensating advantage of a digital library for me, is random access search. With any new tune I’m interested in learning, the first thing I do is see if there is a version by someone in our home music library. For a majority of new tunes, it’s likely we already have at least one version and in some cases many more, by various artists in the hundreds of albums my S.O. and I have collected over the years. But I’m not very good at remembering who played what in all those thousands of tunes, so in comes the digital random access search.

Take the Tarbolton Lodge reel, one of the more recent tunes I’ve been learning. I just typed “Tarbolton” into the Foobar2000 filter field, and up comes versions by Andrea Beaton, Buddy MacMaster, Johhny Wilmot, Matt Molloy, Michael Coleman, and the Foinn Seisiún example. I can quickly compare examples to see which one I want (I ended up using the Buddy MacMaster version). It’s incredibly useful to see how different artists play the same material, and it can be done very quickly and easily in the digital domain.

You can do this kind of search on YouTube, but now you’re wading through hundreds of versions by players of widely varying skill. And yes, it’s a crutch of sorts -- I could do a “search” like this if all the material was in vinyl or CD’s, but the convenience factor means I get to the music quicker, with far less hassle, on a convenient pocket device.

Re: Managing recording collections

“An artist may only receive a few pennies when someone downloads a $9.99 album from iTunes”

That’s not entirely true… iTunes US pays me $6.37 for a $9.99 download (purchase). It’s $4.77 for iTunes Canada. And the rate is the same for single track downloads ($.63 for a $.99 download).

It’s the streaming that makes the internet music services feel a bit like a vampire. Single streams on Spotify, Pandora, Apple Music, etc. pay fractions of a penny, with the idea being that eventually they will add up. And for what it’s worth, Apple Music’s per stream rate is significantly better than Spotify and Pandora (at least in the US). But for trad music, it’s very difficult to get enough streams to add up to the money equal of selling a physical CD. What you can’t discount, though, is the awareness that is created from having your music available on the streaming services. So if I play at a festival, for instance, more people might know who I am and might come listen (and buy a CD…)
