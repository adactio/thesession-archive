Re: Flook and the Alto flute

With all of the extended arm and Alto flute weights hanging off to my side, another factor at play are very minimal dynamic forces involved and basically there are two, one being finger motions which with correct technique are quite small and the other being the abdominal movement required for breathing. Unlike other instruments which could require large arm movements, etc., playing a flute involves very little in the way of dynamic forces. With all of that offset weight I can lift my opposite foot up just to find my balance point.

Ian Anderson is now one of the wealthiest people in the UK including all of his performance gyrations, but if you watch trained flute players in some sort of formal competition you will see them standing or sitting as though they were statues carved of stone and there are very refined reasons as to why they play that way. And those reasons also make it very easy to hold an Alto while standing on one foot.

Kess, please believe me when I say that I’m not trying to pick a fight and really do have a lot of respect for you. That said…I am a “medical person” with 25 years or so in pediatric intensive care. The “recovery position” is a position that a patient is placed it while lying down on his/her side with knees bent for the purpose of preventing aspiration as well as reducing stress on the heart. It does, albeit minimally, cause some restriction on diaphragmatic movement if the legs are bent too far but that’s not the point here. In the case of shock the patient is placed on his/her back with legs slightly elevated to keep most of the blood flow to the body core and brain. Please note that at no time, and in no way, is there a standing recovery position, much less one that involves standing on one leg.

Sure, many of us, but sadly not me, can learn to stand on one leg for a time. There are lot’s of balance enhancement exercises to that end. Rehab and P.T. units , and yoga studios for that matter, are full of people who can teach us that. Still, standing on one leg is exercise and absolutely not rest. The ankle is an unstable joint, you can’t “lock” it into position. To stay upright even on two feet requires an active connection between the brain, feet legs, knees, hips, and spine. Stability is not passive. A one legged stance can only increase the stress on heart and lungs. Frankly it’s just not possible to relax (by that I mean decrease measurable parameters such as respiratory rate and volume, heart-rate and stroke volume, and neuro-muscular activity) by standing on one leg. It just can’t be done. Even the bi-pedal stance is more work than lying down. I say again, test after test of measurable parameters (including those that directly measure balance..P.T.’s use them all the time…O2 consumption, CO2 production….direct tests of metabolism) demonstrate. the point. One of my nearly daily duties was to measure metabolic rates on mechanically ventilated patients. Even the stress of a parent touching and speaking to a comatose child created a measurable stress and skewed the results. So I’ll say it again standing on one leg does not in any way “help” with breathing. I suppose that because breathing rate and volume must increase to support the additional work involved with a one-legged stance you could use that extra volume to play a longer phrase and for that reason say that it helps. The extra air comes from bigger breaths…not from a more relaxed position. You don’t need to stand on one leg for that.

Re: Flook and the Alto flute

Spot on. Good posture, sitting or standing, and diaphragmatic breathing with a relaxed upper body is the right way to do it. Lung capacity and function can be explained in engineering terms with a nod to gas laws. I stand in awe of the many people who don’t get how the laws of physics apply. We could talk about that for hours. When physiology and anatomy are added in you find lung capacity and function can easily be improved. As a talented player you may not need the additional capacity. Often it’s more about relaxing and finding the right place to breath. But that doesn’t negate the fact that capacity can and is improved in some people every single day. I made a good living doing just that and it took a working knowledge of both approaches.

I did use the term “significant” rather cavalierly. The level of improvement depends on where you’re starting from and how you define the term…10%?….50%? Could be either one,or less, or more. In some cases there is a potential for harm and capacity has to take a back seat to function, there is a difference. Realistically in otherwise healthy people the improvement is enough to be noticeable but not necessarily enough to create a “make or break” situation and may or may not be worth it at least as it applies to flute playing. I find the exercises helpful.

Alright, this thread is getting old but let me add a comment. I cannot say that this applies only to flute, but I can say that learning to play a flute involves a player’s ENTIRE body, including an acute awareness of all of its skeletal structure and of every muscle in it. Hello, o’muirgheasain.

I disagree with that statement. Well, let’s put it this way - you could *make* it involve your entire body, but that would surely be pushing yourself to the limits. You only *need* to use your upper body. Two hands and the fingers of both (which of course are used to the same end purpose - playing single notes on a monophonic instrument). And of course your mouth, and all the muscles involved in blowing from it.

As for the lower body, foot-tapping, single/dual stamping, knee-bouncing, coordinated bass-pedalling and erect Jethro-ing is entirely optional, and optionally is the done thing, depending on the individual.

Balance is essential, seated or standing, whether playing an instrument or simply staring into space.

If you had said that a drummer used every muscle in his body, then that would be a fairer claim. Four ‘note’ polyphonic - making sound with two hands and two feet (plus of course all the muscles used there), where in this case the end purpose of each is entirely different. Foot tapping is not an option - there are no free feet to use for this.

So that takes care of the lower and upper body as far as muscles go.

Balance - oh yes - just look at the reaches that the hands need to make to hit all the bits.

Oh, head-nodding and pigeonating are optional in this case, and by all accounts very-well used. The last of the muscles.

I get the feeling you are smiling as you post your posts - I am too :)

Re: Flook and the Alto flute

Hi Jim,

Nope! Playing a flute really does involve head-to-toe participation. That might not be obvious but it is very much true. The flute requires an astonishing degree of physical coordination. I played trumpet for a few years and then guitar for several years but the requirements for playing a flute go far, far, far beyond any of those things. None of the skills needed to play a flute are particularly difficult but they are exhaustively extensive in their depth. And then maintaining those skills is not at all like learning how to ride a bicycle, that once a person learns how to ride one then they will always know how to ride one. Nope! A famous flute player once said that if he misses a day of practice then he would notice, if he misses two days of practice then his fellow musicians would notice and if he misses three days of practice then his audience would notice. James Galway once wrote that if a player could choose to be a professional flute player then they had better plan on having a flute in their hands every day!

I started this thread in response to an earlier thread having to do with why Sarah Allen perched her foot on her knee. She is NOT being a Jethro, but she does remain balanced. And I know how and why.
