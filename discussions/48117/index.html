Differences between sessions in Ireland and the USA

I am curious, for those of you who have played at sessions in both Ireland and the USA, have you noticed any reoccurring differences or trends in one country and not the other?

I’m thinking of things like session speed, occurrences of different instruments, group size, playing style, etc…

Thank you!

Re: Differences between sessions in Ireland and the USA

By far the biggest difference IMO is that in Ireland there are a lot more people who grew up around trad, while in the US there are more people who came to it as an adult. It’s not all one or the other obviously, but overall that makes a big difference in a lot of ways.

For one, I’ve found ability levels to be a lot more varied in the US. There are high-level and low-level/learner sessions in both places, but you’re far more likely to have at least one or two advanced players in an Irish session. In places like Boston and NYC with a lot of Irish heritage you can find a lot of advanced players, but you’ll also find sessions in far-flung areas where everyone is basically a beginner or intermediate player.

You also get a lot more crossover musicians in the US, players coming from classical or bluegrass or other genres. This can lead to some stylistic quirks as they adjust, and also means that sessions can be run a little differently. There’s a strong “folkie” vibe to many sessions, often called “jams,” which make them much looser and inclusive, for better or worse. It’s great for getting people into the music, but also means you get a lot of people who show up but have very little interest in actually learning the tunes. You get a lot more guitars, more bodhrans, more noodling, and more “nontraditional” or offbeat instruments. Session players in Ireland seem to have fewer qualms setting boundaries around who can join, or (usually tactfully) telling people to cop on if they’re screwing around.

It also affects repertoire in interesting ways. You’re more likely to come across non-Irish tunes like Quebecois, contra dance, Scottish, bluegrass, old-time, etc. at a US session, because again, people are coming from those musics or learning them alongside trad. In Ireland I’ve found that “standard” sets like the Tarbolton set, Connemara Stockings set, etc. are well-known, I think because the céilí bands and Comhaltas groups kids come up playing in end up playing those a lot. However, overall a lot of American sessions I’ve been to have been very tied to specific sets, especially the Foinn Seisiún book sets, and will mostly or always only play those sorts of pre-arranged sets.

And finally, there’s a lot more recognition from punters in Ireland, where just about everyone knows what trad is. That doesn’t mean that they like it more; a lot of Irish people have a visceral dislike of trad. But they at least know what it is. A lot fewer “what instrument is that?” and similar queries.

All of those are generalizations, but they’re what I’ve found playing a whole lot of sessions in both countries.

Re: Differences between sessions in Ireland and the USA

As to the dislike of trad, it’s for the same reason most folks in the U.S. have little interest in it. They grew up on pop tunes on the radio, and “diddly eye” music there is like folk/country here, a small niche which they have no interest in. Due to the prominence of “Irish session nightly” in virtually every more urban pub nowadays to appeal to tourists, there has been somewhat of a backlash among the general population.
As to the difference in sessions, there most of the punters are tourists that purposely want to hear Irish music, but that can be anything from Danny Boy on to actual trad. The true sessions are just music, not specifically trad, but it would predominate. They’ll ask singers to sing unaccompanied, ask tourists to play their party piece, and if you’re from the states they might want you to play some American fiddle tune or other just for fun. Much more conversational and less rigidly Irish than U.S. sessions, like I said it’s just music to them. And all the “bod ran” players will be mainland European tourists that are wanna be trad players, mostly for the pints.

Re: Differences between sessions in Ireland and the USA

Depending on where you are at in the U.S. the Trad (including the sessions) is of an equal caliber to that of Ireland. Go to Dunne’s Pub in White Plains, the 11th Street Bar in Manhattan, Chief O’Neill’s in N. Chicago, or The Druid in Cambridge, Mass. Not to mention many other great pubs.

I’ve not yet seen a session that isn’t using standard tuning in the U.S. That seems somewhat more common in Ireland, tuning up/down. In my experience, also, sessions in Ireland can tend to have a better variety of instruments. In my experience, the U.S. is fiddle heavy. YMMV.

As others have pointed out, there are many more adult learners in U.S., it seems, but this varies from session to session. I also find that sessions in the US can generally be bigger. I’ve seen 15+ musicians playing at once, in some cases. You’ll see this in Ireland, but usually at festivals, not at a pub session. Also, in Ireland more young people under 25 play trad, which correlates with more adult learns I mentioned above.

In my experience, at U.S. sessions the repertoire is highly influenced by the Sligo-New York style and tunes.

Re: Differences between sessions in Ireland and the USA

> Visceral dislike of trad? Why’s tat?

Ireland has a particular image worldwide, a rural land of simple folk frolicking in the fields, drinking stout and whiskey, eating potatoes, merrily scraping a fiddle, etc. Think Darby O’Gill, or more recently the Banshees of Insherin is a great example of this, albeit a bit darker.

This is obviously quite far from the truth, as Ireland is a modern country with a highly educated workforce, lots of tech and business, and all the trappings of modernity. For some people, trad is a bit too reminiscent of that “backwards” vision of Ireland. Most people, young and old, listen to much the same pop, rock, hip-hip, etc. that someone in the UK or US would listen to, and for some of those people trad represents everything they hate about that image of Ireland. I’ve met more than a few people (including some of my in laws) who have very little time for trad. It’s understandable, in the same way someone from Appalachia might bristle at the idea that they listen to banjo music all the time, or a Bavarian disliking being associated with oom-pahs and lederhosen.

I think this has to do with what I mentioned above, that there are a lot of other genres like Quebecois, bluegrass, contra, old-time, Cajun, Scandinavian, etc. around in the US, and a fiddler might dabble in a bunch. If you want to have an “Irish session” rather than a “folk jam,” you’ve gotta be a bit more dogmatic, or people will start to stray. In Ireland I’ve heard plenty of non-Irish tunes played at sessions, but ultimately the sessions are more naturally “Irish.”

Along those lines, I have very rarely heard the term “seisiún” used in Ireland itself, but it is somewhat popular in the States for sessions, presumably to A) denote the resolutely Irish nature of the session and B) add an “authentic Irish” flair to the proceedings. I get using it if you’re speaking “as Gaeilge,” but it seems funny to use it in English.

Re: Differences between sessions in Ireland and the USA

My experience in Ireland, which was a couple of trips in the early 2000s (pre-Internet), was that trad there for the locals was about as popular as old-time here among the general population.

In Dublin, there was a lot more American country music to be found than trad. Unfortunately, our schedule didn’t line up with going to the Cobblestone for music.

In Dublin, not finding any sessions in town, we ended up going to the CCE headquarters in Monkstown for the Friday and Saturday night sessions there and had a good time. It was very much like any good session here in the states.

Heading west, until we got to Ennis and Dingle, it was equally challenging to find a session. I ended up sitting in with some locals in a band situation in Kilkenny playing trad for a bus load of German tourists.

Found great sessions in Dingle at two different venues. As before in Dublin, very much like a good session here in the states.

Visited Lisdoonvarna, found a very small pub session of three locals that didn’t seem interested in anyone joining them so we just listened all night.

My experience of sessions at Willy Clancy week on another trip really I don’t think is representative of the general scene in Ireland, played with as many German musicians as native Irish players.

I’m told by those who regularly travel to Ireland that the sessions I host or visit here in California, Colorado, and Texas, are as good or sometimes better than many they experience there. I would expect that the scene around Ennis and Galway would make us look like posers in comparison, so the sense I get from my friends that go regularly these days is that it’s all over the board there just like it is here, and if you want to find great sessions, head to the west of the country.

Generally the sessions I play in here, which are generally higher-level players, don’t have the folkie/Old-time/Bluegrass crossover problem. That seems to be something more prevalent in the slow-session/beginners sessions I’m aware of, where often there is no desire to move into the higher-level pub session scene. They are happy reading out of their books and playing Ashokan Farewell, and that’s just fine.

I’ve played at sessions in Japan that were every bit as good as any session I’ve played in the US or Ireland.

Unfortunately, Southern California isn’t a hotbed of older ex-pat Irish players to get inspiration from in sessions, so we just have to do the best we can from recordings, YouTube, and the occasional concert or workshop.

I’m either up far too late or ridiculously early, but having foregone sleep (or sleep bypassed me), I can’t say which.

“Whn I hear ‘inclusive session’ I expect bad everything”

Yes, been there, experienced that. You get djembe arrhythmias and up-chuck guitar and poser Wanton Merciless jazz riffs smothering the finest jigs and reels.

But it’s not always the case. (No aspersions on Chris Stolz. One person can do only so much.)

On occasion I’ve sat in a pub sailing through Irish trad tunes as pure drop as they come, and then the door eases open and a few people lugging cases cross the threshold. The next thing you know, the most amazing bossa nova or Amharic or Nanguan musicians sit down and the rest of the night sways between their music and ours, finding a common pulse, shared phrases, a universal musicality. Faces beam and feet tap as one. It’s like a worm hole opens between Irish trad and their trad and no translation is needed.

This is relevant to this thread only because some sessions—in Ireland, in the USA, in the Netherlands, in France, etc.—have a core of *that* musicality, that skill and conceptual or emotional insight, and so there’s far more **in common** among them that there is “different.” I recall a session decades ago in Galway City that ranged across the music of four continents yet not a player ever dropped out. It wasn’t that they (we) knew all the tunes, but that they (we) were open-hearted and able to adapt to wherever the music went that night.

I suppose that’s not really just “inclusive,” more an instance of talent, ability, and openness supercedeing genre or style or ethnicity.

So it’s not inclusiveness that makes things go bad. It’s a lack of chops and/or spontaneity among all those being included. Too often, “inclusive” means playing to the lowest denominator.

Ireland and North America (most of my NA experience has been in Canada) both have their share of talented musicians and a surfeit of brilliant sessions. With a lot of shared qualities. In either space, over the course of a night the pace, instrumentation, tune choice, amount of swing, etc., will vary. Such gatherings are not always or often advertised or posted online. The best way to find such nirvana is to know someone. Or at least someone who knows someone.

Re: Differences between sessions in Ireland and the USA

About strange music popping up at sessions:
In November/December 2010 I was staying in North Mayo. Sessions were thin on the ground, “Ah well now, it might be better if ye came back in the summer. Then there’d be sessions all right,” and so on. Of course, persistence furthers. An accordionist I met mentioned a session coming up the following Sunday in a village about fifty miles away. It was to be a session of mainly singers, in honour of a young person who had died. I was afraid it might not be appropriate for me to be there…not being a member or friend of the family. “Not at all, everyone will be welcome. You should go”.

It was early December in the worst winter Ireland had seen in a long time. Snow tyres being unknown in Ireland, the drive was terrifying, slippery and verging on suicidal. I arrived at the pub towards 5 p.m.. Dinner came and went and I waited, it seemed for hours, for the musicians to show up. Singers and players from a wide catchment area had heard about this session and had travelled from all over Ireland, Scotland and England to this small village on the windswept, ice covered Atlantic coast of Mayo.

By 9 o’clock there were enough musicians for things to get under way. The first hour or two were tune, song, story, tune etc. There were ballads, a few Come All Ye’s, (mercifully no Eyes Shining Like Diamonds or Tell My Ma’s) a rich stream of sinuous Sean Nós and laments, punctuated by slow airs and even the occasional muted dance tune. This was getting interesting.

Perched on one of those funny little “milking stools” that publicans might find comfortable (but I don’t), was a young girl with a strong London accent. There was a courteous back and forth of songs amongst some truly beautiful singers. As song followed tune followed song, the sadness of the occasion could be increasingly felt. There was little loud chat, the pauses between songs grew longer and quieter. From out of the contemplative dying notes of Limerick’s Lamentation came a strong and beautiful single voice as the girl from London began a heart-wrenching performance of Lewis Allan’s “Strange Fruit”. When the song ended, a deep, warm silence blanketed the room. Not a word was spoken as we put away our instruments, put on our coats and wandered out into the wind and ice.

I have stood, thirteen years later, on the road outside that pub, courtesy of Google Earth’s funny little yellow street view man. I have stared at the sign over the door and heard “Strange Fruit” and the voice of the girl from London. I remember now…her name was Victoria.

However, my memory of the session I wrote about has remained as pungent and powerful as any I have of my sixty three years as a performing musician. For some reason your retort, inversely proportional as it was to the intensity of the experience I was trying to recount, has stuck in my mind. Either I failed to write compellingly enough to convey the sincerity of the singers and players at this memorial for a young man who had died tragically, or you were impervious to such a story and could only be counted on to fire off a superficial quip. While the former explanation may be true, I’m tempted to believe the latter is also. You managed to show us all that this site is open to all levels of human sensitivity.
