Spirit of Sessions

Now I’m not sure if there’s already been a discussion on this, but I’ll bring it up anyway. I’ve been watching a few sessions lately downtown (somewhere I am not familiar with) as I don’t think I am ready to sit in and I’ll tell you why. Although I know most of the tunes and could probably keep up, there seems to be quite an unwelcoming aura surrounding newcomers. I am not sure if this is because they are already a well-established group that have known each other for years or if it’s because there are just a few cranky folk. I was raised in a tradition where there are kitchen parties, and any sessions or ceilis I’ve EVER been to emphasize welcoming everyone who can get their hands on an instrument.

The group I happened upon were sequestered in their own section of the pub with all their chairs lined up and their backs turned to anyone around them, even had a “session leader” (which I can see would be useful), but it really just looked like a little club had walked in and didn’t really want to associate with anyone. My friend is a bodhran player, but he looks quite young. He actually is very good, so he excitedly pulled up a chair. Someone told him to shut up, he was playing too loud.

As a young musician who is SO passionate about this world, it’s pretty disheartening to feel so out of place. I know sessions to be friendly, lively, and welcoming. More than anything, they help you learn and grow as a musician. Don’t get me wrong, I wasn’t expecting a rowdy sing-a-long, but am I missing something, or is this just the way session etiquette works?

Where are you located? Where is “downtown”?

Every session is different. Etiquette isn’t always prescriptive, either. Your friend the bodhran player may indeed have been too loud for that session. It’s hard to say. Some people sometimes may have too thin a skin and take constructive criticism as harsher than intended (ie “hey, you’re a little loud, and we have another bodhran player, so please take turns and when you do play, play a little quieter” = “shut up, you’re too loud”)… It’s impossible to diagnose remotely!

But not all sessions are (or should be) bending over backwards to welcome newcomers, beginners, strangers, or anyone at all, really. The key is to find one you like, or try something like gam said. Just because your initial impression is that they were less friendly than you thought they should be, doesn’t mean they are always going to be unfriendly… In fact the way you describe it, they could easily have just been in a circle - since it’s a session and not a band performance, this is perfectly normal.

I’ve found some session atmospheres less welcoming than others, here in London. Often it’s something you can’t quite put your finger on, like people not acknowledging your presence after you’ve been sat there half an hour and even joined in with a handful of their tunes, or some factor derived of a humour even more rarified. Sessions associated with local comhaltas’s seem to be the worst for this ---this is entirely MY experience, I hasten to add. And yet other sessions are very welcoming, even *before* they’ve heard me play :-)

Not sure if I go along with the electronics/Justin Bieber analogy, but certainly there are ways of going about gaining acceptance in a session full of strangers which most certainly do NOT involve plonking yourself down in amongst a group of strangers and proceeding to batter away on a goatskin.

I’ve found it best to hang about for a few sets of tunes, identify stronger or lead players then ask them if they don’t mind if you join in for a couple of tunes? And smile nicely, without being obsequious. And compliment them on the grand job their doing on the tunes. Also make sure you have a nice set up your sleeve when it’s your turn to lead a set, as polite session etiquette will eventually dictate that you will be asked to lead a few tunes of your own.

Works for me.

Re: Spirit of Sessions

It can take take quite a while to become “accepted” at a session that you’ve never been to before - no matter how good a player you are. And it’s more difficult if you are not a melody player (e.g. bodhran, guitar etc.).

Speaking personally, I think that visiting musicians can benefit a session - providing that they are competent. If you just get the same bunch of musicians week after week, you mostly get the same tunesets cycling around, and the session can become stale.

True, it’s the job of a session leader to tell someone to shut up if they are incompetent, out of tune, out of time, too loud or whatever …

… But it should also the job of a session leader to identify any newcomers and welcome them to the session.

If the session has a contact name and telephone number, it’s a good idea to ring in advance before your visit. You can introduce yourself, you can ask what idiom is played (Irish, Scottish or whatever), you can ask whether newcomers are welcome etc. You can also check that the session will be actually happening on the date that you plan to visit.

Re: Spirit of Sessions

Adding to Uill Wind’s post above about some sessions being paid gigs, there are also sessions where the core players aren’t being paid to be there, but they play as a performing band outside the session. Inevitably in that situation, the session and repertoire is serving a dual purpose as a band rehearsal.

There’s a session like that in my area. The group does welcome the occasional guest who can play at their level, so it’s not a completely closed session. But it’s an especially tough circle to “break into” as an outsider. If you’re not a member of the band, you’ll never be part of the core circle of players, no matter how good you are.

The opposite extreme is a session that’s a little too welcoming to anyone who wants to sit in, to the point where it devolves into a Grateful Dead guitar and djembe song circle. I’ve seen that happen in my local area too, at what was once billed as an “Irish session.”

There’s a happy medium between these two approaches, if you can find it (or create it yourself).

Re: Spirit of Sessions

FOTR writes: “The sessions I’ve visited with interest are ones that had open invitations, not ”performance“ sessions.”

The use of the word “performance” has become a Red Herring on this website; there have been megathreads debating this point on a philosophical basis. Any time you play music outside of practicing at home or rehearsing with a group--it’s a performance, especially in public. What you call a “performance session” is any session really. A better word to distinguish what you witnessed might be “formal” or “arranged,” I think it would be more to the point. But every session I’ve been to has musicians backs involved because they usually happen in a circle, and circles are funny that way… it doesn’t necessarily mean anything and there’s a lot that can easily be misinterpreted. Musicians having sessions are usually functioning on multiple levels; they might be providing a service for compensation from a publican for the entertainment or ambience factor, but the musicians usually come out for their mutual enjoyment of playing tunes together. The latter is key; and perhaps the main reason in most cases. The problem is that seasoned sessioners might have had many a night spoiled by ambitious and well-meaning visitors who want to join in with the fun--so the sessioners become jaded sometimes depending on the location and history of a particular session. Your friend who sat down with the bodhran might have been preceded by a dozen or so bad experiences where a night of tunage was wrecked… so it might be a daunting hurdle to overcome. An important thing to remember is that a session usually has a lot of history either as an event or the experience of the sessioners… so you have to approach it very carefully so as not to trigger a negative response. As I said, their main concern with newcomers is that they can potentially spoil a night of fun. On the other hand, if you can find a way to assure them you are not one to interfere or disrupt--they will usually become very welcoming and helpful.
