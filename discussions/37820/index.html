Re: Why are basses rare in Irish music / sessions?

It’s basically a solo tradition, and when a group gets together it’s several people all playing the melody more or less in unison.

Any accompaniment, say a guitar, or drum, or bass, is inherently foreign to the tradition.

Same is true in the mountains of West Virginia, where a fiddle and a banjo, both playing the melody just as they would if playing solo, constitute a band. Yes it’s typical to add a guitar, but the guitar is an interloper and not fundamental to the tradition.

No doubt a bass, if used tastefully, can add much. The best example that comes to mind is the band Vent du Nord.

Re: Why are basses rare in Irish music / sessions?

From a pragmatic point of view the bass is really hard to tote around. From a psychological perspective there are just some people who want to be the rock-star. And, it can get pretty dull to play alone. All of these points have been made by others.

Still, I can’t help but wonder what some hairy guys thought when they sat around the fire and rolled their eyes over that new, annoying, thing you blew into and made those squeaky sounds, and that moron that kept plunking his bowstring. They all agreed it wasn’t traditional. Traditional after all was something you banged on and something you made from a sheep horn. Maybe a few other hairy guys were able to think just a little outside themselves and evolve. Look where that got us…fiddles, flutes, whistles, pipes, boxes, banjos, concertinas, guitars, zooks, among others, and the bangy-thing, and sheephorn-thing (bodhrans and drones) are still with us. There will always be some who push a tradition along and others who resist that change no matter how slight. Traditions, like hairy guys, evolve. If they don’t they die.

Re: Why are basses rare in Irish music / sessions?

Just attended a fiddle camp on the Isle of Skye that contrasted Scottish tradition with the geographically close Donegal style. The Donegal instructor, Ciarán O’ Maonaigh, told us that there is no matching cello tradition in Donegal. He went on to say that in his experience if there are two fiddles, then one always plays down an octave to better fill the space and sound richer. He called it “reversin”, and taught us to do it, following the melody player, but a lot “looser” with more drones and longer notes, so as not to detract from the crispness and virtuosity of the first fiddle and not competing with it.

I recorded jigs where the class is playing the melody and he reverses against us, and it sounds grand (I was sitting in the front row and can hear him clearly). I left camp with a goal of improving my down-octave playing ’cause it really adds to the sound when done properly.

Ciarán was talking about local dances and sessions where he has played, but I sure haven’t heard anything like this on any of my recordings of Donegal fiddlers. Maybe it doesn’t occur on recordings because recordings are more presentations and reversin is more informal.

So, in at least one Irish tradition, a base line fills in the space and mirrors somewhat the melody line.

Does this maybe happen in other parts of Ireland too?

Re: Why are basses rare in Irish music / sessions?

I very much enjoyed TomB-R’s kite analogy. From my perspective, the music has a lightness to it, with somewhat intricate melodies flying around seemingly effortlessly (assuming that the session is good enough, and the players feel the groove). A bass often just muddies the water, and you lose a lot of the lift and bounce of the music amongst the mud. It’s not just the low octaves that do that either - I have similar problems with anything that has a lot of sustain, like harp, hammered dulcimer, or even guitar sometimes. All those instruments can be used well in the music, but a session isn’t necessarily the best place for it.

If you listen to what Trevor does in Lunasa, for instance, his bass lines are designed very specifically for supporting and adding to the groove that the lads are cranking out. And they have the luxury of being able to control the mix very specifically. You don’t get that luxury in a session environment, and I’ve played several sessions where I couldn’t hear the other melody players very well because there was a bass player right behind me. In one session I sat in many years ago, there was a bass player who was quite a talent, and he was actually playing a number of the tunes at full speed on the bass. While this was an impressive feat, the music lost absolutely all of the crispness, lift, and drive, because it was being smeared out by the bass, and that was basically all you could hear.

Re: Why are basses rare in Irish music / sessions?

I suspect the reason why basses are rare in Irish music sessions is because the way they would have to adjust their playing just isn’t very appealing, to the vast majority of bass players. Too foreign and not much fun.

In how many other styles of music would a player be told “Stop, don’t do what you’d normally do when accompanying acoustic folk music”? Guitar players have to deal with this when transitioning to ITM, but the path is made easier with tricks like Drop-D/DADGAD tuning, and they have more role models to emulate.

A bass player coming in from other genres will have to discard more baggage and study up on the styles of the very few bass players who have made an impact in bands. It’s not exactly a direct path from what most bass players know, to what Trevor Hutchinson does in Lúnasa, or backing Liz Carroll. Why would they want to go to all that trouble, when there are so many other genres where bass players are in demand, and it’s easier to fit in?

All of which is aside from what happens when a clueless amateur bass player tries to join a session playing what they know -- the ’ol I-V bounce, or walking bass lines -- and trainwrecks the session (which I’ve seen happen). For the better players, I think the adjustment needed to do it well, just isn’t that appealing. Which is probably a good thing for the music, in my view. That kite analogy vs. scaffolding (above) is brilliant in describing the reasons why.
