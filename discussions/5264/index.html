Re: Ireland advice

The sessions I encountered in Ireland were all “open.” But by that I think it means open to anyone who knows ITM and wants to play along. If you hardly know any of the tunes being played, or it’s too fast for you, etc. -- it’s probably not the session for you. This doesn’t mean it’s “closed” or that the hosts are snobs -- it just means that’s the way they play, the tunes they like, and there’s no reason to change any of that just because you show up with your instrument.

Re: Ireland advice

My musical pals and me host an “open session” in our local pub a few times a month. People will show up and play with us, and they are very welcome as long as they don’t expect us to cater to them and change what we’re doing. By that I mean we’re not necessarily playing only common tunes that everyone will know, and we’re playing at a tempo we enjoy and have fun with. Some folks will show up from time to time who end up frustrated because they’ll say they’ve never heard our tunes before. They seem to want us to change what we’re doing to cater to their expectations. Then they go away and tell their friends that our session is just a gig, or it’s a “closed session.” This is far from true because we welcome anyone that comes and plays with us.

We know the difference between an “open session” and a “closed session” because we sometimes do a “closed session” in a different pub. We don’t advertise it as a session, but that’s what it is essentially. We sometimes invite certain people to come and sit in with us, but we don’t encourage everyone to come like we do with the “open session.” I found these same circumstances working in Ireland as well. All you need to do when encountering sessions anywhere is to figure out if they’re “open” or “closed” and if it’s “open,” listen to see if you know the tunes they’re playing and whether or not you’re comfortable with the tempo. If you follow those guidelines you won’t have any problems.

trbenton - do you have a work visa yet? Do you have dual citizenship? It’s very difficult for non-EU citizens to obtain legal work in Ireland. Not trying to burst your bubble, but a company has to apply for a work permit for you after proving they first tried to hire Irish citizens and EU citizens. Americans and most foreigners can’t apply for a work permit themselves without dual citizenship. So it’s almost impossible to apply for an Irish job unless you have a foot in the door with someone high up in an Irish company. Plus most professions in Ireland demand special certification such as nursing, teaching and accounting. I’m an accountant with 15 years of experience but would need to go through re-training & certification (and that’s after I became a citizen).

Re: Ireland advice

Hey trbenton:

I am US citizen who moved to Ireland in 98, ended up getting a job/work permit. I ended up getting laid off but am most likely going back (YAY!!!!) in the next few months on a different work permit for another company….as someone who has been in your shoes, I can tell you from hard personal experience that things are a bit different there in the last couple of years.

If you don’t have a job lined up, and are just looking to earn some money while there to pay for your trip, it’s not quite as easy as it was even a few short years ago. This is due in part to the great influx of immigrants that have moved to Ireland in the last 5 years or so, particularly Dublin. It IS possible(not easy of late) to get under the table work, restaurant/bar etc…but you may find that you have a bit of competition.

If you haven’t got a job, you will be going in on a tourist visa, and if you plan to stay more than 3 months you will have to register with the Garda station, and be prepared to show bank statements that indicate you have funds to support you for the duration of your visit. You can get more info by looking up the Department of Enterprise, Trade and Employment. They have a website, just google ‘work permit Ireland’ and that should get you there.

This isn’t to put you off, go go go and have a fabulous time. But bring a lot of money with you, and if you haven’t got citizenship or a work visa already in place, and plan on looking for work while in Ireland, then I wouldn’t recommend you share this information with the immigration office. Just say you’re there on a long holiday.

Also, I couldn’t comment on the immigration offices outside of Dublin, but the Dublin one is a bit more like Ellis Island these days, and if you go there you have to line up outside at 5am and may not get seen that day. This is quite an experience, but very eye-opening from an American point of view, and very humbling.

Again, this is just a reality check, and not meant to be off-putting. Though I experienced many hard times and lean years in Dublin, I wouldn’t trade the coldest poorest second of it for all the tea in China. Moving over was the best decision I ever made, and I cannot wait until I am able to go back.

Best of luck and have a great trip!
