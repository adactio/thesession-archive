Re: Paragon Plectrum banjo

Lower pitched instruments have a sonically wider octave than higher pitched instruments, which is why Equal Temperament tuning had to be developed. Most fretted instruments go up one octave on the 12th fret, as 1 octave = 12 semi-tones, and the spacing of each of the frets is adjusted to the particular pitch of the instrument. But as the spacing of the frets is dependent on the particular pitch of the instrument, A=435 being pitched lower than A=440, then the spacing of the frets is slightly wider on a 435 instrument than it is on a 440 instrument.

Just Intonation tuning worked beautifully back in the times when the Roman Catholic Church insisted that the human voice was closest to the voice of God, and largely shunned the use of musical instruments with the exception of the organ for use as a background drone. But once musicians began developing a wide range of instruments the Equal Temperament tuning compromise had to be developed.

Re: Paragon Plectrum banjo

I’m sorry but that just isn’t right, and it certainly isn’t supported by the laws of physics. The pitch produced by a string is dependent on three factors: the vibrating length, the mass per unit length and the tension. A change in length relates to a change in pitch ONLY if the other two factors remain the same. I play fiddle, and I also have an octave fiddle - fitted with heavier strings and tuned an octave below a normal fiddle. Both play perfectly in tune.

When you talk about the octave position I suspect you are alluding to the fact that the fretted octave position isn’t exactly at the mid point of the string (although you’ve got it the wrong way round - on the bass the octave is proportionally nearer to the nut than on the fiddle). But the difference between the theoretical and actual octave position is caused by a phenomenum known as ‘end effect’, which is a function of the string’s elasticity, and unaffected by pitch or tension. On fretted instruments compensating for end effect is simple - you just put the bridge slightly further away from the 12th fret than the distance from nut to 12th - exactly how much further depends on the elasticity of the string, which is why the bridge lands up at a slight angle - further away for the thicker bass strings. If you change string gauges you may have to move the bridge slightly, but not if you change pitch whilst still using the same gauge strings.

On instruments like the banjo or mandolin with a floating bridge, that slight change in bridge position is very easy to accomplish (less so with guitars). If you look at tenor banjos or mandolas you will find people playing identical instruments tuned either to CGDA or GDAE a fifth below - they are using different string gauges so their bridges are in slightly different positions, but you won’t find that one is ‘more in tune’ than the other.

Stringed instruments are not designed for a specific pitch. If you go through the process of laying out and designing an instrument from scratch, at no point does pitch come into any of the equations. There is a very vague relationship between size and pitch, but it is not exact, it is the result of finding a compromise between tonality, string tension and ease of playing - amongst similar instruments designed to play at the same pitch you’ll find string lengths that vary by 20% or more, yet all the instruments play in tune at the same pitch, so clearly the idea that a change from 435 to 440 needs a change in the design of the instrument is nonsense.

Re: Paragon Plectrum banjo

I think you are very confused: - “I did not refer to the specific location of the octave position, but rather to the position which actually produces an octave.” What on earth does that mean? It might help you to think (as instrument makers do) about the difference between the theoretical and actual string lengths of the instrument. If we say a guitar has a string length of 25“ we mean a theoretical string length of 25” - the 12th fret will be exactly 12.5 inches from the nut. The actual string length will be slightly longer to compensate for end effect, but that extra length is all between the 12th fret and the bridge - if you change string gauges you only need to move the bridge, not the frets, and you don’t even need to do that for changes in pitch, only changes in string gauge.

The situation with a flute doesn’t correlate to stringed instruments. In a flute the frequency is governed by the length of the pipe and the velocity of sound in air - there is nothing you can adjust to change the pitch as you do by changing the tension of a string - you can’t adjust the velocity of sound, so the only way of changing the pitch is to change the pipe length. The position of the holes relates to the nodal points in the pipe, so if you change the pipe length in order to change the pitch of the lowest note you also move the nodes, so the holes need to be moved slightly to maintain their relationship to the nodes. But that doesn’t apply to strings - it would if you were to change the pitch by making the string longer or shorter as you do with a flute, in that case the nodes of the string would move and you would need to re-space the frets. But that isn’t how you tune a stringed instrument, you do it by changing the tension of the string. And when you do that, the string remains the same length, the nodes remain in the same places and the frets need to stay in the same places too.

“where on a 435 instrument the lower hand positions, closer to the nut, could be quite close to being in tune to 440 standards, but the further up the neck the hand goes the further out the tuning goes.”

A guitar will only play out of tune up the neck if the bridge compensation is wrong for the gauge of strings fitted. AS a guitar maker I spend a lot of my live measuring and adjusting these things, and I can assure you that once you have the compensation right a guitar will play in tune right up the neck, and it will still play in tune right up the neck if you re-tune some of the strings by a tone or two for alternate tunings.

Re: Paragon Plectrum banjo

Nobody is arguing with the laws of physics.

Note that all the strings on a guitar are the same length (give or take a couple of mm for compensation), yet the high E is 2 octaves higher than the low E. A guitar with all 6 strings at the same pitch - or with the bottom string 4 times the length of the top string - would not be a very practical instrument. The laws of physics state that the fundamental frequency of a standing wave in a string is determined by:
i. length
ii. mass per unit length (i.e. material(s) and gauge)
iii. tension

So, give these three variables, it is possible, subject to certain practical limitations (see my comments above on violins and double basses), to have a string of a given length vibrate at whatever frequency you choose.

Re: Paragon Plectrum banjo

“But a tone cut in half becomes an octave higher, and the lower/higher the tone the longer/shorter the sonic wavelength becomes.” This is where you’ve got it wrong.

Yes, when you touch a string exactly in the middle it sounds an octave higher. Yes, at a higher frequency the wavelength IN AIR is shorter, but not the wavelength in the string, that is set and fixed by the length of the string. As you probably know, frequency x wavelength = velocity. In air the velocity of sound is fixed, so if you increase the frequency it inevitably decreases the wavelength. But in a vibrating string the situation is different: the wavelength is fixed by the length of the string. but you can still alter the frequency by altering the tension in the string - what you are actually doing is changing the velocity of sound within the string. And it doesn’t matter what tension/pitch the string is at, the first node (the point where you have to touch it to sound the octave harmonic) is always exactly in the middle of the string, it doesn’t move. If you’ve got a stringed instrument handy try it: play the harmonic on a string and mark exactly where it is, then tune the string to a different pitch and find the harmonic again - it will be in exactly the same place.

Plectrums typically were tuned C G B D or alternatively D G B E (Chicago Tuning). When you pluck an open string, you mostly hear the fundamental note of the string (at that particular tension) - but you also hear all notes of the rest of the harmonic series, albeit at much lower volumes. If you incease (or decrease) the length of the string (whilst keeping the pitch of the fundamental the same), the relative volumes of the harmonics will change. In other words, the tone will be different.

That is why a 17-fret tenor banjo doesn’t sound the same as an otherwise indentical 19-fret tenor.

If this is what o’muirgheasain is really driving at, he is correct. Otherwise he is wrong.

Re: Paragon Plectrum banjo

o’m : I think you are being very hard on equal temperament. ET isn’t something that arrived out of the blue in the 18th century to ruin music, it is the culmination of several hundred years development in tempering systems, and gained near universal acceptance because it represents the best possible compromise.

Yes, JI is very sweet (or bland, depending on your point of view) when playing solo on flute or fiddle, but if you want to do anything more it is very, very limiting: forget about fixed pitch instruments, you can’t even have a trio of flutes, because you can’t play triads in JI: If one flute plays a root note, and another adds a just 5th, if a third adds a just 3rd above the root it forms an unjust, dissonant 3rd with the 5th. The only way you can make the triad sound reasonable is too temper the 3rd to distribute the error equally between the two intervals. If you want to limit yourself to playing in JI you rule out any form of harmony other than parallel 5ths - in effect you take yourself back to a point in time before tempering was introduced, when the only form of music possible was Gregorian chant.

In reality tempered scales are an absolute necessity in any for of of harmonic music, and the enharmony of certain intervals form a vital tool for composers and arrangers, who use them to add tension and emotion to their music. There is a strong case for using earlier tempering systems for period music, so that the enharmonies occur where the composer intended, but JI? no, it’s a whim. To be honest, in the real world when someone (almost always a fiddler) starts banging on about how much they love just intonation, I just assume they are making excuses for the fact that they can’t play in tune with other instruments.
