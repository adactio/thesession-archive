muscle/cellular memory

Did you ever go to play a certain tune and find that you can’t remember it offhand, but then you go and get your instrument and ‘noodle’ around a bit and--voila--your fingers found the tune and your mind had nothing to do with it?
Or you’re playing instrument A and go to play a tune, but can’t think of it, but you know it on instrument B (in my own case I know tunes on the fiddle, but can’t start certain ones unless I’m playing the mandolin). Maybe it’s because I learned certain tunes on specific instruments, my muscles/cells have a localized memory peculiar to that instrument skill set.
My theory is that memory lies in all the cells of the body, not just in brain cells; and that specific to ITM, within my fingertips/muscles of my fingers as well as brain cells.

Re: muscle/cellular memory

Muscle memory is a fascinating thing. Neuromuscular facilitation, more commonly called muscle memory, stems from repetitive training over time. Both the brain and the muscles involved become “trained,” or conditioned to the task, music being one of the more common examples of this. Because the muscles are repetitively involved, they become somewhat conditioned to the tune, and the tune becomes somewhat programmed, but still in the brain, not the muscles. However, because the muscles are conditioned to that particular tune, simply noodling about and trying to play the tune, as you say, will trigger the brain to remember the tune that your brain alone was unable to. Over time, if you ignore the tune, the conditioning of the muscles gradually goes away, as does the programming in your brain, hence the longer you are away from the activity, the more difficult it is to bring it back, kind of like playing the rust away. So, you are right in that the muscles are involved in the programming, but they themselves do not hold any information. The instructions still come from the brain, but the training of the muscles helps to facilitate the instructions, so both are involved in the process.

fiddlerdan,

Unless you have some new scientific proof, there is no such thing as cellular memory the way you describe it.

There are plenty of studies showing how the memory process works and especially how it relates to musicians. Musicians are a dime a dozen on the campuses of research universities and need the money. They are studied all the time with functional MR and other show and tell methods. The magic is in the brain, not the fingers. The muscles themselves have no capacity for memory, but they are connected to the brain by a series of nerves and specialized nerve endings. When left alone, say by a nerve blocking agent, they tend to twitch randomly.

But as a practical matter, you have it just right. The brain often needs a trigger to open up the pathways you describe and the outstanding (and emotionally satisfying) parts of tunes usually do it. Tactile memory (which is a brain function with feedback from the specific kinesthetic nerve endings in the muscles, tendons and joints) is often a very good way to induce memory. I know that is the way it works when I try to remember a tune.

While I may be all wet, and you all have valid points coming from the scientific disciplines, I still feel there’s another dimension to memory and its storage locations within the body. While there are many ways to describe the phenomenon, I’d like to think of it (and I make no pretense at being a scientist, just a guy who knows a few tunes and is in wonder of it all) this way:
Within each cell is the nucleus; within that is the DNA; within that is the RNA. (At least that’s what I remember from 2 years of college decades ago). Within each cell is the genetic blueprint of the whole: if you take just one cell, you could theoretically reproduce the whole person from the blueprint of just that one cell. It’s all there--the genetic information to build liver cells, heart cells, brain cells, and so on. But each cell is not the same cell it was at a person’s birth. Cells constantly die off and are replaced with new ones by mitosis within the body. But the thing is that each new cell is ‘born’ (for lack of a better word) with the memories and accumulated experiences of the previously replaced cell. My intuitive extrapolation of this is that memory lies within every cell of the entire being, not just within the brain. It very quickly enters the realm of quantum physics.

fiddlerdan -

While I don’t agree that the muscles or cells outside the brain can actually hold memories, I do agree, as per my post earlier, that the muscles are directly involved in the remembering process. It’s a fascinating process. Your muscles get conditioned to the tune through practice, which programs the brain to the tune. Your brain may not remember a particular tune by itself, it needs muscle activity to trigger the memory. So, first you pick up the instrument and begin to play, this requires the brain to instruct the muscles. There is a rudimentary memory of muscle movements that you are not conscious of, but because your muscles are conditioned, they just sort of obey, depending on the level of conditioning. This triggers the memory in the brain that remembers the tune, whether strictly by muscle movements or even sound association. So, yes, the muscles are certainly involved, as the mind and body are one, not separate, I believe that firmly. From a scientific view, however, while I know that DNA holds coding, I don’t believe it can hold memories. I guess we won’t know for sure until we can “see” the microscopic world in which these things happen better than we can now. I do believe in a spiritual connection between the mind and body, however. Some cultures acknowledge the mind and body as one, not as separate, as traditional science tends to study them. Personally, I’ve never quite understood how western medicine has so isolated the two when our basic reason tells us they are too dependant upon one another to be so separated by science.

To summarize the experiments briefly:

"In 1953, Dr. James McConell began performing labyrinthine experiments with planarian worms (Dugesia dorotocephala) at the University of Michigan, training them, using the punishment operand of classical conditioning, to learn over a period of months to consistently follow the most effective route through a maze….

…Planarian worms are cheaper to buy and keep alive than lab rats. Nothing new. But it was what he did next which raised a few eyebrows.

Keeping track of how long it had taken the entire first group of worms to successfully learn how to run the maze, he killed them all and fed the remains to a second group of hitherto untrained worms.

Keeping this second group and a third control group of planarians (on a normal, non-cannibalistic diet) isolated, they were all trained to run the same maze using the same method as with the first group of worms.

The control group of worms took roughly the same amount of time to learn to run the maze as the first batch had, but the group which had eaten the first, educated bunch learned to run the maze considerably faster than both the control group and the original group on which they had dined. It was as though by eating the first group of learned worms, they had in some small way attained a not insignificant glimmer of the knowledge which had been previously imparted unto their dinners.

“That maybe explains the custom among cannibal tribes of eating the wise man after his death in order to recieve his wisdom. General, you could go into a delicatessen and order Einstein on pumpernickel…” - Alan Moore, The Anatomy Lesson

Similar results were later got when unschooled worms were injected with the RNA of successful maze-runners, leading to a controversial theory of the relation between RNA and memory, and later similar experiments produced further similar results in rats, goldfish and canaries."

McConnell, JV. 1962. Memory transfer through cannibalism in planarians. Journal of Neuropsychiatry 3 (Supplement no. 1): 542-548.

Hartry, AL et al. 1964. Planaria: memory transfer through cannibalism re-examined. Science 146: 274-275.

So, in a nutshell, irrespective of the cannibalism issue, when the worms, (and later rats, goldfish and canaries) were injected with the RNA of the trained predecessors, they all received the memories/knowledge of the trained predecessors. While SOME subsequent studies attempting to duplicate or discredit the results of the cannibalism transfer of memory were unable to duplicate the results, NONE of the subsequent studies could account for the memory transfer via RNA injections.

So doesn’t that point to memory lying within the cells, nonspecific to the brain, and that memory transfer can be facilitated via chemical assimilation?

A colleague forwarded this excerpt from http://wapedia.mobi/en/Planarian -

Biochemical memory experiments

In 1955, Thompson and James V. McConnell conditioned planarian flatworms by pairing a bright light with an electric shock. After repeating this several times they took away the electric shock, and only exposed them to the bright light. The flatworms would react to the bright light as if they had been shocked. Thompson and McConnell found that if they cut the worm in two, and allowed both worms to regenerate each half would develop the light-shock reaction. In 1962, McConnell repeated the experiment, but instead of cutting the trained flatworms in two he ground them into small pieces and fed them to other flatworms. Incredibly these flatworms learned to associate the bright light with a shock much faster than flatworms who had not been fed trained worms.

This experiment intended to show that memory could perhaps be transferred chemically. The experiment was repeated with mice, fish, and rats, but it always failed to produce the same results. The perceived explanation was that rather than memory being transferred to the other animals, it was the hormones in the ingested ground animals that changed its behaviour.[3] McConnell believed that this was evidence of a chemical basis for memory, which he identified as memory RNA. McConnell’s results are now attributed to observer bias.[4] No double-blind experiment has ever reproduced his results.

Re: muscle/cellular memory

My understanding is that memory resides in the synapses of connected nerve cells. A successful action is rewarded throughout the neural system by making the synapses used in a successful action more sensitive - this facilitates a repeat of the successful action. It all works quite well when there are a lot of interconnected neurons - each with a number of synaptic connections with other neurons.

These neurons do not only exist in the brain. For instance - a large nexus of them exists in the heart. The spinal cord has a ton of them also.

When you connect a sensory input to a neuron complex that connects to motor functions, you have a self-organising system (see network-processing and self-organising systems). This is an over-simplification, but it works.

The common house-fly has a very small brain, but it seems to cope with some very complex actions and behaviors.

Mathematic versions of this are already at work in many computer aplications - voice recognition, fraud detection etc etc.

Not sure how introduced RNA can influence synaptic sensitivities.

With something as complex as musical learning and expression, I suspect the whole organism is involved - that would include muscular training - not that the fundamental memory is there, but that the sensory systems would find the trained muscle-fibre easier to use for the right note to be produced. That information would become part of the total memory. Cetainly, it would assist in re-discovery of a memory that has faded. Conversely, it might impede the total memory if the muscle becomes damaged or loses its training somehow.
