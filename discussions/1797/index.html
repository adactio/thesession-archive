Re: Traditional instruments

And of course usually then that all plunges us into the “define ‘traditional’” debate. Andee, the piano is very traditional, but hard to tote around to most sessions, so it could be argued that it’s traditional if there’s a piano already in the room and if the person playing it is playing it traditionally. :) Add to that the fact that it wasn’t til fairly recently that people began being able to *afford* to have a piano around. (The other name for the box was “the poor man’s piano”.)

Dave, the thing is that NO ONE knows what ancient Irish music sounded like. We know that they used harps, drums are mentioned, and there’s an occasional mention of the pipes (keeping in mind that the whistle would fit under the time’s definition of a pipe), but that’s about it, except we also read that the music was considered very lively and caused people to dance and laugh (and, supposedly, sleep, which makes it sound like a night of hard drinking, except no one talks about vomiting on their shoes that I know of). There’s some mention of the music being, variously, magical, coarse, common, and/or strangely fascinating. I think the first less-generally written accounts of the stuff that actually talk about the music itself and its forms (as opposed to a general description) are in the 1700s, but there’s not much there. So “pure” would depend how you’re defining “pure”. For all we know, Will’s rockist could have gotten it more right than anyone else, although, frankly, I doubt it. Heh.

As I understand it, ‘Traditional’ is the name of a genre of music, much like ‘Jazz’, ‘Opera’ or ‘Jungle’. Like all these words, it has another of other meanings as well. In Ireland, the term ‘Traditional Music’ is readily understood by the majority of people, and doesn’t need defining.

The problem for us Exohibernians is, most people outside Ireland don’t know what we’re talking about when we say, “Traditional music” (after all, Jazz and Opera can be ‘traditional’ as well - not sure about Jungle). So, to try and be more specific, we extend it to ‘Irish Traditional Music’ (and then shorten it again to ITM). Then we have to define ‘Irish’, define ‘Traditional’ and define ‘Music’, and we get in all of a pickle.

Before anyone nails me to a cross, I realise, of course, that Traditional Music is much more than just a genre of music - like any art or music form, it is far greater than the sum of its parts. There’s far more to it that I could ever possibly understand, my background being far from Irish. But I don’t think it pays so sit and think about it too much.

Re: Traditional instruments

Diddley indeed,
traditional instruments can only be defined as those which have been accepted into the tradition. If they work, and people (musicians) actually like hearing them and playing alongside them, then they ‘become traditional’. So Bazoukis and mandolins have quickly assimilated. Other instruments which people have tried to introduce have not really been accepted (e.g. Bombardes, clarinets, saxophones, djembes, basses and cellos), because people on the whole don’t like them, except as an occasional novelty; and some instruments are so manifestly unsuitable (trumpets, euphoniums, kettle-drums, tubular bells come to mind) that they obviously are not going to count.
Synthesisers don’t really count because they are not ‘an instrument’ as such, more a sort of musical chameleon, though I don’t personally like thinking of an eelectric/electronic instrument as being traditional - the idea somehow jars.
Piano has gone from being a common accompaniment, on which the player might also have a few tunes he/she could lead, to something fairly rare, mainly I think because there are fewer around. Once apon a time lots of houses (private and public) had pianos. Now sadly they are uncommon. I personally like hearing (and playing with) piano accompaniment, it gives a nice ‘old-fashioned’ sound.

Re: Traditional instruments

I find that, although digital pianos are faithful to the sound of a real piano, they still sound like they’re coming through a speaker and not from a wooden box. Perhaps it’s just my preconceptions of electronic keyboards distorting what my ears actually pick up, perhaps its that I’ve never heard one played through a decent set of speakers, or perhaps it’s just that you’d never get a piano that well miked up in the real world (after all, these things are sampled one note at a time, in an anechoic, sound-proof, airtight, watertight, disinfected studio.).

One of the best piano accompanists in Irish Traditional Music, I think, is Charlie Lennon. He is also one of my favourite fiddlers and ‘traditional’ composers. On his albums, ‘Musical Memories’, Vols. I and II’, all his own compositions, he accompanies himself on piano - and synth, on one track. While I’m not a great fan of multi-tracking in traditional music, I wholeheartedly approve in this case, as I don’t think he could have found anyone else to accompany his tunes as well as he does.

Re: ‘Irish Traditional Music’. The problem with this term, as has been hinted at several times in this thread already, is that it is loaded with connotations. ‘Irish’ suggests that it must all have its origins in Ireland, among the Irish people, and be played by Irish people - which it its not, necessarily. ‘Irish’ could also refer to the Irish language, yet there are plenty of songs included the genre which are in English.

‘Traditional’ suggests that all the music is ‘old’ and has been played in the same way, on the same instruments, for centuries. This is not necessarily true.

‘Music’ - There are doubtless some people who would count the activity in question outside their definition of ‘Music’ - presumably, none of them will be reading this.

It would save a lot of trouble if we came up with another name for it. Michael’s ‘Diddley’ is perhaps a little too general, since some Finnish or Italian music might sound equally ‘diddley’ to some peoples ears. What is needed is a word that has no onomatapoeic or semantic connotations whatsoever, yet is easliy pronounceable. Something like ‘Velquith’ or ‘Treeve’. If an ignorant friend were to ask you, “So what is this Velquith, anyway?”, you could just play them your 200 favourite records out of your collection, take them along to your local session every week for a few years, persuade them to take up set dancing and the pipes (not necessarily at the same time), and they’ll at least get a vague idea of what your talking about.

Re: Traditional instruments

The huillean pipes, or oillian pipes were originally named after the very similarly shaped bags made of sheep’s bladders and used for conveying and dispensing olive oil in Brittany. The complex system of pipes and valves dispensed measured units of oil of different grades (Extra-Virgin, Virgin, and Shop-soiled). Other instruments with culinary antecedents include the frying pan shaped Banjo, once actually known unaffectionately as the Panjo, and of course the Melodian, or ‘Miel-odean’ as it was known when the bellows were used for squirting the smoke that rendered the honeybees docile enough for the hive to be opened.
