Session Etiquette - Choosing what set to play when prompted?

I don’t have a session around me (and in fact have only made it to two sessions so far as I live on a small island in the PacNW).

There’s one session etiquette question that I’m curious about.

When you’re visiting sessions, and you’re prompted to start a set, how do you choose your tunes?

I assume you pick tune types based on what has / hasn’t been played in the session, know your tunes, and match the tempo of what’s previously been played.

Do you try and do tunes that fewer people know? Or try and stick with things that you think many can play along? A mix of both? Is it based on the general skill of the session (and yourself)?

Re: Session Etiquette - Choosing what set to play when prompted?

“Show ‘em what you got”? Are we now advocating for indecent exposure in sessions?

The last thing I’m going to do when first invited by a session host to start a set is “show ‘em what I got”.

That, it it ever happens, would be later in the evening, after I’ve established some connection with the players and sort out what sort of group I’m playing with, and only if there are others in the group who could play along with some obscure or technically challenging set of tunes I might share.

I’ve been in sessions where a visiting player used an initial invitation to start a set as an opportunity to showboat, playing a long set of tunes nobody knows at breakneck speed. It seldom makes a good first impression.

When visiting a session, take some time to lay back and figure out who might be the session leaders/hosts, what the level of the players is, are sets started by anyone or just by the leaders/hosts. Are they all newbies, experienced players, pros, or a mix of all the above?

It’s good to have several different sets you can start if invited that are appropriate for the makeup of the group and can maximize participation in your set.

I find that is the best way to be welcomed and hopefully make a good first impression in a session I am visiting for the first time.

Re: Session Etiquette - Choosing what set to play when prompted?

You’ve gotten good advice above. I just want to add a couple of things. Playing a set when prompted is something that you should expect when you visit a session. First off, it’s acceptable for you to decline, or defer. It’s pretty common that someone will say “maybe in a bit after I’ve warmed up some”, or similar. Secondly, just keep in mind that playing in front of other people is very different than playing by yourself. You may feel fine, but subtle subconscious nervousness can derail your playing very easily. So the advice to have a couple of sets you could “play in your sleep” in your back pocket for when you’re asked is really good advice.

As for what tunes to choose, some of it depends on how comfortable you are playing solo in front of strangers. In those cases, you may want to either choose some fairly common tunes, or even better, play a few notes of each tune and ask if they know it. But in general, I would advise only starting tunes that you know well and are sure you can hold together even if nobody else is playing. And make sure you can get from one to the next. At least until you’ve gained a level of comfort with the other players. That might happen in a night, but it might take multiple visits to the session until you start to feel comfortable.

Re: Session Etiquette - Choosing what set to play when prompted?

AB’s mention of Calliope House raises the point that one of the advantages of fiddle (which the OP plays) is that it’s relatively easy to play in a wide range of keys. Now, this is by no means a requirement, or even an expectation, but it can be a wonderful asset to get comfortable playing tunes in a range of keys, at least for the not insignificant cohort of tunes that often show up in more than one common key. For example, I’ve come across Calliope House in various sessions in E (Dave Richardson’s original key), D, and G, and it’s not much bother to play it in any of those on fiddle.

Sessions where D flutes and pipes are the norm may favor G for tunes more commonly played in A elsewhere (e.g., Foxhunter’s, Miss McLeod’s), and fiddle sessions may go down the rabbit hole of playing G tunes in F, Edor tunes in Ddor, Amix tunes in Gmix, and so on.

On the surface, this may seem like extra work, a “chore” of learning the same tune more than once, when there are already so many tunes to learn. But really it’s more a matter of fully learning your instrument, and finding that playing the same tune in several keys actually frees your fingers (and often bowing as well) from one static pattern.

More to the point of the OP, most sessions hinge on the notion of people playing together, rather than solo, so it usually makes sense to play tunes the other musicians are likely to know. You can suss this out by going to the session a lot as a listener before you decide to bring an instrument—no pressure to play, and you can get a sense of what tunes they know and play. Some sessions repeat a smallish repertoire, while others collectively know a vast number of tunes and seem to rarely play the same tunes from one week to the next. Take notes, aski if you can record some sets, and get to know their tunes.

You can also bring your instrument the first time and just politely decline to start a set until you’ve sat in with that session several times and are more comfortable with the people, the repertoire, pace, etc. Given your distance from likely sessions, this might be the better option.

When you do sit in with the goal of starting a set if asked, it helps to show up on time. This way, you’ll hear all the tunes played that night, from the start, so when your turn comes, you can avoid the “session foul” of playing a tune that’s already been played that night. Some sessions don’t mind playing the same tune again, some sessions will grumble but get on with it and then tease you about it, and some sessions will come to a screeching halt until you veer into a different tune.

Regardless, the more often you attend a given session, the easier it will be to know what to play. No two sessions are the same. Some tend to be grounded in a certain repertoire (e.g., Donegal tunes or Sligo tunes) while other play tunes from anywhere, and in many genres (Irish, Scottish, Breton, Appalachian, Trazz, and so on). Some sessions welcome O’Carolan or polka marathons, or lots of syncopated hipster tunes, others cringe.

It’s also okay to play a set of just two tunes. Before you launch into them, you can say something like, “I’d like to play The Mountain Road, then Man of the House, four times each.” Don’t be surprised if at the end of the fourth time through Man of the House, someone jumps in with another reel to keep the set going (though in some sessions this would be considered rude).

Bottom line, it’s better to play simple tunes well than to attempt a tune at the edge of your ability and thrash it. Same with tempo—start at an easy lope, no need to race (even if the last set was blazing fast). Better to play with lift and liveliness than white-knuckle panic.

Finally, you can try chatting up one other musician on the side and ask for their help in holding the tunes together in case you falter. Ask if they know the tunes you want to play, agree on how many times to play each tune, and then set a shared pace and groove. And remember that in the beginning, it’s easy to overthink all this. Many sessions are friendly, eager to welcome a newcomer. You don’t have to be the center of attention, and if you don’t expect to be, you’ll likely fit in just fine.

Re: Session Etiquette - Choosing what set to play when prompted?

Jerone, in the online age, is anything really obscure any more? Tunes are more “discoverable” than in any previous era—there are more recordings (new ones always being added to the old), more concerts (in normal times), more written collections of tunes, and a zillion sources, videos, audios, abcs, etc., on the web.

So I think of some tunes as very widely-known-and-played (the so-called “bog standards” that everyone knows) such as the Mountain Road, Silver Spear, Connaughtman’s Rambles, Tripping Upstairs, and a thousand more, and then there are less-widely-known-and-played-tunes. Not “obscure,” just not in the center of the bull’s eye of session tunes. As you get further from the bull’s eye, you’re less likely to have people join in on any given tune. Except when they do.

The other way to think about this is that a less-widely-played-tune can be well known and routinely played by everyone at a particular session. So at that pub, it’s the furthest thing from obscure. Yet maybe nobody plays it at the session just across the county line. And sometimes even players who can play hundreds or thousands of tunes at the drop of a hat may not know a particular bull’s eye tune. “Farrell O’Gara? No, sorry, never got around to learning it.” It happens.

It may help if you buy lunch or beer or coffee or whatever.

Re: Session Etiquette - Choosing what set to play when prompted?

1. Choose tunes that haven’t been played already that night. If you haven’t heard any of the session before this, don’t play - say you’ll wait to hear a set - wait a few sets to get the feel of the session

2. Play tunes other people will know - well-known sets / well-known tunes (common reels and jigs you’ve heard in other sessions). It’s a session. You don’t want to be playing alone for three or four tunes or whatever. That’s not the point of a session. It’s no fun for everyone else that way. (check out Dow’s List if you don’t know what common tunes are: http://www.cheakamus.com/Ceilidh/Downloads/Dows_List.pdf )

3. Match the pace, tempo, tune mix of the previous tunes / songs.

4. If in doubt, get a drink and wait another set or two.

Re: Session Etiquette - Choosing what set to play when prompted?

And before anyone posts “Well, I’d never go to a session where the host starts all the sets, how elitist…”, let me explain why they did that.

In this case, the session venue is a very loud pub where you almost couldn’t hear the person sitting next to you. The session leader played a fairly loud button accordion, generally the only one at the session. Everyone could definitely hear him and play along immediately.

If someone else on a quieter instrument were invited to start a set, pretty much only the players sitting on either side of them could hear, they would join in, and then the players next to them until you can finally hear what’s being played and everyone joins in. At tune changes, the same game of telephone has to happen to get everyone on the same tune. Fun, but kind of messy.

So, in this case, it simply works best to have the loudest instrument start a lot of the sets, there is no elitism going on here, (actually, probably one of the most friendly and inclusive sessions I’ve gone to many times over the years) it’s mostly a practical solution.
