We’ve had our constant return to raking the bodhran over the coals, and we’ve this week had a natter about singers:

https://thesession.org/discussions/4695

Now what about ‘dancers’ - all types, sizes, ages and inclinations - and any stories you might have, good and bad, about dancers and sessions, or even outside of sessions in your other music making ventures. There must be a load of funny and sad tales to tell, and curses and blessings to be made.

Jack Gilder wisdom:

There’s a pub in California, Berkeley to be precise, where a man named Terry O’Neill (RIP) taught Irish ceili dancing. He had a style that was very energetic and even aerobic where teh dancers would jump around like gazelles. Some of these dancers would show up across the bay in SF at the Plough and join in with the set dancing. You could always tell who they were because as they moved through the figures their heads would bob up and down. This became known locally as the “Berkeley Style” of Irish dance.

Re: Dancers and Dancin’ - Pleasure or Pain in the Arse

I remember staying a week in Doonbeg with my family, the week after Willie Clancy week. Miltown was dead, thre was very little music going on. However, I ended up by accident in a pub called the Igoe Inn (owner: Sean Igoe!), or one close by. There were only two musicians, box and whistle, having a session so I joined them. After and hour, the locals started to get up on the stone floor for a few sets. Well, it was some of the best dancing I’ve ever seen, and these people were by and large senior citizens. The way they whirled around the floor, their natural rhythm and the sheer joy they exuded was a sight to behold.
I do a bit of set-dancing myself, but this was well beyond me. I was just glad to be part of the whole thing.

I think the question here is: what do people think of dancing at sessions.

Personally I think it’s great if not overdone, or as long as it’s clear that the music is there specifically to promote dancing.

Re: Dancers and Dancin’ - Pleasure or Pain in the Arse

Similar thing happened to me, Conan, on my way back from Miltown to Ennis.
Miltown had been disappointing that Saturday night, all the sessions were jam packed, we couldn’t find that “magic” session where everything would have fallen into place, so we were driving back and decided to stop off at the Kilmayley Inn to see if anything was happening.
Well, there was a ceili in full swing. The group was the West Clare Ramblers and included Tommy Macmahon, ex All Ireland concertina champion.
The dancers seemed to be all locals, farmers in suits and ties.
Well, the swinging and the battering and general leaping around that was going on was sheer magic.
Tommy asked me up to play a couple of tunes with them. I was shaking like a leaf - this was the REAL thing ! But it turned out OK.
IMHO the music is made for the dancing and there was no better example of it for me than that night. The two complemented each other perfectly.
So if you’re driving to or from Miltown, stop off in Kilmayley (where Eamonn Cotter is from) and you might be lucky enough to catch a ceili. You won’t regret it !

Re: Dancers and Dancin’ - Pleasure or Pain in the Arse

Perhaps this is the “exception proving the rule,” but I’ve recently had a very humorous, and enjoyable, dance-related session experience.
One place I frequent is a kind of family restaurant/pub, which means there are often a number of little kiddos (i.e. elementary school age and younger) about. The 7-yr-old daughter of one of the session regulars is a budding step-dancer, and she and a friend worked out a routine, if you will, for when her Dad sings “The Bog Down in the Valley-O.”
It goes kind of like this: They do the step-dancing during the verse and through the cumulative chorus, and during “Roll, roll, the rattlin’ bog,” they make a rolling motion with their hands and other kinds of invented sign language.
Then the musicians present all play the tune of the song, and she and her friend circle the room in a drag-step motion, followed by as many other kids as they can get to come with them.
Repeat as necessary, or until someone falls and gets hurt.

I’m sure not everyone is wild about the idea of children at sessions (or wild about children, period), but if you’ve got a family atmosphere, you might as well have fun with it. Personally, I can hardly keep from laughing, it’s such a hoot.

Both Dale Russ and John Williams have told me that if you want to play ITM you should put some time in playing for sets. I have taken their advice to heart and have been doing so for a few years now. It has helped me to understand the differences between tune types as well as understanding energy and tempo. The crowd here in SF that dances sets are all very nice and supportive. It’s a pleasure to play for their dances, or have them lash one out when they show up at gigs and sessions. So like Dale & John, I too recommend the same.

In the last few years we have been offering to hire a caller and have Céilís at weddings. This has caught on big time and now most weddings we play include them. There’s nothing like having all the wedding guests on the floor forming two lines with men on one side, women on the other, and having the bride and groom “stripping the willow” down the middle for the “Haymaker’s Jig.” I have witnessed wedding photographers running around, and even colliding with one another like the Keystone cops to get the great shots. I now tell the bride that she should warn her photographer about the céilí dance so they don’t get injured.

Knocknagree and the changes over seasons:

We’ve had the pleasure of visiting with and dancing to the Music of Johnny O‘Leary, as well as giving space for Dan O’Connell’s occassional high kicks, back some time ago for that. In the old days he was really the only one that ever did such high jinx.

What we used to notice, session or dance, is the way you could distinguish the time of year by the visitors and by who was in what area of the dance floor by ‘style’. Most of the locals, going back a long ways, tended to dance a nice tight set, low footwork, not loug or high, very considerate and patient dancers. When the tourists got there, as sets became ‘popular’, and I mean in particular the ‘Irish tourists’, from Cork and Dublin and the likes, the city folk, they took up more floor space and floor-to-roof space too. The footwork was larger and the dance space they claimed was also fatter. The locals tended to give space to these acrobatics not out of appreciation but more out of self-survival and fear.

I’ve even seen the musicians, and been in that situation, where the music had to back away into a corner or even back a bit from the end of what you might think was a safe place - the edge of the stage nearest the dancing, so as to avoid damage by the zealous. I’ve seen musicians kicked and I remember one lad dancing out of control and ending up in amongst the musicians and coming damned close to destroying an insturment. Fortunately we were expecting something of the like and were prepared to move out of his way when he started his grand finale. Sadly, that didn’t stop him, and there were those of us who would have liked to see him escorted off, along with his contengent who were hogging the area just in front, as such tend to do. Why?

All that, I feel much like the folks I’ve enjoyed playing music with and learning from, which is that I’d much rather have dancers out there to answer the music. It’s just a shame that some of them can be such self-obsessed, ignorant and inconsiderate shights.

If your playing polkas up to speed -- it’s another good test of a bodhran player’s mettle. Unless the polkas are played at a more moderate pace, the players who figure out how to cut their movements in half are the most successful, but some of the wiser ones will just sit those tunes out.
