Re: ‘Carrickfergus’ the Song not the town near Belfast??

Free Reed, you’re right -- Carrickfergus is clearly quoting the older song in tune and lyric. In traditional English and Irish folk song, crossing over water (usually but not always wide) is symbolism for dying. Calling for a boat or boatman to carry one over the water is a standard way to express desire for or expectation of death, and appears in a number of traditional and traditionalish lyrics.

The Water is Wide, far from being a tender love song as supposed by careless listeners, is about and from the point of view of a jilted woman killing her out-of-wedlock baby and then herself. (My 4th grade teacher sure didn’t realize this when she taught a class of girls the song.) Carrickfergus, far from being a tender love song, is about and from the point of view of a broken down old drunk who has lost everything and is about to die, or would like you to think he’s about to die so please buy him a last drink real soon. (This is why I think the Van Morrison version is definitive.) I sometimes suspect the lyrics may be intentionally humorous.

I’ve been asked a number of times to perform these songs at weddings, as well as the equally inappropriate Greensleeves -- a jilted sugardaddy complains that his mistress has dumped him even though he’s bought her all sorts of stuff. (What part of “alas, my love, you do me wrong” do you not understand?) These lyrics are intentially humorous.

#3. Hence, the tale is one of some poor Irish lad probably in love with an English or Scots soldier’s daughter. His “childhood friends and close relations” have likely been slain or immigrated away, and also likely he may have been a “rebel” and is “on the run,” a vagabond wandering with no home, and a whiskey monkey on his back. Poor lad.

Re: ‘Carrickfergus’ the Song not the town near Belfast??

Carrickfergus is in Antrim. Ballygrant is across the Irish Sea on Islay in Scotland. “But the water is wide…” Almost 80 miles of the turbulent North Irish Sea separate Carrickfergus from Ballygrant. You would definitely want “a handsome(i.e., skillful) boatman” to make that crossing.

- “Now in Kilmeny…” Kilmeny (not Kilkenny) is the parish in which Ballygrant is located. If the singer’s beloved had died in Ballygrant, Kilmeny Churchyard is where she likely would be buried. It has a noteworthy burial ground in which there are numerous…

- “…marble stones as black as ink.” The black marble stones come from the nearby quarry, which was Ballygrant’s primary industry in the 18th and 19th centuries.

The unexplained “Kilkenny” reference is probably an artifact of mis-hearing, substituting the name of a well-known Irish city for a little-known Scottish parish. But Kilkenny has no logical connection with either Carrickfergus or Ballygrant.

- “With gold and silver I did support her…” The other major employer in Ballygrant and Kilmeny Parish in the 18th and 19th centuries was lead and silver mining, which attracted miners from across the water. And Ballygrant lies over the Dalradian geologic complex, which is the source of gold being mined in Northern Ireland even today.

Re: ‘Carrickfergus’ the Song not the town near Belfast??

A. Quigley: It’s all but impossible to be precise about the words of traditional songs. The opening of the third verse could be:

“Now (or but) in Kilmeny (or Kilkenny), it (or she) is reported (or recorded) on (a) marble stone (stones) as black as ink…”

I doubt anyone can claim authenticity for any version of ‘Carrickfergus’ - we can only make our best guess. Unless a song is copyrighted, it’s really up to the individual singer to choose or invent the words at will. That’s why so many traditional songs are found with many versions, and with bits and snatches of other lyrics and melodies (“…the water is wide…”).

There is an Irish song, ‘Do Bhi Bean Uasal,’ which is purported to be related to ‘Carrickfergus.’ I don’t know its melody, but the words certainly don’t match up.

Re: ‘Carrickfergus’ the Song not the town near Belfast??

<i>The whole part about not having “wings to fly” and wishing to “meet a handsome boatman”…feels tacked on with no effort to make it fit the story. Anyway, on reflection, it also seems more than a little impertinent to try to make literal sense out of the song. </i>

I have to disagree with both points. First off, the song is about wanting to be somewhere across the sea, so the ‘lift’ from ‘Waly Waly’ (or wherever) makes perfect sense. Secondly, trying to make literal sense of a song can lead to deeper understanding of times and places past.

In searching for the meaning of the ‘Carrickfergus’ lyrics, I have learned much about Islay and Kilmeny Parish in the 18th and 19th century, about migration of mine workers from Ireland to Scotland, about silver mining and quarrying of ‘black marble’ in Ballygrant, and about actual connections between those places and Carrickfergus. The result is a story that makes sense out of previously recorded nonsense.
