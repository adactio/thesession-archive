Re: breathing…

I suppose you could start doing sprints every morning, but the key is to use good posture, play standing when you can and don’t be afraid to move when you play (as opposed to locking into a position). Playing should be like doing anything else - allow your body to be as natural as possible. As you learn how to phrase the music the way you want it to sound, you will take breaths intuitively and also take enough to complete the next phrase. Above all, the more you play, the less breathing becomes a conscious effort. Don’t make a labor of it.

Re: breathing…

Id just add that breathing is best done useing the Diaphragm to draw air in rather than the chest and to push it out ; Belly breathing its called. Most people lose this natural approach as exemplified by babies and resort to a partial breath from the chest which severely limits the amount that can be inhaled.
For an exercise I suggest breathing in as deep as possible with a stop watch and see how long you can blow a single tone on one lung full. As your body remembers belly breathing you will notice a big increase. its also about useing as little air as possible so try to use just the air you need to attain the sound your after.
I also recomend walking and playing as this frees up the body and forces you to be more aware of your breathing and where you can breathe in a tune, and how to fit many more breathes in and still maintain continuity of the tune.

Re: breathing…

My small contribution, as an early stage flute player:

All the advice about breathing from deeper than the chest is good, but the one thing that helped me is practicing that “quick gasp” of air intake. That’s not something one does in normal life, unless you happen to fall into a lake of freezing water, where the body does it involuntarily.

It’s something you see every advanced flute player do, but it doesn’t get talked about much. Breathing for rhythmic expression is all well and good, and so is figuring out where in a tune to take those breaths. But if you can practice that very quick air-intake, it’s easier to do things like play “across” the phrases and bar lines where you can disguise which notes you may be dropping to take a breath.

Re: breathing…

When you breath in, it should feel like the air is going into your guts.
Obviously it isn’t really, but you should feel your lungs inflating down at the bottom,
pushing out your stomach.

When you breath out to play a tune, it should feel like you’re using
your coughing muscles. Next time you cough, notice how you feel
activity way down in your stomach area as well as your chest muscles.
This gives you powerful support - in fact more than you’ll ever need on the flute.

You can’t play clarinet, trumpet or oboe without that kind of support.

Re: breathing…

I think both conical bore and Mark Huppert may be over-thinking the process. That much concentration on breathing is, IMHO, a very bad habit to develop. There are times when you may need to skip a note to get a breath for a tune that has a long or complicated phrase for which more air is required, but beyond that, let the process develop naturally. So-called quick gasps can be heard and sounds awful. It sounds like the player is anxious and uncontrolled - no, no, no! If you need to do that, you are likely using too much air to begin with. Perhaps the best exercise is making sure you can get through a phrase on one breath. Practice that rather than how to breathe, per se. It does not take a great deal of air to play a flute, just the knowledge of how you want to phrase a tune and the practice of having done it. When I first started wooden flute (after ten years of Boehm flute), I found myself hyperventilating, but in time, I adapted to the difference in flutes and the technique of playing ITM. I do not have extraordinary lung capacity, but even amplified, you will never hear me take a breath and I have never done a breathing exercise in my life. Learn how to play the instrument and how to play Irish and the breathing will come.

I agree completely with Ailin. I think that efforts should be concentrated on good postural form - on eliminating any un-needed postural tension. ↳ Posted by David50 on July 21st, 2014.

Re: breathing…

What Galway is talking about with the quick breath is quite different than gulping air because you ran or are running out. He is coordinating the short breaths with the alternating notes he is playing to sustain that bit for an extended period because there is no other way to get through it. That’s not at all the same as grabbing notes with a gasp.

By the way, he spends the bulk of that video talking about how his teacher got him to hold his body up and his chest out. In my earlier post, I recommended playing standing up. You will be amazed at how much better your breath control and overall tone is if you do that. Even when you observe good posture in a chair, it isn’t the same as standing up. When you stand, you get that stance Galway was demonstrating. When you become accustomed to how you stand and how your body feels when you stand to play, you can approximate it better when you sit because you mind knows how your body should feel and what you should be hearing. You will unconsciously strive for that if you practice standing up.

Re: breathing…

As I said, he does it in a pulse between notes. Skipping a note gives you the full count of the note in which to take a breath. Breathing between notes must be done very carefully and skillfully to avoid mucking up the time. His technique is to set up a pulse of a breath out for the note and a breath in to replenish his lungs. The effect is that he is not taking a breath at all. Actually, I do the same thing throughout a tune. The difference is that I am not doing it in a pulse, as he does, and I do it for all my breaths. In other words, I am breathing between notes, but it is a light intake of air - only enough to sustain a short phrase - and thus can be taken both quickly and quietly. However, I also use the skipped-note technique quite frequently, both as a style of playing (I like the syncopation and lift), and a means to get more air than I can between notes. I use what I need when I need it. Knowing how to do that is the skill, not trying to develop greater lung capacity or taking gulps of air.

Re: breathing…

Ailin gets my thumbs up.
One thing, in my opinion, is to enlarge your breathing volume. Playing in a standing position is one great thing to do so. I use to play in the park (early in the morning) or even outside the city (after noon), where I live, because I don’t want to disturb any neighbours. So I have to take a walk or go a long way by bike. That’s a big difference to playing at home. The lungs have had filled with air and it’s a complete different feeling to play.
But another important thing, I think, is to structure a tune in phrases before I start practicing it.
In this respect, another fine preparation for playing is to lilt the tune/s I want to play, while I walk/ride to those places. Thus, without my mind beeing distracted with fingering problems, I can easily check out where to breath.
Structuring the tune in phrases before I start practicing means: I breath, when I want to phrase, and not, because I’ve run short of oxigen. Grey Larsen says: “Always breath before you have to”.
There are always different ways to structure a tune into phrases, but there’s no way to learn a tune without structuring it consciously.
It is no superhuman thing to play tunes legato, if you’ve studied well, how to omit a non-pulse note or how to break up a long roll/long crann etc. in order to breath, as long as you’re doing this consciously. One doesn’t need the chest of superman to breath a whole tune without interrupting it.
I always try to interrupt the flow of the melody at different places to get to know how that sounds. That gives you more freedom and experience in different scenarios of breathing.
With those exercises you can avoid those ugly “quick gasps” as Ailin described it. They are an absolute no-go.

Hi Dan,
that is a really good and important question.
Renowned trumpet soloist Jens Lindemann, former lead trumpet player for the Canadian Brass, and currently Professor of Trumpet at the University of California, Los Angeles, answers it as follows:
http://www.artistshousemusic.org/videos/the+physical+aspects+of+breathing+as+a+wind+instrument+player
My wife played the oboe for many years in an orchestra. First she had a teacher, who focused on playing technique. Some years later, she had another one, who told her, that her breathing was absolutely underdeveloped, and she had to learn the whole thing again, from the beginning. She told me, that this was the real beginning of her “playing”.
Here come the most important things she had to learn:
If you’re playing seated (playing upright is easier): Always sit on the front edge of the stool with a straight back. Put your knees apart, as wide as your shoulders are. This is to allow your organs and your lung, to have enough space in your body, so that you can breathe without hindering yourself. Never play like a couch tomato, your back leaned against something.
Make some exercises with your diaphragm: Put one hand on your belly, whisper “ha-ha-ha-ha-ha” in a kind of staccato manner, and feel, wether your belly is really moving jerkingly (This is just to relax your breathing muscles, kinda warming up). You can easily practice this more often, when you’re out for a walk.
Always start your practicing with playing long tones. Try to play them even and straight, kind of mezzoforte (That’s also good to get away from too much fingering and doodling - an excellent practice to make you conscious of your intonation and your fingering). Then you can try to play them crescendo-decrescendo or with an exaggerated vibrato - without changing the pitch.
That’s all.
