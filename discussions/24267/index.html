Re: Sean Nos Singers……not my cup of tea

‘But seriously does any outside of the Sean Nos Trad enjoy listening to it?’

Yes, I for one certainly do enjoy listening to sean-nós, though perhaps my understanding of Irish Gaelic might be better than premierflute’s.

Ireland has produced an incredible number of of superb unaccompanied singers. Even if one doesn’t understand the language it’s impossible not to be moved by the singing of Joe Heaney, Nioclás Tóibín, Seán ’ac Dhonncha, Máire Áine Ní Dhonnchadha or Darach Ó Catháin and, in more recent times, Róisín Elsafty, Iarla Ó Lionáird, Lasairfhíona Ní Chonaola, Muireann Nic Amhlaoibh and Séamus Begley.

You’re wrong, premierflute, in assuming that all sean-nós songs tell a story. There are plently of dandling songs, comic songs, nonsense songs, fishing songs, ploughing songs, obscene songs, etc. The ‘Big’ songs, such as ‘Na Connerys’ form only a small portion of the local repertoire (as you’d discover if you’d ever spent a night on Tory Island).

I was at a wedding a while back in Donegal. The initial ceremony was small, but one of the guests there was Gearóidín Breathnach (a wonderful singer from Donegal) who sang a most poignant song after the marital vows were completed and, when we celebrated back at the couple’s house, came up with all manner of appropriate songs.

I think you should listen more, premierflute, and broaden your horizons.

Excuse me but who said Sean Nos Singing, was a part of “sessions”?

Every “Sean Nos Seisun” I’ve experienced was not in a session format at all, and in fact would have been deemed inappropriate.

The most memorable sean nos singing I ever experienced was at a special group in an upstairs room above a pub during the Famine Festival in Louisburgh eight years ago. I can’t recall the name (maybe O’Toole) of the obviously revered
singer, but I can swear that I never ever heard a more delicate and moving tribute
to the ancient tradition. I was literally stunned by this gentle rendition of a ballad
in a language which was full of meaning, and to me, who had no clue to the gaelic
language. It didn’t really matter. I was moved to tears by the beauty of expression,
the courage of the performer, and the validity of the expression.

I hope this helps you to rethink your conclusions about sean-nos singing and
stirs you to find that same experience, that has meaning for you.

Re: Sean Nós ……the stuff of life itself

Whether you know nothing about sean-nós or you think you do, whether you like it or think you don’t, whether you think you could like it or not, you might benefit from reading these two articles to gain a fresh perspective:
Julie Henigan’s 1991/99 study of Donegal style(s) is the most helpful;
http://www.mustrad.org.uk/articles/sean-nos.htm but you might want to read Anthony McCann’s 1998 Bluffer’s guide to sean-nós first to put her survey and analysis into perspective;
http://www.folkmusic.net/htmfiles/inart378.htm
McCann cites Séamus Mac Mathúna; “Sean-nós singing is at once the most loved and the most reviled, the least often heard and the least understood part of that body of music which is generally referred to as Irish Traditional Music … It is the least understood because, technically and emotionally, it is the most complex part of that body of music, and many of those who dislike it do so because the techniques of sean-nós singing are not the techniques which they have come to regard as the ”proper“ or ”correct" ones. For the feeling and emotion of sean-nós singing is not expressed by the standard European ‘bag-o-tricks’, and so it is that to some not unbiased ears it sounds ‘uncouth’, ‘untuneful’, and ‘unmusical’.

There is also a brief list of names and resources at ;
http://comhaltas.ie/music/treoir/detail/amhranaiocht_ar_an_sean_nos/

…But since ‘premier’’s question is about our personal feelings, I’ll give you my own story;
I was never exposed to the stuff before I moved to Ireland where I first heard it -mostly on the radio at first. I used to want and try to sing a harmony line to ‘complement’ most of the songs I heard on the waves, for this style often sounded too bare on its own for me to bear! Since I couldn’t harmonise properly without knowing the airs I started to do drones on them instead (and sometimes simply to get a sense of the tunes themselves: some of them sounded like they were all over the place or felt rather plain until ‘revealed’ by the drone). Soon enough though, this form of accompanied listening gave way to appreciation of the tune by itself and for itself (the melody, the style and tone of the voice now carried enough force).
Besides, not having any Irish seems to matter only when in actual company, since you know you’re missing something: you can’t share or appreciate it the way the other punters do (the best of them anyways! ;-)) “Sean-nós English” could pose the same sort of problem sometimes (e.g.; if you listen to the likes of Cathal McConnell singing, you’ll realise they nasalise their delivery to the point where ‘poor’ innocent consonants of the text become n’s!)
All in all, my experience may help illustrate that ‘familiarity does not necessarily bring contempt’: as we change (and we all do), our tastes change -and vice versa: our changing tastes change us.
And if you don’t like live sean-nós yourself, I suggest you make an effort to learn or refresh your Irish: see what a change that would make!
