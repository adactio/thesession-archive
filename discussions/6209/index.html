Added by grego on March 28th, 2005

It all sounds the same.

The other day my 15-year-old complained about some nice traditional music I had playing on the stereo. “It all sounds the same,” he said, somewhat but not entirely tongue-in-cheek.

I explained to him that Megadeath, Metallica, and Iron Maiden all sounded the same to me too until I was subjected to large repeated doses of each during trips to the soccer centre. He couldn’t imagine that this could be true.

Still, I think he was right. Traditional music does all sound the same until you take the time to absorb it and learn to appreciate the subtleties. Or you listen to impure drop varieties that have artificial flavouring added. The stuff I want to play and listen to is an acquired taste, and I got to it through the “pop” versions like the Bothies and Planxty.

In my younger days I tended to view educated-pallette type activities as either eccentric or snobbery. I’ve had to change my mind.

So, what do you think? Are we the musical version of stamp collectors?

↳ Posted by Phantom Button on March 28th, 2005.

Re: It all sounds the same.

Truth to tell, it DOES all sound alike. Structurally, ITM has fewer differences from reel to reel or jig to jig (etc.) than almost any other musical form. There are no rests, no dynamics, no intros, breaks, or choruses (in the dance music). The basic recipe is very set. The instrumentation is standard, and even where it varies, every melody instrument is playing the same line. There is no harmony and no counterpoint. Without ornamentation, there would be absolutely no variation in how a tune was played. Even the tempo is fairly standard.

Still, I’d be ashamed not to say I love it!

One of the things that attracts me to Irish music is the variety of forms and rhythms. I mean, compare it to American pop/rock and most modern country, where virtually everything is 4/4 time and the instrumentation 99% of the time is electric guitars, electric bass, standard drum set and maybe electronic keyboard or violin. Every genre sounds monotonous to an outsider, but I’d say ITM is an order of magnitude less monotonous than most of what’s in the air.

But if you’ve grown up imbued with the music the difference will seem like something every baby can tell, because you *learned* it as a baby.

If you aren’t familiar with classical Indian music you might be able to simulate this by listening to a raga. There’s a famous story about Ravi Shankar doing a command performance for E.R. and getting a standing ovation for tuning up, so he took a bow and left the stage. The peasants wouldn’t have understood the actual music anyway.

And I have personal experience with classical musicians who take up Irish music learning tunes, by ear even, from a recording, who play neither the right notes nor the right rhythm, but *they* cannot hear the difference

Because until you’ve already learned the difference you can’t hear it, or, at best, think the fiddler has “bad intonation” if you have a really good pitch sense, because he’s hitting the right notes and your brain will insist on fitting what you hear into what you already “know.” You might even go so far as to refuse to be taught properly by said fiddler because you won’t take lessons from someone who “can’t even play in tune.”

Okay. *grin*

I do some organizing for our local sesh--sending out a weekly email reminding people that the session exists, touching base with the publicans to make sure we haven’t overextended our welcome, and setting up the occasional tune swap or learning session. And a couple of the core players do get together now and then to swap tunes that we later haul out at the session.

But any sense of stability comes from the simple fact that the core players do show up, most weeks, and we’ve been playing together for almost a decade now, so we know each other’s repertoires and can read each other’s moods, and the beer is always free.

Beyond that, the session has a life of its own. Some nights it’s low energy and some nights it’s steaming. Sometimes the singers all but take over, and sometimes it’s nothing but tunes. A lot depends on exactly who shows up. We’re also lucky to have fairly frequent visitors, which destabilizes the session in a wonderful way.

I mean no offense to anyone, but it seems to me the people who most want stability in their session are those with less experience and toonage under their belts. The longer you’ve been doing this, and the more tunes you have that don’t get played often enough, the more you welcome the serendipity factor, detours, and the unexpected.

Re: It all sounds the same.

The difference is that I don’t necessarily go into a session expecting “the full range of music” all in one night. Often, that wouldn’t be appropriate--to the mood, the crowd, the musicians themselves.

I guess I take the longer range view--that I play ceilis for a chance to play for dancers, gigs to do listening music, tune swaps to learn and teach tunes, time at home alone to do whatever I want with the music, and sessions for the craic and group energy and the mix of familiarity and the unexpected that sessions are good for. Sure, sometimes our sessions turn into mini ceilis, and sometimes the crowd hushes and we do two hours of listening music, and on occasion we teach a tune on the fly, and once in a blue moon one of us will go off on a solo ramble as if no one else was in the room. All part of an ongoing session. But I wouldn’t anticipate any of that on any given night.

To me, the point is to be mindful of what you can contribute to make it a good session for others, rather than coming to a session with your own expectations already lathered up and hoping to get something out of it for yourself.

As one of the core players at my local sesh, I like to think that I play a supporting role. My job isn’t to dominate the tunes, but to bring my experience to bear in a way that enhances everyone else’s evening. In any one evening, I might start a tune or set that I know a newbie is just dying to play, and join in to support another player, and be the group metronome to settle the tempo, and dredge up an old tune we haven’t played in ages, and launch some new tunes that only two or three people know, and ask a newcomer to start a set, and ask for a song, and sometimes carry a string of tunes just to keep it going so we get in some tunes everyone knows and crank the energy up a notch. In any given night, I might carefully play a pre-arranged piece as a duet to make one person happy *and* totally cut loose on another tune just to appease someone else’s urge to go wild. But I wouldn’t expect any of this to happen, even if I’m prepared to help it happen should it feel right.
