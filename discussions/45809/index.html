Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

I play both -- mandolin melody and guitar backing -- although focusing more on flute the last few years. Backing on guitar can be fun, and if you know the tunes and rhythm pulse from playing a melody instrument, then you have a great start on being a supportive and non-intrusive backer. Of course there is the typical “one guitar player at a time” issue with many sessions, so unless there is a dearth of good guitar backers in your area, that could be a problem once sessions start up again.

I disagree that mandolin is “not much of a session instrument” except for small gatherings. True, in a very large gathering it will get lost except for those sitting near you, but if you have a high quality instrument and decent playing skills, the initial “ping” of the note attack can make a good contribution to the group sound.

In the Before Times at one local session, I was able to kick off sets and tune changes on mandolin with no trouble in a medium-sized group of 5-6 fiddlers, whistle, guitar, and one piper. A session that grows so large that the mandolin would get lost, is usually one where I would be packing up and calling it a night.

Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

The general sense of your original post is that you probably already recognize that you don’t have the deep level experience with the music to be an effective backup player at this time.

It’s a major challenge to be a competent backup player in sessions, I would not advise it for someone who primarily has been playing in slow sessions and still building basic repertoire.

You would be much better off spending the time on mandolin and concertina expanding your repertoire over the coming years, and then possibly consider taking up backup.

Backup players, in general, to not be problematic in a session, have to know the tunes as well as understand and put into practice the modal chord structure and flow inherent in the tunes based on years of careful listening and practice. Otherwise, as you astutely observed, things can go sideways very quickly, as has been endlessly beaten to death on this board.

Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

A mandola in standard CGDA tuning can be made more ITM-friendly with a capo on the 2nd fret, which puts you in DAEB tuning. You have basically the upper three strings of a fiddle/mandolin with an easy high B note, pitched an octave down. Sort of a mini octave mandolin.

I tried using a mandola that way for a while, eventually with string gauges where I could just keep it in open DAEB tuning without a capo. I finally gave it up because I missed the notes on a fiddle/mandolin bottom G string in some tunes. Also, I already had an octave mandolin, so it felt like a duplication of effort without the power and “oomph” of the larger bodied octave mandolin.

One more comment on the subject of not being heard, or being able to hear oneself on mandolin. I think this sometimes happens because players choose mandolins with a “warm” sound and lots of harmonics. That tone can sound great when playing alone at home, but in my experience a mandolin with F-holes and more energy focused in the fundamental pitch will cut through the sound of other instruments better in a session. A “clear” tone instead of a “warm” tone, if that makes sense. In my experience, that kind of tone is found more in the Bluegrass-type mandolins than “Celtic” designs.

Another trick for tenor banjo players to flatpick guitar if handed one in standard tuning is to retune the B down to A and you end up with:

EADGAE

Then play primarily on the
xxDxAE

strings (similar to the advice for the mandola), jumping over the G string.

Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

Sorry Will, I strongly disagree with you.

Learning backup by blindly copying someone’s annotation in a book is in my opinion a terrible idea. Sure, it feels good at first, but for the long term it’s a recipe for being an eternally mediocre and inflexible backup player in real sessions where anything might come your way.

Do the work to learn to listen and actually distinguish the fundamental key and modes on the fly, which is absolutely possible.

Get a copy of “Celtic Backup for All Instrumentalists” by Dr. Chris Smith (excellent tenor banjo player and music professor):

https://www.amazon.com/Celtic-Back-Up-Instrumentalists-Chris-Smith/dp/0786640650

and do the listening exercises in the book and included audio CD to learn to hear the root tonality of a tune and the modes, as well as hear the changes and develop the tools and distinctions in listening required to really be an effective backup player.

If you choose to go the route of learning from other people’s arrangements, perhaps a more traditional Irish tune centric source like the later editions of the Seattle “Smoke in Your Eyes” tunebook that had chords added by those with a clue might be a better starting point. I just think “The Fiddler’s Fakebook” is a terrible source for both tunes and chords for Irish music.

Hi Mike, a third alternative might be to pick up a bouzouki, which is usually tuned like a mandolin 1 octave down (often the high E string is tuned down a step to D). ’Zouks tend to be session-friendly backup instruments-- chords can be minimal and as such less likely to argue with the melody line-- plus you have the option to use a bouzouki as a melody instrument, playing either in first position or with a capo, typically at fret 5 or 7. Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

“If you blindly slavishly follow the melody of a tune composed by someone else … why then turn your nose up at following the chord patterns written by someone else for those tunes ?”

Because as is often said about this music, the melody is fixed and accompaniment is improvised. It’s the reverse of many other Western forms of folk music. There are some tunes where guitar players would probably choose the same chords, but many others where they would choose different chords.

Where do you put the emphasis in tunes that have an ambiguous tonal center? Do you use the third interval in a chord to make a strong major/minor statement or leave it ambiguous? How do you handle the last part of The Butterfly? Do you shift to a major chord feel, or do you retain the minor feel of the rest of the tune? There is no right answer here, no correct set of chords, just a personal decision.

I don’t disagree that looking at someone else’s chords when just starting out might be helpful, but in my opinion that should be abandoned as soon as possible, in favor of listening carefully to how very good guitar players back tunes on recordings. You’ll hear a variety of approaches to chord choice and voicings for the same tune. It’s chordal improvisation, a more difficult skill in this music than learning the tunes.

Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

I think there are a couple of characteristics which determine what makes a mandolin easier to hear in a session, beyond volume alone.

Clarity of playing - hitting each note clearly and cleanly, and at exactly the right moment - is an important factor in how well I can hear the playing of a mandolin or guitar.

The presence of the instrument is important too in being heard. The quality of coherent and intelligible sound determines presence, which usually has to do with the quality of the instrument. A mandolin or guitar with a lot of presence can sound fuller and louder than what the readings on a decibel meter would suggest. Easier to hear at a given volume.

Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

As promised here’s the result of cutting a sound port in the side of my mandolin. I started the process with an electric drill and a sharp 3/4 drill bit and finished the rather rough hole with a carving knife and sandpaper. To my surprise there was a rather remarkable increase in my ability to hear myself play. Cool, even a little better when I took the Tone Guard off of my good mandolin and put it on this one. Another player tried it and agreed. when he played the instrument for me so I could hear any changes I found no noticeable harm. He agreed here too. If I may I’ll add the caveat that this was not a great or expensive instrument to begin so think long and hard before you reach into your toolbox. Personally if I were to add a port to my other instrument I’d have a luthier do the job for a better looking finish. A better instrument might be more easily prone to developing cracks along the grain that may require reinforcement. The port need not be massive to be effective. All in all I’d say it was worth it and no harm done. Were I to have a fine instrument made I’d ask for a tone pastry and I know many makers provide them. I’ve certainly heard many instruments with the sound port and found nothing awry. If you believe as I do that not hearing yourself play is detrimental to your ability to play well in sessions or a noisy environment You might think of this as a solution. I would probably not do this with a truly vintage instrument.

Third, I wonder how much of the effect you found is due to the port’s placement pointing at your head vs. just adding the port (i.e., enlarging the total sound hole area) anywhere. As someone mentioned above, mandolins with f-holes are widely thought of as being louder, having greater projection and less sustain than similar instruments with round/oval sound holes. My understanding is that much of this difference comes down to the fact that typical mandolin f-holes are much larger than typical oval sound holes which affects the cavity mode frequencies obtained with each hole style. Do you have the impression that the instrument’s tone/projection/sustain changed after the surgery, especially from a listener’s standpoint? Re: Guitar or mandolin for session playing?

Just as an aside from a luthier, cutting a soundport into an existing instrument isn’t something I would advise anyone to do: the instrument really needs to be designed for it from the outset, with the front sound hole size reduced to compensate. Although you might not have noticed it just listening subjectively, if you had recorded the projected sound before and after and then done an AB comparison of the recordings you would notice the ported version sounding noticably thinner, with less low frequency content and less projection.
