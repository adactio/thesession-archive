Excellent review with some thought provoking observations of the terms “Celtic” and “traditional”, etc., and their evolutionary meanings. Also, who decides what “traditional” and/or “traditionally” means as regards Irish music?
:-)
It might imply that the music has always or mostly been played in a particular way and has passed down through the generations or between musicians. Of course, gradual changes and variations will develop over time too.

Or it might also include newer music which has been composed and/or performed in a traditional style? Could De Danaan’s version of “Hey Jude” fall into the latter category?

Also, it might be something completely novel which catches everybody’s imagination and will continue to be popular and get played for many years to come. So, it becomes “traditional” in its own right.

Having said all that, there doesn’t seem to be a general consensus of what “New Age” music is on this thread.
For me, it’s NOT newer or modern treatment of traditional tunes or songs. Nor new songs and tunes written in the idiom. It certainly doesn’t include the younger and more innovative so called “trad bands” etc.
As far as I’m concerned, it was specifically an eighties phenomenon(although it still exists) and a very “airy fairy”, ethereal, other worldly type of music which had little relationship to Irish or other traditional music at all. It hadn’t anything to do with folk/rock, singer song writing, folk/jazz fusion, or even Euro Celt etc etc either . All of these were/are different beasts, in my opinion.

Re: “New Age” Irish Music

To my mind “New Age” music typically lacks rhythmic drive and narrative force. It tends to just “float” evoking some sort of mystical feeling. You can hear “new age” in lots of genres, but it always seem to have this floaty static feeling. I get fed up with it quickly.

Irish and jazz: as a lifetime fan and player of jazz I feel like they have some things in common, mostly rooted in the swing feel. Yes I realize people will insist they aren’t the same feel, but Francis O’Neill used the terms “swing” repeatedly in his early 20th century descriptions of Irish music. Irish-American and African-American music and dance have a lot of overlap in US history, and I feel like ITM and American jazz, while clearly not in any way the same, are cousins. Along with the swing feel the emphasis on improvisation--again while it’s not expressed exactly the same way, it reflects a shared origin.

ITM as it has evolved is not much interested in harmony, whereas jazz took a turn much more towards harmony as the element of music to stress. This makes them hard to “fuse.” If I were interested in a “jazz fusion” I’d look to earlier forms of jazz less concerned with complex harmony. The Carolina Chocolate Drops it seems to me experimented some in that line.

Re: “New Age” Irish Music

[excluding England]?

I agree this would be the case for much of England but, of course, we also have Cornwall and the surrounding areas. There are big Irish “scenes” in places such as Manchester, Liverpool, and other parts of The North of England. In London too where there are also lots of Scots.
As Bex says, much of Northumbrian music is related to Scottish and Irish music too.

I wouldn’t say that all of Scotland is particularly “Celtic” either. Shetland fiddling owes as much to Scandinavian music as anything else. There’s even North American influences and the likes of the late Peerie Willie Johnston developed much of their style through listening to short wave radio broadcasts. Also, the NE style is quite unique to that particular area of Scotland and very different to those on the West Coast which have more similarity with Irish music.

It’s highly probable that there will be disagreement with what I’ve said here but this should only reinforce the argument that “Celtic Music” is a meaningless concept.

Even the large “Celtic Connections” music festival in Glasgow has more or less given up on emphasising the “Celtic” side of the music and now seem to be pushing the “Connections” aspect in recent years. This, of course, gives them the opportunity to promote whatever music they wish in their programme. So, it doesn’t need to be Scottish, Irish, or whatever, although this still makes up an important part of the proceedings. Nor particularly traditional, just as long as it’s “connected” in some way!

Galician vs Galatia
Galician is a Romance language spoken in NW Spain, which is closer to Portuguese than it is to Castilian Spanish. It has nothing to do with Galatian, the language spoken by the people who settled in what is now central Turkey (a long time before the Turks arrived there). Galatian (the name is related to “Gauls”) must have been a Celtic language, but it died out over 1500 years ago, so I doubt whether this is the culture referred to in some of the earlier posts.
