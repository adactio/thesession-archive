5 string banjo at Irish Sessions

Hi everyone,

So this is a really cool thing I’ve tried and I think people should do more often. I play both clawhammer and tenor banjo, and I have been finding new ways to terrorize people in musical traditions with them. Tenor banjo is not a common instrument at old-time jams, and it’s really interesting showing up with one.

On the same end, I’ve gotten alot of use out of my five-string at Irish sessions. There are a couple crossover tunes that play very well on it (Highlander’s Farewell to Ireland, Red Haired Boy, Mrs. McLeod’s). There’s also a surprising number of non-crossover tunes that work really well with it. Some good one’s I’ve found would be Innis Oir, the Bally Desmond polkas, or Lord Mayo.

Anybody else had any luck bringing the “wrong” kind of banjo to a session? Any tune recommendations?

Re: 5 string banjo at Irish Sessions

I had a clawhammer player appear at one of my sessions a few times. He was a very good musician with a great ear, and could play Irish tunes in a pretty convincing fashion. However, I found it to be a problem in the session. It was a constant source of muddiness in the overall sound, and it seemed to suck all of the lift and drive out of the music. Part of it was that he would start with accompaniment on tunes that he didn’t know (and would often learn the tunes well enough to be playing with us by the time we were moving on), but the style of accompaniment felt foreign to the music.

But even when he was playing the tunes, I think a big part of the issue was the sound profile of the notes. The attack was quite a bit softer than a tenor banjo would provide, and and sustain seemed to be longer, too. So it had a similar effect as someone playing a harp or hammered dulcimer without damping the strings at all, and it would take the edge off of the bounciness and twists and turns of the tunes. So as you progress with this idea, maybe be cognizant of the fact that people might not be enjoying it.

Campbell’s Farewell to Redgap does get played in Old Timey jams, so I’m not surprised to hear it on clawhammer banjo. And doing so makes it sound Old Timey, not Scottish.

Just my tuppence: many of the clips above showcase wonderful playing. But none of them sounded convincing to me as well suited to Irish trad.

One issue you might run into is moving through different keys/modes in a set on the fly. If you’re tuned in G, and with that 5th string G pinging away, jumping to A or F or even D could set you up for a clash. One of the clips above was in one or another modes of A, and the player had a capo on the 2nd fret. Probably also had the 5th string raised to A (either by retuning or clipping the string beneath a “railroad spike” in the fingerboard). By comparison, moving among the common trad keys/modes on GDAE tenor banjo is quick and easy.

I enjoy an occasional Old Timey jam, and in that genre the sound of fiddle and clawhammer together is hard to beat. But at an Irish session? As a one-off novelty, a little clawhammer at a session wouldn’t bother me, but to my ear it doesn’t sound like Irish trad, the strumminess and sustain could muddy things, and retuning or capoing on the fly would likely be a flow killer.

Monty, you’re obviously sensitive to those concerns or you wouldn’t have joked in your OP about “terrorizing” musicians by crossing genres. Some people in some sessions will agree with you that bringing the “wrong” banjo is “really cool.” But other people at other sessions—or even just other people at the same session—will vehemently disagree with you.

Count me as one of the people who would happily play Appalachian tunes with you all night long at an Old Timey jam, but who also doesn’t relish the thought of clawhammer players regularly showing up at my local Irish trad sessions. I’d much rather hear Irish tunes infiltrate Old Timey jams. :-)

Monty. No disrespect intended, but I interpreted your intent as someone coming from a clawhammer style actually trying to emulate Irish Traditional Music style. It was the farthest thing from my mind that you were actually suggesting playing clawhammer style banjo in an Irish Seisún. Would you also just drop in on a chamber orchestra or a wedding band?

ITM is not just a collection of tunes. It’s a family of closely related, long standing, deeply rooted styles of playing that, until not too long ago, had actually almost died out.

All of the posted videos are examples of talented playing. But they are not examples of Irish Traditional Music by a long shot.

If you find a session with a sign that says “Fusion Wanted,” go for it: play clawhammer banjo style. Decades ago I remember one or two such sessions in northern New England, where styles ran from British Isles to Sea Shanties, which is what I grew up with.

Or get together with like-minded friends in a park. I love music, so would be happy to play with a clawhammer banjo (plus a cello for dramatic effect) outside a session.

But if you get into a real Irish Seisún, you need to be there with an intention to do what the other players are there to do, which is to play Irish Traditional Music: a highly nuanced repetoire and set of regional styles.

But I’ll be honest: Based on the above videos, you’d better be the Eddie van Halen of clawhammer or it can’t be done.

Re: 5 string banjo at Irish Sessions

This talk about tenor banjos being “traditional” really is cherry picking your timeframe… Unless I’m mistaken, there were no tenor banjos (or bouzoukis, much less 10-string citterns) in traditional Irish music 100 years ago. These are all relatively recent additions to the “accepted instrument” for traditional Irish music.

As far as I’m concerned it’s all in the playing and the feel combined with setup of the instrument (whether tenor or 5-string) to reduce ring and sustain. I’ve heard plenty of tenor banjos that awful long ringing sustain as well.

This discussion reminds me of the “no English concertinas in sessions”, again because of some conception that it’s impossible to play an English concertina in a way that sounds like traditional Irish music. That’s garbage, I know at least two players who have the understanding of the mechanics of the Anglo and are able to play the English concertina in a way that if you put them behind a screen you wouldn’t know they weren’t on an Anglo.

A similar, and more common situation exists for piano accordion players, they really have to understand how to get the feel and lift inherent in the physical nature of the diatonic button accordion to get the right feel.

Now, that being said, I agree, I’d absolutely suggest to a new player that they start on tenor banjo, Anglo concertina, or diatonic box, because to emulate the feel of those on another instrument you really have to understand the mechanics and playing style of the original instrument.

I asked Michael how he came to play tunes on the 5-string, and basically he was already a very skilled 5-string player before he got into traditional Irish music. Rather than change instruments, he spent a lot of time listening to tenor players and figuring out how to get the tenor sound and feel on the instrument he already was proficient on. We’ve talked about it for hours on rides to/from sessions, it’s a fascinating story.

Re: 5 string banjo at Irish Sessions

> This talk about tenor banjos being “traditional” really is cherry picking your timeframe… Unless I’m mistaken, there were no tenor banjos (or bouzoukis, much less 10-string citterns) in traditional Irish music 100 years ago. These are all relatively recent additions to the “accepted instrument” for traditional Irish music.

This is in many ways a completely separate topic, but “traditional” does not mean the same as “old.” Traditions change over time, and what’s more important than how long one thing or another is around is whether or not it is seen as traditional and accepted as part of the tradition by the community that carries that tradition. In fact, as a tradition what we call “Irish traditional music” is really only about 150-200 years old at most; much further back than that and it starts looking quite unrecognizable. I think it’s quite fair to say that tenor banjo is “traditional” to the ITM community in a way that 5 string banjo is not, even if we can actually place the 5-string banjo in Ireland before the tenor banjo really even existed!

> Now, that being said, I agree, I’d absolutely suggest to a new player that they start on tenor banjo, Anglo concertina, or diatonic box, because to emulate the feel of those on another instrument you really have to understand the mechanics and playing style of the original instrument.

This is really the heart of it. I have heard ITM played quite well on the clarinet, and a good clarinet player would almost certainly be welcome at many sessions. However, I don’t think many if any people would count the clarinet among the “traditional” instruments. Just because something isn’t “traditional” doesn’t mean we should exclude it; if we did, then we wouldn’t have most of the instruments we now consider “traditional!” But it also means that it becomes more incumbent on the player of that instrument to mold themselves into the community. I have played ITM on the 5-string and it can be done, but there’s also a reason I bought a tenor banjo a couple years ago. I would humbly suggest any fellow 5-stringers do the same, since it’s a lot easier (IMO) to sound right on a tenor.

Where 5-string banjo *can* shine, IMO, is in accompaniment. Played clawhammer-style it lends a nice touch to reels and anything else 2 or 4. But it takes a fairly sensitive and knowledgeable player to pull it off; otherwise, it’s a very loud instrument to have clanging around on bad chords or off the rhythm!

Re: 5 string banjo at Irish Sessions

Clawhammer *does* in fact involve playing the 5th string, as a rhythmic high drone, with your thumb. So to play Irish, you’re now saying that the musician has to develop a new technique, a new method, and abstain from using a particular string when it’s ill suited to the tune at hand. Yes, we’ve seen this in some of the clips posted above. But with so much new learning and adapting required, why not just play tenor? (The mind boggles at the lengths to which some people will go to justify an awkward choice of instrument. Here’s a thought—why not tune your clawhammer banjo gGDAE, ignore the 5th string, and use a flat pick. 8-))

“…you do need to have studied ITM and worked on a representative number of ITM pieces before venturing into a session. I can’t think of any instrument for which this would not be true.” Of course there’s a crucial difference, which is that when you play one of the instruments traditionally used for Irish trad, you’re playing an instrument ***on which at least some of the music was composed and with a well-established approach for playing Irish trad.***

Even johnny-come-lately tenor banjo, most commonly tuned GDAE for Irish, has its own body of tunes, and besides shares the same note patterns as the fiddle. Plus, when you play tunes that were composed on one of the other customary trad instruments, generations of musicians before you have already worked out how to fit any potentially awkward bits to your instrument. (E.g., a long roll on the second octave E is easy enough on whistle, flute, or pipes, but fiddle and banjo will likely do a triplet or a simple sequence of notes (maybe efe) instead.) These are known quantities, no need to invent new concepts or techniques, and—surprise!—the results are readily accepted by the community of musicians you’re working so diligently to join.

FWIW, banjo may be “fully chromatic,” but most banjo players retune at least some of the strings to play tunes in keys that are less friendly to open G tuning. Because any other approach is too awkward. With clawhammer, alternative tunings are downright common. But then they don’t run consecutive tunes in wildly different keys. And they’re not going to retune on the fly while leading a set at tempo.

I love the sound of a well-played clawhammer banjo rippling through Sugar Hill or Sail Away Ladies. And I also love the sound of a well-played tenor banjo dancing through Beare Island or growling away on Tuttle’s or the Castle Jig. Each playing to their strengths rather than struggling against their limitations. Why use a hammer to cut a board in two or a saw to drive a nail home?

A quick search turned up an interesting but very long interview in which he talks about his journey from old time to klezmer (and back). Here’s an excerpt, where Henry talks about klezmer with respect to banjos. It’s a cliff hanger:

"Q: Had you thought about integrating all the banjo skills that you had developed into this [klezmer] music? Or was that not appropriate?

A: At that point no, I was approaching the music in a very orthodox, unbending manner. I wanted to play an instrument that had an historical precedent in the genre. Once I realized that I was not serving the music to the best of my ability, that my fiddling required a far longer incubation period than I could give it as a working musician, I decided to let the fiddle go, and go to the banjo and adapt. There was no way I was gonna go and work out klezmer tunes in clawhammer style. I once did it as a joke: I had been teaching for Jay Unger, he had a fiddle and dance camp, and at one point I had worked out “Freylekhs fun der Khupe” in clawhammer style, but it was a joke. There is no justification for playing klezmer music in clawhammer style. Clawhammer is about context: I wouldn’t work out “Maple Leaf Rag” in that style, that had already been defined in classic style. It would have been a freak show.

As it turns out, there was an historic precedent for klezmer dance bands of the 20’s using tenor banjo."

Coincidentally, I asked Kevin earlier this week (before having noticed the present discussion thread) how an Old Timey group came to back him on that album. So — no trend-setting effect intended, none attained.
