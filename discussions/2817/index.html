Re: Searching for peers

We have a great mix here between “old-time” and Irish music. The two crowds don’t really try to mix musically (except for a few extrodinarily talented musicians who really add to both groups when they play) but we mix a lot socially, often having joint parties. The “old-time” crowd here is a nice community and we’ve certainly learned a lot from them about setting up gigs and keeping everyone informed of what’s going on. They’re very organized and active. Very helpful.

(A little funny, side story. A bouzouki and guitar player in town is from Tipperary. He’s in the habit of calling people “craythur”, as in, “how’s it goin’ dere craythur, what’s the story?” Well, one of the old time musicians thought he was saying “critter”. It has now become acceptable to say “hey, critter.” This is how language progresses.)

Of course, it’s hard to find peers here too. I played “old-time” like crazy when I was in college but I never play here. I play Galax-style clawhammer banjo. Not really in vogue here, it’s all Round Peak style. Galax style certainly doesn’t dominate in N.C. Maybe in the north-west counties like Ashe and Watauga. I have to go back north to play old-time.

My best friend is a really great fiddler and we play tunes sometimes, me backing him with “irish” style guitar. Usually, it’s West Virginia tunes. I really love those tunes and their style. It’s fun, but I don’t really mean it for public consumption. I’m, regretably, kindof’ a purist by nature.

I’m sure you do the festival circuit in the summer, but you might try going to some of the smaller festivals. Clifftop is fun, but you’re right, it can be generic “hippie” old-time - ageing folk revivalists insisting that they know how the music should be played because they met Tommy Jarrell once. The ones I used to frequent were Elk Creek, Sparta, West Jefferson, and… I can’t remember where else right now. Maybe go to a festival and just sit down and play. Someone with a similar interest might hear you and you’ll strike up an interest.

I think “old-time” and Irish musicians could mingle more and I know many who would like to. I don’t have a lot of interest in bringing the two together, but I think some people could pull it off nicely. John Doyle did a nice recording with “old-time” fiddler James Leva that you might want to check out. Pat Egan, from Chulrua, did an album with an “old-time” band from Missouri, can’t remember what they’re called right now.

Anyway, good luck.

Joyce, you were at the Frankie Kennedy festival? So was I, my 4th trip in 5 years. We must’ve brushed past each other a couple times at least. (If you saw a tall Chinese fiddler, with an overgrown beard, that was me.) And your assessment of the Donegal fiddle style is pretty much spot-on. (But not to give anyone the wrong impression, the fact that there is less swing than e.g. Clare style is not to say there’s *no* swing. You want fiddle music with no swing, try real Scottish or some Jerry Holland Cape Breton stuff. (I can’t stand Jerry Holland’s rhythmic style, too robotic…)) Every year Gearoid has a surprise in store for me, this year he got me a spot on RTE Radio na Gaeltachta playing live. What a hoot, the Chinese fiddler from Los Angeles playing on the all-Irish national radio, and being interviewed in Irish as well.

Donegal fiddle is very much influenced by Scottish of course, but it also has some other weird connections - to Kerry, for example, to Poland, and some other far-flung places. One of the great things about the Donegal repertoire is the vibrant diversity of tune styles. Beyond the run of the mill jig and reel, you get the strathspeys, highlands, mazurkas, marches, barn dances, polkas… And as you’ve already discovered, some of the “common” tunes are quite a bit different in the Donegal repertoire. There’s tunes like Merrily Kiss the Quaker that are barely recognizable because they’ve got so much more of the original Scottish melody. There’s the Con Cassidy versions of Rakish Paddy and Drowsey Maggie that are real gems…

One could make a case that other styles like Sligo etc. are the more “pure” Irish styles. The people who stayed in other regions don’t seem to have traveled around much beyond their own county borders; the people who left brought their style with them in fairly pristine condition. The Donegal folk traveled a couple times a year following the work. Off to Scotland to work the land, off to sea to fish, wherever, and they brought the influences of those other places back home with them. Personally, having played in all 32 counties, although each had something distinctive to recommend itself and to love, I still find the Donegal music the most engaging. It just gets inside of you and doesn’t let go…

Re: Searching for peers

Buddy MacMaster would describe the music as “scottish”, as in “I think this sounds more Scottish”. As opposed to the Irish influences in Cape Breton, I suppose. The “scottish” was definitely what he was after, but I don’t know whether that applies to other Cape Breton musicians too. There are lots of influences over there, and I guess you have to choose where you want to align yourself (as do all of us, in these days of widespread music, I guess).
