Re: Have you ever “barred” somone from a session for bad playing?

The Internet has its trolls, the ones who steer a thread off-topic, etc., and yeah, sessions can have their noodlers, too. Some websites ban trolls, while others either ignore them or tolerate them. Musicians know the score and are well aware of noodlers. And audiences are well aware of noodlers, too. Yet how to handle that situation becomes a judgement call. For instance, other than music, do you otherwise like the person who noodles? Maybe that person has the potential to improve once they discover that they are not playing music with the rest of the group? But whatever, unless your group could be up for some sort of competitive or formal performance then having a noodler in your group will not hurt the reputation of the good players. Another thing to consider is that noodlers often have a short career as they often quickly burn out.

Re: Have you ever “barred” somone from a session for bad playing?

Only in the US have I been in a session from which a couple of players were asked not to return. They had been playing for a couple of years and still didn’t cop to the music. It was apparent that they didn’t practice -- or know how to practice. It was with relief and some moral conflict when we heard that they were asked to stay away. Most of us felt sorry for them though at the same time the music was much better without them.
In Ireland the situation is handled with a mix of rudeness and ridicule. You want to be kind to everybody but at the same time you want the music to be good. Nice people are not always good players. It’s easier to ask a player not to noodle between tunes than it is to tell a flute-player that he is out of tune. So you say, “Please play your high A, hard. I think I am out of tune,” rather than “You’re out of tune!”
It’s much easier to tell somebody, both to say and to hear , “I can’t play that fast. If you slow down I can play with you,” than to say “You’re playing too fast and you’re out of control.”
Contrary to what we’ve often been told, it is not the case that there is a solution to every problem.

Re: Have you ever “barred” somone from a session for bad playing?

OK, so he is persistent, but that is not a bad quality, just needs improvement. This might be a good time for a face-to-face confrontation. Have one of the really good players play the opening phrases of a popular tune and then ask your noodler to do the same. That puts a fine example up for the noodler to match. Tell the noodler that this is what is expected and that he will be expected to have made improvement on the next occasion. But do not ask for too much, too soon. Any bit of future improvement is a step in the right direction. And if the noodler has been at this for many years, dedicated, then he might just surprise you the next time around.

After all, even a noodler could become a musician if they get just a bit of community support.

I think Ailin’s got it just about right. His approach is almost a ‘session’ skill in its own right :)

No, I’ve never been around in a situation where someone has had to be ‘barred’ for musicality crimes. It’s always tended to be the ‘cold shoulder’ approach from others, or some other device (when polite and subtle hints are not enough).

It’s an awkward call at times, isn’t it? By definition, a session is simply an event where musicians meet to play music and have fun. That’s all. There’s no audition, there’s no ‘minimum standard’. So, it’s hardly surprising that people who can’t play anything properly turn up, with the best of intentions, to what looks like a friendly gathering, then let rip and offend their musical neighbours, and usually they are oblivious to this.

If it’s just the odd one, and you think it might just be a one-off, then moving away for the offending noise is usually OK. However, if it’s a rimshotting bodhran, a sharpie whistler, a piccolo, a bass-heavy piano accordion or a hung-hand banjo player, then it’s a bit more difficult.

Even more so if they turn up time after time.

If that’s the case, I usually just give the session a miss for a while, and come back later to see if things have improved.

Just by way of balance - I’ve attended what I believe to be the top sessions in parts of Ireland. Beautiful music, so well played.

I only listen. I never join in. What could I add?

Re: Have you ever “barred” somone from a session for bad playing?

In a way, but ‘barred’ is too strong a word, since we would have no right to bar a person from bringing his/her instrument to an open session at a public pub. In the case of our session, this problem usually relates to players who won’t bother to learn the tunes. If the playing goes for Gravel Walks, for example, what will happen when the naive backer gets to the key change in the last part? It’s not pretty, and the music suffers.

The most honorable way IMO is to personally confront the person one-on-one and explain that the music is suffering due to their lack of knowledge of the tunes. It can be helpful to point out that most examples of session etiquette policies explicitly discourage playing when one doesn’t know the tune. Appeal to their sense of aesthetics, tradition, and openness to criticism meant to help improvement - constructive criticism.

Another relatively non-confrontational method, on a personal level anyway, is to loudly point out, during the playing of the tune, that ‘THIS IS IN D’, thus helping the accompanist correct since they are obviously not playing the correct cords. This has the added benefit of ‘shaming’ the player in a modest way, which might discourage a normal person from continuing in that vein. However, guitar, bodhran, bones, shaky eggs, and really any other players at all, may not be behave as normal people.

Good luck. Some of our most enjoyable sessions have been ‘home sessions’, where we can invite whomever we wish to play music with - might be a good option for some.

In the end, you have to do the cost-benefit of permitting one asshole to stay vs how many good people are we going to lose.
