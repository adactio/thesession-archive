Re: About those chords you found online :-/

I started out in my naive youth trying to dope out the chords by reading the sheet music and figuring out what each block of notes “must” be, chordally. Worked great for a couple of tunes. Worked lousy for most of them.
At some point, I started noticing that if I listened to where the tune was going, it was often obvious to me how it was moving, although I often had trouble finding the starting point. I would hear minor chord to VII chord, but I had to hunt for the root. Same thing with major tunes, I could hear that the tune was going to the IV chord, but I didn’t always hear what the root was, so I’d be at sea. I was able to convince some people to give me a boost and call out the home key when they changed tunes, and that helped a lot. Got better at hearing the home key, but it doesn’t hurt to have someone call it out anyway- some of the tunes are pretty ambiguous, and it’s nice to be able to know where we’re going next. Unfortunately, I can’t really talk while I’m playing the box, so I can’t give others that help, but maybe that’ll come next.

One thing that I’ve found, and this might be just my own peculiarity, is that knowing the tune doesn’t help me to hear the changes. In fact, a lot of times if I know the tune well enough to play it, that gets in the way of playing an accompaniment. This might be because when I’m listening for the changes, I’m not thinking about the notes, I’m thinking about the overall movement of the tune. Knowing the tune, I’m thinking about it at the wrong level, so to speak. Sometimes, though, that leads me into a fun bouzouki style, more Alec Finn, more single notes than fully-specified chords, and closer to the melodic movement than to the harmonic movement.
Depends on what you want to hear, I guess.

Re: About those chords you found online :-/

There are a few tunes where there is one set of chords associated with that tune and that will be what most people play. This could be because the tune was written by a box player, like Phil Cunningham. Most people would find it disconcerting hearing “The Pearl” accompanied by anything other than Phil’s chords.
There are a large number of tunes written with a chord sequence (or progression) “in mind”. Often that chord sequence is fairly simple and, therefore fairly, simple to hear. There are many “standard” ways of “spicing up” basic chord sequences by using chord substitutions and extensions. As a result, different accompanists will use different chord sequences on the same tune (even when there is an obvious simple sequence.
Then there is another large body of tunes which were not written with a chord sequence in mind but were written purely melodically. You can have huge arguments about what chords to use for those tunes.
When I am presented with an unfamiliar tune I may stick to pretty basic fare. I can usually find the tonal centre by playing a couple of notes on the guitar and can sense the mode. I’ll start simply and then try variations. I would never ask what the chords were.

Re: About those chords you found online :-/

I find that the idea that you progress from simple to more complex as you repeat the tune each time is helpful for a beginning guitar player….

Usually the first time round I suss out the key and try for the usual 1, 4, 5 routine - or if it’s sounding modal or minor I’ll do 1 & 7. (I’m a fiddle player and if I really can’t figure out the key I wait until the end of the A part and see what note the fiddlers are ending on - that is the key - harder for me with whistle players!).

If the tune holds up (meaning it doesn’t go minor in the B part, go the 4 chord or even change key!) then the next time round I might do some simple minor or major substitutions for the chords to see if it gives the right color. Then the third time round, I’ll get even more experimental - luckily I play my guitar at a slow session and wrong notes are tolerated more than in a regular session! It’s alot of experimentation……

and there’s things like walk ups and downs in various keys (e minor, g, d, etc) you can develop before hand and then try them out on the fly when you get to say a reel in e minor….

just always land back at the 1 so whatever nonsense you’re trying resolves itself….

And play in DADGAD - much easier to try non-offensive one finger chords until you can figure out how the wind is blowing….

All of the above Dave, here’s how it happened for me, (it’s turned into a bit of a novella, so sorry for that but then this has been done to death so a bit more drivel want clog the mustard board anymore than it already is);

When I first started actually thinking hard about backing ITM/STM I was already playing guitar and a little mandolin so not a complete beginner. I understood keys and the basic chords you could use within them, didn’t have them all at my finger tips but I had a certain amount of understanding. I was inspired by acoustic music and the local session scene, which was very random but very good, was always ticking over in the back ground. So as a young guy I found myself “sat in” on occasion, with people who knew what they were about, usually frantically looking for chords to fit what was being played.

Being young I was keen to make all the usual mistakes. Luckily I was usually sat in with understanding people who would offer up a bit of advice, often in the form of constructive criticism which occasionally I would take on the nose, but I always made a mental note.

I know I learned a fair bit back then, “in that bit of a scarce of tatties, try an E major”, “wow, thanks”. Or “It’s a stock progression over that bit, I ii iii IV V I”. “cool”. And “this tune is just two chords really, i VII, but you can play a Vm there etc”.

Also my accompaniment hero back then was a nice understated local man called Alistair. He played standard offered no advice but offered lots of encouragement. The thing that caught my attention about his playing was that 1. he didn’t seem to need to be told the tune coming next as he got by with a key call and would suss it out from there. 2. he played all over the neck 3. he had good dynamic control and always seemed to be just at the right place in the mix. 4. because of my basic understanding of chords etc I could follow some of the stuff he was doing so I was able to make more mental notes to take away with me.

Then I met a guy at a session in the greentree in edinburgh, playing guitar and he was jaw droppingly good. I asked him how he knew all the chords to those tunes. His answer both shocked me and yet confirmed what I’d started to realise from watching and listening to Alistair. His answer was of course, that he didn’t know any of them. I instantly knew what he meant, although I was still a long way off nailing a workable method at that time.

My big break through came from an electric guitar playing pal who called in to visit just to tell me about this cool chord he’d found that may be of interest to me. He called it “the Dougie MacLean Chord” and that big modal A chord, played at the 5th fret and involved muting the odd string changed my approach for evermore.

Then I played folk rock and pop, my big passion was Bluegrass picking, Doc Tony Russ Clarence Norman etc all heros. But then my 5string buddy moved away, the acoustic band I’d been getting a tune with and playing a few youthful gigs with, wanted to go electric so I stopped playing with them.

Next I got in with another banjo player and a fantastic mandolin player both older and wiser than I. The plan was to practice a bit and then go out doing gigs. A ITM/STM/BG mix, I was onboard for my bluegrass twang and singing not my trad backing. So for the first time I was playing arranged sets and having to learn progressions dictated by others. This band didn’t workout (I got a good job, the mandolin player didn’t want to play for money and that was that) but I took a lot out of it. From Dave the banjo guitar bass player, I learned a bit of discipline. From Duncan I just got completely inspired. A truly amazing mandolin player who could play exciting and up to tempo BG, improvise fantastic breaks and play infectious back beat woody chops that just gave the over all sound a dynamism that made me really want to get better. But more importantly a fantastic trad mando player who’d been around when I first started going out and now here I was, older wiser and able to make a far better job of accompanying, so I started to get both more able and enthusiastic about backing trad.

The job meant that I couldn’t commit to a regular thing so I focused on doing my singer song writer phase thing and attending sessions where I could. Then my fiddle playing pal returned to the north from hothousing ITM in london and we started dueting for cash and craic. Armed with my basic skills and “the dougie maclean chord” I then really began to workout ways round the tunes and develop. Playing 26 hours a day and 12 nights a week helped.

I didn’t want to know the chords to any tune, I wanted to be unassuming like Alistair, sound like the cool guy from edinburgh, stay together and disciplined like Dave, and address the tunes with the enthusiasm of Duncan. I then met a guy called Ian at a musical party. Ian could play (very well) so I was well aware that I’d have to be on best behavior in the twang department. He caught me off guard by asking me how I got round a particular tune so I showed him adding that it was a fluke and I was playing by the seat of my pants. His reply was the best single pice of advice I’ve ever been given. “Seat of the pants IS the only way to play, because your playing in the moment”.

Much water has passed under the bridge since then and I don’t play the guitar as much, mainly because I don’t have a regular tune I can attend with a guitar slot going. I don’t want to keep time for people I want to play. So for now it’s a back burner thing until I get a regular tune sorted. Anyway I’m enjoying learning tunes at the moment and if needed, I’ve still got the dougie maclean chord mind should things get serious.
