Re: fiddle--left hand

In the classical hold, the neck doesn’t drop into the base of the V between thumb and finger. It sits on the bump where the finger joins the hand, and the thumb contacts opposite the position of the E on the D string, or a little higher in some schools. It feels counter intuitive initially and a lot less grounded, but in the long run is a lot more capable when you start dancing around the fingerboard.

Re: fiddle--left hand

In answer to the original question: “Were you taught and/or do you continue to keep your thumb at the nut while playing?”

The answer is a qualified yes. If you are only playing in first position then the thumb stays in the same place the whole time - it provides a valuable reference point from which to gauge the positions of the fingers. But once you start playing in higher positions your thumb will have to move, and it has to move in very specific ways, either before or after the rest of the hand changes position, so that you can use it to measure how far your hand moves to get to exactly the right place.

Re: fiddle--left hand

Carole H, having small hands probably makes it even more important to keep your thumb in one place. You should be able to rotate your hand to stretch for the high notes without having to move your whole hand up the neck. By keeping your thumb anchored you always have a reference point and will be able to switch instantly between stretching and not stretching, with accurate intonation. If you move your whole hand about you are likely to have problems playing in tune.

Re: fiddle--left hand

Ah, Carol, we cross posted. Really good question! Whether or not you ever shift on the fiddle, a relaxed and balanced hand position is important to support the violin and at the same time move with ease, right there in first position. It’s really not about going all “classical” or staying “fiddle”. It’s about hand mechanics and doing any of this injury free, within the first weeks and over time. I have helped a great many students - all ages - with hand strain, shoulder issues, so on from doing all the usual things “wrong” that simply cause more problems than they might seem to. Classical folks and fiddle. All the same. A wrist is a wrist is a wrist. Same with the other relevant body parts. ;)

Specifically to answer your thumb question, it does not need to stay at the nut, and I don’t teach placing it there anyway as it closes the space between the thumb and the rest of the hand under the neck of the violin, which should be open and relaxed. Someone came up with the phrase “make sure you have a mousehole” years ago in teacher speak for kids, but if you look at your hand when it is relaxed, that shape should be what is under the neck of the violin. And there is natural space between your thumb and hand. This is why your thumb will rest more opposite the first finger then anywhere else, when the hand is placed in that same “natural” position under the neck, resting a bit on the “shelf” of your index finger. (Wrist straight, both front to back AND sideways!) The thumb really should not be “stuck” anywhere; it will at times during playing reach back towards the nut. Not a problem, but making it stay anywhere asks for the hand to grip, which you do not want to teach it to do.

In my studio anyway, this is an important lesson when I am working on the left hand and takes more than an entry here, but the point is that it is not about having to shift anywhere, but healthy hand mechanics right where you are. :)

You are going to see all sorts of fiddle holding positions. (And bow!) Mostly from self taught folks just doing the best they can. Or with limited instruction from someone else with limited technical training. It is what it is. Teachers can only teach what they know. If they don’t really know much about how it all fits together, they can’t share that. I didn’t, even after a few decades of both training and then performing, which included conservatory training, “famous” teachers, yada yada. One of “them” now long gone, actually tried to change my left hand technique so drastically because he recently “discovered” some better way and caused me to suffer extreme tendon inflammation for a long time until I quit using him. (So did many others.)

Exploring all this stuff years later, I learned some valuable things from others who knew so much more than I, teachers who understood biomechanics and even a few orthopedic surgeons who were happy to explain what the heck was going on inside that hand. I had to know. This was all pre internet. Anyway… yes it matters. lol

Good for you, undertaking both the instrument and the music. Both are are a wonderful adventure. :)

Re: fiddle--left hand

Interestingly enough, the collapsed wrist is a natural feature for many learners, even when there are no physical problems - they try to support the weight of the violin with the heel of the hand, instead of supporting it between the thumb and first finger. It’s a natural reflex in the absence of the knowledge of good form.

The collapsed wrist hold has no advantages, and quite a few disadvantages. Firstly, it limits the reach of the fingers, for obvious reasons. Secondly, it inhibits shifting to a different position. Some people can change position of course, but it means un-collapsing the wrist to do so to avoid the heel of the hand pushing the fiddle into your throat :) No, it doesn’t stop you from playing the notes in first position (and people with large hands can still reach some higher notes), but it is still very limiting in the greater world of fiddling.

As Diane mentioned above, many good players sometimes find ways round these issues, but it’s really about best practise in the broader sense. In the same way that you can still hit a ball with a golf club while holding the club with both hands, halfway down the club shaft, you’d find it difficult to make an efficient swing. You can be a boxer with a devastatingly powerful punch, but if you constantly stay in too close, you’ll just get hit all the time - and your own punch will have little power. But, you’re still a boxer.

Kind of stating the obvious, really :)

Re: fiddle--left hand

Carole,
First, don’t worry about what the classical folks say about the “collapsed wrist.” That’s the training they grew up with, probably since they were 5 or 6-ish, when their muscles and ligaments were still developing. The fact is, you don’t have to play with a “collapsed wrist” all the time - and you certainly don’t want to hold the neck in your left hand (although I’ve seen some Cape Breton fiddlers do just that, and they sounded fine.)

But you also don’t want to be so inflexible that you can’t narrow your wrist sometimes. Why? So, for example, you can play a cut, or grace note, by brushing your finger across the string rather than hammering it down and up very quickly, which you’ll tend to do with what I’ll call a “classical” wrist. Watch some Irish fiddlers closely and see how they do their ornaments - brushing across strings tends to give a softer result.

Second, yes, expect to feel awkward. Try some stretching before you start. It takes a while to get comfortable. But once you get there, it feels very comfortable. The main thing to avoid is squeezing, whether it’s your left hand, right hand or arm, or even neck and shoulders. There’s a tendency to tighten up when you hear something you don’t like, like the squeal of a violin. Try to relax when you feel any tightness, then focus back to what you were working on.

And take special care not to squeeze the stick or grind the left finders into the neck. You’ll be fine.

Ergo, you appear to be implying that cuts are easier to play using a collapsed, or partly collapsed wrist. Sorry, but that is simply totally wrong! Cuts (as you correctly say) are executed in a lateral direction. They have similar mechanics to left-hand pizzicato, where a fingered note is ‘plucked’ laterally - except that with a cut (whether on its own, or embedded in a roll) there is often just a non-aggressive brushing action, as the finger comes down on the string and off the fingerboard. As you well know, there are some Irish players who actually do sound cuts aggressively, and sound the notes, although it is primarily a rhythmic device.

As for “hammering it down and up very quickly”, that is a “trill”, and a totally different technique altogether, and frankly I’m surprised that you appear to confuse the two. Some fiddlers play cuts and some fiddlers play trills. Some fiddlers play both. It’s got absolutely nothing to do with having a normal playing wrist posture (which you call “classical”), and which I call “correct” …

I would really stay away from using words like ‘correct’ when describing something that has so many obvious and legitimate counter-examples.

There are plenty of children who are brought by their parents to take violin lessons, and spend countless hours doing exercises while concentrating on posture and the position of their hands, trying to satisfy some model of correctness that their teacher insists on. The vast majority of them give up the instrument, and many come away scarred by the experience. It seems more sensible to focus on the music, and not worry so much about things that hardly matter. For Irish trad, a bent wrist is just fine is quite comfortable, and can give some early stability that gives you easier access to the music. Learning the music is ultimately what most people want. Cuts, rolls, slides work just fine with a bent wrist. You do not need vibrato or higher positions for Irish music.

There is nothing wrong with learning the classical hold, but I would not spend too much time worrying about it, and if it doesn’t work for you, don’t let it keep you from enjoying the music.
