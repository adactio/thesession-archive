Re: modal tunes

Just to go into your “modal chord” thing for a minute, Christy…

The “chord” with just 2 notes in it, like A and E, is often known as a “power chord”. A standard chord (triad, technically) is made up of three notes: the root, third and perfect fifth. The third can be either major or minor, which dictates whether the overall chord is major or minor.

If you miss out the third entirely so you are just have the root and fifth (as per your “modal A chord”) there is no bias towards either major or minor. In other words, you can get away with playing a power chord in many more contexts, even though it has less flavour to it.

Follow Ben’s link for church modes to get the rest of your answer.

“would ‘Chief o Neill’s favourite’ be an example of d Aeolian , it sounds like its in d major but goes to an f natural in the 2nd part?”

Not really. It’s an example of how many tunes in the Irish tradition *don’t* fit comfortably into one mode or another. It has C#s, C-naturals, F#s and F naturals all in the same tune. So you could say that it drifts between D major (Ionian), D Mixolydian and D Dorian.

…but not Aeolian :-) - that would require a B-flat.

Re: modal tunes

another way to look at it- a C major scale (CDEFGABC) has a half step between the E and F, and the B and C. It’s most easily seen on a piano keyboard. So, any major scale has a half step between the 3rd and 4th steps, and the 7th and 8th steps.

Minor scales and modal scales have their half steps in different places. Once you learn where they are, you can play any minor scale and any modal scale.

If you know the ‘formula’ for your major scale in terms of the intervals then it is fairly easy to work out the modes and they’re order. E.g. Re: modal tunes

I think it can be counterproductive to try to shoehorn Irish diddley music into these very strict definitions. What’s the difference between a tune changing mode and a tune having an accidental? There probably is a technical difference, but it won’t help you play and understand the tune. Also, the whole concept of modes requires a fixed root, which many diddley don’t have. This can be a problem when strummers come into it. Using parallel harmony always implies a resolve and this totaly ruins the linear harmony of tunes that don’t resolve.

For example, a where a tune that you might think is in G, but finishes on a D (common in diddley music) is strummed to, the srummer has two options, to finnish with the G chord so the tune sounds like it finnishes on the fifth, or to finnish on the D chord where the Fsharp in it wants to resolve to the G. Both sound wrong.

Christy - whereas its certainly possible to work out chords by ear without knowing the keynote or the mode, if you know these things it helps, as this limits the number of suitable chords from which to chose. Try this approach:

1) Work out the note on which the tune resolves (not necessarily the note on which the tune ends, as benhall.1 quite rightly says).

2) Next, work out the mode by looking at the key signature. e.g. if the resolution note was A, and the key signature contained
two sharps, the tune would be A-Mix.

3) Set your chords with reference to a list suitable for that particular key and mode.

For more help on this, I have a page on my website devoted to this very topic:

http://www.intermix.freeuk.com/modes_and_scales.htm

Knowing this stuff, (although certainly not essential), becomes more important when setting chords. It’s also sometimes useful when deciding which tunes go together in sets.

Music (including ITM) is a subject that sits between art and science. In the case of ITM, the artistic element is by far the most important. But that doesn’t mean that you can’t learn anything from from also looking at it in a theoretical or “scientific” way.

Ragaman - the issue of chords for modal tunes arises because you get get guitarists at sessions.

Any given chord can be:

(a) dischordant.
(b) musically correct, but out of keeping with the mode.
(c) musically correct, and in keeping with the mode.

It’s rare to get instances of (a), but instances of (b) are all too common.

Guitarists that understand what is “in keeping” are more often than not melody players as well.

No doubt the G# was originally added as an embellishment and was not part of the tune in its oldest form. But it’s exactly the kind of thing I was talking about a few posts up from here (although I didn’t articulate it particularly well - read ‘C#/C-natural’ as any semitone-pair from which modal ambiguity might arise). There *are* tunes which fit perfectly into a particular mode, or can be played in such a manner that they do. But there are also many that don’t.

You can force established terms on a tune like this and call it ‘Mixolydian/Ionian’ if you like, but that only proves that the terms are inadequate for the purpose. Why can we have a mode/scale that sometimes has a G# and sometimes a G natural? There’s rhyme and reason to when which is used - it’s not just random. We could call it the Bundorian mode.

“”Synagogue modes“ ??!?”

The modes traditionally used for the Jewish liturgy - and which are found in many Klezmer tunes.

In the modern Ashkenazi synagogue service, owing to the high degree of assimilation anong German Jewry, with the exception of specific functions such as the reading of the Torah and the Prophets, the old modes have been largely replaced with more ‘Western’ sounding melodies.
