Re: Musicians: what’s the best way to get gigs?

There are different ways, and you should try them all if you can.

1) Go to the places where you’d be likely to play and ask who books the music. You’ll be pointed to someone, or told that he/she is only in on Wed and Fri, or given a phone number and a name, and go from there.

2) If you’re starting out, don’t be afraid to offer some stuff for free -- for charities, nursing homes, etc.

--- point is, you may have to put a few miles on your car, but it’s better to do it in person than via email.

That said, you can also

3) Look up agents, folk clubs or whatever else you can find and send emails, and maybe attach an mp3 or two.

4) Be creative in coming up with ideas. Check out the local library or church or town fair -- anything to get your face out in public.

5) Print up some “business” cards to give to folks who ask about your music/band -- they can be future employers. You can print them on your own computer -- no need to spend big on pro printed cards.

----------

Don’t take it personally when you face rejection -- the business of music is a lot different than the music itself. Do what you need to do -- got a PA system you can set up? -- and just keep plowing ahead.

_______

I’m sure I haven’t covered everything -- anyone else please chime in.

depends what sort of gigs - cabaret? concert? ceili? They’re not all the same thing, demanding different ranges of resources and skills (apart from that it’s a given that for all of them that you need to be able to play your repetoire very well).

Re: Musicians: what’s the best way to get gigs?

First, look at what you play, and want to play. There may be some adjustments required. Our little band started with tunes, but soon realized that what folks wanted was more songs, with a healthy number of the old ‘stick to your ribs’ favorites. And while a big group is fun, you want to limit the number of folks in your group--there is a ceiling on what pay you can expect, and with a large group it quickly becomes impossible to get reasonable compensation for everyone.
Then look at venues, start dropping by and leaving a business card, or a CD with a few cuts of your best efforts on it. Around here, the number of venues with live music has dropped drastically because of the economy, and as yet, shows little sign of improvement. If your band members participate in sessions, bring business cards, as a lot of people approach the musicians if they like what they hear. That can get a bit cutthroat, though, if you have representatives from different bands at the same session! (And I have seen that happen.)
In our area (NE USA), especially in March, libraries and nursing homes are on the lookout for folks who can put on a show of Irish music--we sent a form letter out at one point that brought us a number of gigs, and some repeat calls in following years.
But most of all, you get gigs by playing well, and word of mouth from those who heard you play well. And always have business cards handy, so people who are interested will know how to reach you. And if you are lucky enough to get a recurring gig, keep bringing out new material so you stay fresh, and the gig keeps recurring.
Best of luck.

Re: Musicians: what’s the best way to get gigs?

You need to decide what you want to do:

Pure drop traditional to appreciative and respectful audiences?
Bar band playing the tried and true, maybe with a bass?
Corporate-middle of the road gigs?
Weddings?
Ceilis?
Good karma gigs at care homes and the like?
Busk at markets?
Festivals?

Knowing that will allow you to figure out the repertoire, what instruments/voices you need, what the market will pay for, and thus how many members in the group, what each band member needs to bring in terms of time, chops, maturity. It will also tell you whether you are enjoying participation in a loss-making hobby, or running a business, and thus how much you can realistically invest in sound gear, website, promotion, rehearsal, travel, time off work/away from family etc.

You need a name that people can spell and remember, and business cards on card stock. Ideally you should have miniCDs, or proper CDs.

You need a well-designed website with photos, sound clips, you-tube links, examples of gigs you have played to show what can do and have done.

Be reliable. Find out what the venue/person hiring you wants, make sure you can deliver it, recommend someone else if you can’t. Show up early, start on time, be respectful, courteous, adaptable, neat, and remember who is paying you. Thank people.

Watch the audience. Do more of what they like/respond to, less of what they don’t like/respond to.

Have a good time. If you are having a good time,and engage with the audience, the audience is more likely to have a good time, buy drinks, support the establishment, and everyone is happy. Work on your patter, s0me stories and jokes for those moments when someone is replacing a broken string, can’t find their pick, the bride is late.

By itself, playing playing well isn’t sufficient to get gigs. It’s surprising how many talented people get dropped from bands because they are difficult, unreliable, can’t organize a trip to the bathroom….You can be steadily employed as a band that plays merely competently, if you run your business professionally.

The personal traits of bands matter at least as much as their talent. At least one member of the band needs to be checking Craig’s List every day, touring appropriate venues, trying to drum up business. Someone in the band should be able to set up and run the sound, or to own sound gear, or the vehicle….It’s necessary for someone to be a natural organizer, who prepares set lists, confirms travel plans, makes sure everyone can get there, sends out maps to the venue etc.

Both bands I am in started small, taking any gigs they could get, playing for free, and put a year into rehearsals to build up repertoire and skill levels, and continue to practice and learn new stuff. Both bands have got more gigs now that there is more emphasis on songs than tunes, even though we get wedding and cdili where we just play tunes.

Hugh

Re: Musicians: what’s the best way to get gigs?

Someone gave me sage advice many years ago (actually from another field, but applicable to any)

“Always undercharge and overdeliver.”

I’ve always followed this. I keep my prices a bit below what most are charging, and I do my very best to deliver the best performance I can. I show up early, work very hard at being prepared, am well dressed and groomed, am polite and friendly to everyone, and cheerily do what the client asks me to do. Often I’m the first to arrive and the last to leave. To me, it’s what ‘being professional’ means.

But I’ll readily admit that the biggest number of gigs don’t go to people like me, or to the best players, but to people who are good at self-promotion, the ‘hucksters’ as I might call them. These people are sometimes terrible players, and spend most of their time marketing themselves rather than practicing. Often it’s more about the look than the playing, and many of these people are attractive people who dress in showy ways and do a lot of ‘emoting’ on stage. To be fair, many of the people who do a lot of self-marketing are decent players, and some are fine players.

I’ve never been interesting in the marketing side. I’d rather sit at home than spend my days huckstering.
