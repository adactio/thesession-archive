“Has anyone attempted to teach people how to learn more tunes? Or even investigated what’s involved in being able to remember the things in the first place?”

Two quite distinct topics are touched on here:

1) strategies of picking up tunes by ear

2) keeping tunes, once learnt, in long-term memory

About the second topic I have no idea, I’m no neurologist.

About the first, it surprises me a bit that this aspect of ITM seems to be rarely taught or discussed. People just say “do a load of listening” which addresses a third topic: the choice of material one should be exposed to, and in what modality (aural v visual v kinesthetic, the stated or implied preference usually being for aural ).

There are teachable strategies that help the beginner pick up new tunes by ear. It’s all about recognising patterns; you teach the newbie patterns and teach them how to listen for them in new tunes they’re exposed to.

Re: Tune memorization workshop

I run a weekly tune learning workshop, so I spend a lot of time helping people learn how to learn. And there are a few things that I have picked up over the years of doing this.

To begin with, the more tunes you have learned, the easier it becomes to learn tunes. To some people, that seems contradictory, if you think of your brain like a hard drive with limited storage. But thankfully, that’s not how our brains work. We are very good at pattern recognition, and categorizing patterns in our brains. People like to point out that there are a lot of common phrases that are a part of this music, which can be helpful (but also a hindrance. When there are tunes that share phrases too much, then we can get confused).

Another thing that I preach is that remembering tunes is a practicable skill. If we didn’t grow up playing music, then we typically tended toward being passive listeners, letting the music wash over us. Maybe a song’s lyrics caught our ear, and we went out of our way to learn all the words, but typically people don’t put that kind of attention into learning the melody. But you can certainly focus on the melody as easily as you focused on the words of a song. And you can actually practice listening. Like lilting along with a tune that you’re listening to, to see how well you’ve learned the melody. And once you’re well versed in this music, you can actually lilt along to tunes that you’ve never heard - you won’t get it all right, of course, but you’d be surprised how much you can guess right, and then the next time through the tune, you can remember where you were wrong, and maybe get it right. This is how people can “pick up tunes on the fly” in a session.

One good piece of information about memory that sticks with me is that “the ideal time to remember how a tune goes is right before you forget it”. Which sounds like a platitude, but it is a real thing. The thing that strengthens our neural pathways for remembering something is the very act of recalling the information. You could learn a tune and play it for two hours straight, but you’re not having to do the work of recalling it from long term memory when you’re doing that. So you might not be able to recall it at all a day later. But if you learn it, and then put it aside. And then recall it before forgetting it, (say, an hour later) then you’ve actually helped strengthen the pathways, making it easier to recall later. If you continue that process of recalling it, then giving it a rest, and recalling it again later, it will eventually become entrenched (and the amount of time it takes to get to the point where you’re “about to forget it” each time will get longer and longer…)

And finally, I talk with people about the difference between “memorizing” a tune and “internalizing” it. To me, memorization harks back to remembering the first 20 Presidents of the U.S., or memorizing a multiplication table in grade school. I think of memorization as being like a brute force cramming of information into your memory. And when people first start learning tunes by ear, they inevitably try to do just that. But it’s hard. There’s a reason that phone numbers have parenthesis and spaces and dashes, and that’s because we have a hard time remembering 10 numbers in a row, but if we chunk it up into smaller bits, we can remember it more easily. But try remembering 128 letters in sequence, and that’s what trying to “memorize” a tune is like. It’s overwhelming. Instead, I work with people on thinking of tunes in phrases instead of sequence of notes, and internalizing the contour of the melody. With practice, you can go from having to remember 128 things in sequence to only having to remember one thing, and that is “how the tune goes”. ;-)

So I don’t have a very good idea of how many tunes I know. My tunebook on thesession currently has 375 tunes in it, and when I actually sit down and learn a tune, I usually try to add it to my tunebook. But over the years, I have learned so many tunes that I don’t know where I got them, and I don’t know what they’re called, and sometimes I don’t even know whether I’ve ever played it before (speaking of “distraction” and “disability”). So there are different levels of “knowing”, and my guess is that my tune repertoire is probably somewhere about double of what’s in my tunebook…

Re: Tune memorization workshop

“Memorization may be a part of the process but learning is so much more.”

That’s a good one, Ross!

On a related note, I think is important to stress that learning a tune is a process, not an event. Real internalization of the tune comes from not just knowing the notes, but having played it in differing circumstances, and exploring different things you can do with it. You can get some of that by listening to (or playing along with) different recordings to see how other players are playing it. But the real deep familiarity comes from having played it to the point that you can start putting your own expression into the phrasing.

Re: Tune memorization workshop

I use SRS software for language study. You can probably adapt ANKI to this problem if you wish. The idea is recognition and review. First you acquire the information. . .i.e. ‘learn the tune’, then you review it at spaced intervals. ANKI allows you to review information at successively longer and longer intervals, moving the information from short term memory to longer term memory. ANKI flashcards can embed multimedia. . .you could have a card for each tune. Look at the card, it names a tune, flip the card, it plays the tune. If you remember the tune well and can play along with it, return it to the ‘card deck’ for a longer period of time before review. While learning a tune, every few minutes. While reviewing a tune, successively longer periods, one hour, one day, one week, etc. This is what most people do anyway, but something like ANKI can lend orgaization and tracking to your pool of tunes. Space Repetition Software is a very powerful memory tool.

This has puzzked me as well. I am 61 years old. I memorized 95 tunes up to this point in my life, and these are tunes that I actually like to play on the whistle; other memorized tunes don’t make it to into the inner sanctum.

Here are my conclusions , at least as they apply to me:

1. When I fall in love with a tune, I make a concerted effort to memorize it to make it a part of my life.

2. Tunes with lyrics are easier to memorize.

3. It was easier to memorize tunes when I was younger.

4. Not every tune that made the cut is Celtic.

5. If I can sing or hum a tune, it is not difficult to teach myself to play it on my most forgiving whistle.

6. Some ironclad memorized tunes entered my brain differently from the original version or I processed it differently. I either basterdized it or I am part if the evolution of tunes. I have repaired some tunes or hybridized them, albeit close to the “original” version.

7. Ultimately, listening to a recording of a fetching tune and VOCALLY following along imprints the tune in my brains. Simpler the recording, the easier it is.

Re: Tune memorization workshop

The dots/incipit/mp3 issue has more to do with the size of the Anki deck(s), and whether you want to use Ankiweb (if you want to access your repertoire from any computer) or a phone app (I’m not sure which formats it can handle, and if there’s a difference between Android and its counterparts).

Personal thoughts: as with languages (and its words, grammar structures, phrases etc.) you reach the stage where the software/structured repetition isn’t “necessary” anymore - when you know enough, you “review” it along the way, when you use the language, read etc.

In regards to tunes, those with a small repertoire need many repetitions per tune as well as many repetitions in general in order to develop a feeling for the recurring patterns. They might also feel that they “need” a list of the tunes. Those with a big repertoire (say, Kevin above) have stored the tunes in their long-term memory, and developed a way to activate the tunes when needed. For instance, if they attend sessions on a regular basis and/or listen to new recordings, this kind of “review” takes care of itself.
