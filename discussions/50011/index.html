Re: Why are there so few backers in Irish session?

Here are my thoughts on this backing topic, which has popped up in so many threads lately.

How did backing arise in trad music? It’s a dance thing. This is dance music, and if you’ve ever played for a large, loud dance crowd you’ll understand why a backbeat is important to keep the music and the dancers (who are not professionals) on the same page. The same reason why céilí bands arose when dances were pushed into parish halls. For a small, ‘Round the house and mind the dresser’ type of dance? One fiddle should do fine, nothing else needed.

Why do backing instruments appear on trad recordings and performances, from the 1920’s to the present? Presumably because more people enjoy hearing a continuo behind a melody, providing more color to the music and enhancing the salability of the record or event.

To state the obvious, sessions are not dances or commercial performances, so ideally no backing should be needed. But I’ve played in extremely loud session environments where having a good backing really helped keep things together and was missed when absent. Some here have stated that they absolutely disdain and ignore backing instruments of any kind in a session, and fair play to them-- but others playing melody (including me) enjoy the extra color and drive good backing can provide to the group sound.

I found an interesting blog on McNeela’s site that deals with piano and guitar backing for Irish trad -- here is the link https://blog.mcneelamusic.com/irish-music-accompaniment-how-to-accompany-traditional-irish-music/

Re: Why are there so few backers in Irish session?

I am both a fiddle melody player and a guitar backer and I have been playing this music as well as other folk-related musical genres for nearly 50 years. I would humbly suggest that there is room out there for all kinds of sessions including ones where backers are present. I take part in a couple of large group sessions, sometimes with (perish the thought!) more than one guitar player. And, I play in another setting where three or four of us just play fiddle together. I love both situations. The large settings are rowdy, the craic is great, and people have fun sharing the music they love. It might not be your type of session, it may not be “pure drop”, but we are playing the same tunes and everyone has a blast. The fiddlers-only sessions are intimate and sublime, and I cherish those too.

I get the feeling that some on this forum feel that backers are a knuckle-dragging, lower form of life. Having done both, my experience is that both are challenging to do well. I have primarily been a guitar player and have only recently started playing fiddle (recently enough that many I play with would much rather hear me play guitar!). Oddly, I find that it is often easier to remember and play melody than the backing stuff – because backing, at least on chord-dense tunes, is more abstract.

Traditional music hopefully has room for all that love it. If you don’t like playing with backers, then don’t. But please don’t disparage those who love that same music with a different instrument in their hands.

Re: Why are there so few backers in Irish session?

> From that, we can safely conclude that rhythm guitar players are essential to ITM sessions.

I’m not sure how that follows from the premise, which is itself dubious since most jazz outfits don’t include a guitar. But if we stipulate the premise, that would then lead us to the conclusion that we can substitute any other folk tradition for “ITM sessions”, and clearly it is not true for most traditional music.
Obviously, the difference between the genres you mention and trad music is that in each of them the default form is for one voice - a singer or a lead player - to improvise against and across the rhythm and the chords, so it’s a big help for the listeners and for the musicians to have a rhythm section to make the lead instrument’s syncopations and harmonic tensions stand out a little more, and to give the audience something constant to hang onto.

In trad, on the other hand, the musicians are _already doing this_. There is no need for a rhythm section in a trad setting because the players are providing the rhythm and the harmony - it’s built into the tunes.

EDIT to add:
In a small group setting, of course, the musicians might want to have a rhythm player or two to allow them to play a bit more freely, that is, with more rhythmic and melodic variation. This is a fun way to play, and a great player can do a lot with this sort of music, Martin Hayes being a great example. But this is definitely not typical session playing!

gimpy, I think you’ve put your finger on it there.

I think it’s safe to say that any general, nontrivial statement about sessions is contradicted by a nontrivial number of sessions. The only definition I can think of for a trad session is just “what you get when a few people come together to play Irish music together” - everything else is custom, habit, and local preference.

So I’ve played in sessions where the fellow who convened the bunch would invite the listeners to come up and “sing a song, do a dance, tell a joke, whatever!”, and been in rooms where the session was N musicians in N chairs, and if anyone not in the in crowd tried to pull up a chair to join them, they’d be told that there was no more room, and I’ve seen lots of examples along the range between those extremes. Weirdly, I lean towards the more inclusive side of that spectrum if I’m at a pub session, but I sort of prefer playing in small groups with people I know which I think of as more of a house session.

To be clear, I too lean toward public sessions being welcoming to visitors.
