I have some news for YOU

I was contemplating the late thread on playing tunes in the wilderness and the sub-thread about eccentricity. I was in the shower with my parrot thinking about it and, well, it struck me how odd that would seem to someone (the parrot’s presence, not me thinking)..

My mind went back to my son’s assessment of sessions and tunes. He couldn’t understand why it would be considered enjoyable to sit in a little circle in the off corner of some run down bar, playing the most aggravating music on the world’s most aggravating instruments. Hmmmmm.

And here we are. Not only involved with actual playing very old and unusual music, but spending hours discussing it on computers under the names of Batlady, Llig, lazyhound, bogman, etc (none of which were recognized by my spell checker).

I have some news for you…yes YOU YOU YOU….you are most likely eccentric and don’t know it..

Are there no clues, some reaction of others, some behavioral nuance, some whispering behind your back, maybe a hint of avoidance, some chuckling when you are around, anything that you see in others that may confirm my suspicion that the world, generally, thinks YOU are odd because of tune playing?

So how strange is tune playing in your parts. What are some clues that you have picked up that you, indeed may be eccentric? Have you had any sudden perspective realization of your oddity?

Okay, I’m the one who started the eccentricity side-argument in the other thread, so I guess I have some accountability here.

Genuine eccentrics are unwitting, and they are few, and they are intrinsically unique, and everyone else sees it. It’s like a nickname. Just because you want a nickname doesn’t mean you get one.

And here’s a word about originality and uniqueness. In this modern generation of “everyone is special.” I hate to break this to you, but very few people are special, and very least of all the ones who claim to be. Are there people who cling to ITM as an affectation, a cloying grasp at attention? Absolutely. Some play it because of a cultural connection. Personally, and I doubt I’m alone here, am drawn to it because on the whole the people I run into in its pursuit are NOT self-decribed eccentrics clamoring for attention, I can show up, share a tune, learn a tune, share some talk, all among people who are generally devoid of ego and pretense, and go home with a small bit of peace and happiness, and no baggage. I used to play rock, which is full of ego and people constantly saying “Look at me,” which is partly why I was not able to succeed in it. I love ITM because I am mostly able to avoid that.

Moral of the story: beware those who call themselves eccentric, unless you’re prepared to placate their need for attention.

Excellent post, SWFL. Behaviour has been over-pathologised and if you’re outside the norm -- asyncronimous development the article you referenced above called it -- there is a good chance you will be labeled as having a disorder.

Not every variant of weirdness is a brain disorder. I really like the series of articles on that SENG site presenting an alternative way of understanding certain behavioural traits which, to me, seems a far more positive construct than labeling them ADHD, autism, or Aspergers. Essentially when you use the latter three labels (or bipolar or whatever), you are using a “disease” category and the “unwanted” behaviours are considered symptoms of the disease. Disease constructs don’t take into account the complexity, richness, and interrelation of emotion and intellect. What I mean, as a kind of basic example, is that an individual might be described as too intense and hypersensitive in their interpersonal relationships, but that might go hand in hand with being intense in their academic, musical, artistic, or whatever pursuits. Is this a brain disease? I hope not. Modern psychiatry would use the disease model and I think the only people who benefit from that are the pharmaceutical companies.

To go further out on a limb, I suggest that the modern disease model of mental health has significant foundations in the eighteenth century Enlightenment philosophy which propounded that rationality and emotions were two separate things, and in fact what made man better than animals (and as we are talking white, European philosophers here, many other people on the planet) was that he had rationality and intellect. If it appeared that your emotions were controlling your intellect, or that your intellect was diseased in some way, you were labeled as insane. I can tell you that many 19th century asylum inmates (at least in Scotland) were in fact pretty bonkers. Often insanity in those days is described in words like a “defect of reason.” However, when and if the patient seemed to be in control of their emotions again they were generally discharged from the asylum. Even though there is all this evidence now which shows intellect, emotion, and body are very much interrelated, I don’t think psychology has moved on from the reason-emotion dichotomy.

Sorry to go off on one… this touched all sorts of nerves with me.

No I agree with C and TSS - I don’t think it is right at all that individuals are ‘diagnosed’ with some spurious disease, because their take on the world is different. With the word ‘diagnosis’ there is an implication that there has to be an intervention often of a pharmaceutical kind and, when this is used instead of creating an environment where individual differences are allowed to thrive on their own terms, it is inexcusable in the 21st century.

Yet schools sometimes do still seem to have either a dustbin mentality, where there is a special place for some kids to go and be taught separately. The alternative, inclusion in the mainstream classroom, is often half arsed and underresourced and just pays lip service to the idea of inclusion rather than actually thinking through and throwing resources (well trained TAs for instance) at the situation.

Having said that, some mainstream schools really do get it right. Sadly, I believe they are few and far between.

We need to be quite clear what we’re speaking about. If someone is describing specific learning difficulties such as dyslexia, dyspraxia, ADHD as brain disease, they are patently wrong. These are never ‘diagnosed’; an assessment is made by observing a range of indicators, third parties are question in the case of ADHD and a pattern of difficulties is detected - the ‘condition’ is a response to certain elements of environment, usually where strict behavioural formalities are required or the need for speedy processing of information.

The word ‘diagnosis’ is totally misused. There is no blood test for any of these conditions, only the mirror of cause and effect when certain drugs are administered in the case of ADHD. So how does a practitioner test for the efficacy of the drugs? - by the same means of observation and questioning as was used to prescribe them in the first place? I have very mixed feelings about the use of drugs in these cases. However, some people actually say that taking something such as Ritalin makes them feel like themselves for once - things become calm - focus is established for the first time and they can express themselves coherently and therefore become heard as individuals. Who am I to say that that is wrong. Then there’s the down side of the side effects and the potential dodginess of diagnosis, especially in adults.

As far as I’m aware, Attention Deficit is the only one of the above mentioned difficulties to have a dedicated prescription. Individuals with other assessed difficulties are supported by non-medical helpers in a variety of ways and in many different circumstances. A great deal of time is spent by those in the field in bolstering self esteem, helping get rid of negative baggage which has accumulated and showing individuals that the way they think and look at the world has real value.

“ ~ don’t rush to judgement and put people into boxes.” ~ Al Brown

Language is categorization ~ the act of language is to find tags for objects and actions, nouns and verbs, and refining further with modifications from other senses, such as the eyes to colour, the feel of a thing ~ “a rough red cloth”. I don’t think that is the problem, seeking a title for something, to help hold the idea of it for further analysis and disection. That is what language and communication and the search for understanding is all about. The problems raised here are when we misuse tags, when we neglect their potential for abuse, neglect, stigmatization. We must not forget that within any category or sub-category there can be great variety, an immense area of measurement, and numerous potential influences within and without.

We do not live in a world of only primary colours, one red, one blue, one yellow, one white, one black. There is no single autism. There is not one disability or ability that can serve as the identifier for such infinite possibilities. And we can’t pin them done as constant, unvarying. Some prefer that comfort of surity, that a thing is as it is and never varies, but that would be like thinking that something made of iron will never rust, or cannot be formed and sharpened into a useful tool, or a weapon. We cannot deny there are seasons, not can we deny that these vary from year to year, day to day, second to second… For me to say that it is a cold sunny Winter Thursday on the 4th day of the month of March, the year 2010, gives us a point of reference, but it comes nowhere near describing what is about me now, the environment and soundscape. Further on the lack of clarity, we don’t all share the same day. For some it won’t be cold, or sunny, and for some it might not even be the same day, and their measure of years might be different. Categories of convenience for ‘need’, as with children who struggle with social and learning skills, is equally varied. Categorization serves the purpose of communication and inquiry. As Llig has raised, they give us a focus toward improving our understanding, with the hope of finding a way to lessen the struggle for child, parent and society. It is the abuse of terms that is the concern here, and the dependancy on them, their misuse and too free use, not necessarily their existence.

To deny categorization is to deny language and inquiry, curiosity and exploration. I don’t think that is what is being said, at least not by me.

The problem is when a category is treated in the general sense instead of specific, unique and individual ~ and when it is an excuse for inaction, or an excuse from responsibility. It is also a tool of bullying, realized or not, intentional or not. When it belittles or subjugates a person to little more than a number in a statistic, then it is abuse, and it can become, as is too often the case, a stigma, like a name shouted in a playground or sent as a text to denegrate or embarass.

There’s no denying the complexity of life, and that it is chemical in nature and that those chemicals can be out of balance. Nor can we deny that we can be damaged in so many endless ways, including damage to the brain. That such variables can also affect, damage, or alter ones behaviour, moods, perceptions, is also without question.

The term ‘eccentric’ has generally not been one that falls under the category of ‘complement’, but ridicule, in line with calling someone a weirdo, or in the older use of the word, ‘queer’, to belittle, a put down, a quip for a cheap laugh. It is good that our attitudes have softened some toward that term, but it still carries some of the power of its use as what was essentially an insult. Similar use can be put to other categories, as happened with ‘spastic’, again, as in a children’s playground. Some may have found a laugh in thier abuse, but others will have felt the boot in it. Some might use the term ‘eccentric’ in what they percieve as an affectionate way, and I admit I’ve done that myself, but the other baggage this term carries shouldn’t be ignored, and is usually there in the shadows.
