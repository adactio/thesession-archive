Why we’re changing our name from Celticado to Gaelicado

The music is the same, as are the members and instrumentation. We’re changing our name because of what we’ve learned about the history of this music. It’s no more “celtic” than it is “Irish”.

This is our opinion, based on research, and we have no intention of hurting anyone’s feelings. We just wanted to explain the name change.

BTW, the Gaelic language is prevalent in many of the former Celtic states, including Ireland, hence the name change.

Celtic references a set of pre-Roman languages that were prevalent in western Europe around 1200-800 BCE. We have no idea what the music sounded like because nobody wrote it down. Instrumentation isn’t known either, other than archaeological horns and bone whistles, also evidence of Lyre and similar instruments’ use in the region (Crete influence).

Irish music is really a mix of dance forms that became popular in Scotland and England in the 18th and 19th centuries and really weren’t available in Ireland until after the Penal laws were lifted.

Re: Why we’re changing our name from Celticado to Gaelicado

Celtic is the overarching term for the language group which includes Welsh and Breton and it is still correct to describe Irish as a celtic language. Irish and Scottish Gaelic are part of the goidelic language groups. What you call “The Gaelic Language” does not really exist. The term “gael” refers to the people who spoke goidelic languages in Ireland, Scotland and on the Isle of Man.

The term Celtic is still widely used when referring to Ireland specifically and now has a meaning which is distinct from the roman-era celts of Europe. For example, Celtic football club does not refer to the language spoken in 800 BC.

I would also say that it is not fair to attribute Irish music to England and Scotland 200 years ago. The polka is originally a polish dance, but it would be absurd to suggest that any Irish polka is really Polish music. Even if the forms themselves originated elsewhere, they have been developed by a very strong tradition of music making in Ireland and are now very much a strong part of Irish cultural heritage.

The usual criticism of the term “Celtic” when applied to music is that it is often used as a marketing term synonymous with “Irish” and ignores the other Celtic cultures. However to interpret “Celtic” in terms of its ancient roots ignores that these languages and associated cultures still exist, even if they are now minority languages.

“Gaelic” is just a subset of “Celtic”. Gaelic was/is spoken in Ireland, parts of Scotland and the Isle of Man. Welsh, Breton and Cornish are a different branch of the Celtic languages. Large parts of Scotland were never Gaelic and spoke either Scots or a brythonic language similar to Welsh. Lowland Scots were often scornful of Gaelic culture. A lot of what we now think if as Highland culture was a 19th century reimagination.

Re: Why we’re changing our name from Celticado to Gaelicado

Howard …. if I may can take issue with your statement …. “Large parts of Scotland were never Gaelic and spoke either Scots or a brythonic language similar to Welsh.”

Depending upon what exactly you mean by “large parts” and “were never Gaelic” ………… Are you saying large parts of Scotland were never Gaelic speaking? If so, the evidence suggests that this is not the case.

Looking at the linguistic roots of place names in Scotland as a means of determining language spoken in the past, the vast majority of place names of the Highlands and islands (excluding the Norse /Scots influenced Orkney and Shetland) are derived from Gaelic. Aberdeenshire, Fife, lowland Perthshire and Stirlingshire are a mix of Gaelic and Scots - with older place names being mainly Gaelic with also a scattering of Pictish or Norse. In the south of Scotland, including Strathclyde, Ayrshire, Dumfries and Galloway etc, older place names are still linguistically dominated by Gaelic - e.g. out of 8,000 place names of Galloway identified in a 1930 survey, 7,500 were of Gaelic origin (with Gaelic being spoken there until the C17th). Only the area of East / and Mid Lothian hasn’t had a (dominant) Gaelic-speaking past - and the place names of that area reflects this. Here is a useful map of languages spoken in Scotland around the year 1000 …

https://gaidhligdumgal.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Gaelic-leaflet-8-lge.pdf

which shows almost the whole of mainland Scotland spoke Gaelic. The only exceptions are the Norse-speaking areas on the fringes of Caithness and Sutherland, Lewis and Harris and Orkney and Shetland. And in the south, only the area east / south of Edinburgh was where Old English (now Scots) was spoken, and a mix of Gaelic and Cumbric in the Borders and Lanarkshire. Of course over the centuries since then Scots began to dominate in the south and has spread northwards.

The rich cultural heritage of Scotland has been influenced by all these languages, and this is also reflected in our music which, thankfully, is a still-thriving and vibrant tradition. That people from other airts enjoy this music, identify with it want to join in is, I think, (overall) a good thing. No, they do not always get it 100% right, but if imitation is a form of flattery, then we’ll take it. And frankly, it is a whole heap better than if the tradition was lifeless / culturally moribund.

In reference to the original post, I personally I don’t get too exercised by what term is used to identify the music that is played. “Celtic” is not what people in the trad world in Scotland would use, but perhaps for some of the rest of the world it gives an idea of what to expect musically. Would however suggest that using the term “Gaelic” to describe a style of traditional music could be confusing / misleading …… certainly to me it implies Gaelic song rather than wider trad music and tunes. A quick listen to the tracks on the Celticado Soundcloud page indicates that they play a mix of Irish and Scottish trad, with nary a Gaelic song in sight (or sound).

Re: Why we’re changing our name from Celticado to Gaelicado

Howard, I’m not disagreeing with much of what you say. But you saying that large parts of Scotland were never Gaelic (speaking) wasn’t an exaggeration - it’s just incorrect.

And I have to say that I’m not sure that I agree with you that Gaelic culture (of the 11th century) doesn’t have relevance to discussing “modern” traditional music. Of course it absolutely does. Scottish traditional music is utterly shaped by the past - and that goes back to the Scotti tribe’s invasion from Ireland in the 5th century and the resulting dissemination of Gaelic replacing the Pictish language. Gaelic culture (including language, stories, poetry and song) continued largely uniterrupted, certainly in the Highlands for the next 1,300 years. For example stories and songs relating to the Irish legendary hero Finn McCoull were popularly told / sung throughout the Highlands well into the C19th. This culture gave us amongst other things Gaelic song in its many, varied forms, the great Highland pipes with its pibroch, ceol mor and ceol beag, and a wheen of clarsach music - all of which have had an enormous influence in shaping the Scottish traditional musical culture that we see today. Of course there have been all sorts of other influences along the way that have gone into the melting pot including the fiddle, European dance traditions, Scots song, Irish jigs and, shudder to say it, even English Morris Dancing. But it could easily be argued that Gaelic culture is the greatest single influence on our musical tradition. It certainly is the most distinctive element, and the one which the rest of the world recognises and epitomises as being Scottish (think of all that clan / thistle / shortbread / piper / tartan / bagpipe / Highland games vibe).

Re: Why we’re changing our name from Celticado to Gaelicado

Howard - I remember reading somewhere - and seeing photographs of - a form of dancing taking place in I think the Northern Isles in the C19th which was obviously directly related to English morris dancing. The only other morris dancing I’ve ever seen was 30 years ago in a crowded Sandy Bells in Edinbugh where a visiting set? / group showed us their morris moves (it is a v small pub so suspect it wasn’t the best venue for dancing or viewing).

Re Scottish dance, its music and influences ………. Historically the main external influence on Scottish dance was from France. Up until the union of the crowns in 1603, France had been the principal destination for wealthy Scots to further their cultural education and thus they were expected to be able to speak French - as well as Latin and, for those with connections / land in the north, Gaelic. At that time all classes and sectors of lowland Scotland spoke Scots (including the aristocracy). That changed when the Scottish court moved south and English became the preferred language of the aristocratic Scots (and thus soon also those who were aspiring to social mobility). The Highlands remained Gaelic speaking - and although there was a gradual decline over the forthcoming centuries, Gaelic remained the lingua Franca of the whole of the Highlands through the 1800s and up until around 1900. This was the case even at the southern edge of the Highlands - I’ve seen an 1890s advert for a primary school teacher in Logierait (which is only just in Highland Perthshire being maybe only 10 miles north of the Highland fault line) stating that applicants had to be Gaelic speakers as the local children had no English when they started school at age 5. Likewise, throughout the 1800s ministers in the Atholl / Breadalbane areas had to be Gaelic speakers and were expected to give two services - one in English and afterwards / before, one in Gaelic. Alas, in little over a century, Perthshire Gaelic has declined and disappeared -- with the native Gaelic speakers now all passed away, taking with them a precious part of our culture heritage and traditions.

Given the cultural dominance of France throughout Europe right up until the 1789 French Revolution, dances from the French court were probably also the main dance influence in England too (though I am clutching at straws there as I know very little about English dance traditions). Starting around 1710 English “Country Dancing” (itself influenced from France?) started to migrate northwards to be danced by the Scottish upper classes, and were added to other dance types that were already danced in the parts of Scotland less constricted by the church. In the mid-1700s minuets were hugely popular in Scotland … though musicians who played for dancing up until then had no experience of the dance form or the music that went with them, so they adapted existing Scottish tunes. Pretty soon though Scottish fiddlers were writing reems of new minuets - usually dedicated to the daughters / wives of their wealthier patrons. Alas minuets soon went out of fashion, and these Scottish minuet tunes are now almost never played - by anyone. Interestingly I have a friend whose great Aunt grew up near Aberfeldy before WWI - and they still danced minuets then (with the pronunciation used being the French way … “min-oo- ay”.

However, the mainstay of Scottish dancing (for all classes) throughout the mid / latter part of the 1700s and into the 1800s was the reel, and its related form, the Strathspey Reel. All of the information from that period that I have read state that the dances and music that went with them were uniquely Scottish. There are many accounts in writing (diaries, travelogues, biographies, letters etc) of visitors from elsewhere stating their astonishment at the reels danced and their slightly wild character, along with the obvious enthusiasm, energy and vitality of the dancers. I suspect that this might have been due to reels being a huge contrast to the more formal court dances seen elsewhere in Britain. It certainly seems that C18th Scotland was “mad for the dancing”, with all sectors and classes of society taking part: from the dances in the cramped tenements of the old town in Edinburgh being replaced with the new formal assembly rooms in the New Town, and in Perth, Glasgow, Aberdeen and Elgin. However rural areas were equally obsessed - with dances in barns, houses, outside, at weddings etc. There is a lovely account of a dance held in the early 1800s in Rothiemurchus by a young Lady Elizabeth Grant where the whole community from top to bottom of the social scale took enthusiastic part and let rip. Oh to be able to time-travel to be a fly on the wall, if not to join in.

For anyone who is really interested, there are a couple of very detailed books written in the early 1970s by George Emmerson: “Rantin‘ Pipe and Tremblin’ String, a history of Scottish dance music” and “A Social History of Scottish Dance”, both of which gives chapter and verse on the subject.

Re your assertion that “The tunes and dances themselves very often seem to have travelled widely and were simply adapted to the local style” ….. yes, you may well be right. It is difficult to know definitively – and would take a PhD or two to unravel. However I think it very much depends upon what period you are talking about. The further back you go, the less uniformity and homogeneity there was in all things including music and dance. Certainly, in the mid-1700s there were still significant regional variations in styles of music, Gaelic song, dances etc and there are enough musical collections from then and later periods to be able to identify these variations. However, there were certainly some common tunes that were widespread - passed on and shared by travellers, drovers, soldiers / military bandsmen and musicians. But with the explosion in printing of books of dance music from 1780 onwards, this must have led to a softening of those local and regional musical idiosyncrasies and differences. Whichever way, there are an enormous number of dance tunes collected and composed in this period. My local library in Perth has an archive of traditional (mainly fiddle) music collected from the period around 1800 which contains 14,000 tunes. If you also include tunes written in the 200 years since then, and add music from the piping traditions, dance band music, Gaelic and Scots songs etc, I think that you could conservatively double that number and still not touch the sides of the tradition.

By the 1920s when Miss Milligan set up the (Royal) Scottish Country Dance Society, any regional variations in dance styles were quickly quashed in their desire to standardise and identify the definitive “correct” form of each dance. What a shame …… The same pressure to standardise happened with piping in the C19th through military / pipe bands / competitions. Fiddling has probably had a more diverse story, but there are still many who would advocate there is a “correct” way to pay Scottish fiddle music, and a need to have consistency of playing / bowing etc for fiddle orchestras, Strathspey and Reel Societies, for competitions etc. The resulting tendency to uniformity and ever greater musical polishing can, in my view, be a musical cul-de-sac that leaves less room for grit, soul, emotion, individual creativity, expression and evolution. As such, I suspect that this slightly constricted view of Scottish fiddle tradition has within itself the seeds of its own destruction.

Fortunately there have aways been round pegs that refuse to be put in square holes, and on the fringes there were and are fragments of our more diverse musical and dance traditions that have survived and were never standardised ….. and thankfully these are increasingly valued. And these renegade elements of the traditions also tend to be the ones that are more fun. So I am hopeful for the future.

There, long ramble over. I hope some of it was of interest, and that most of it, even my personal opinions, are reasonably factually correct!

As to whether the terms “Gaelic” or “Celtic” should be used to describe the music of a New England band ……. to me neither are particularly accurate or useful. But given the underlying issue is one of the band creating an image and selling themselves, I suspect that they will know what will work best in their own local market place better than I do.

If we’re talking about “Classical” music, as it’s commonly known, that’s one thing; it’s its own world, and I have nothing to say about it. But traditional “folk music” is another thing. And that’s as far as I want to go with that, now that I think about it, as it’s liable to turn into an argument that’s played out a hundred times on this forum without coming any closer to resolution … !

I’m a bit uncomfortable with the use of the word ‘Celtic’ to describe any aspect of life in the last five hundred years, apart from within a scholarly discussion about language groups. I don’t think there’s any evidence that any part of what we now describe as traditional music in Ireland or Britain dates back to the Celtic tribes who lived here a couple of thousand years ago.
‘Celtic’ has also become something of a sectarian signifier, which I’m also not comfortable with.
