Advice from the Irish please on the sensitivity of certain songs and tunes.

I don’t know why, but a tune that seems to flow spontaneously and frequently out of my low D whistle is the tune to “Come All Ye Black and Tan’s”. But I can never play this without wondering if it would be politically and/or socially acceptable to play this tune in certain parts of Ireland, or in front of certain Irish people. It may merely be my English ignorance, but I was little surprised many years ago that this song seemed acceptable when our lead singer (from Belfast) sang it at a St. Paddy’s day gig. So I was just wondering if anybody could enlighten me as to how socially sensitive some of those political Irish songs (from both sides) really are nowadays. Also, a brief associated question:- could anybody tell me the name of the slower and original song from which the music to “All Ye Black and Tan’s” was taken? I need to listen to it again.

Re: Advice on the sensitivity when playing “Come Out Ye Black & Tans”.

Gobby, in your original post you were asking about playing a particular tune. As far as I can tell you’re not asking about singing words with the melody. Although in your mind the tune is associated with Dominic Behan’s song; “Come Out Ye Black & Tans” ~ https://play.spotify.com/album/6W1y6vCjxGkSzrpBGmMgGs
and also an earlier song, air or march; which you vaguely recall. I’m guessing the latter is the march version of a poem by Piaras Mac Gearailt ~ “Rosc Catha na Mumhan” (Battle Cry of Munster);
played here by the Chieftains ~
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4AZlGHql6fU

The fact that you’re sensitive to how the tune is received by others is a strong indication of what you bring to your music & what you give out. If you play a tune well it will strike the mood you intend. That doesn’t mean all who hear you won’t associate a different meaning than you, or infer political connotations, even though you are not singing words.

My advice is play the tune the way it feels best for you. But if you’re going to sing verses that’s a different kettle of fish.

Re: Advice from the Irish please on the sensitivity of certain songs and tunes.

I grew up in East Belfast playing in accordion and flute bands and played all sort of tunes some of which might be deemed as ‘offensive’ by some but whilst I respect their right to be offended, come on really?
When I joined an accordion band, they only played about 30 tunes, but they were tunes that I’d heard many times so it didn’t take me long getting to learn them. But I got bored. So I decided to join a flute band. After learning their 60 tunes, I left and joined another. I loved learning and playing new tunes.
I remember going into Smithfield Market in Belfast and into a record shop that sold rebel music on cassettes. I looked at the tunes list on the back sleeve and didn’t recognise any of the titles. I remember how much I was looking forward to getting home and listening to the tape so that I could write out the tunes. Guess What…? I already knew most of them, they were just called something different.
I have maintained the ethos that If it sounds like a good tune it is a good tune and worthy of learning, if some poet or lyricist wants to add words to the tune, turn it into a ballad to make himself feel better then go ahead, but I find it difficult to get offended by music unless it’s played really badly.
Today I mainly play traditional Irish Music on various flutes and whistles but my repertoire includes Northumbrian Rants, Scottish Reels and Jig, English Hornpipes and what some would call ‘Orange tunes’, I am not ashamed of the tunes I play or where I am from, and if I can appreciate and play music from other traditions, why should mine be any more offensive than anyone else’s?
