Great spirit, Kellie.

Years ago I asked my guitar teacher that same thing, and he said (pertaining to guitar) — It can be done, but you’ll have to spend all your waking hours playing the guitar - so you better love it.

That’s all true, but it’s more like you’ll have to always be figuring out ways to earn money. If you’re lucky, they’ll all involve music. If you’re really lucky, most of them will involve playing whistle. But you’ll have to get really good at figuring out how to market yourself. There are many ways, in addition to performing, that you can make money in music, some better than others.

Advertising, not so good. I knew a keyboard player who wrote jingles for TV ads and spent his time trying to write a country hit. Teaching, pretty good; studio, better.

As a performer, you’d have to watch and listen carefully to other performers and bands to cop on to their sound and style, but more the sound, I think. Then figure out what you personally have to offer. I think with your spirit and ability, you’d have plenty.

Just don’t rule anything out - including learning other instruments in the future.

For an independent artistic life, one where you make your own rules and get to choose what you do, figure out two other legs to stand on: Massage Therapist and Barrista. Computer Programmer and Dance teacher. Audio Engineer and IT networking specialist.

Seriously, very few artists, musicians or athletes make a living at their craft. I’ll bet most people who make a living at music, actually earn the majority of their income from related activities rather than performing: teaching, instrument repair, online-marketing for a venue.

Re: Future planning

You’ve got a great attitude. If I were your age and had some talent(ha!) I’d be wanting to do the same thing.

U of L is a great music program by all accounts. World class teachers, and they even offer a Masters in Trad music. The college is pretty close to the most musical part of Ireland - Clare, and Ennis in particular. I’m not sure how difficult it is to get in or how proficient you actually have to be on your instrument, but it’s worth asking about for sure. Do they take tin whistle players? You will need to ask them. Couple of good videos here on the University’s website. http://www3.ul.ie/courses/IrishMusicAndDanceNR.php

IMO, to get into a program like that, you need to be progressing faster. Wanting to jump into a music program with only 2ish years of music experience is a big(huge) task, and you need to start now. I don’t know of a single college music program that allows entry to beginners - they all expect the students to be competent players . Weekly lessons and tons of practice and excessive listening and all that sort of stuff. Email the music director at the link I gave you and ask him more about the audition requirements so you know how much you need to improve.

As for making it a job, performance and teaching are the two most common ways. Some people do lots of gigs playing for dances, at bars, house concerts, festivals, farmers markets, busking etc. Private students and group classes will both be necessary, IMO. You will need to be able to sell yourself, and you will need to actively promote yourself, and you will need to live in a market that loves niche music. Hippy/hipster areas like Seattle are desirable for niche musicians. Maybe other types of cities, but others who make a living at this would know better than I. Even better if you can sing or play guitar, or both. Punters like words. And they like guitars. Form a band and play with them occasionally, but remember a 3 or 4 way split won’t make you much money. You will need to travel extensively, but can fix some of that by living in the right area. Also, this is a genre that really needs friends and personal connections. You cannot just be a studio whistler and expect gigs to come flying your way. You need to make lots of friends and play lots. And be a good player, of course.

It’s all about your talent and dedication level. You don’t need to have world-class talent, but you do need to be pretty good. And you need to be highly dedicated. It is not an easy business.

*Of course, a degree means nothing, really, but you will get way more exposure to the real deal at a place like U of L than in your current situation.

In the arts, it tends to be feast or famine, the reason being you don’t generally have a consistent employer. Even if you are famous, you can have ups and downs. If you are not famous, you continually have to prove yourself to someone who will pay you. If you want it enough, are fearless, and are willing to do what it takes, you’ll be able to succeed. Don’t let anyone tell you it’s about luck. Luck contributes to good timing, but ultimately, success comes from never saying die, continually perfecting your art, and embracing every opportunity to be seen and heard.

Two things you will have to accept: Success doesn’t necessarily equate with high income. Additionally, you may have to prioritize your career over other factors of your life that are important to you, like where you live or relationships. If you can do that, success is almost guaranteed if you are from very good to excellent at what you do. If you place any limitations on what you are willing to do, then the answer to your question is maybe yes, maybe no.

You won’t get a more direct or truthful answer than that.

If not the U of L, don’t disregard the possibility of doing a standard undergraduate degree in music at any reasonable university, and then specialising in your own particular musical interest as a post-graduate. It’s only what you specialise in as a post-grad that will define you. But if you get uni qualifications it opens up other music possibilities. Look at Dr. Mat Cranitch:- He teaches engineering at Uni, and he also teaches Irish music (and, of course, writes great books on fiddling). And he also tours, performs and teaches. A basic degree in music opens up many unimaginable opportunities, and the further you go academically, the more you will be allowed to pursue and present your own musical interests.
But that’s the thing about the future;- i.e., it just WON’T turn out as you imagine. Things will happen to dictate changes in your directions that you simply can’t imagine. That’s how life works. All you can do is maintain the enthusiasm you have for your goals, pursue them, and, as somebody said earlier, remain flexible. I do believe that it’s reasonably possible for a young person to create their own living through music, though not necessarily by just paying it . You may not ever get rich and famous, but making a reasonable living by doing what you love is 100 times better than being rich doing something you hate for somebody else. I gave this advice to my nephew when he was about your age. He proved me wrong though, because he is now, at 30 years old, actually quite rich and well known, and working as a music producer in L.A. Re: Future planning

Making a living playing whistle is not practical. You can certianly make money doing it, but it’s probably not going to be playing trad music. There are studio gigs available, but gigs for wind players are much easier to get if you double other instruments, ie flute/sax/clarinet/whistle. Playing uilleann and/or highland pipes would probably help you get “celtic” gigs or whatever, but that still won’t pay the bills. It’s also worth pointing out that you need to play at an exceedingly high level to be a studio musician, be able to sightread in every key, know all of your scales on every whistle, etc.

If you want to continue playing whistle that’s all well and good. Move to an area that has a lot of trad music around, take as many lessons as you can, play with as many people who are better than you as possible, and practice deliberately. But at the same time prepare yourself for a different career. There are many career options in music that don’t require you to play as your main source of income. Look into them.

Re: Future planning

If you’re not already getting offers to tour with a professional outfit, don’t do it. At least not now.

Focus on your education or learning a marketable trade.

Many excellent musicians I’ve known in many styles were able to play more music, to a better standard, with better players, because they didn’t have to support themselves with near minimum-wage jobs that require 70 hours per week just to get by. That left nights and weekends free to gig, and they had vacation benefits so they could use paid time off days to tour or do the occasional festival.

Independent contractors have even more flexibility… people who do masonry, the construction trades, carpentry, etc., and these are terrific, too. Big shortage of HVAC technicians, welders, and the like if you’d rather work with your hands. And as a skilled tradesman, you can run your own business that can give you the flexibility to tour or record.

There are are people making a full-time living at trad music, of course, but just a few. The ones who are doing well run it like a business. They sign contracts, they work a lot at the logistics and the marketing and the salesmanship (you’re always hunting for the next gig), and music is just the tip of the iceberg of what they do.

The ones who earn more than a subsistence level income doing it? They work their tails off, and are very disciplined about working the non-musical parts of their careers, too. Which is most of their time.

Alison Brown, for example, is as good a 5 string banjo player as there is on the planet. She got a Master’s in Business from UCLA, is smart as a whip, and owns her own record company, Vanguard, Liz Carroll’s/John Doyle’s label among many others.

James Kelly? Hangs wallpaper last time I checked.
John Whelan? Call him to help remodel your house. Very good carpentry/masonry/drywall guy. I forget which, specifically. Just talked to a general contractor who used to hire him a lot! :)
Brian Conway? Lawyer with a steady, secure government job.
Lots of others in technology, design, engineering, etc.

Most of us can go on and on, with very well-known players and their day jobs. The more ‘pure drop trad’ you are, the more important it is.

There are certainly people doing it full time…. especially combining it with teaching. But if I’m in your shoes, and knowing what I know now, I’d concentrate on finishing schooling in a subject or learning a trade that I have every expectation will support not just myself, but my family as well. Especially if your parents are supportive for the next few years while you do so.

That way, you can say “no” to gigs and projects that aren’t professionally rewarding, and you can play because you WANT to, not because you have to. And when an opportunity comes along, you have the freedom to take it.

Good luck! I look forward to buying your CDs and going to your concerts. But hey, there’s no rush!

This can be offered as a two-year part time, or full-time for one-year course. The Certificate differs significantly from other music and dance programs on offer. As it can involve learning by distance, it enables the learner to complete modules from home, community centres or indeed whilst still in employment. The course includes occasional master classes and tutorials. Designed to suit the needs and timetable of the individual learner, the course is both flexible and diverse.”

One path, unfortunately currently on the wane, is to go the military band path. Sadly subject budgetary woes, but a stellar berth if you can get it. One relative made it into the Marine Band. Insanely high standards for admission. Then he traded services to join the Army Presidential Band Detachment. He started from age five. Even if you cannot go this route, get the
military specs for joining, and use this as a blue print to prepare yourself any foray into education. The competition is insane. Beginning music education course assume you to have a background of accomplished so-fa. At least one in five of your class mates will have perfect pitch/twelve years of keyboard. The pace is very brisk.
They have no time for slackers/slow learners. An ex-girlfriend was being taught keyboard and chord recognition from the age of five. Get a tutor now, start working several hours a day and prepare to really work if you get into a college level course.

There are three types of military bands in the U.S.:

The reserve component ones, including the National Guard bands. Those are part time jobs. Once you do your initial active duty training (basic and then a specialty music school) you’ll rehearse one night per week, usually, and drill two weeks per year.

Then there are the active duty bands. These are full time positions, typically in the division bands, though there are others. You’re in the service of your choice full-time. Your primary job is attending and playing for formal events, change of command ceremonies., parties and dances if your band breaks out into stage ensembles.

In both cases you’re deployable… your band has a wartime mission as well - in the Army, usually doing guard duty and other kinds of security duties and assisting with mass casualty evacuations, under the control of the Division headquarters.

The recruiting commands may also field bands to do community outreach and recruiting events. These are often country, rock or hip-hop ensembles designed to appeal to enlistment age youngsters. There’s less of that than there used to be.

Most of these band programs are in close contact with high school and college band directors and are always pinging them to be put in contact with sharp young players with professional potential.

The third type of bands are the special bands. For example, the U.S. Military Academy Jazz Knights, or the Army Fife and Drum Corps in Washington, D.C. These are not deployable… meaning the band doesn’t have a wartime mission. They’re permanent residents of the USMA at West Point or Washington DC, or the Air Force or Naval Academy. I think the Marine Corps Band in Washington DC also falls into this category. These are EXTREMELY competitive and only take the best of the best.

In the case of the Jazz Knights, they don’t bother sending you to music school AIT (advanced individual training). You are expected to walk in knowing your stuff. Just do basic training and that’s it.

Josh Dukes, an excellent Irish trad guitarist and flute player, was a senior NCO in the Fife and Drum Corps for a long time. I have a friend who was in the Jazz Knights at the US Military academy for one enlistment, too. Both are exquisitely good musicians.

You do need to pass the Armed Forces Physical Fitness Test and be height-weight proportionate. In practice, you’ll be expected to be in very good physical condition, so start working out now if this is something you’re interested in.

Re: Future planning

Kellie, you may be able to feed yourself for awhile at points in your life by playing music, but making a living at it? No. And don’t think that studio musicians make bank - they don’t. I have a very decent paying day job and make way more than most principle players in orchestras, studio bassists, or pit orchestra musicians. Most professional musicians I know rely on arts grants, teaching (music or regular school), or other activities to provide the bulk of their income as well as having spouses with a good income and health insurance.

Enjoy ITM, get good, heck get great at it, form a band, cut a CD, go on tour, have fun, but have a day job to earn money. Financial independence is a great way to support musical independence. If you have to rely on playing music to make a living you’ll find that you have to play a lot of music that’s not that much fun to play. Of course there are exceptions to what I’ve just said, but very, very, very few. And I bet that you are not, nor will ever be good enough to be one of those exceptions (no offense to your abilities but you’re wanting to enter a field that’s harder than becoming an NFL football player).
