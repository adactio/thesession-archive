Re: Boehm flutes

I play both – Boehm and simple systems. After years of “Boehming” I can now just about do acceptable trad ornamentation. I can now also near enough do simple-system ornamentation.
It is a bit more difficult to ornament on the Boehm system than the simple, but not impossible. The keys seem to act as a kind of “barrier”, or at least an extra layer, between your fingertips and the holes, until you get used to it. But then, you need to learn to stretch your hand (mostly right hand) and ensure all the holes are covered, for simple system playing. The so called “piper’s grip.” Not used in Boehm playing.
As she is already competent on the Boehm system it might be worth staying with that for a while until she knows plenty of tunes and plays in the ornamented trad style (if she doesn’t already.) If she can afford a wooden Boehm, so much the better.
Also, does she play the whistle? If so, how is her ornamentation on it?
Or suggest a relatively cheap simple system to get started on (depending on financial constraints.) Maybe a Tony Dixon plastic flute. Of course, ultimately, if it all works for her on simple system, she would need to go for a good quality hand-made simple system wooden or Delrin flute.
Not a complete advice package, but I hope it helps a bit.

Re: Boehm flutes

I play both too. I would subjectively recommend getting a simple system flute as well - the conventions for ornamentation and style in this music are easier to do on the simple system flute. Exactly why this is has a lot to do with the direct contact with the main holes and the unevenness of the simple system scale. I personally find having two different instruments which I use for different music directions helps keep my correct stylistic hats in place.
A keyless flute would be fine to get going - a Boehm player always has the option of picking the Boehm up if the key signature gets awkward, though in time, expect to want to get a keyed Simple System flute. F nat is the most-missed accidental and G# is pretty important when you try the scottish repertoire. A C natural thumb hole is also an obvious option for Boehm players.
Send her to the next FluteFling event that happens and she’ll get loads of useful tips there.

Of necessity, I now play on a Boehm. I’ve played one for 50 years, but until hand issues intervened, I had always played Irish on a wooden flute. I have some of the finest and played them for 40 years. Here is what I have to say:

1. The Boehm flute is a superior instrument. That is why the old flutes are not mainstream anymore.

2. Ornaments are not an issue. However, the Boehm does not finger like a whistle. Doesn’t bother Joanie Madden, who plays both.

3. The trick, if one already plays Boehm, is not about fingering - it is about embouchure. Everything about the Irish sound emanates from there.

4. I personally recommend a solid silver flute with a wooden head.

I’m happy to elaborate on any of these points if greater clarity or justification is desired.

Re: Boehm flutes

Please all stop trying to sell one flute over the other. Both can be used for any sort of music.
1. The Boehm flute is built to be acoustically even, i.e. every hole is as big as the bore and they are positioned where they need to be to be in tune. The mechanism makes those holes usable for normal human hands. The effect is a flute that is evenly loud in all tones, uniformly quite powerful and playable by most people. The world in general recognises these advantages and applies them in most genres. Just the BFlat mechanism is genius all on its’ own!
2. The wooden flute is idiosyncratic in its’ hole sizes and locations to make a compromise between acoustics and ergonomics. The result is a flute where different tones have different strengths/character and the holes can be harder to reach. Just compare the E and D on a typical flute… This unevenness makes up a large part of the characteristic sound of the conical flute as played in trad. Also, the immediate, unmediated contact between fingers and holes makes it easier to do the finger based articulations that are so important for this music.
3. All discussion of lip plate, embouchure holes et.c. is largely irrelevant since either sort of flute can come with either, though a lip plate on conical flutes is rarer. Putting a wooden headjoint on a Boehm flute does not change the basic evenness of the flute, nor the impact of the mechanism. It still may be something one might want to do. Personal preferences - I have a quite good wooden Boehm headjoint for sale if anyone is interested…
4. The “Irish” sound can be got out of a Boehm flute easily enough if the player is competent and has practised manipulating their tone quality. It is harder to get a conventional classically respected tonal range out of a simple system flute. But still possible.

If you have one of these flutes and cannot/do not want to buy another then play everything on that flute. If you can afford a second instrument then I personally recommend getting a simple system flute for the traditional music as well as a Boehm for the rest, particularly when moving further from G, D or A key signatures.

YMMV - I feel no need to convince anyone else that my choices in this area are valid. They work for me.

Re: Boehm flutes

Ailin, I wasn’t suggesting that the players were tonguing, just that it sounds like a typical recorder player, and I’m not totally convinced that it is just down to the player not the instrument. In the clips above, listening not to the notes but to the bits between the notes where the music lives, I hear a very distinct difference between the wooden flute (where one note flows into the next) and the two Boehm clips where the transitions are very abrupt. I have a feeling this may not be down to the player, but the difference between a small finger hole and a large pad: as the finger or pad comes near to the hole the open area is a cylinder the height of the finger/pad off the hole x the circumference. So with the finger/pad 1mm off the hole, for a 6mm finger hole the open area is 19mm2, with an 18mm pad it is 56mm2. That means that the distance between where the proximity of the finger/pad starts affecting the note to where the hole is completely closed on the Boehm is about 1/3 that of the simple flute. If the finger moves at the same speed on both instruments the Boehm hole will close in 1/3 the time it takes the simple flute. That probably adds clarity to fast runs and would be seen as an advantage in classical or jazz, but maybe isn’t in trad.

I have a solid-silver Haynes flute (C-foot joint) made in 1962 which I’ve had for many years and which I count myself very fortunate to own. I couldn’t afford such an instrument today.
The comments:
(1) Steph Geremia has a very good course on the Online Academy of Irish Music (OAIM) on how to play Irish music on a silver flute. Even though she normally plays conical-bored simple-system wooden flutes, she does a fine job with the silver flute. Her sound is comparable to that of Joanie Madden. It is interesting that she plays a Miyazawa flute as this is also the flute played by Madden. It is a bit of a ‘tank’ compared to many other modern Boehm flutes, and this may not be an accident. I would not want to play Irish trad on a more delicate Murumatsu, Powell, Haynes, Altus, etc. flute.
(2) My own preference is to play Irish music on a wooden flute. I like the feel of the open holes under my fingers and I like having the 123 -4xx finger pattern play F# rather than F natural as F# is so much more common in trad. I also prefer the six-holed flute for playing cuts, taps, rolls, etc., although these can certainly be done on the Boehm flute.
(3) While there is some variety of sound obtained by different players of the silver flute in the classical world, to my ears it is not as great as the variety of sound obtained by players of the simple-system wooden flute. To name just a few, Matt Molloy, June McCormack, Tara Bingham, Kevin Crawford, Steph Geremia, Paul Smyth sound as different as night and day to my ears (all fine players). The first three play Olwell flutes and the last three play Grinter flutes. If one compared classical flute players from the beginnings of recorded sound down to the present, one could possibly find a similar variety but not if one confined oneself to players active today. I love this variety of sound.

Mark hits on something I heard discussed when I was playing Baroque flute in University:

“I hear a very distinct difference between the wooden flute (where one note flows into the next) and the two Boehm clips where the transitions are very abrupt.”

I was told that in the modern Baroque flute scene there are players who feel that the transitions aren’t abrupt enough on the wooden Baroque flute, so they tongue every note, even in legato phrases. The tonguing is subtle and only serves to make the transitions from note to note clearer.

What I didn’t hear explained was whether it was the result of people who grew up hearing Baroque music played on Boehm flute trying to make Baroque fluteplaying more Boehmlike, or if it was an actual 18th century practice.

In any case Mark is onto something there, and I would expect wooden flutes with smaller holes to have smoother transitions than metal Boehm flutes with huge holes.

I didn’t even start into traditional Irish music until about 10 years after I graduated college, initially on, of all things, hammered dulcimer, but how I got from there to today is a very long and convoluted story.

I only recently gave my lovely old and fairly recently fully serviced Armstrong model 90 that I played for many years to my sister who also plays flute and is in a community band in Northern California. I decided that there was virtually no chance I’d every play it again in favor of either of my two really lovely wood flutes, a keyed restored 1857 8-key Metzler flute with a modern Hamilton custom headjoint, and a Olwell boxwood unlined keyless. If I ever get back into classical music, I’d most likely be playing in a baroque ensemble on alto recorder.

Re: Boehm flutes

Sometimes there is a reverse snobbery in the trad “scene” about “classical” players who come to sessions and think trad is easy and then proceed to massacre it - often quoted is the broadcast with JamesGalway and Matt Molloy. It can happen.

There is a world of difference between someone who has played in their school orchestra or similar and has learnt that there is “one true RIGHT way” of doing things and someone who has actually studied the flute in a real modern school of it. I know there are conservatories that are still stuck in the 1930s, but a real musician from today is expected to be able to study a genre of music and work out how to fit into it - often this is directed towards baroque or other historically informed music which may or not be played on historical flutes… That is on top of the technical mastery to get a wide range of tone out of the instrument including the raspy dark overtone-loaded. Also quarter tones, overtone notes, multiphonics, beat boxing (cf. Ian Anderson), whistle tones, finger articulation, tongue and throat articulation, singing into the flute while playing, throat tuning, improvisation, knowing the dance steps to the dance rhythms, playing on genuine baroque instruments with historically informed cross fingering, et.c. I know a couple and they have actually come to me and asked about how this type of music works.

Sometimes all this ability may be matched with a misjudgement of how seriously to take the challenge (I’m not calling it arrogance, because I quite like James Galway’s other playing) with the resulting train crash..

That said, some things are just plain wrong/unhealthy for everyone, e.g. not using enough air, resting the flute on your shoulder, bad posture, finger injuring grips. Others such as how high you raise your fingers really are variable and affect the sound you make - horses for courses here.

Yes, I know some respected flute players that I know and personally admire rest the flute on their shoulder, but I cannot help believing deeply that they would be better off not doing so. Or maybe they just know good chiropractors?
