Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

Hello, I hope this question is not inappropriate, but there is a thing that I have been wondering about, for a while.
I heard and read in multiple interviews from different Irish Trad musicians, that they described a lively session scene in London, or at least that they went to to sessions and had lived in London, some decades ago. However, what did happen during the Irish troubles with this scene ? Were there aggressions against Irish musicians (in England) during this time, or was there a clear distinction between music and politics ? Was it more difficult do find a place, willing to host sessions ?
Was there a special view on people with English origins, playing Irish music ?

↳ Posted by Davidread on May 6th, 2017.

Re: Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

There has been a strong trad session scene in England going back to the 50s and 60s. There are and were lots of pubs, usually in cities and towns, run by Irish people or frequented by Irish people which provide very conducive environments for Irish trad sessions. There was also a big overlap for performance music with the English folk scene and Irish trad was very popular in that scene too. As a consequence, there are lots of non Irish origin musicians who play Irish trad in England (like elsewhere) to a very high standard and who are equally regarded.

How the troubles impacted on Irish trad in England is probably a whole research thesis. The music still thrived and, on the surface, people’s politics were mostly self regulating in sessions - eg in my experience Irish rebel songs were rare compared to sessions in Ireland or Scotland. For any diaspora, the cultural relationships and compromises made between an adopted home and your origins are quite complex, even though our politicians (and some people) want these to be really simple - “you’re either with us or against us” and all that shite.

Re: Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

I was involved in playing music in Irish pubs and clubs in London right through the troubles with an Irish country type group. Some pubs were a bit wary and the Guv usually requested you to avoid doing rebel songs. In some pubs especially in the Kilburn area there were regular collections by men going around with tins for the ‘Political Prisoners’ in the North, and the Irish national anthem was always played at the end of the night.. In some Catholic clubs were where there was a mixture of English and Irish patrons you were warned about playing any rebel songs. In the run of the mill working mens or local community clubs that had a near 100% English clientele we were requested on a few occasions to avoid doing any Irish songs and in one instant we got a bollocking at the end of the night from the entertainment guy for singing ‘One Day at a Time’ For some peculiar reason he thought it was Irish. All in all it was not a nice time and in at least two pubs I was playing in we had to evacuate due to a bomb warning. ………..

Re: Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

I lived in London with my husband in the early 1970s. My husband is from Glasgow with Irish blood on his mother’s side & a great affection for Irish culture.

We went to an Irish pub in Ealing which even had a juke box where you could select Irish songs. There was a band that came on Sundays & used to sing all sorts of popular songs as well as Irish, and play jigs & reels on an accordion too. Gradually they came to sing more and more rebel songs as this was given such a warm reception by the largely Irish crowd. When it came to ‘open mike’ at the end of the evening, some of the regulars also sang rebel songs.

Finally they started playing The Soldier’s Song at the end of the evening, and if you didn’t stand up at once you were stared at. I couldn’t stand the pressure so began using the last song as a chance to visit the loo. They also collected money, ostensibly for a woman to go back to Ireland to see her parents - naively, I contributed on that basis, but now I wonder.

After the Birmingham Pub Bombings, we became scared that someone would retaliate against the pub, and we had been feeling uneasy about the ambience for some time, so we stopped going.

Re: Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

Things were a little different in Scotland.

Although the sectarian divide was very significant, and still is to a certain extent, in the Central Belt..particularly in The West….. , “The Troubles” didn’t impact here to quite the same extent.
Also, there seemed to be a distinction between the rebel versus loyalist songs and the more traditional Irish Music Scene although inevitably they did overlap on occasion.
Certainly,in Edinburgh, we all got along fairly well. Sandy Bells attracted many Irish musicians and punters along with many Scottish “folkies” and trad music fans.
There was the occasional robust political discussion, of course, but this wasn’t exclusively related to Irish matters. Very rarely, you might get the odd nutter who would try to “stir things” but we didn’t rise to the bait.
On the whole, good times as I recall.

Re: Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

AB - Yes I lived in London from 1955 to 1990. It was not a nice time to be Irish and worse if you were playing with an Irish influenced band. Getting stopped by the police late at night we would let the guy with the London accent do the talking. Your Irish accent usually meant a long delay while they checked you out. As far as playing traditional music is concerned I don’t think that the people who were playing trad all the time in the same pubs were too affected by what was happening. It was more the singing of rebel songs that became dodgy and believe me some people relished in singing them after the some of the most outrages incidents. On many occasions we’d be requested to do what we would class as a rebel song. In cases like that we would say that Landlord has told us no rebel stuff. I remember on the night of the incident in the Mall when the horses and soldiers were blown up we were playing in an Irish pub in the Harrow Road about two or three miles away from where the outrage took place. A punter got up and started blasting out James Connolly. The line in it “God’s curse on you English, you cruel hearted murderers” caused great cheering and punching of the air from many of the patrons present. Now to witness something like that was inclined to put you off playing. Thankfully we all survived to play another day……

Following up on Johnny Jay’s comment, tunes from Ireland and Scotland have a peaceful co-existence: indeed we share some of the same tunes but under different names.
Songs in Scotland can be tricky: “Fields of Athenry” has become the Celtic football song, so if you sing it in a pub where Rangers is the main club supported, you risk being thrown out. (Or, as happened to me, 3 other regular session members walking out: I thought they understood that I was singing it as a folk song and not a football song, and that our session was for folk, not politics: pretty ignorant behaviour, and I don’t think my singing is THAT bad!) On another occasion, repeated requests from a very drunk visitor to sing “James Connolly” were studiously refused, in case it ended up as Free Reed has described. You can get away with “Four Green Fields” and “The Foggy Dew”.

Re: Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

Just as a rider to a few of the recent posts I always hated the way that the lovely airs from some grand old songs are hi-jacked and became associated with songs depicting recent political events. For instance the grand old marching song Roddy McCorley from the days of the United Irishmen circa 1798 became known and sang as Sean South from Garryowen about the IRA campaign of the mid fifties. The air of the lovely scottish song Dark Lochnagar is used for the Ballad of James Connolly and so on. Following a party in a pub one night we finished the night by playing a Long way to Tipperary and Pack Up your Troubles. I was verbally attacked by a punter who told me that I shouldn’t be playing those tunes as they were British army recruiting songs. I replied that I was actually commemorating my Grandfather and a Grand Uncle who marched off to France to those two songs.
On another occasion a guy told me that I ruined the beautiful Ragland Road by playing it as a march. I took a certain amount of pleasure in telling him that I was actually playing the Dawning of the Day. as a march….Sometimes I just despair and ask myself "what the hell am I doing this when I could be at home watching Little House on the Prairie on the telly…lol……..

Re: Irish Music in England during the troubles ?

I spent a couple of years in the Willesden/Kilburn/Cricklewood area of London in the early 1980s and visited a few sessions and more than a few pubs around N.E. London, most of which were very Irish. I witnessed the tins going round Mother Macree’s “for the boys” and all that, but most people were apparently more concerned with the state of the Guinness than politics. The nearest I got to any ‘trouble’ was when there was a bombing in central London while I was nearby, and shortly afterwards travelling on the tube. For some reason there was a flash rumour that it was the Scottish Republican Army, and as a Scot, for a brief moment I fancied I had a glimpse of what it was like to be Irish when such incidents occurred. Of course the SRA were terrorists in their own minds only, and it turned out to be nothing at all to do with them (they were probably both too drunk at the time).

London was a great place when you were young and had money, but I would never live there now.

Nigel (who lives in a Perthshire town of 6,000 people now and loves it).
