Relevance of ITM

How relevant is ITM in today’s world? A lot of uneducated listeners tend to say that it “all sounds the same”, the same thing I used to hear about jazz. Personally, I believe that as more ears open globally, this infectious & technically demanding music will gain greater international currency, the tradition evolving with players looking to the future, while keeping a mindful ear to the past, and punters the world over tapping their feet or dancing (or sniffling into their pints). Sadly for some contributors here, this may also mean that there will be more backing instruments.

Re: Relevance of ITM

ITM is as technically demanding as one is prepared to make it, but on whistle, flute and box it’s not that difficult to make satisfactory progress on no previous musical experience (I think strings and the pipes are another matter, at any rate more difficult). It’s a hell of a lot easier to learn tin whistle up to reel standard than it is to master a piano keyboard, which usually involves a kid practising throughout his / her schooldays, at least. So I think catchy tunes, some apparent ease, and some actual, real, ease in learning to play the music, are potential attractions to ITM throughout the world where people are used to Western scales and modes of music.

Re: Relevance of ITM

All the different genres sound the same to people that aren’t familiar to them; and it’s not because they’re being “swallowed up, trivialized and mass-distributed by the pop music machine.” Think of non-pop music like Indian classical music, or Persian and Arabic music. Anyone that isn’t familiar with those styles will swear that whatever they’re hearing is the same thing they heard before. Old-Time, blues, Dixieland jazz etc. etc. Until you become a fan of that particular genre and become familiar enough, this will almost always be the case.

I remember the first encounters with Irish sessions that I had in the very early 80s where I was a fan, but still a novice, and I could swear they were playing the same tune they played 10 minutes ago. I still have some of the tapes I made from those days and I confirmed that it wasn’t true. Now I know when a tune is repeated in the same night, but I’m also aware of when similar sounding, but still different tunes are being played. It’s the same with any genre.

Re: Relevance of ITM

There’ll always be a need, and a place, for tunes, in which of course ITM is extraordinarily rich. But it’s sheer human nature to want to make new tunes, songs and rhythms. Future singers in the manner of Dylan and Clapton may annex or cannibalise ITM as those guys did the blues and country.
The relationship between The Music and dance is obviously important but strikes me as rather strained, possibly going back some time. (I find listening to The Kilfenora Ceilidh Band informative rather than exquisite.) I assume dance clubs will persist at any rate across the English-speaking world and probably well beyond it. And many TM bands need ceilidh gigs.
I’m trying to think of the fortunes of other trad musics. Flamenco has its afficionadoes and clubs. Latin and Gypsy music seem to have had more attention in the past than now, outside their bases. Same with Greek music (though so many now go there) and Paris accordion music. ITM might peak and then fade a bit, though it seems to have struck pretty deep roots in Northern Europe.
The one related instrument that seems to have crossed the barrier into the Orient proper and naturalised itself there, among people not acclimatised to the West, is the Highland Bagpipe.
