Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

I worked in computing till I retired and that’s terrible for neck pain. I was told the trick was to keep moving. There is no natural position from which you can do something as unnatural as use a computer and all you can do is keep shifting everything about. Stand up. Sit down. Put your chair up and down. Your body was intended to be relaxed and to move, not be held in any position, natural or otherwise.

I started playing the fiddle at sixty and for nine years have applied this same advice to the fiddle. I have a long neck but I use a low chinrest and shoulder rest and I keep moving everything. I think a high chinrest would tend to fix you into one position.

I only get pain if I am tense and stressed - which happens if I lead a tune in a room full of better players than myself or if I try to read written music and play, for instance in a workshop. Then I can do more damage to my neck and shoulders in three minutes than in three hours of ordinary playing.

Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

Everybody is different, and you just have to experiment to find the chin/shoulder rest combination that suits you personally. High chin rests suit some people, not others. The thing that you should be looking for is that you can firmly hold the fiddle between chin and chest without using your left hand, and with your head almost upright - not hunching your shoulder or cricking your neck over to the side. One quite common problem is to set the shoulder rest too tall or in the wrong pplace on the fiddle. It should fit snugly on your collar bone, if it is too tall or wrongly aligned it sits below bone and doesn’t ‘catch’, so the fiddle tends to slip and needs a lot more force to hold it in place.

Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

Sorry to hear about the problem Sharon S.

This suggestion definitely won’t suit everyone, or even most people, and it depends on your physical size, but getting rid of the shoulder rest is right for some people (including me.)

The key things are the height between your collar bone and chin, and also the angle of your collar bone.

The fiddle needs to rest right up against your neck, sitting on the collar bone. The height of the chin rest needs to be such that you can easily hold the fiddle between collar bone and chinrest or not as you wish.

This position takes the shoulder right out of the equation, the fiddle doesn’t touch your shoulder and the shoulder needn’t hunch or have any tension to support the fiddle.

The fiddle is left free of the weight, and arguably damping effect, of the shoulder rest. The only part of the underside of the fiddle that is touching you is the area around the heel block. That area is damped anyway and most of the back is free to vibrate.

The fiddle may be flatter than a shoulder rest user is used to.

BUT it only works if the dimensions of the player fit the fiddle/chinrest combination.

Good luck whatever you do and I hope you find a solution.

Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

I echo the comments above of a) do not take advice from non-experts as you can seriously make things worse, it is sadly very complicated to diagnose and fix shoulder and neck issues related to violin, and not one size fits all and b) An Alexander practitioner with experience with violinists would be the best bet to find the expertise.

I would take it very seriously, as it can take time to heal if you push yourself too far in the wrong direction.

---

Having said the above, I can share my experience, in the knowledge that you will not take it too seriously - I am not an expert, but I had violin related neck pain, and no longer do. YMMV etc etc

Context:
Shoulder rests are a recent invention, all the old masters extending back into history did not use or need them. They did not support the weight of the violin by gripping it with the chin, or weight of head, against the shoulder. This was a recent technique developed to help children with small arms and fingers learn classical pieces with shifting. You do not need to be able to hold the fiddle up using chin and shoulder, as is often repeated - and this insistance by my first teacher is what caused my problems. Players throughout history support the fiddle using the left hand, not the chin - and periodically grip the fiddle with the chin only transiently when shifting position. Especially in Irish fiddle you do not need to shift, so you can essentially play the fiddle without shoulder (or even chin rest )- it just rests, supported on the shoulder and left hand. --You do not need to be able to move the left hand from 1st position--

What worked for me:
My Alexander lessons focused on the idea that there should be NO tension whatsoever in the neck of shoulder, and to do this, you need to support the fiddle with the left hand, and basically be able to almost play with your chin OFF the violin. Later, the chin goes on the rest, to prevent the violin rotating with bow strokes, but that’s it, no tension, not even the slightest amount.

You can use a shoulder rest or not (I don’t) - but the key for me was to be 100% intolerant of any feeling of tension or grip in the neck and shoulder, and to learn a way to support the fiddles neck with the left hand.

Best of luck!

Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

I’d agree with the advice to actually get the problem checked out by a medical person first of all. If the problem IS to do with the way you hold the fiddle, then here are some ideas.

I’m of the view that ideally the shoulder rest should simply plug the gap between shoulder and chin, leaving the left hand entirely free. A perfect setup will allow the fiddle to sit comfortable and securely with no need for support with the left hand. In fact, you should be able to take you left hand away and the fiddle will stay supported, without falling downward.

Still, easier said than done, for many players. There’s a natural tendency to push the shoulder up, or clamp the chin down (or do both) and this can contribute to tension and pain too. Some players eventually settle with a particular type of rest that works and is comfortable. Others get by without one, only requiring some light padding or even just a cloth on the shoulder.

I used to play without a rest, but it soon got quite uncomfortable, and I got neck ache and left arm pain too. It was down to bad positioning really, and I was advised to use a shoulder rest. I tried a Wolf Primo Forte, and it worked fine. I’m still using it 50 years on.

As for the high chin rest, I know a few people who use just that, with no shoulder rest, and it works OK. It’s a very individual thing really. Also, two people of almost identical build can use totally different methods (ie one with a rest, the other without). There are no hard and fast rules.

Good luck !

Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

I wanted to add a few more thoughts. I would say the holding the violin with the chin is the prevailing way things are taught, and it’s obviously working for many many people. The question of holding with left hand vs not, and shoulder rest vs not, are considered controversial - with both sides thinking the other side are mad.

But in my camp are Jascha Heifetz, Augustin Hadelich, Alexander Markov, Mischa Elman, Nathan Milstein, Itzhak Perlman, Anne-Sophie Mutter - and the list basically goes on and on, so it’s definitely not some wild out there craziness, those of us who don’t use a shoulder rest - its a variation that works for many people.

For someone my height (6ft 2) with my neck length to hold the violin up without tension is categorically impossible without an -absurdly- large shoulder rest. My chin disappears into my neck if I keep the neck straight and lower my chin to the rest, as the common advice goes….

This is why the solution is always unique to each individual, to each their own.

What convinced me is the argument - why go to all the efforts of holding the violin with my chin when the intuitive way to support a violin would be with the hand? Ok- The reason for not holding it with the hand is to give freedom to slide around positions more easily, but happily Irish music is all in 1st. So, if holding it up with the chin causes me pain, and is unecessary, then my simple solution was to hold it up with my hand. Then, the shoulder rest became an optional extra.

Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

Yes, IMHO. First lesson as adult (with retired symphony player), I was told my neck is too high, and I should get a Mach 1 shoulder rest and a fairly high (2-3 cm tall) chinrest. Made a great improvement. Now the Mach 1 is set up quite high on the treble side, so as to bring the fiddle into an idealized playing position. However, it puts the “feet” at a position where if I bear down with my chin too much, the rest will topple over and fall off. I added more postal elastics which helps, but occasionally I get frustrated and play without the rest, after which the skin on my collarbone area is reddened, and my neck is sore. So I can say that the high chin rest and shoulder rest combination helps reduce neck pain. As far as reducing tone quality, I feel that since the “feet” of the shoulder rest clip over the edges of the fiddle (a mostly non-vibrational area) the Mach 1 does not hinder tone. YMMV

Re: Fiddlers: Does a high chin rest help reduce neck & shoulder pain?

Bit late to the party here but very interesting to read everyone’s thoughts and experiences.
Not intending to be prescriptive, and I agree with others’ advice about getting professional advice where the aches/pains are significant/persistent, but my own experience is as someone who always used a Wolf shoulder test originally (I started on classical violin) and who moved over to a piece of quite firm foam after 6-7 years, which I have been using for the subsequent 9 years.
This coincides roughly with when I really moved over to focussing on traditional fiddle playing (and at the time I was obsessed with Kevin Burke, who uses a similar foam pad), though as I remember it the reason I changed the shoulder rest was because I was getting some neck ache and was concerned that I was getting too ‘set’ in my posture because of how ultra-stable the Wolf rest held the instrument against me, and had heard some thoughts from others that align with some of what’s been said here, about how a more dynamic setup might help me avoid this sort of locking-up.
So now I just have the foam pad (I can’t remember the brand but can find out if helpful) held on with a rubber band, giving comfort and a little height at the collar-bone end of things whilst still leaving the fiddle quite free to move and pivot, and once I got used to this I found it quite liberating. Plus I can still change positions, on the relatively rare occasions that I now do so - not perfectly, but I could never do it completely smoothly before anyway, on the way down at least…
Overall, I think changing my setup like this made me more aware of how I was using the different parts of my body, because I could no longer take it for granted that the fiddle would just ‘stay’ once in position, and that helped me to kind of relax and trust the instrument to move a bit. Again, none of this may be the solution for you, but I thought it might be helpful to hear.
