What do you see for public playing of Irish traditional music 10 or 20 years out?

It seems that there are fewer young people playing this music these days. Maybe, because I’m and old codger, and probably a crotchety one, it just seems that way. Even so, I attend a lot of workshops, regular sessions, home sessions, concerts, and so forth - the audience and players are mostly old folks like me, even though all are welcome. What will happen to this tradition in the long term?

There was a time when participant-played music was the norm at any significant event - people brought instruments; people played, people danced, it was expected, it was assumed. Will this become a bygone tradition? Has it already?

It seems the norm now, with regard to playing music, for it first to be determined: ‘Is/are the player(s) good enough to be playing for other people?’ Playing this music used to be a participatory activity, now it seems to be viewed almost entirely as a performance activity. Playing for dancers and for the enjoyment of all isn’t intended to be a performance, it’s intended to be a collaboration - ideally, to create something greater than the sum of the parts, to bring the participants together in a way that can’t be artificially induced.

Do you think this tradition has staying power in this modern age?

↳ Posted by Mark Huppert on May 9th, 2015.

Re: What do you see for public playing of Irish traditional music 10 or 20 years out?

An advantage Irish traditional music has is that it’s quite tangible and yet still malleable. It’s sorta easy for the person wanting to play it to wrap his or her head around what that means, and it’s malleable like a rubber band, meaning that it’s stretchable - to a certain point - but it also bounces back close to its original form post-experimentation.

Will it survive as a folk music? Literally? No. It could be argued that it hasn’t quite been a “folk” music for some time now. It’s probably more in the category of art music -- so far out of the mainstream that one has to go against the natural course of events to play it. I suspect this is becoming more and more true even in Ireland.

Toward the larger concern that “music played by ordinary people, rather than exclusive elites, is fading from popular culture”. Well, yes, this is definitely happening. 100+ years ago the only music people ever heard was made live on the spot. Playing music wasn’t considered something that only those with “God given talents” could do…it was more directly related to each individual person.

Now you have music on-demand and in the background at all times of day - on TV, in advertisements, on Spotify - so the layperson just pushes a button to hear it. Sure, this person might have tried picking up a guitar at some point or took some piano lessons as a kid, but they weren’t able to instantly make it sound like the recording so they quickly gave up.

There definitely is the pressure and assumption that if you are going to play music today that it must be “good” and that your goal was to entertain me the audience. It’s not seen as a viable amateur hobby like golf or knitting or TV watching. This is fueled by an American-Idol mentality that places too much emphasis on the singing voice and the need to instantly impress.

Enjoy the fact that you can make your own music!

Being a still young player(24 years old), I will say that I love this music, and picking it up has been one of the best decisions I’ve ever made in my life. The music is good, the music is great. Irish music is very interesting, and brings refreshing new music to those that discover it.

I will admit this though… The only thing I don’t like about going to sessions, festivals, and dances, is that most of the women I meet are at least 10 years older than me, and there’s hardly ever anyone less than 5 years older than me. Re: What do you see for public playing of Irish traditional music 10 or 20 years out?

The ITM scene in Belfast is very healthy indeed.

Children in primary school (alas mainly only Catholic ones) learn Irish tunes on the whistle as standard. Many schools have trad groups - including our children’s secondary school, which has a large and enthusiastic group which performed to great appreciation at a recent spring concert. There are lessons in the city in many venues, including two cultural centres near to us. More expensive instruments can be hired - my daughter has an excellent harp on hire for a year for a very modest sum.

There are many welcoming venues across Belfast, and beyond, for all skill levels, and ITM support groups that are organised to encourage good learning, with many young people (and not so young) taking part. For anyone who wants to get involved, they are pushing against an open door here!
