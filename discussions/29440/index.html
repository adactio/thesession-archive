The Session reels with bowings indicated

For fiddlers beginning to play reels, the discussions of reel bowings are not much use. It’s fine to write ‘I bow it differently every time through’, but that is of little use to someone who needs some models to get started with. There is one common pattern (three notes slurred between 2 groups of four--e.g. the 3rd, 4th and 5th on an upbow) mentioned, but that isn’t enough. Has anyone noticed if there are any posted tunes (reels) where bowings are indicated (whether from a well-known recording or from the poster)? I’d appreciate link to these or the names of the reels. Thanks!

Re: The Session reels with bowings indicated

There’s certain places where you could mark bow direction but these points will be obvious to an experienced player. There’s whole phrases in between where the bowing is variable for every note and combination of notes. When slurs don’t work change direction. Sometimes your bowing traps you somewhere where you have to learn a new technique to get through it. Happens to me in the last phrase of the first part of foxhunter’s where you have to hit a drone note on a lower string with an upbow or be damned. I usually have to try different things until it works, and again, if slurs don’t work, change directions more. For tricky phrases I change direction for every note if it holds the phrase together, though I try to keep it no longer than 5 notes, while holding my slurs to the same limit as well (roughly, don’t actually count slurred and switched notes while you play for god’s sake!).

Re: The Session reels with bowings indicated

Start phrases on a downbow at first. You will only find after learning many many tunes that some phrases should be started with an upbow. Then slur upbow until you want to accent something and draw the bow quickly downward (to make up for lost bow length). Once you master this you can practice accenting with an upbow if you want, but most of the time accented notes are bowed down. The first and last notes of most tunes will be bowed down most of the time, if that note is a resolved note. But really the point is to play around until you find what works. It would probably take an observer to accurately mark down my bowings because I can’t remember them often after I’ve just played them, it’s a process by which the intuition rules in the moment. You’re not going to discover that aspect of playing the fiddle if you follow bow marks. Though I admire the ability to anybody who can sight read bow markings well, I don’t have that skill despite being a fairly good sight reader so my hat is off.

Re: The Session reels with bowings indicated

Rather than think about bowing, you have to think about the
rhythm and structure of the tune. Then try to do bowings that
bring these out. And modify the bowings for pragmatic reasons
because of string crossings or running out of room in one
direction, etc. And then modify to get drones or double stops
in certain spots or get the bow prepared for a treble in a certain
spot. Or modify for the purpose of putting in a variation …

So it’s hopeless trying to write down bowings or trying to copy them,
or trying to make up rules except for copying a general style of bowing - for example a style that has a strong backbeat or downbeat, or a style with
lots of droning. You can copy that “feel” without needing to copy all the bowings.

Re: The Session reels with bowings indicated

Bow direction isn’t what to focus on. Whether the bow is going up or down doesn’t matter--what matters is putting the emphasis in the right places so you create a pulse. And you want to be able to do that with up *and* down bows.

Are there bowing patterns common among Irish fiddlers? Yes, but the patterns themselves don’t help much if you don’t already have a feel for where the emphasis needs to be. And the patterns aren’t typically used steadily through a tune like they might be by some old timey or bluegrass fiddlers (who often latch on to the back beat with a Georgia shuffle and don’t let go).

I’ve said this before, but I think it’s worth repeating here …

A lot of people ask about where they should put slurs, i.e. where not to change direction with the bow. I think this is completely the opposite of what to ask. My advice is to play a tune without changing direction at all. Just one big long bow stroke. Then see if you can hear where you think you need a little accent. And gradually build it up from nothing.

You put accents in, you don’t leave them out.

Re: The Session reels with bowings indicated

Patterns? You betcha.
Right off the bat James plays a down-bow one, up-bow three pattern (thinking in terms of eighth notes) repeatedly. That’s exactly the sort of thing Irish fiddlers do a lot of, interspersed with single bows and other sorts of slurs. Kevin Burke also does that pattern a lot, but he flips the bow direction (up-bow one, down-bow three).

The point to that pattern is that the single bow lands on either the downbeat or the back beat, and the 3 slurred notes stitch the phrase together, typically “across the bar lines”--because the phrases weren’t developed with bar lines in mind.

Re: The Session reels with bowings indicated

I think one of the most important and interesting things in good phrasing is playing through the bar lines. And not just the bar lines, but from one part to the next, from the last part back to the beginning and even from one tune to the next.

The idea is that you accent wherever the tune takes you or you take the tune. And you mix up the accents, vary it.

Listen to how good flute players stream through phrases with one long breath, and then chop it up a bit by taking a few short “sips” of air in odd places close together.

Learn to accent the way a flute player would by lifting the bow off the strings for a fraction and then before whacking back in with your change of direction. Let your fiddle playing breath.

Re: The Session reels with bowings indicated

Michael, I pointed out Burke’s penchant for hammering away on the backbeat with up bows only because he’s so consistent (or relentless, as you put it). And he does it far more than most.

Sky fiddler, just to get the feel for it, you can do the one-twothreefour bowing all the way through the C part of Morning Dew, but put the first “one” on the *third* eighth note in the first measure. Then stick with that pattern until it feels natural.

|:BAGF EFGA|BAGB ADFA|BAGF EFGA|BcdB ADFA:|
Try it:
(up BA) (down G) (up FEF) (down G) (up ABA) (down G) (up BAD) (down F) (up ABA) (down G) (up FEF) (down G) (up ABc) (down d) (up BAD) (down F) (up ABA) and you started the loop over again.

Then reverse the bow directions, all the way through.

You can see why this sort of pattern gives a nice strong back beat--you have to draw a quick bow on the single notes because you’re making up all the hair from three notes slurred on the next bow stroke. That quick draw creates an accent.

But this example also shows how easily the pattern becomes monotonous when overused--most of those single note bows land on the G, so you’re pounding on the G every four notes. Yes, it’s a lovely spot to put an accent, but if you accent every G, and bow it the same way, you’ve turned a melody line into a MIDI mess….
