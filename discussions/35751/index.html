Re: Oak tree for inspection

[*I don;t understand how some players play apparently straight (in terms of no swing) but still have good flow?*]

Arthur, I think a few things need clearing up here. There is *no such thing* as ‘no swing’ (in any music), apart from a MIDI file, where the note durations are mathematically identical.

‘Swing’ is a just a general term to describe the relative differences in note time values. Take a simple example - an 8-note bar in reel time. Some of the notes will be fractionally longer than the others, often in the pattern long-short-long-short etc. It matters not what type of tune it is or what fiddler is playing the tune - there will always be a difference in note lengths. Admittedly a very small difference, and it’s not particularly noticeable as an entity in itself - but take that element away, and immediately the sound becomes more like it’s being generated by a machine and not by a human.

It’s *degrees* of swing (among other elements) that play a major part in how a particular player can make a tune sound. The Donegal style, containing lots of one-note-per bow phrases (technically) has less ‘swing’ than other styles, eg those which contain a lot of slurring, or grouping several note in one bow stroke. The ‘slurring’ also amplifies the element of ‘swing’ as well, because there’s a tiny added time gap taken up by the bow returning in the opposite direction, thus lengthening / shortening the sub-divided notes contained in the slur even more.

So, all the players you listen to - Tara Breen, Johhny Doherty, Evelyn Healy and everyone else - they all ‘swing’ to some degree.

Arthur, that’s the whole point. In a way you are being ‘too accurate’ in The Oak Tree (it was suggested earlier on that you sounded a bit MIDI-ish). It was not meant pejoratively, as it was just an observation - but there’s a bit of truth in it.

Piece said “Go listen to a lot of fiddlers with real swing and drive to their playing, and learn learn learn.”
Now, that’s good advice generally, but it won’t work for everybody. Which is why I’m trying to get right down to basic mechanics here to show how to put swing and drive into tunes.

You already know about accents (varying bow pressure, bow lengths and all that stuff). Now, on top of that, you need to incorporate the varying note lengths that make up ‘swing’.

Go back to the Oak Tree and concentrate on just the first bar. Forget *all* ornaments for the moment. Play the first note a little bit longer, second note a little shorter, third note longer, fourth note shorter. Play it really slowly (use your metro or click track if you want). The time difference between the notes will probably sound a bit exaggerated, but that’s normal.

Once you’re comfortable with that, crank up the tempo a bit.

If you keep to this pattern, and have the relative note lengths correct, I guarantee you’ll begin to sound more ‘swingy’ and ‘driven’.

Once again, I repeat : ‘swing’ comes from slightly differing note durations. ‘Swing’ can also be enhanced by the use of other devices (bow pressure, bow travel, grouping of 2+ notes per bow stroke [slurring], off-the beat change of bow direction, rests [missing out a note completely]).

Re: Oak tree for inspection

Arthur, this is my interpretation of the swing that I look for and what I understand lilt to be…

The way I think about it is like talking. Every note is a syllable, every short melodic phrase is a word, and the phrases tied together are sentences(rhythmic dynamics being the grammar markings: commas, exclamation points, question marks, hyphens etc… and then the musical accent dynamics: how we prounce the letters. How we use these things when we speak determine our overall accent.)

It’s not that we have different swing/accent because we are imperfect(because perfect grammar doesn’t equal perfect accent). We have different swing because we choose to say the “syllables” differently. To me, the “lilt” is in how we say these syllables.

When you’re still learning how to talk you may not be able to express a distinct accent because you’re still learning how to use your tongue, your lips, your voice, your breath. So no, you may not be able to emulate an accent even though you know what it sounds like.

As we grow up, we develop a style, a rhythm in our speech, an accent. Immerse yourself and you’ll take on the accent. Because just like your throat and mouth have to learn how to use your words; your fingers, hands, arms, still need to learn how to play the notes. Don’t delude yourself, we eventually gain perfect control over how we *say the notes. When we have this choice, is when we are finally able to express our style.

ii) Stop trying to learn tunes that take your fancy. Most trad musicians teach essentially the same fifty to a hundred tunes to beginners for a reason:
they’re melodically simple, and have obvious phrasing and rhythm patterns that are easy to manipulate to generate pulse and lift. Again, this’ll help with a) and b);

iii) Stop trying to learn and introduce ornaments. It’s far too early. If you can’t generate lift and musical interest in a tune played plain, adding ornaments (as evidenced by your recording above) is only going to make things worse;

iv) Stop trying to emulate particular musicians or play in a regional “style”. It’s like trying to paint like Rembrandt without knowing how to prepare a canvas or mix paints;

v) You seem to have Matt’s book. Why not stick with the tunes and sequencing in that? At one to two hours a day for five to six hours a week it’d take someone in your situation perhaps two years to master the material;

vi) Then perhaps pick up Seamus’ double CD “Tunes for Practice”. Arranged in common sets, he plays tunes first plain and then with simple variation and ornamentation, all at an easy pace. There’s easily twelve months of study there alone;

vii) Take any opportunity to learn to set dance. It’ll do wonders for a) above.

Learning music, to whatever standard, in whatever circumstances is a great thing. Lots of practicing and listening are the watchwords given to beginners on this board. If only it were that simple. The real trick is knowing what to listen for, what to practice, how to practice and in what sequence.

Fortunately these things can be developed. Lilting I think will help a great deal. Think in terms of “phrases” and not in terms of getting the notes out there.

I wouldn’t worry about what Tara Breen or anyone else does. There is only one Tara Breen, and there is only one Arthur Gondolor. The things that will most help you be a better player at this point are not the things that will make you sound like Tara. In fact, I would anticipate that there are some things that will take you further away from her kind of style and towards a more flowing, fluid style with longer bows. At least for a time, as you develop more of a sense of where the phrases are.

You don’t yet seem to have internalized the rhythmic structure of the tune. You know where the beat is, most of the time… you just throw an extra beat or two in from time to time. I believe this is rooted in not quite understanding the musical phrases, the call and response, and how the phrases interact and balance one another.

Foot tapping may help, if you don’t already do it. Try alternating foot taps. Yeah, the classical types will tell you not to do it. But this is dance music. If you can’t get a toe tapping, what’s the point?

Some folks get distracted when they first start foot tapping, but if you stick with it it, but I think movement helps.

A programmable metronome with an audibly different sound on the “one” may help. It will keep you honest if you accidentally add a beat or two at the end of a section.

Mostly I would say, slow down, don’t worry about ornaments… AT ALL. (I think they have a tendency to take care of themselves once you have the foundation down, anyway). Concentrate on playing the melody very plainly, wistfully, musically and in time, articulating phrases, not notes.

Slow down as much as you need to -- even to the point where it’s not even recognizable as music… to get it right.

Get some one on one instruction from someone who knows what you’re trying to do. This might not be from your local Suzuki teacher. I know James Kelly has put a lot of thought into helping players address the very issues you’re facing, but there are others as well.

Once you master these things, then you may be able to execute a more staccato-type sound like Tara Breen does so well. But I think you will need to take a step or two backward and master some more fundamental things before you move in that direction… if indeed you choose to do so.

“Phrasing is how you accentuate the tune in order to make that structure apparent to the listener.”

Yep. Without an accented beat, the ear doesn’t understand what the music is doing. That accented beat determines whether or not we percieve something as a jig, reel, hornpipe etc.

Here’s a graphic example in a generic sense(a very generic sense).

123
1234
123456
12345678

Reel
1234, 5678: 1234, 5678
Polka
12, 34: 56, 78: 12, 34: 56, 78
Hornpipe
1, 23, 45, 67, 8: 1, 23, 45, 67, 8

Waltz
123: 123: 123: 123
Jig
123, 456: 123, 456
Slip-Jig
123, 456, 789: 1!23, 456, 789

See how all the numbers are the same, but the placement of the commas and colons give them a rhythmic character?

So Arthur --

This is all good advice, but I just want to add something very fundamental to an understanding of “phrasing,” or at least my understanding. Pardon me if you feel I’m over-simplifying it, but I’ll point out that a “phrase” is just what it says -- a grouping of notes within the tune. How you interpret that phrase, how you bow it or accentuate its parts, is how you bring musicality to the tune. But it helps to think of a tune, or any kind of music, as a series of interrelated units, or phrases.

What you’re doing in the last tune you posted is breaking the music up by pausing between phrases. That’s different from what you were doing with the first tune, and it sounds way better.

You can think of phrases as questions and answers. Like, the first phrase -- say it might cover the first two measures of a tune -- acts like a “question.” The next phrase -- which might cover the next two measures -- is the answer to the first phrase.

And if you put multiple phrases together, they can combine to make larger questions and answers. Like, think of a tune’s A part as being answered by the B part.

@Arthur There is a lot going on in McGinley’s video. If it sounds ‘straight’ to you, then you need to listen much more carefully. If you can’t hear the phrasing when people are playing tunes, there is little hope of being able to reproduce it in your playing. Phrasing is a lot more that simply accentuating certain beats. The timing shifts in rather subtle ways, and the shifts depend on the specific phrase being played. The way each note of the phrase is presented relative to the other notes builds the point of the phrase. Not every note is as important as every other one -- and, some can be added, and some can be left out. Also, the important notes that create the phrase are not always the ones that are ‘accented’. You really need to listen very carefully to appreciate the point of the phrase before you start trying to recreate it in your playing. The swing aspect is actually the trivial part -- and you can still recognize a tune when it is played with different amounts of swing.

As with the jig, you should start with something very simple and work on it until you get it mostly right. Pigeon on the Gate is actually not very complicated, although I don’t think the one McGinley gives is the best version to start with. I suggest you start with something even simpler -- like Merry Backsmith. That has fairly easy to understand phrasing.

And, by the way, you still have a lot of work to do on jigs. It can take a very long time, with careful listening, to really get inside the phrasing. There is an enormous advantage when you grow up hearing these tunes all the time. Do not underestimate how much effort it takes to make these things feel and sound natural. But it can be a fun and rewarding journey.

Re: Oak tree for inspection

Don’t get stuck on using bow change to emphasize notes. Notes are emphasized in lots of different ways -- and in fact a very useful (and common) technique is to purposefully slur into the downbeat.

There is simply no substitute for learning the music. Listen carefully to how people play the tunes. Do the simple tunes first. Play them until you can make the phrases sing or weep, fast and slow. Listen listen listen to different people play the tunes. Play along with people who play the proper phrasing -- either with a teacher, or at a session, or with a recording. Focus on the phrasing --- getting the ‘right’ notes at the right time. Don’t get stuck on bowing patterns. Listen, listen, and listen some more. Don’t get stuck analyzing the details. Train your ears (and your head and heart) to recognize what sounds/feels right -- then the rest follows.

It takes a lot of work, and shortcuts are fraught with pitfalls. But it can be really fun and rewarding work -- which is why we call it playing.

@Jerone < what about in schools such as scottish and donegal where slurring isnt used much at all? Surely if there isnt much slurring then the accent/phrasing whatever must be brought out in some other way?>

The whole idea of schools or regional styles in Irish music is overblown. Perhaps this is a topic for another thread. Clearly there are leading Donegal fiddlers who love to use slurs, and there are people outside of Donegal who prefer to use few slurs. For the past 100 years there has been sufficient exchange of music that the idea of a regional style is now largely inappropriate. I am not sure they ever really existed. Martin Hayes likes to tell the story of the famous ‘Clare style’ -- of which he is often named as the leading example. He said he was largely influenced by his father and Paddy Canny -- who played in extremely different styles, but were both from Clare. He says that when people started visiting Clare to hear the true ‘Clare style’, the local people were more surprised than anyone -- since they never thought there was such a thing as a ‘Clare style’. But, they went along with it (perhaps because they liked to get visitors seeking this imaginary style). Who is true Clare -- PJ Hayes or Paddy Canny? Is Tommy Peoples real Donegal -- or maybe Johnny Doherty? In the end it comes down to individuals. People have styles -- regions much less so.

The music was largely changed by the explosion of recordings in the early 20th century. All over Ireland people were listening to Coleman -- and copying him. Then you had the mixing that went on in such places as New York, Boston, Liverpool, London, Dublin, etc. Everyone influences everyone else. And these effects have only accelerated over time.

Good luck trying to find the differences as they existed before music freely moved from region to region. I doubt there is much influence left from those days.

The slurs all occur on up-bows, and are preceded by a down-bow. So, as the notes are generally all of the same duration, you need a longer bow for the down-bow, then on the slurred return, you should end up at roughly the same point on the bow.

Two results from this - you keep good bow balance, and there’s a bit of an accent when the down-bow is longer.

Bowing into the triplet, bowing out of the triplet,( more common I think but I could be wrong) adding cuts and taps to the triplet, separating bowed notes with cuts and taps, long and short rolls, never mind imitating piping ornaments like crans or backstitching, are some of the incredible range of articulations possible in Irish fiddle music. I think it would behoove you, Arthur, to explore these on your own rather than to rely on faceless internet posters like myself. Nor should you assume that when someone tells you that a certain technique “isn’t part of Donegal style fiddling” that they know what they’re typing about or that you can’t use that technique in your playing if it suits your musical needs.
Take a single phrase from a tune you know and try every way of slurring that you can. Try playing the entire phrase with a single bow- difficult but very valuable , both for bow control and left hand articulation. Try slurring from up-beats to down-beats, with up-strokes and down strokes, two, three or more notes to a bow.. Above all you need to listen, to hear how each articulation affects the sound of the phrase, its internal rhythmic accents as well as the overall flow of the melody.
Another thing that would help you, since by your own admission you have a hard time hearing slurs, is to listen to some tunes while following along with an accurate, full transcription, one that includes bowings. When you hear a bit, a phrase or even a measure that sounds interesting or similar to the style you like, try playing it using the bowing indicated and try to sound as close to the recording as possible. Ultimately, of course, you don’t want to slavishly imitate any player, but you can learn a lot this way. Slow-down software can be helpful, too.
One other thing- I noticed you, and a couple of other posters, referred to “seeing the slurs” in video clips. I don’t think this is particularly helpful because 1-the camera angle doesn’t always allow you to clearly see the bow, 2- these tunes are played so fast, and often with very small bow movements, that it’s not always possible to see the bowing, and 3- You tube videos are are often notoriously out of sync with the sound. You really have to rely on your ears, so start training them.
