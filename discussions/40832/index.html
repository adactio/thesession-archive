Complex or poly rhythms in normal reels (over 4/4 time)

I am trying to find out more about complex or poly rhythms used in normal (4/4 rhythm) reels.
In other words, during a normal 4/4 rhythm reel, the guitar or bodhrán is occasionally playing a different rhythm.
Here is an example that I found in “Celtic Back-up” by Chris Smith:
Assume guitar is playing rhythm: each character is an 8th note where
v = down-strum
^ = up-strum and
- = no-strum (the example was for guitar)
In 4/4 eight notes: v - ^ v - ^ v - (counted: 1 - 2 and - and 4)
This would be followed by a more normal strum pattern: v - v ^ v ^ v ^ (1 - 2 and 3 and 4 and)
It give a syncopated complexity to parts of a normal real; the rest of the group played normal 4/4.
That is, it briefly created a rhythmic tension that resolves in the next bar or two. It’s a nice technique.

Here is an example that bluegrass banjo players use (sometimes excessively). They pick every 8th note as an arpeggio in this pattern (each number denotes a string):
3 2 1 3 2 1 3 1 (or something similar) You could count the 8th notes off as: 1 2 3 1 2 3 1 2. This is actually a poly rhythm because it is a 3 3 2 8th notes rhythm over the normal 4/4 rhythm.

In rock and blues the guitarist will sometimes play 4 bars of 3/4 rhythm which syncs up with the 4/4 after 3 4/4 bars: 3+3+3+3 = 12 beats and 4+4+4 =12 beats. This is (slightly) common in modern Chicago blues (Stevie Ray used it pretty often).

I would appreciate if anyone could describe Irish rhythm techniques or point me to a source where the rhythm instrument plays poly or complex rhythms when the rest of the group plays 4/4. Are there any traditional, acceptable, or common sub-rhythms used by a rhythm player, or is it totally an individual technique?
I apologize for the lengthly description. I wanted it to be clear that I am not talking about complex rhythms where the whole band plays (for example) 7/8 or 15/8.

If you look at the course of secular Irish music over the centuries, it appears to start with song then instrumentation is add. I am guessing it started with whistles and pipes. The fiddle could not have appeared more than about three hundred years ago. I also assume tunes were played solo more often than not, for a long time.

Using early recordings as a guide, we don’t hear the guitar until quite recently. Unfortunately, in many cases the guitar players are untutored, by themselves or anyone else. This standard of accompaniment is wide spread and I believe it originates with the popularity of the guitar in the folk revival of the 1950-60s. Because of the ease of first position guitar chords, almost anyone could strum along.

Again unfortunately, this has meant many guitarists try to follow ever beat of the lead instument, which gives the tunes a rushed feeling that ( I believe) is not the intention of the music. For example, Michael Coleman’s records with the piano, do not exhibit this rushed feeling.

The understanding of poly-rhythms is a great asset to any musician. It does not have to be complex. Guitar players in particular would contribute more to a tune, if they played less. Just halving what they strum would make the music less “muddy.”

“I think people are confusing syncopation with polyrhythm. Different animals.” - Posted by Jason_Van_Steenwyk

Warning: Readers who dislike over-technical discussions should skip this comment. It will probably give you a headache.

I agree that syncopation and polyrhythm are different terms. And I am glad that someone invented those terms.
But I think we should give people a little slack. Because those terms can be ambiguous when applied to real (or reel) examples.
If you will forgive me, here is a long winded explanation of why the difference is not always apparent. Syncopation and polyrhythm are terms that make communication easier.
But, just like all my marriages, it is complicated. The same tune can exhibit syncopation or polyrhythm depending on what the performers do. In many cases only the composer knows what was intended. And they may not even care.

The terms:
-- Polyrhythm is the simultaneous use of two or more differing rhythms, usually requiring two instruments.
-- Syncopation occurs when a temporary displacement of the regular metrical accent occurs.

Some simple examples:
-- Two instruments playing two distinct rhythms is definitely poly-rhythm. Example: (V v v V v v V v over V v v v V v v v). Very common in bluegrass. But also found in some Celtic tunes: Frank’s, The High Drive Reel, etc.
-- Vertical hemiola (V v v V v v over V v V v V v ) is a true poly-rhythm.

Cultural bias adds some confusion:
-- Horizontal hemiola (V v v V v v V v V v V v) is (usually) syncopation because (usually) all instruments are playing the same rhythm, but it is also thought of as a horizontal poly-rhythm. However, if you are a Flamenco (V v v V v v V v V v V v) or a rock-and-roller (v - V - v - V -), you would not consider it syncopation because these are normal rhythms to your culture.
-- People refer to the “backbeat transformation” (v - V - v - V -) common in rock-and-roll as syncopation. But the “backbeat transformation” has regular metrical accents and it is not a temporary displacement. Unless, you consider the lyrics as the regular metrical accent (V - v - v - v -). But then it would be considered polyrhythm.
-- Afro-cuban rhythms add more confusion.

Now let’s add even more confusion.
-- Let’s say the fiddle is playing this rhythm for part of a reel | V v v V v v V v | (Examples: Frank’s, The High Drive Reel, The Countess Cathleen, Catharsis). If all the instruments are play in unison it is technically syncopation. But if the bodhrán or guitar is playing a normal reel 4/4 rhythm throughout, then it is polyrhythmic.
-- Let’s say a bodhrán is playing this rhythm segment: | v - ^ V ^ - | (one bar of a jig). Some players (most?) think of this as syncopation. But I know of at least one bodhrán player who thinks in poly-rhythms. In this case (v - - V - - over v - ^ - ^ -) that is (3-3 over 2-2-2: vertical hemiola). I have seen both of these ways was of looking at percussion documented in jazz, rock, blues, Asian Indian, Persian and Arabic music literature. I am pretty sure that Irish percussionists also have different ways of conceptualizing what they are doing, but I haven’t done that research. Frankly, I don’t think it matters.

In the end, I have no idea what Frank’s, The High Drive Reel, The Countess Cathleen, Catharsis are supposed to be. They fit the terms syncopation or polyrhythm depending on how they are performed. If you could interview the composer, you could tie it down, but the composer could have been comfortable with ambiguity.

God gave us mathematics and octaves so we could know some absolute truths. But God also made sure we would never get bored. Even perfect fourths and fifths are not perfect.

Hello AB: “”Trip to Pakistan“ . . . appreciate any enlightenment you may have about how to play it with polyrhythms.”

[[[[ :-) Everything mentioned below is to be applied with taste, discretion, musicality, not excessively, and not as a gimmick or trick. And probably should not be used much in sessions unless it is part of the melody. And, this is not “modern” stuff. Many cultures have used these techniques and rhythms for many hundreds of years. I suspect, also in Ireland, but don’t know that as fact. :-) ]]]]

I hope the following answers your question. I’ll be a little verbose in case some other readers are totally unfamiliar with this stuff. If you are solo playing just the melody, much of the tune (but not all) has a 12312312 syncopated rhythm. It is most obviously apparent in measure 11 of (measure 3 of part C)
https://thesession.org/tunes/112
DFAD FADF. As and exercise count in eighth notes 12312312, Try accenting on ‘3’ (the A notes). Then try accenting on ‘1’ (the D notes). You’ll get two distinct but compatible rhythms.

A quick listening to (I might not have it exactly correct, but you’ll get the idea)
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RmS1Wwyidm0
They are mixing it up a lot (but in some parts) the cello is playing a rhythm like.
V - - v v V - - | V - - v v V - - | V - - v v V - - |V - - -V - - - | (the last measure is typical 4/4: (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + )
1 - - 1 2 3 - - | 1 - - 1 2 3 - - | 1 - - 1 2 3 - - | 1 - - - 3 - - - |
While the fiddle is (in some parts):
v v V v v V - - | v v V v v V - - | . . .
1 2 3 1 2 3 - - | 1 2 3 1 2 3 - - | . . .
but it mostly 12312312 (eight notes) where sometimes the ‘1’ is accented and sometimes the ‘3’.
then every 4th measure perhaps doing v v v v V - - -

I think if you play around with the C part
|:FGBF GBGF|EFGE FGFE|DFAD FADF|AGFG E4:|
you will start to feel the syncopation. Then if someone else jumps in, accenting different notes you have a polyrhythm. The other person could do just straight 4/4 rhythm and you would still have two distinct rhythms.

After you do 12312312 rhythms for a whole it becomes natural and you just feel it. Usually not every note is played. Depending on the musical interpretation you can do patterns like:
| v v V v v V - - |
| V - v V - v V - |
