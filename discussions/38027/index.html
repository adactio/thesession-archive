Decomposing composings

The current couple of topics about memorising and learning music has sidetracked my thoughts towards composers. When it comes to learning and playing a tune I consider myself to be a very able ‘imitator’ (probably the Chimpanzee in me), and at best can only claim to add a bit of my own flavour. But composing original music, as much as I have struggled, seems to be a magical and mysterious feat that lies beyond my capabilities. So, all you composers… Please explain….how the hell do you do it? Is there any formula or trickery, or does it just come into your head like magic?

Mostly, my compositions are light jazz vocals, ballads or contemporary Christian and not ITM. For those cases, I find a nice set of chord progressions and fit the lyrics/tune to it. For the very few times I’ve written a tune that could conceivably called ITM, the tunes were borne of mistakes made while playing something else or just completely fubaring a practice scale. The one and only time I specifically wrote a tune from scratch was a waltz that I wrote for my wife and I borrowed Jay Ungar’s style extensively since I’m not a violin player and the waltz was intended for the violin. I suppose a purist would just state that I plagiarized Jay’s style and I can’t say that I didn’t. Still, it’s a lovely tune, my wife loves it and the local pub band that used to be a beginners session plays it occasionally for bar patrons that want to slow dance. I doubt it will propagate more than 4 miles from my house so I’m not too worried about it coming to anyone’s trademark sensitive ears.

I could say that if you have a deep desire to originate something - whistle while you work. There’s a much more flexible connection to the creative subconscious when you’re occupied with doing something else and your lips are a very capable instrument. If you find yourself whistling something new and worthwhile, then you need to document it by some fashion fairly quickly.

Re: Decomposing composings

It’s magic sometimes(no sarcasm), but a systematic and methodical person like me, I simply put together a combination of melodies and harmonies that I would like to express. People thinks it takes the magic and genuiness out of the music, but that’s not true. As long as the inspiration is there, the magic is still there.

For example, say that I just got engaged to be married. She said yes. My dream is coming true. I know she’s the one. I’m happier than I’ve ever been. But it’s not an ecstatic, hyper, excited joy as it was in the moment of the proposal. It’s a sweet, warm, loving joy, as it’s been say 3 hours since she said yes, and I’ve already called all of my most beloved family and friends and I’ve calmed down a bit. Now it’s just me and the piano, left alone to ourselves.

The first thing I decide is, what kind of piece do I wanna write? A tune, a solo piece, or a full on song? For this occasion, I would choose an instrumental ballad. A solo piano piece with a story telling melody line and progressive harmonics in an “Intro, Verse, Bridge, Chorus, Ending” style, Free Form and open to interpretation with breathing varying tempos.

I probably already have some melodic ideas floating around my head and between my fingers so next is choosing a key(Not very crucial, but will help focus my ideas. This could change and will transition throughout the music for this occasion.) Something smooth and warm. I’m thinking DM, or C#M. Absolutely lovely keys with a lot of potential for some nice mixed 7th chords and bright transitions.

Next, which is probably the most important part, is I choose my melodic themes. While the key and harmonic progressions give the music it’s overall personality and feel, melodic themes will give a composition form, order, and direction. Without a musical idea, it will sound empty, plain, and monotonous with no direction, no matter how complicated the music. Themes are both in the contour of the melody AND the rhythm of the melody. Irish tunes are great examples of music with very strong melodies.

How I come up with melodies is hard to explain. I pretty much just play around with scales and arpeggios until I find something that sticks and resonates with how I feel. Getting that first melody is the hardest, but for me, the stronger the inspiration, the easier it comes. Naturally, the melodies come from my “in-mind” music collections, a mental reservoir of music that has influenced me over the years. Any other themes and melodies will generate from the first one, as well as any transitions, in keeping with the overall theme and direction of the composition.

Once you learn what you like to hear, and what you want to come out of you, you can write music on the fly. It took a while for me to get there, but I’m here. I can play and write whatever I want, no matter what emotion I’m under. But I only compose with the intent of the music matching the idea/emotion.

My method is influenced by teachings from advanced music theory and composition, as well as the famous jazz teaching, “Play how you feel”. I’m proud to say that in the last 5 years I’ve composed about a dozen pieces that I am very proud of. Best of all, they all sound different and unique.

I hope this helps! If I can find some of my most influential resources, I’ll be sure to post them! Happy playing!

Re: Decomposing composings

I think Jerone’s description seems like the process used but several composers I know. It takes a high level of familiarity with ‘music’ to be able to do that well -- including a good sense of musicality.

The easier it is for you to turn what you hear in your head into the notes you play, the more efficient the process will be. A larger step to take is to cause musical ideas or feelings to flow into notes, perhaps even without hearing them in your head first. A lot of composers do this with their instrument, letting the ideas flow fairly freely, and reshaping them once they hear them. It often starts with a seed, and grows from there. A lot of composers collect seeds and store them (in their head or some other medium), until the time when they find a suitable mood or feeling or occasion to develop them into a composition.

Intervals.

Hey man, unfortunately it looks like my old music journal hasn’t stood the test of time too well. The cover is gone and it’s missing several pages. It’s full of watermarks, rips and tears, and the poor thing is falling apart. My first entry in that journal was the notes I took from my piano teacher giving me the basics of composition. It may be gone, or lost in my junk, but I won’t have the notes from that page today. If I ever come across it, I’ll be sure to pm you on it.

I do remember however that the main point he made that day was that the most important thing was to have a musical idea when composing. Something you wanted to say musically. Sticking with that idea and not letting it drift and go on tangents. He also talked about Tension, which I’m about to touch on now, with intervals.

There is one music lesson I’ll never forget, and that is when I learned about intervals. The awesome thing is, I had this lesson twice, once with my piano teacher and once with the school band director in music theory class!

We’re told that intervals are the space between two notes. I was taught that intervals are the relationship between two notes. Let’s look into this a little deeper…

…An interval is the relationship between two notes. In other words, an interval is how one note relates to another note. What is this relationship? Tension. Consonance and Dissonance. Do these notes sit well together in harmony, or do they clash? Is that melody begging to be resolved, or does the last note leave it fulfilled?

What does this have to do with composing? Because music is about the build and release of tension. A push and pull. A give and take. A flow of energy. And different intervals have different levels of tension. Play a C/G interval and notice how clean and solid it sounds. Now play a C/F# interval and see how densely dissonant it is. This science is how we come up with motifs and cadences. So if you learn how to create a flow of tension and resolution, you can come up with your on melodies.

Back to composing pieces though, remember, keep the idea simple. You can expand it and embellish it along the way.

Re: Decomposing composings

Jerone’s posts have resonated very much with my experience as a composer. I’ll add a couple thoughts.

When I write, I think of myself as a craftsman. I am building something out of raw materials - notes, intervals, melodic fragments, lines. Harmonies are built from melodic lines and not the other way around. I tend to think very contrapuntally, and I like to see how many different things I can do with one idea. This is not the only possible approach to composition! Plenty of composers focus more on what they are trying to *say* than on what they are trying to *make*. But you see both goals all over the composition world, often in the same composer.

Another note: much of the stuff I’ve written is vocal, written to a text that I’ve chosen but someone else has already written. When that is the case, I try to let the flow of the words guide my composition.

Just to echo Nico…I have spent a lot of time composing tunes and reworking them and frankly throwing away or at least indefinitely putting aside a lot of material. So when you hear me playing a tune that I think is “ready for the world”, you are generally hearing something that has made it through a vetting process. It is something not obviously stolen, and something that hopefully balances novelty and inevitability in a way that makes people want to listen and/or play the tune. I have composed a fair number of tunes. Mostly crap. There are a few that get played around from time to time. But mostly they feel a bit contrived in retrospect. Although, I have one that I thought was crap for a long time, but I’ve been playing it again, and my ability as a better player than I was when I wrote it kind of brought it to life a bit.

I have had most of my tunes appear when I’ve been playing a lot. And usually I am just noodling on mandolin, trying to find tunes that I haven’t played for a long time. Some little hook will appear and I just build on that. So it’s a combination of both letting it flow, and guiding it.

I encourage my students to try composing. Not so that they can write a masterful tune, but so they can learn more about what makes a good tune good. And it can really give you appreciation for the genius behind some great tunes.

I think like everything else, the only way to get better at it is to keep doing it, and see what you learn in the process.
