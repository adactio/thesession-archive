Re: accompaniment chords “rules of thumb”

“A G mostly, also Em; sometimes F#m in turnaround’s”

Does this mean A major? If so, A major doesn’t play G. It can play a G# diminished. A also plays withe Emaj and Dmaj chords as they are the IV and V, with F#min being the relative minor.

Am plays F and G as the IV and V.

Dm has Bb major chord in there.

Basically, you want to play the I, IV, and V method. For the key of D; D is I, G is IV, and A is V. You can also you the relative minor, which is Bm, which is iv. Using variations is nice.

I= D, ii= Em, iii= F#m, IV= G, V= A, iv= Bm, vii= C#diminshed(or sub with C#min). You can apply that with any keys for chord progression. I, IV, V= major chords, and ii, iii, iv= minor chords.

Re: accompaniment chords “rules of thumb”

I’m a fiddler but have played with lots of guitarists. Here’s my two cents.

To me, rhythm and responsiveness are the two most important qualities in a guitar backer. And by those I don’t mean someone who, knowing the chords, puts their head down and locks into the tempo. Instead, someone who can be responsive to subtle melody dynamics -- and who supports the tempo rather than trying to drive it.

The best guitarists feel almost as if they’re not even playing “chords,” but are filling in the tune in an extremely interesting way. And then, they’re adding a new dimension somehow -- coming from not just chord changes but color and energy as they move through chords. A player might go from a G to a D, for instance, but use a few passing notes that fit perfectly and liven up that moment in the tune -- and give the melody player new inspiration.

The best guitarist I ever played with was over to the US from Ireland for a short stay. He accompanied me and a flute player on a gig, and I remember *not* wanting to change from one tune to the next in a set, because each time around was so much fun to play and to hear -- and the flute player and I had played these same sets hundreds of times.

So I guess two things:

-- If you know the tune, look for ways to simplify the progressions rather than filling them up with chord changes. Be open to modifying your approach at all times based on what the tune wants to do at the moment -- and that might even include not always automatically going for the I chord with each new pass. Be open to anything.

-- That said, for tunes you don’t know, I would say (others may differ) that I’d be good with you strumming the I chord very lightly at first to get the rhythm-feel down, and then maybe staying there until you hear the V. Then putting that in and listening for the other important chords. But all the time making tempo and feel your first priority. For instance, for an Em tune, I’d rather you vamp lightly on the Em bass strings the entire first time through, and then go about finding the D chord, etc., rather than trying to find all the chords at once.

Re: accompaniment chords “rules of thumb”

Keep things simple for the modal keys.

For example, most EDor tunes can be backed just using Em and D. Similarly, ADor tunes just with Am and G.

Most mixolydian tunes can be backed using just three chords: e.g. AMix tunes need A D and G.

In ITM, melody is king. Having said that, good guitarist can enhance a session. Conversely, bad one can drag it down.

Here’s a useful rule-of-thumb - if the chords that a guitarist is playing are noticeable against the melody, they are probably the wrong chords. Conversely, if a guitarist suddenly stops playing - and that seem to leave a big hole in the overall sound - the guitarist was probably playing the right chords.

I often use youtube for chord ideas.

Either:

- find a few versions with chord accompanyment and try to work out what they’re playing (some versions will differ in parts but you should get the main ideas)

Re: accompaniment chords “rules of thumb”

A lot of people will say unequivocally that one should never try to accompany tunes one does not know. I think this is good advice for the most part, and is generally given to discourage backers from mucking up a good set while they search for the right chords. It’s also in your own interest, since looking like a jackass for playing dissonant chords is probably the most embarrassing thing in human experience. Take it from me :)

That being said, I don’t think it’s quite right to say that you should *never* accompany tunes you don’t know. But I suppose it depends on what you mean by ‘know’. There are a great many tunes that I don’t know properly, in the sense that I probably could not spontaneously and accurately play them from start to finish, that I would still accompany with relative confidence because I’ve heard them at least once or twice before, enough to remember “what happens next”, harmonically speaking… There are also times where a tune will come up that I’ve never heard before, but I can come in on the second time ’round just from hearing it the once. Tunes vary in complexity, but a great many of them follow very simple patterns that you come to hear pretty easily after a while. If you’ve accompanied a tune with a certain pattern hundreds of times, and you hear a new tune that follows the same pattern, there’s no need to sit out just to follow the “never back tunes you don’t know” rule (though there might be plenty of other good reasons for sitting out).

It’s really about self-awareness, something accompanists are rather notorious for lacking…; if you hear an unfamiliar tune with a familiar pattern, and are in touch with how reliable your ear for the harmony is, and think that you can effectively add to the music, go for it. But then again, if you are that self-aware then you probably don’t need me telling you this :)

And the opposite as well- if you’re a bit shaky on what chords go in a tune, EVEN if it’s one you know well, you would be doing yourself and your fellow musicians a favor by chilling out and listening. There are tunes I know quite well to play on a melody instrument that I still wouldn’t try to back out of the blue. There’s no reason you need to be playing all the time when you go to a session, and I’ll often sit back just for the sake of it, to hear the other musicians play unaccompanied for some variety.

That’s just my 2 cents as a relatively inexperienced musician, who tries to be self-aware and learn from his experiences of looking like a jackass ;)

I think one key thing to realise is that no one notices someone backing a tune they don’t ‘know’ if that person is doing it well. It’s not something anyone feels the need to advertise unless they’re trying to show off or something. I know it is not always the case, but often the people who are loudly outspoken about accompanists not backing tunes they don’t know are people with little knowledge of accompaniment themselves, who might not realise that some of the best accompanists do it all the time without anyone noticing. While not necessarily ‘knowing’ the tune’s every note to pick out on the spot, they nevertheless know the harmonic topography of the tune. For many tunes the harmony is blindingly obvious from the first listen. The chords I would play for a tune like Ms Mcleod’s would be the same after the first time hearing it as they would if I had a week to select them through meticulous analysis of the melody.

I’m not saying people should be going around trying to back every tune regardless of whether they know it or not. I just wanted to emphasise that backing in Irish trad isn’t a matter of having chords laid out ahead of time, though this is useful when starting out. It’s about developing your ear, like so much else about the music.

Like Will Evans said, “A good guitarist knows hundreds of tunes and plays by ear, picks them up as they go along, just as a good tune player does and vary rarely gets caught out.”

Re: accompaniment chords “rules of thumb”

After playing off and on irregularly as an accompanist/backup musician/sideman at the local Irish Sessions for nineteen years, I have a few comments to add to this discussion.
Since these tunes are sturdy enough to stand up on their own without accompaniment and they sound good without accompaniment, they don’t really need to be accompanied.
However, if a melody player wants an accompanist, I have a few suggestions and guidelines based on my own experience.
A good accompanist should be unobtrusive and in the background. If there is an audience, the attention of any listeners should be focused on the melody player instead of the accompanist. The listeners really shouldn’t notice the accompanist. The accompanist should try to remember to keep it simple instead of trying to play fancy, complicated, elaborate chords which might clash with the melody. You aren’t there to show off your ability to accompany someone else. Instead, you are supposed to try to make the melody player look better. As I said, the center of attention should be the melody player instead of the accompanist.
If you don’t know a tune, it is wiser and more sensible to listen to it first before you try to accompany it. That way, you are less likely to “muck it up” and make yourself look like a “jackass”. (Of course, if you ask my ex-girlfriend, she will tell you I look like a jackass no matter what I am doing. My wife has a different opinion of me.)
I came to Irish music from other genres but I had very little trouble adapting to accompanying Irish music.
I have a copy of the Fiddler’s Fake Book and I have found it to be very useful for learning tunes and figuring out chords.
When I am not at the local Sessions, I will play through the tunes so I can at least be halfway familiar with with and comfortable with them. I don’t play tunes at the Sessions because there are plenty of good melody players at our local Sessions.
If you are an accompanist, it is Very Important to Listen and Pay Attention.
Speaking from my own experience, if you want to be a good accompanist, ear training does help.
To paraphrase one of the other comments: Be Self Aware when you are playing at a session.
Sometimes it can be nice to just sit there quietly and listen while one of the melody players plays a tune unaccompanied. At the most recent local Session, one of the flute players played a tune accompanied by only a bodhran player and it sounded good. No you shouldn’t try to play along with every tune--sometimes you should sit there quietly and listen. And sometimes you should play along. One of the secrets to being a good accompanist is knowing when to play and when not to play.
As Ergo said, rhythm and responsiveness are important as well as responding to subtle melody dynamics. As an accompanist, you are supposed to support the tempo instead of trying to drive it because you aren’t in the driver’s seat.
Mix is correct when he says that a good guitarist can enhance a session and a bad guitarist can drag it down.

Laurence

cskudder. Like someone above mentioned, I have tried to put together some of the good advice I have received over the years regarding accompaniment, and include it in my profile. So click on my user name, and scroll down to look at the article.
Some general comments on your chords: on major tunes, the old I/IV/V approach works pretty well on many. I would say that on most, the V chord is the second most used chord. Your chord lists show the root, the IV and the minor II chord being the top three chords, which I find not to be the case. That minor II chord is a useful one, but would definitely be number four on the list.
On Dorian tunes, such as Em, you show the VI chord (C, in the case of Em) being an option. That chord works on classical minor tunes, but does not work on dorian tunes. The melody notes for Edorian include a Csharp, not a Cnat, and a Cnat chord clashes with them. Imposing minor chords onto dorian tunes is a common problem with folks coming into Irish music from other traditions.
Sevenths in chords are frowned upon by some--they are far more common in American music than Irish, so take that into consideration.
And finally, you refer to turnarounds. Be advised that many of these tunes do not resolve at the end of a part, and sometimes not even at the end of the tune. Trying to put a turnaround on them, and resolve them at the end of a part, when the tune doesn’t go there, is another mistake newbies often make.
You have put a lot of thought into it, and what you have presented shows you are on the right track. I got my start in this music accompanying my wife as she learned the fiddle, and we had lots of fun together doing that. So you are fortunate to have a musical partner right in your own household.
Enjoy the journey!

I think a good backer learns to hear around the corner. Take my friend Pete, for instance. He’s a fine backer. I’ve seen his process on an unfamiliar tunes. He will play a little quieter, and he’s always got the tonal center right when he starts, if he hit’s an odd chord, he gets real quite, real quick. If you can hear him three chairs away, he’s got the tune. He has no problem sitting one out when he’s stumped or when it’s otherwise appropriate. It’s certainly no more disruptive than what you’ll hear as a melody player or two tries to grab a tune back from the memory hole. It comes down to having good situational awareness, and realizing that it’s not all about you.

That’s the downside of the honor system though. It’s a problem when someone lacks the self awareness to realize the ‘bull in a china shop’ effect they are having on a group.
