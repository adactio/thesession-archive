Do you have anything in mind out there, or would you rather not say?

A hobby. At least that’s what I tell people. Even chatting among other players we all down-play it all by just saying things like, its just a wee hobby, nippin’ down to the local session for a tune, being a tune-geek, and so on. But playing these tunes does become all-consuming.

And also you get to go places and see things you normally wouldn’t be able to. Walking up the hill after the session tonight I allofasudden remembered being at a house party in Harlesden, NW London, many years ago, where a few of us were playing tunes in a wee session. Being Harlesden, the first and 2nd generation ethnic mix was Irish and Caribbean (and me.) The session soon evolved from just trad tunes to a mix of Irish trad tunes and songs but also beautifully sung Soul and Gospel singing. More than just alcoholic drinks were imbibed. When we arrived I didn’t know any people besides the few other players, but before long we were accepted into a social group which would have been much more difficult to access had we not been playing the music. Just one example of the passport that is your music.

Re: What’s it all about, Alfie?

Ailin- “Best not to set goals. If you want it, you’ll do it. Imposing a discipline can work, but it’s not the way I prefer to do things.”

Goals help some of us keep track of our progress and make the progress more thorough and efficient. When you have a lot going on, you can lose track of your priorities and in turn, hinder your progress. Setting something from a low-priority(back-burner) to a high priority(goals) can refocus your attention.

“I’m glad I didn’t scare myself away by setting goals that might not have taken me in a positive direction. We tend to value accomplishment over what has been accomplished. Setting goals feeds into that, IMO.”

For me, if it hadn’t been for setting goals and following through with them, my progress wouldn’t have been made as efficiently. We’re not all afraid of failing. If I didn’t fulfill a goal one year, I would just set it again for a next year and set it as a higher priority. Like bowed-triplets for a example. I didn’t work on them much my first year cause I was working on other ornaments, intonation, bow-control, and tempo control. After seeing that triplets were the only thing that hadn’t gotten any of my attention, I set it as an official goal for my second year, meaning that it had to be the ONE thing I worked on if I didn’t work on anything else. Now going into my third year of playing this music, I have most of the ornaments down-pat, even after being out of practice for several months. I even learned how to do them on piano, when before I didn’t understand how they were even possible :-)

Looking back at all the things I thought I would never be able to do, i’m glad I made those lists of goals. When I get down and began to doubt myself, asking myself if i’ll ever sound Irish, I remember where I started and how far i’ve come. I haven’t always been the most confident guy, so this has encouraged me to keep moving forward while I was my only and worst critique.

Speaking of critiques, people tend to pass a lot of flattery, distorting the view of our flaws. If we flatter ourselves, we distort that view on our own. Keeping track of my goals also shows me if i’ve truly been lazy or diligent since my peers may not always tell me.

That’s my view. Nothing is wrong with setting goals.

Re: What’s it all about, Alfie?

I know for my wife and I, it’s part of rediscovering the heritage that was lost when our great and great great grandparents came over from the auld sod (Ireland for her, Scotland for myself). We appreciated the music early on in our seperate ways as students and later after we married it became a common theme we could play together. Initially it was for our own enjoyment, but as time went on others (non family) heard and asked where we regularly played at. At that time we hadn’t played any ITM or STM in public. Shortly after that we were introduced to some other musicians who shared interest and it went from having a session with several musicians to playing at the local pub for a few drinks and a pittance. As our repetoire grew, so did the requests to play at other venues and we were able to start asking for a little more money. It may sound cutthroat, but strings, bow rehairing, reeds, travel and all those other incidentals that go along with our instruments cost money and it helps to have even a small fee to help offset those costs. Otherwise, unless your independantly wealthy, you’ll soon go broke. This doesn’t mean that we don’t play for free anymore. Both of us are still willing to go to the schools and introduce new generations to the musical heritage of Ireland and Scotland and we do this free gratis and with a smile on our face and in our hearts

Re: What’s it all about, Alfie?

If you’re not getting something out of the lessons now, then yes by all means you should cut down the number or quit that teacher altogether. I know that a lot of music teachers don’t paid what they’re really worth (I tried to get my piping teacher to take more per hour; she wouldn’t but that’s a different topic), but neither should you view your time and money as a charitable contribution. If it’s not win-win for both of you, it’s time to move on.

After several years, my lessons were getting further apart, and my teacher finally said that she had taught about all she could. She’s still a much better piper than I am; part of it was that I was going musically in a different direction than she (I still am). I appreciated her candor, and we’re still good friends and play together at workshops and tionól.
