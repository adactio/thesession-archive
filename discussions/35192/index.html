Seeking advice re: session tour in Ireland

My wife and I are life-long amateur musicians with formal musical training who have been playing Irish music for three years in sessions and at contra dances. I know 200+ tunes on my beloved Burke narrow bore D whistle and can usually improvise on acoustic bass guitar on the tunes I don’t know. My wife plays bodhran and djembe. We would like to take a tour of Ireland in 2015, seeing the sights during the day and playing in sessions in the evenings. We understand and respect that not all sessions are open sessions and would like to hook up with a tour company that does such a thing, but it seems that most tours are geared toward music enthusiasts and not players. We’re not looking for flashy sessions led by big-name musicians - just common folk like us who love playing the music together. You can check out our session etiquette at www.oberlin.net/~irishsession. All constructive advice is welcome!

Re: Seeking advice re: session tour in Ireland

From another American, who’s only been over three times, so take my advice with as big a grain of salt as you like: I might leave the bass guitar and the djembe at home, and I’d expect to listen a lot more than you play. In fact, if you’ve only been at this three years, I’d expect to feel way out of your depth pretty much all the time.

That’s not to say you can’t enjoy yourself thoroughly. If you want specifics, I’ve had good times at the Crane in Galway - the Saturday night session is legendary - and at the Roadside Tavern in Lisdoonvarna. Plenty of other great places, too, but beware the pub that advertises “Live Irish Music” and it turns out to be one dude with a microphone and a guitar. The best sessions don’t always advertise well, but often rely on word of mouth.

Re: Seeking advice re: session tour in Ireland

One common faux-pa that Americans can make is to think they have to constantly participate to fully enjoy the session. After attending a weekly session in Derry for awhile, I found that appreciating other musicians is as much a part of both a good session as well as becoming a good player.

As some of the above folks have said, advertised sessions are rarely “real” sessions but paid musicians for a night. You could find some music stores in the towns you’ll be visiting and ask around there or find an advertised beginner friendly session with perhaps a more experienced session afterwards. Pack light, go with the flow, and remember when your on the road many things are left to fate, chance, etc. Hope Ireland is everything you expect it to be and more (because it always is, there’s no getting around that one!)

Stay in one place…

I think you should land in the one place, where you know there is good music and friendly, open sessions, and just stay there the whole time. The tour idea means seeing the country from a bus or a car window and then having to break new ground every night. You’d just be skimming the surface of the culture in a tour situation, and the total experience gets diluted. Stay in one place. Get to know the locals and they you. It will deepen your experience and your appreciation of the music and the history.
The music is different from town to town (and often from pub to pub even in the same town) so that what would work in one town might not be appropriate in the next. Most people coming to Ireland for the first time think they can get more out of their trip if they cover a lot of ground. The opposite is true.

Re: Seeking advice re: session tour in Ireland

I’ve just returned from a trip/tour (https://thesession.org/discussions/34276) and let me just start by saying that I never even opened my fiddle case while in Ireland! :) Granted this was my first time traveling around the country, second visit to Ireland overall. So from the experience - take it all in on a few first visits, don’t make big plans to play music. Instead, have high hopes that you will be able to listen to as much music at its origin as possible!

Agree on all advertized “live trad Irish music” at bars - not to say that their paid musicians are bad, or that they don’t play a set or two that would otherwise belong to a session, but those won’t be sessions as such. Still good to listen to in most cases.

And one important advise if you are planning on driving around there - to get from point A to point B multiply time your GPS tells you by two. And best do your sightseeing by day, check into a hotel or B&B and then go out looking for sessions in that town.

Enjoy your trip!

Edit: don’t be surprised if most events start as late as 10pm. i never expected a mid-week night at a bar to start at 10pm and get extremely packed by midnight!

Re: Seeking advice re: session tour in Ireland

I always think the trick with visiting sessions is to think about what you might bring to the session rather than what you might get out of it. If your playing is at a level where you can contribute something new and useful to the music and add to other people’s enjoyment, then you’ll probably be made very welcome. If, on the other hand, you’re not at that level and want to join in for an evening ‘just to be part of it’, without really contributing anything useful, then it’s probably best to leave your instrument in its case. In my experience visiting a strange session is a very different thing to being part of your own local session.

I very strongly, say it with me, strongly echo David Levine’s advice. Land in one spot and settle in. Get to know where you are. Ask around and you’ll likely find several sessions. It helps too, if you can find a festival going on. My experience was a month in one spot, thanks to the generosity of many and was made to feel welcome just about anywhere (not everywhere, but that’s no different than right here at home). Also leave the djembe and bass guitar home. I say that as a lifelong (55 years and counting) bassist. Bring the bodhran but be super careful. Good taste was always welcome. Re: Seeking advice re: session tour in Ireland

Galway or somewhere in Clare would probably be your best bet for music if you wanted to stay in one place. The Crane in Galway has open sessions, usually of a high standard, many nights of the week, and in the summer Taaffe’s has an early evening session (5.30 I think) that is fairly welcoming to all levels of playing. The Western Hotel has a session on Thursdays that is very welcoming, and again all levels are invited.

In Clare, Ennis is a hub for music, but especially in the summer many of the towns have sessions most nights. Miltown Malbay is famous for the Willie Clancy Week; just walk up and down the main street and you’ll hear plenty of music.

In Dublin there are many pubs with music, although this varies widely from open sessions to de facto gigs; it’s wise to sit back and see what the craic is before jumping in. The Cobblestone is a great place, usually open and welcoming, although the level of play is usually high and sometimes the leaders just want to play amongst themselves. The owner’s a nice guy, too, it’d be a good place to stop into and have a pint whether you’re playing or not.

I’d second the Willie Clancy Week or a similar festival (I hear Drumshanbo is good, but watch out for Father Jimmy Ranable). The Fleadh Cheoil is back in Sligo in 2015, and not only offers sessions but concerts and a chance to watch the competitions. Be aware that for many of these festivals, especially the Fleadh, you should book accommodation very early, as it will fill up quickly.

If you’re set on a tour, the southwest (Cork and Kerry) is IMO the most stunning area sights-wise, but it does get overrun with tourists in areas, especially around Killarney and Ring of Kerry. Still, tourists are looking for the “authentic” experience which means sessions abound, even if some of them are just a couple guys singing “Dirty Old Town” and “Whiskey in the Jar.”

Cork City’s got great music, and would be worth a couple days on a tour. The Spailpin Fanach has a session every night of the week all year, many of which are quite welcoming. The Sin É and the Corner House also have sessions most nights, and are right next to each other for ease of comparison.

As for etiquette, people above have you covered. Bring the whistle and bodhran, leave the rest, and listen before you sit in. Be honest about your abilities and follow the lead of the regulars, be courteous, etc. But try to add something to the night as well; maybe you’re not the best player there, but if you’re good craic you’ll be welcomed and have a great time. If you sit silent and check your phone when you’re not playing, you’ll be just another tourist who brought along a whistle and bodhran (and there are many).
