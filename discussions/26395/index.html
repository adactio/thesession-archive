Why does everyone hate ceili(dh) bands? Some of them are bloody good, i.e. the Tulla and the Kilfenora. Anyway, their purpose in life is to play for ceilidh dances. Those are dances in a big hall where you have a large (if the ceilidh is any good) number of people dancing figures.

I’m speculating here, but I suspect one of the reasons traditional Irish ceilidh bands are so big and use a snare drum is that they needed to be perfectly in time for the dancers and more importantly, be heard in a large, noisy hall, full of people stomping around in the days before PAs existed. The snare drum cuts through everything so the dancers will hear that and if you have enough melody players, they’ll hopefully hear that as well.

Ian, most of the music we play is *dance* music. Irish ceili bands emerged in their present form in the 1920s and were immensely popular. I think you need to be aware of imposing your cultural norms and constructs for “good” music with lots of “drive” to this particular style of playing, which appealed to the kind of music people wanted to hear in the 20s, 30s, and 40s, and also was pragmatic, as you needed to project the sound and rhythm as much as possible in a large, noisy hall.

Slightly different sense of compartmentalising. Most people, I guess, have lives with *forced* compartments.

I was thinking more in the sense of tribalism. Like it or not, music is used as a social badge - either self-chosen or imposed/perceived by others.It’s a statement of identity - but used like that it implies that if you like a certain sort of music, then you probably also live the rest of the stereotype too, shut away in your own little sub-world.

Most of the local musicians I have got to know over the past few months do seem to conform more or less to this identity; which is the chicken, whic the egg?. (That is not to say I despise it, I just don’t subscribe to it). Plus there is the geographical provincialism (as in “Why the hell are you obsessed with Scottish stuff?” which is quite common, even from certain musicians round here).

There are many who feel quite comfortable with it being tribal - it’s part of any ‘special interest group’ and it *is* about comfort and belonging - that’s ‘community’ for you… Trouble is, in the process it does become seen as a bubble, a social clique, and these days IMO it is more off-putting to most younger people than it ever was…

I can see that belonging to tribes is comfortable, but I guess like many people I have toes in too many camps to ‘belong’ to any of them - and I don’t especially like other people judging me by which tribes they *think* I belong to, either. Apart from anything else, tribes tend to be very inward-looking, which is not healthy in terms of a sense of proportion!

I just don’t see that something as universal as music - especially human-scale ‘art’ music (as opposed to mere mass-entertainment) *has* to be cast in that way. All the more so because everyone here keeps telling me how universal it actually is. This music is entirely relevant to the modern world - or would be so long as it doesn’t ghetto-ise itself. It ‘speaks’ as much as it ever did.

How the hell does this relate to the OP? Well, ceili bands will do nothing but reinforce many people’s perception of where this archaic music belongs. Stereotypes of folkies do exactly the same - and cause a lot of people who just might enjoy it to dismiss it prematurely.

One of the things I admire about Sharon Shanon, Mike McGoldrick, Deaf Shepherd, Flook, Julie Fowlis, plus Blas Ceoil, Horo Gheallaidh, Celtic Connections etc. is that they have refused to be constrained by outdated concepts, and have taken on modern perceptions of music on their own terms. That’s the point: for want of a better word, they are making the music cool, not sulking about the fact that it isn’t. The trick is to do so without compromising the musical integrity - and that’s where Celtic Women goes so badly wrong. The $$ cynicism of that just makes me retch.

I am not after changing people’s tastes or anything like that - but what’s wrong with pluralism? In the Ireland of the forties, fifties and sixties there were numerous Ceili Bands all gainfully employed playing at Ceili Dances The Ceili Dances were usually billed as Ceili and Old Time. The band had to be capable of playing the appropriate tunes for such dances as The Haymakers Jig, The Siege of Ennis, The Sixteen Hand Reel, The Bridge of Athlone, The Siege of Carrig, The Waves of Tory, the Walls of Limerick, Barn Dances, The Fairy Reel with lots of old time waltzes in between the figure dances, Consequently there was a certain discipline in the bands and most bands wore either a dress suit or a uniform of sorts. The line up in the bands varied but it was usually a couple of fiddles, a couple of boxes, a couple of flutes but in many bands the piccolo was to the fore. Some bands also had a vocalist for singing the tunes played for the waltzes. Some had a double bass, and some even had a whistle player who could double on the sax for the waltz tunes. Many of the more famous ceili bands had a big following and could pack a hall with people who just wanted to watch and listen. When Jimmy Shand and his Band from Scotland toured Ireland with his band in the fifties thousands turned up at his venues and over three thousand people turned up at one particular venue to hear his band. If your musical talent was limited then there wasn’t much room for you in a ceili band. it was a long way from a group of players stuck in the corner of a pub playing the odd tune in between a lengthy conversation

Re: What is a ceili band?

Ceilidh has a bit of a different take in Scotland.
A country dance band plays for RSCDS dances, where a list of set country dances is proveded (with number of times through and bars), and the band plays exactly that. Callers are generally not used as the dancers go to dance class. Tempo is very strict as is tunes/hnumber of times through. No deviation, no smiling allowed.
Think Jimmy Shand, White Heather Club etc.

Scottish ceilidh is the off-shoot where a repertoire of a dozen or so dances are played, often with a caller. No restrictions on tunes or number of times through. Lots of deviation (musically) and lots of smiling.
Think Dashing White Sergeant, Strip the willow, Canadians, Bostons, Friendly waltz, St Bernards etc.
My favorite, and a nice little earner.
