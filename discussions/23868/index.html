The X-Factor

Isn’t the inevitable result of competition exaggeration? Just look at the usual costumes associated with dance. Tell me they’re not OTT, and completely out of synch with the folks that nurtured and kept these traditions alive, before organizations grabbed hold of traditions and started re-writing history and pushing things into the glaring lights of stage, media and a panel of judges, re-directing and processing for their own profits. The medals and trophies are also pretty garrish, and shelves and a wall full of them? Why? I can’t count how often some flash act has won out of someone steeped in the tradition they stepped forward to represent. And let us not discount those with ties to the judging panel who slip through with a win.

And on those exaggerations, instrumentally speaking, how many have gone for the route to emulate the pipes, whatever their instrument may be? And if you push that to its extreme, the result is usually something top heavy on the ornementation and lacking any dynamics. And worse, when they forget the pre-existing traiditons of their particular instrument, or music in general. Every instrument has its history, its capabilities and limitations, and that is in part the joy if each, finding those and exploiting them, as to drive and inspire dance, and emotions. Some of the worst I’ve heard are from instruments that breath by aid of bellows, where they might as well be hooked up to a vacuum cleaner, just needing and aspiring to a straight and steady flow, cramming in as much ornamentation as they can, playing everything flat. They forget that their instrument breaths, that it can with each breath add another layer of interest and inspiration in dynamics, to lift the music and drive it and dancers and passion along. But instead, to follow thier drive to be like a piper, their choices result in as much loss as gain, flattening everything and taking it all so steady so they can focus in on twiddly bits, and not necessarily to define the pulse and rhythmic interest of a melody, as a good dance inspired piper would.

Willie Clancy was not only a piper, he was also a dancer. Chris Droney is not only a fine tina player, and regularly for dance, he is a grand dancer too.

Is this X-Factor approach, through competition, redefining this tradition, distracting us from the heart and putting the focus on the mechanics of this thing we care about? Is it one of several influences, inlcluding things as commercial, directing things toward greater exaggeration? ~ including the classicization and standardization of our expectations and aspirations? Is there any hope? ;-)

If you only have examples of the exaggerated, from stage, hotel or session, from wherever ~ brilliant! Your descriptions and opinions of such are welcome here… You could forgo the opinion and just write the description and await the reaction of others…thought that’s probably unlikely…

The Xtreme-Factors

I had a short life as an adjudicator, but I was interested in seeing how things went, from the little local fleadhs to the county, to regional, to the all-Ireland. I attended things before, during and afterwards. On a few occassions I’d get high listening to some great ‘traditional’ playing, representative of its place of origin, connected, beautifully executed, my feet moving ~ and I’d be somewhat taken back when they were passed over, the accolades, title and trophy, given instead to some impressive technician, something that did catch my mind, but not my heart. I’ve even seen those in the associated distraction mentioned here take the ribbon playing like they were pipes but on bow or bellows. And yes, I’d ask, and such was their inspiration. But that was just an example of how competition can lead to exaggeration, in this case missing the nature of the instrument in their hands for some kind of copying. Equally irritating would be those that did their best to copy Coleman or Matt Molloy or anyone ~ exactly. Imitation is great, but I don’t think it should overshadow the qualities that are part of the instrument one chooses to play, tina, box, fiddle, flute, whatever.

I admit this general bias, that I’m not fond of that direction, toward the x-treme. I don’t believe that ‘more-is-better’. But, I also realize the fun of occassionally going OTT, even with a tune or ornamentation, for the hell of it, and with full realization that I’m leaving orbit, that it’s cack, for a laugh. I can remember instances, without giving names, where medal winners specifically sought to emulate the pipes in their striving to be top ~ fiddler, squeezebox, whatever ~ and had no hesitation in admitting that goal. The problem is only when it reaches such an extreme that it looses touch with those qualities inherent in the bow and bellows, things and choices the pipes haven’t got, things that can add increased interest and definition to this music, for dance, or airs for listening and tugging at the emotions. Yes, I do believe that competition and commercial success can feed this greed for quantity. I don’t think it is wrong to experiment, to emulate the ways of different instruments, but not to the sacrafice of what’s in hand, and the drive, lift and interest that is inherent in this music, if handled with respect for that.

The ignorance of judges and organizations has had its part in this too…

You don’t have to apologise C, it was me that brought up Mr Molloy’s playing. But only in response to him famously having specifically (and in my opinion commendably and successfully) done what you specifically said you didn’t like. Namely, transferred piping technique to another instrument. You said you didn’t like it. You’re allowed not to like it. No need to back track.

My opinion of Matt Molloy is that while he has successfully (and, of course, hugely influentially) assimilated much piping technique to the flute, he hasn’t lost anything of the essence of what a flute is, in its most fluteyness.

I grew up with a piper, best mate, we learned together, pipes and fiddle. So my fiddle playing is very pipey. But I can’t recall, through the years, ever once having a conversation about what the fiddle can do against what the pipes can do. It simply never came up. It wasn’t an issue. We just learned the tunes, the music, and subconsciously and consciously strived for a unified approach to it. We didn’t distinguish between what a piper and what a fiddle player can contribute. And I just can’t see how this can be married to competition.
