The Use of Variations When Playing in Sessions—Yes or No?

A recent thread on learning to sight read veered into a difference of opinions over playing variations in a session. Some say it’s not a priority, others that it’s central to the music and the tradition.

Over a wide range of Irish trad sessions, I’ve run into an equally wide range of practice on this.

Some sessions mostly play in unison with little or no variations.

At other sessions, variations are common. If you’re listening, you’ll hear substitutions in ornamentation (playing a treble instead of a roll, say) or minor rhythmic devices (such as holding one long note instead of playing three short ones: B3 instead of BAB), combinations of the two, combinations of those two, and maybe also melodic variations (of varying scope), syncopation, snippets of harmony or counterpoint, and more. Though some of these may be substantive variations, they typically hold to the basic rhythmic and harmonic structure of the tune so they blend rather than clash. In other words, people may not be playing in strict unison, but they are playing together. (I’ve heard bigger, jazzier variations done at some sessions, but they tend to done as a one-off for the craic, not all night.)

I know my own preferences on this, but I’m curious to hear others’ experiences and personal tastes.

What’s the norm at your local session?

What about you personally?
Do you play the tune the same way each time?
Or do you do variations? As a sparse or sporadic fling? Or routinely, as a matter of course?

Perhaps you play variations in some circumstances (alone at home, say, or at gigs) but not in sessions?

Re: The Use of Variations When Playing in Sessions—Yes or No?

I play with variation constantly. I will pretty much never play a tune the same way twice during a set. As you mention, it’s most common that I am using different articulations (or ornaments) as part of my expression of the tune. I might move ornaments around, leave them out, slide or hammer on a note to delay it, add double stops, or hit notes early (on purpose) and let them ring longer, etc.

But I will also do melodic variations a fair amount. They’re typically small, and are still part of how I am expressing the musical idea (maybe swapping a couple of notes, or playing an unimportant accidental to change the flavor, etc). I will most often play melodic variations the last time through a tune, or if were playing a tune more than 3 or 4 times, I will start using them more as the tune keeps coming around. (I try not to do anything that will clash with what I consider the “base setting” of a tune, but every now and then I do something that doesn’t work all that well, in which case, I mentally file that one away as a miss…)

Pretty much all of my variation is done on purpose, but also done spontaneously. The way I think of it is that as a tune goes on, I just loosen the reins and let my creative musical brain take over. And I will say that when I started putting variation into my music, it is also when I started to be a more prolific tune composer. The two are related in my mind.

As to whether anybody else even notices my variations (or is annoyed by them), you’d have to ask the people I play with regularly. Nobody seems too taken aback by any of it, nobody has ever asked me not to do it, and people generally don’t noticeably react to them at all. In all honesty, I do most of my variation for me, not for other people, but I know that a good number of people have stated that they really enjoy playing with me in general, so it can’t be all that distracting…

Re: The Use of Variations When Playing in Sessions—Yes or No?

Another aspect of variation is the ability to scale the variation to the tempo.

For example, I know some players who learned very specific versions of tunes, fully ornamented, from an instructor.

They are able to play them quite well at a slow/leisurely tempo.

However when playing in a session, if the tempos are faster, they are constantly crashing and burning struggling to shove in every last ornament they learned every time.

This happens because never took the time to practice the framework of the tune and then experiment with scaling their ornamentation and variation appropriately to the tune at whatever tempo they might have to play it at.

I’ll almost certainly play a reel at 125 bpm (or faster) for step dancers quite differently than the same reel in a leisurely session at 90 bpm.

As the speed increases, I’m going focus primarily keeping very steady rhythm and lift with less and less ornamentation, particularly on concertina or box, where the clean execution of rolls and such can be fairly physically difficult compared to whistle or flute.

In this case “sparse is good” best to just leave out what you can’t pull off at speed and focus on rhythm and lift.

Re: The Use of Variations When Playing in Sessions—Yes or No?

“playing variations in a session . . . I’m talking about any one player varying (or not) the tune each time around ”

The Boy From County Cook: “I should think that would be one way to make oneself unwelcome at sessions.”

Not at all. It is just how traditional music is played. ‘Variation’ could range from changing breathing points, subtle changes in accents and phrasing, through re-arranging a few notes here and there, to radical re-interpretation of the melody. Of course, diverging too far from the basic tune would interfere with the cohesion of a session and it would be reasonable to expect the more fanciful player to rein in their creative urges in that situation. But heterophony – the sound of multiple variations played simultaneously, with passing dissonances, consonances and unisons – is inherent to the sound of this music, in my opinion. Variation is not obligatory but is most definitely welcome in any session I play in.

Incidentally, I am not generally a fan of harmony lines/countermelodies* and chord-based improvisations in Irish music (although they can work in some other forms of traditional music – I also play a fw Swedish tunes, in which two-part harmony is ‘de rigeur’). This is not what I mean by ‘variation’.

*Countermelodic accompaniemt on bouzouki etc. is perhaps the exception. But when a prominent melody instrument plays a countermelody, it obscures the subtleties of the melody for me.

Re: The Use of Variations When Playing in Sessions—Yes or No?

BfCC, I’m not sure if you’re trying to be obtuse or maybe you don’t have much experience of actual trad sessions.

In the OP, I also wrote: “Some sessions mostly play in unison with little or no variations.”

Note: “mostly in”—not “perfect”—unison.

I’m not asking about hypothetical extremes but about the kinds of sessions I’ve experienced myself in the real world.

Maybe it will help (everyone) if we throw in a few thoughts from outside this forum.

I’ve heard many Irish trad musicians say, “The same tune is never the same tune twice.”

This is echoed in the Field Guide to the Irish Music Session by Barry Foy, who writes:
“The experts generally agree that the highest level of Irish musicianship is reached by the player who never plays a tune exactly the same way twice. Recordings of a master such as Bobby Casey of Clare, for example, may exhibit him not so much playing a tune as living in it, with epic explorations that require a good few repetitions.”

Discussing personal musical style, Tomás Ó Canainn offers this in his book, Traditional Music in Ireland:
“Style implies a selection by the performer of certain traditional patterns or clichés of the tradition in his improvisation. His ability to select will obviously depend on what material he has at his command: a poor performer without much experience will have a small store of such material, while a top-class performer, having a wide choice, will be able to make his performance more interesting, varying his treatment of the tune in its successive repetitions.”

I don’t think he’s talking only about formal performance here; the same ideas apply to anyone playing on their own or in a small, intimate group. But Ó Canainn also has this to say about group playing:
“Playing in groups may, in certain circumstances, be detrimental to a player’s individual style, as the requirements of group playing are quite different from those of solo playing. The importance that Irish traditional musicians attach to ornamentation and variation means that the music can only be fully satisfying in the context of a solo performance. Spontaneity in group playing is, of necessity, subject to the requirement that the overall sound has a certain togetherness….”

I started this thread to find out how other people think about variations in their playing and at their local sessions. (I know my own thoughts on this.) My experience at a wide range of sessions over nearly 50 years of playing this music is that most sessions welcome a significant amount of playfulness and variation, as long as the overall sound still gels, what Ó Canainn calls “togetherness.”

But I have sat in on sessions where there was very little wandering from the basic tune. The focus seemed to be more on everyone getting in the same groove with as few “distractions” as possible.

By comparison, when I’ve sessioned with top-flight players, they interact musically by feeding off of one anothers’ variations, and so encourage them, all with no loss in the group’s groove.

So through this thread, I’m hoping to learn (1) in terms of variations, what kind of sessions other people frequent, and (2) why some people prefer sessions with little or no variation.

Over the years, I have had this conversation with many different Irish trad musicians. The ones who are full-time performing and recording artists all voiced the general idea that variations are central to how this music is—or should be—played. But I wonder if that’s partly because developing a tune through layers of variations is so central to playing for a concert or on a recording, and the mindset simply carries over to sessions (and any other time they play).

I suspect it’s less that and instead it’s much more their deep appreciation for the art of expressing each tune, and they have more to say than any single interpretation of the tune can convey. This latter reason is also what I’ve heard from many excellent trad musicians who don’t perform or record. It matches my own sense of the role variations play in making this music (which is based in large part on how my mentors shared their music with me).

I’ve also heard musicians with less experience in this music who’ve questioned or even complained when another player varies from what they perceive as the baseline setting of the tune. In sessions, I’ve heard statements like, “You didn’t play it as written,” or “The way you played it was a little different and it threw me off.”

In part, I’m genuinely curious if there are musicians with more experience who prefer mostly unison (little to no variations) playing, and what their reasons might be. (I do get the groove thing, like the way American Old-Timey jams will play the same tune 20 consecutive times with little or no variation. There can be a trance-inducing appeal to that.)

Yes, I have my own preference, but I’m NOT trying to advocate for one approach over the other here. Just wanting to hear other informed perspectives, thanks.

I am constantly changing up tunes when I play them but generally not doing major melodic variations. Most of what I do is switching in and out different articulation and ornamentation variations for repeated notes and such. None of it is pre-planned.

There are also simple things like filling intervals and descending note bounces and from below note slides that I will switch in and out on the fly to keep it interesting.

The sorts of melodic variations I tend to do generally would be simplifying the melody. For example extending a series of G repeated rolls instead of playing a G roll followed by GAG, that sort of thing, nothing that would generally attract any attention or alter the fundamental harmonic structure of the tune.

Gimpy, this has been a very instructive thread for me.

First off, I think the disagreement Michael and I had in the thread you reference had more to do with how a person who learned a tune from sheet music deals with variations as opposed to how an ear musician does. I don’t see why that would make a difference; it certainly doesn’t for me. Michael appears to disagree.

The value I got from this thread is a better understanding of how most who have contributed define a variation. I think in terms of what a musician like Matt Molloy does. He reframes entire phrases and sometimes even jumps octaves. I have no talent for what he does, but then it’s not something I’ve ever sought to master. To me, it is the province of the soloist, although Matt likely plays the way he does quite naturally because his head is so embedded in the tune, it’s not an effort or a skill he regards as another tier above simply playing the tune.

Our lot here, on the other hand, seems to regard variation as an inconsistency in the use of ornaments or the addition of things like syncopation, sustained notes or other devices that do, in fact, vary how a phrase is played, but not in the way pros like Matt do.

As a result, I now see the concept of variation in a new light. Using embellishments and ornaments spontaneously is what I do. Restructuring a phrase while remaining true to the melodic line is above my pay grade.

Finally, in my opinion, either approach is fine, but neither should become a priority unless the player considers it to be. Or, to put it another way, I would never evaluate a player’s skill on the basis of whether they used any variations at all. I may or may not notice if it’s there, but it likely wouldn’t dawn on me if it wasn’t if the tune is otherwise well played.

Re: The Use of Variations When Playing in Sessions—Yes or No?

Ailin, yes, this thread has helped me too—giving me a better understanding of the diverse ideas other people have about variation.

What sparked me to start it was your comment in the sight reading thread:
“I also see no virtue in varying how you play a tune unless that’s something you like to do. I play everything I do in music in a somewhat spontaneous manner, but who the heck cares?”

In reading that thread, I struggled to make sense of why you would spontaneously vary your playing of a tune if you didn’t think it was better than *not* varying the tune. If you didn’t find any virtue, value, or utility in doing so. And I wondered if other musicians felt the same way.

From this present thread, it seems clear that for many session musicians, variation is the “spice” that makes the tunes come alive. For many musicians immersed in the tradition, variation is both utterly commonplace and magical. It is integral to how this music is played and so becomes a core, often subliminal, element of our own playing.

But I’ve also heard that not everyone puts so high a value on variety. I like and agree with your assertion that a tune can be well-played without variation.

As I play this music, I live comfortably in a paradoxical world. On one hand, I can love a tune just the way it is the first time around. As just one example, the bare bones of Splendid Isolation are lovely and don’t *need* to be changed. There’s magic and beauty in how one phrase leads to the next in a slightly “two steps forward, one step back” fashion, climbing in the first half from G to g and then back down, and then the symmetry with the second half starting at g and walking its way down to G. The parts build to a wonderful whole. This holds true no matter the setting, no matter which version you think of as the baseline.

On the other hand, Splendid Isolation lends itself (don’t all tunes?!) to such a profusion of equally magical and beautiful variations, and these too can build to a wonderful whole. Are such variations necessary? I don’t know, but I can’t help but play them and see where the tune leads me.

For me, the degree and type of variation I might do depends on my mood, my level of focus in the moment, and the immediate circumstances. At one end, if I’m playing the tune so someone else can learn it, I’ll try to just play the bones. Even then, some small variations are likely to creep in. At the other end, in full flight, I’ll vary ornamentation, use different rhythmic devices, move where phrase breaks occur, vary the micro-dynamics, add brief drones, add passing tones, change the color of a phrase by flatting or sharping a crucial note, shift up or down an octave, and more.

I’m particularly keen on melodic variations. Often these are short snippets, filling in a section of longer notes with a flurry of shorter ones, turning a phrase inside out, etc. But I also routinely do longer melodic variations, reframing a 2, 3, or 4 bar phrase, say. Sometimes I find a new way to play an entire half of a tune. I enjoy finding “new” strings of notes that sound fresh yet still stay within the contours of the tune.

I like the tune-as-a-river analogy, and I enjoy exploring all the riffles, eddies, pools, and bends while never straying beyond the river’s banks.

I don’t think you have to be a master musician to do this (I’m certainly not). It’s mostly a matter of having (1) long, deep familiarity with the tunes and the idiom, which gives you (2) an ear for what works and what doesn’t, and (3) a willingness to let the tune lead your imagination and to take chances. Like any other skill or mindset, you can grow and hone your melodic spontaneity by “doing” it, by wandering inside tunes in the comfort of your kitchen. The more you do it, the easier it gets.

To your point, Ailin, the “virtue” or utility I find in playing with variations is that, besides being hugely rewarding in its own right, it has been a key to befriending and sessioning with many brilliant musicians, players much better than myself. And that in turn has made me a better musician than my meager workaday native talents could’ve anticipated.

Re: The Use of Variations When Playing in Sessions—Yes or No?

Yes, “virtue” in your post on the other thread was part of what threw me.

Whether “necessary” or not, variation is indeed considered a virtue in this tradition, and this has long been so. It’s true that not all players do it, but it certainly marks a distinction between the huddled masses and the better players.

In Last Night’s Fun, Ciaran Carson highlights this in the words of the Reverend Richard Henebry from his A Handbook of Irish Music (1928):

“…as a matter of fact, no two stanzas of any Irish song have exactly the same music, for that must yield to the words, and if the music persists the same throughout the song it is an infallible sign that it is not the work of a traditional singer. In fact, this very changing constitutes a main ornament and beauty of Irish singing. The same rule was observed in playing dance music, for all the good players introduced similar changes every time the tune came around, and the musician who could not play in that style was not regarded as a master. And as to the version of dancing tunes, it may be useful to explain that whenever a musician heard a note or an accent change in a tune that he considered an improvement, he straightaway incorporated it into his own version. I particularly remember in my own case, as a boy, how, on fair days and other occasions of visiting towns, I used to listen with the closest attention to such tramp pipers or fiddlers as I chanced to hear, especially if their style was good or they happened to be playing any of the great reels or other dance tunes, in order to assimilate for myself such changes of version or interpretation as I considered suitable. And I never knew an Irish musician who did not do the same.”

Henebry was a professor of Irish at University College in Cork, and he was an adept fiddler and piper (and he made his own pipes). He also amassed an important collection of early recordings of a wide range of Irish trad musicians. Henebry’s observations are informed and insightful, enough so that Carson quotes him at length (the above is just an excerpt).

So within the tradition, not only is variation highly valued, but it’s paid close attention to, sought after, and borrowed or “lifted,” incorporated into one’s own repertoire, much as the tunes themselves are.

Henebry was writing before public sessions became as abundantly widespread as they are today, so much of the playing he was listening to was likely primarily solo (and primarily amateur—even the best trad musicians back in the day had day jobs). I wonder if we’re gradually losing that focus among session players and amateur trad musicians. As I Tomás Ó Canainn notes in the quote I posted earlier, group playing (including sessions) may put a damper on spontaneity and variety in an individual’s interpretation of the tunes. Some Irish music pundits (Tansey, MacMahon, others) have raised concerns about the rising “sameness” of tunes, the erasure of regional stylistic differences, and trend toward conformity to generic session settings of tunes. This is one of the criticisms sometimes lobbed at CCE and the Foin Seisiún books and CDs.

All food for thought.
