Back to Backers…

The subject of backers in sessions has recently been discussed at some length here, but something that surprises me even more is the number of recordings by established and highly respected players whose music, I think, is marred by mediocre accompaniment. I am surprised that these players tolerate such backing. At the end of the day, it’s all subjective of course but, in my opinion, albums by, for instance, Kitty Hayes, Billy McComiskey and Tony McMahon’s jigs on Masters of Tradition would have been better sans accompaniment.

Re: Back to Backers…

“Unfortunately, the rest of the CD is spoilt (IMHO) by guitar backing by Tich Richardson. Lots of inappropriate bass runs, etc. Ugh!” - MOL

as a point of reference:

Concerning Tich Richardson, who was tragically killed in a car wreck in 1983, (brother of Dave Richardson from the Boys of the Lough), Tich, who played for a while as guitarist for the Boys of the Lough, was a game changer.

The well known Irish bands were, at that time, just beginning successfully tour in the US, and this innovation, because there was little to compare it to, was looked on as fresh and worth trying - a new conceit for the time. Tich’s playing, as explained by the link below, was much different than what was expected from a group as traditional as the Boys of the Lough.

Having been there to hear this music in 1982 was extraordinary. The new sound of the Boys of the Lough was as captivating as their banter on stage was charming.

But, Tich wasn’t the only touring guitarist experimenting with this ‘swing’ style.‘ It was happening with Jock Tamson’s Bairns and later Easy Club, in the guitar style of Jack Evans, Rod Patterson, and Jim Sutherland (bouzouki). It was also beginning to appear in Arty McGlynn’s early recordings, it was heard in the playing of Gerry OBriain (who toured with Kevin Burke in the early 80s), also in the early recordings of Cormac Breatnach’s Deiseal, and many, many more players. (Now it’s heard in John Doyle’s playing as an occasionally ’nodded to’ hybrid of the full-out swing style).

Once the listening audience heard this back-up swing style, it was much copied and adapted. But, because the chords were complex and required a lot of study, not every player could do it tastefully, or with facility, so in a sense it became somewhat a novelty, and secondary to the, now standard DADGAD backing style.

There are many great rhythm players back-up players, mostly unsung, who play guitar or piano (evidence Verena Cummins who played on Niall Keegan’s 1999 recording ‘Don’t Feed The Elk’), but in my opinion it comes down to a matter of taste, your own taste that is; and whether you like it or not is purely subjective.

…..and whether one of these great players is in your midst - so you can experience what is happening to the music while you’re playing it with them.

Re: Back to Backers…

Well folks, you’ve all told us what you don’t like. Opinions are great things but still are only opinions.

I agree that bad backup is a real pain (I was once caught at a non-irish jam sitting between a reasonable and a totally wrong chords and rhythms guitar). But it still seems to me that these days there are only a few reasons for whatever the accompaniment happens to be: The recording company demanded it, the folks who made the album liked the result even if some of the rest of us didn’t, or the accompaniment was added later and perhaps without the permission of the lead player. The first and third reasons are quite uncommon these days so I think we have to limit ourselves to complaining about the accompaniment but accept that it apparently was what was intended for some reason.

As to the old records: My favorite story is about the Old Time fiddle recordings made in the 20s and 30s where the recording studio hired a guitarist to “back” the old fiddlers. Frequently the guitarist didn’t know the tune and the result was whole pieces accompanied by, say, a G chord. Then folks began to study those albums and decreed that the old time musicians didn’t understand harmony…or worse that that single G chord was the “right” way to back the tune. Be very afraid of basing any opinions on those old records beyond admiring the playing of the lead folks.

Re: Back to Backers…

Yes, Tich died in September 1984 in Scotland. Other dates and places seem to have got into the record, but they are wrong.

Tich must have recorded on that one of Alistair Anderson’s pretty early on in his career, or before it had really started. But he went on to learn about Swing style accompanist not only from Peerie Willie Johnson but from an old guitarist called George Wilkinson who lived in the Durham area and who had, like Peerie Willie, kept on playing Swing right through from the interwar years - his choice mainly centred on the Reinhardt / Grappelli / Quintet Of The Hot Club Of France material, as far as I could tell.

George never achieved the (even niche) fame of Peerie Willie, but during the 80s if not before became increasingly known and respected by the guitarniks of the area, who got him out to this and that. He was what they call “a musicians’ musician”, and a fine player. He died some years back.

I Love Great Backers

I love great music played in communion with other players. It is the exchange and support, the intertwining and mutual soaring that makes my heart sing. Why else go to a session? Why is a guitar or bouzouki player any different than a piper or a fiddler? Any instrumentalist can be out of time, tune, or tone, or play with strange lilt or accent, or they can play sweetly or drive brilliant groove, and be wonderful to play with. Why single one instrument out? What could be worse than an out of tune shrieking whistle playing out of control fast and dropping half a bar in the B part?
The thing is, musicians come in all sorts with all their individual quirks and blessings. You deal with it, and treasure the moments when it all works, and hopefully handle the less than treasurable moments with humour and grace.
