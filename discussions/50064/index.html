Popular Tunes

I’ve been having a look through the list of *Most downloaded tunes over the last week* or so and all the predictable ones are there…e.g. Kesh, Morrison’s, Blarney Pilgrim and many more.

However, I’m fairly sure this isn’t really representative of what is happening in the so called “real world”. In my experience, although these tunes still get played they don’t feature in every session and certainly don’t make up the bulk of the repertoire. It’s handy to know them, of course, and most of them are very good tunes but I wonder what motivates members here to download them?

Most teachers and workshop leaders don’t tend to focus on these old “chestnuts” either. Many even make a point of teaching a tune that none of the students know. Presumably so that it’s a level playing field? So, the “student repertoire” is unlikely to include most of these either.

So, how do people know to download them? Perhaps, they check out the historically “most popular” section as opposed to the last week or so and just check out the more popular ones there? To be fair, I did that myself when I first came here. Or, perhaps, they have listened to the older classic albums and liked what they heard?

I’m not criticising, by the way, but I’m curious. I can understand the popularity of these tunes *over time“ but still puzzled as to why they continue to be the first port of call to newcomers of the music when they are not really representative of what’s going on. After all, even most ”Beginners’ sessions" feature a much wider variety today.

Re: Popular Tunes

Johnny, I’m assuming the page you’re referring to is the one that’s tracking tunes added to peoples’ tune books. I’ve often wondered the same thing, but in thinking about it, it makes sense that historically popular tunes are going to continue to be added at a higher rate that a less universally popular tune, because it’s not a random process (presumably for most of us!). That is, when people fill up their tunebook on thesession.org with tunes they “know” (and I’m aware not all users employ tunebooks in that way, but I’m asserting that many or most probably do), they will fill them up with those bog standard tunes as well as others that may be less universally common.

Maybe a more interesting question is how did tunes achieve their current (historical) level of popularity as measure by tunebook presence? A related question would be how popularity changes over time. So it would be interesting to explore the dynamics of tune popularity over time as measured by changes in rate of addition (presumably the rate of removal would be negligible). What tunes are on the ascent and what others are fading? What are the long term trends here? And, are some tunes resistant to fading? Sort of like tracking Covid variants over time.

Cheers to all and happy post-St. Paddy’s day.

Re: Popular Tunes

While I enjoy this site, I doubt very much that it’s responsible for a trend that started long before Tim Berners-Lee came up with the HTTP protocol.

People have been recommending largely the same set of “good beginner tunes” since before I started playing the music. I suspect that it’s mostly due to the self-perpetuating feedback loop already mentioned - there’s a set of tunes that are known as ones that “everyone plays” and “anyone can learn”, so people steer beginners towards those ones, which means that a new generation of players knows these as the “good beginner tunes”, and on we go.

> I once had a theory (I still have), that these are tunes that are popular in tunebooks aimed at beginners

Yes, I’d particularly point to the Fionn Seisiun collections, which seem to have really become a first port of call for new players.

I’m not sure I’d call this a “vicious circle”, though. I don’t think it’s such a bad thing to have a core repertoire. I’ve got a small collection of tunebooks, probably comprising something in the low thousands of tunes all together. It’s kind of nice to know that of those thousands of tunes, there’s a subset numbering in the low dozens that players from all over the world can rely on as a sort of starting point when they meet with someone new to play.

> Virtually none of the recordings I’ve purchased in the past 5 years contained any of the common tunes played at sessions.

Well, sure, but that’s largely a matter of the current fashion. If you go back to the Great Spate of the ‘70s and ’80s - the flood of records from folks now regarded as legends - there’s a lot more tunes which are session standards, and based on liner notes, many of those were session standards at the time. I don’t know if this was due to pressure from labels to include tunes that people would know, or if it was a commercially-inspired choice by the artists and producers, or if it was just that those were the tunes that the artists liked and wanted to record, but for whatever reason that was what people recorded then. I could speculate on why the fashion has shifted, but I suspect that that is a whole topic on its own.

Re: Popular Tunes

Sharon, by “assuming” I meant “if.” I wasn’t presuming a local session. But if you do have a nearby session, my advice stands.

The trouble with beginners focusing on the old warhorses is that, when they finally get to a festival or distant session, they’re likely to awkwardly lunge into Drowsy Maggie or the Kesh with little to no pulse or lift. That’s no fault of their own—they’re early on the learning curve. But playing old warhorses badly isn’t the best way to win acceptance at a session.

There are other tunes more suited to beginners that fall in the Goldilocks zone of being easy to play and widely known but not as worn out. As examples, instead of Kesh, I would teach the Lisnagun Jig (in both G and D). Instead of Drowsy Maggie, I would teach Terry Cuz Teahan’s or Killarney Boys of Pleasure. And I would help the learner build a set of jigs, a set of reels, preferably all from that same Goldilocks zone.

If you click on this site’s list of “popular” tunes, you get 10 tunes per page, with 2,116 pages. That’s more than 21,000 tunes. Some are surely duplicates, and there’s no shortage of tunes outside the tradition or that don’t often come up at sessions, but surely there must be some excellent Goldilocks candidates beyond the first 100 or 300 tunes on the list.

Also, I can vouch for the reality that a player can know a couple thousand tunes yet still end up listening more than playing when sitting in at an unfamiliar session. Beginners sometimes get the mistaken notion that they’re being “excluded” when the rest of the circle is playing “obscure” tunes. But those tunes aren’t obscure to the people playing them.

I think it might be a mistake to think that a tune’s being in a “tunebook” reflects much about its popularity. What do people actually use these “tunebooks” for?
One scenario, presumably the one originally intended, is that people use their “tunebook” to keep track of tunes that they’re interested in for some reason. But I can’t for the life of me imagine how a “tunebook” would help with this, since there is a very effective search widget. If I want to remind myself of how some tune goes, I just look it up.

I think the more realistic scenario is that new players find their way to this site, get excited about the idea of learning tunes, add these “good beginner tunes” to their list, and then probably forget all about the “tunebook” and get on with learning and playing tunes. Result: the “good beginner tunes” dominate the rankings, at least as far as appearances in tunebooks are concerned.
