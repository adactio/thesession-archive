Re: What makes a good fiddle bow for ITM and how do you select one?

Bernie, the hair on a bow is perhaps the least critical factor, because it can be (and should be, periodically) changed.

It’s the stick that counts. Basically, you want a bow that is well balanced (not overly heavy either toward the frog end or the tip end, stable (not bouncy) but lively (the camber provides a force to push against), and capable of drawing good tone from *your* fiddle. Each bow will have its own ability to produce tone. Each bow also produces its own amount of hair noise on the strings. Find one that suits your ear.

When I go bow shopping, I tend to play through a dozen or so, narrowing it down to a few that just feel right to me. Then I check those to see that they’re straight, if there are any repairs (broken tip?), and that the screw works properly.

Typically, full-size violin bows range in weight from 58 grams up to 62 or 63 grams. The middle of that range--59 to 61.5 grams--seems to cover the majority of good bows. Within *that* range, you’ll want to compare bows that feel heavier toward the tip vs. those that feel lighter toward the tip. The “right*” balance for you will be different than for another player, and will also likely change as your bow skills develop.

You can run a bow through some moves to make sure it will work well for you. Do some long frog-to-tip and back again bow strokes and feel for any bounciness or skittishness. Do bowed triplets on every string and listen for clarity and response from the bow. Play a variety of string crossings slow and fast to see how the bow feels.

Re: What makes a good fiddle bow for ITM and how do you select one?

I am not a luthier or bow-maker. As a reasonably experienced cellist/violinist/fiddler, here are some of my thoughts on the OP’s questions.

Individual bows affect not only the tonal quality of the violin but the volume of the sound you can get out of it. In this respect no two bows will be exactly the same. Some bows deaden the higher frequencies, while others produce noticeable noise (which may indeed only be audible for a short distance but uses up some energy that would otherwise be used for projecting the tone). So bows need to be compared, one against another in a gven price range.

The balance of the bow is important. If you find your bowing hand subconsciously moves up the stick while you are playing that is a sign that the bow is top-heavy - look around for something else. Bow weight, within reasonable limits, isn’t so important. There are some musicians who are happy to play the fiddle well with a viola bow, which is 10% or so heavier than a violin bow.

You don’t want a bow stick that is too floppy, twists sideways, or is too stiff. One of the things I look for in a bow is that when I tighten the hair sufficiently to give a gap of about the thickness of a pencil between the hair and the middle of the stick I can use that bow for everything without the stick banging down on the string. I also expect to feel the vibration of the string through the stick.

A “good” bow, at whatever level you play at, or what music you play, will give you the clear feeling that it is helping you to do what you want and is not holding you back. You certainly don’t need to go into the 4 and 5-figure price ranges for such a bow; a good bow in the low to middle 3-figures (and there are plenty ) should see you through for the rest of your life. Drop below 3 figures and I’d think hard about it - you’d probably get dissatisfied and be looking around for an upgrade within a year. Carbon fiber bows offer remarkably good value for money, usually being about half the price of equivalent performing pernambuco bows, but there again I wouldn’t go for the bottom of the pile (less than 3 figures).

A beginner or intermediate fiddler buying a bow is facing a big problem: they are not yet skillful and experienced enough to home in on the ideal bow for them in their price range. They need their teacher or a very experienced player to go along with them and give advice.

There are violin shops staffed by experienced and knowledgeable people who really do have the long-term interests of the customer at heart, will give first-class advice, and will not rip off the customer. Unfortunately, there are also retailers of whom this cannot be said :-(. Get personal recommendations from orchestral players and school music departments if you’re not sure. A good violin dealer will usually let a potential buyer borrow two or three bows to try out for a week (it can take that long to decide), for a nominal deposit.

Bows intended for classical playing are usually more expensive because they are designed to do far more at a much higher level then is needed for folk music. Classical violin music makes acrobatic and other demands on bowing that are never made in folk music. Some classical bows are made with bowing fireworks by a top soloist in mind; others are more suitable for general orchestral playing; others again would be preferred for chamber music or early classical.
(An open secret will now be revealed - the bow that can cope properly with EVERYTHING in the classical repertoire has yet to be made!)

An aside about the ultra-expensive bows (high 4-figures and 5-figures). Although they obviously perform very well it should be remembered that a large proportion of their listed values is made up from their age, maker, and provenance (a list of great players who have owned a particular bow), usually further inflated by collectors’ antics at auctions. In contrast, the average professional or advanced amateur is unlikely to be using a bow much more than £1000-£1500, and if it is a bow that does everything asked of it then it will likely stay with the player for the rest of his career, even through violin changes.

Re: What makes a good fiddle bow for ITM and how do you select one?

When it comes to looking for defects in a bow, first look for repairs to the stick - use a magnifying glass to look for glue lines between the back of the frog and the screww, and across the head. A repaired stick is practically worthless.

If it’s a cheap bow, check that the grain of the wood runs straight along the stick, and not diagonally.

If the seller claims that a bow is Pernambuco, check that it really is - Pernambuco has medullary ray flecks (like the flecks on a good bridge, but much, much smaller) Brazil wood doesn’t.

Next over-tighten the bow. Check that it stays straight laterally, and that the camber stays smooth and even, even when over-tightened.

After that it is a case of playing all the bows you are considering side by side. When it comes to bows, one man’s meat is another man’s poison. And the difference between a bow you love and one you hate might be 3 grams, a difference of an inch in the centre of gravity, or a couple of ounces of tension.

When it comes to types of bow, it’s something I’ve been thinking about a lot recently. Much of our music was written in the age of the baroque bow. The baroque bow was about 3" shorter than a modern bow, and the dance-masters’ bow was even shorter. Given that many fiddlers choke the bow short, I can’t help thinking that even if they don’t want to switch to a baroque bow, they might be much better off with a 7/8 or even 3/4 modern bow.

Re: What makes a good fiddle bow for ITM and how do you select one?

Lets say, for example, you have £300 to spend on a bow.

Get thee to a good shop and ask them lay out on a table all the bows they have in that price range. But also get them to slip in a £2,000 bow and a £50 one. And make sure all the bows don’t have prices on them.

If you can easily locate the £2,000 bow and the £50 one, then buy what you think is the best £300 one, go home and save your pennies and come back a year later and get a better one.

If you can’t spot the £2,000 one but you did spot the £50 one, then buy what you think is the best £300 one and maybe in a year or two, go back to the shop and try again. If you still can’t spot the £2,000 one, stick with your £300 stick. And you’ll be happy. And you’ll have saved yourself £1,700.

If you can’t locate either the £2,000 bow or the £50 one, buy the £50 one. And in a year or so, go back and do the test again.

Will, could you remind us how many years you’ve been playing the fiddle? Or, in rough figures, how many decades?

I think Michael’s advice was aimed at someone with about a tenth of your experience, and in that sense it’s pretty good advice. Try playing the top and bottom of the price range, but mostly stick close to the amount you think you’d be willing to pay - that’s about what I do when I help someone pick out a guitar.

You shouldn’t be buying a bow on a whim, which means you should have a pretty good idea of what your budget will tolerate and what it would prefer not to tolerate. It seems to me that anyone helping you with that purchase, they should know those figures and help you stick to them.
They certainly shouldn’t be urging you to buy something that’s ten times what you budgeted for. That’s not a friend, that’s an enabler!

But really, you shouldn’t be going in to buy a bow, cold. The best plan, I find, is to do your research in advance of need. If you think you’re going to buy an instrument (or a bow, in this case) in the foreseeable future, go into the better shops in your area and check out their whole range. Let the staff know you’re not buying now, but you want to know what’s out there. If they’re smart, they’ll show you the whole range - and on this trip, you shouldn’t mention a price range. Spend a few hours on it, play a lot, ask them questions, take notes.

Do that from time to time - any time you need a set of strings, plan on spending an hour chatting and checking out some new instruments.

Do that enough, and the question is moot. When you’re ready to buy, you’ll already know what you want, and what it should cost, so it’s easy.

(Needless to say, you should buy your instruments from the places you do your research at. Using your local mom-and-pop shop as a showroom for the new Taylor and Martin models (or whatever the equivalent would be in the bow world) and then buying them on line - that’s pretty low, and leads to broken strings, bad intonation, and odd buzzes and rattles that you can’t track down)

Re: What makes a good fiddle bow for ITM and how do you select one?

That’s been very interesting so far, thanks everybody. I have a few comments.

Jeeves, Jerry, yes, I had precisely those examples in mind: Padraig O’Keeffe and his bizarre bow with a cork stuck under the hair, and that bloke playing (remarkably well) with a saw for a bow.

And Gam, you wrote: “they all feel different, and the duds are pretty obvious as soon as you pick them up. After that it is largely a matter of getting used to the bow you have chosen, and then using a different one feels weird.”

Jerry said: “Well, if the hair takes rosin and bites the strings, you’re in business. A bit of spring in the stick is helpful. Everything else is a matter of personal preference, and can be adjusted for, to a degree, in grip and pressure and whatnot.”

And Dogmageek tells us, if I understand correctly, that he uses the very cheapest bows for performance, although he has a more expensive (and better) one.

So that’s one school of thought, “they’re all much the same, apart from obvious duds, and you adjust to what you have”.

Will, you said: “the hair on a bow is perhaps the least critical factor, because it can be (and should be, periodically) changed.”

Another way of looking at this is that the hair is the most critical factor, because it can be changed and because of the effect that can have. In fact this is what started me thinking about what makes a good bow.

I bought a Chinese fiddle for a few pounds from a charity shop, because I wanted the case it was in. There was also a bow in the case, horrendously low quality, for example the hair was simply glued into the plastic frog. I left the bow lying around, then one day I picked it up accidentally and used it. It sounded pretty good! I realised that the reason was, it had brand new hair, had hardly been played, and my other bows needed new hair.

Trevor, you said:
“The balance of the bow is important. If you find your bowing hand subconsciously moves up the stick while you are playing that is a sign that the bow is top-heavy - look around for something else. Bow weight, within reasonable limits, isn’t so important. There are some musicians who are happy to play the fiddle well with a viola bow, which is 10% or so heavier than a violin bow.”

And Sandy said: “a bow that fattens your sound, and feels light in your hand, and across the strings is a good bow for ITM. IMO.”

Balance: all the bows I have in the house balance at a point which is well within the specifications I have read. Including the Chinese one with the plastic frog! But I still find my hand creeping along the stick, closer to the point of balance, and it seems to happen whichever bow I use. There’s more to be said about this, I think I’d better start another thread.

Weight: if people want a stick that is light in the hand, why are standard sticks so heavy? I have two old pernambuco bows which weigh less than 54 grams and I understand that many older bows are light, falling outside Will’s specified range. I really like these bows.

Price: I live a fairly simple, low-maintenance life, fiddle playing is one of my main interests, I suppose I could spend thousands on a bow if I needed to, I suppose I am trying to establish if I need to.

Fat sound: I read a musicologist’s description of traditional Irish fiddle style: the tone was described as “thin” in comparison to a classical tone, and “intimate”. My favourite fiddlers at the moment are Breda Keville and Joe Ryan. Her tone has been described as “slightly edgy” and his has been called “thin”. So I am wondering, is a “fat” sound from a bow really what we are looking for?
