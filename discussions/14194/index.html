Re: Why Birds Sing

The Hungarian composer Kodály, who along with Bartok in the first half of the last century saved the Hungarian folk music tradition from extinction, used birdsong as the basis of the slow movement of his Sonata Op 8 for unaccompanied Cello. I cannot do better than to quote from a 1958 review of this work:

“The slow movement is perhaps the most extraordinary piece ever written for a single [string] instrument. It begins as the first movement ended, but blossoms out from a single bird-call into a song of a thousand voices - nature singing to itself. With the finale, Kodály brings us down to earth … here is the folk music of his country - the fiddler in the barn, and the stamping feet of the dancers. It is developed into one the biggest virtuoso pieces of the century, yet always the virtuosity serves a cause higher than itself.” (Frank Hampson)

In the sonata Kodály uses the Baroque scordatura tuning technique and requires that the two lower strings be tuned down a half-tone, giving an open-string Bmin7 chord. The scordatura tuning gives an extraordinary resonance, which is very useful in a work that extends more than 4 octaves above the bottom string.
If anyone wants to hear the limits of the cello’s potential then they could do worse than to listen to the recordings of this work that the cellist Janos Starker has made several times in his long career. It would be difficult, in my view, to find better.

Re: Why Birds Sing

nicholas - you suggest that musicians basing their music on birdsong would have been in marginalised. Definitely not so with Olivier Messiaen (spelt wrongly above - this is the correct version, in case you want to google). He was one of the most respected ‘classical’ composers in the world, and was professor of composition at the Paris Conservatoire from 1966 to 1978. He taught Boulez and Stockhausen, among others, and was, incidentally, a champion of the ondes Martenot, an instrument related to the Theremin, as used in the great Midsomer Murders theme tune.

Anyway, here’s what Wikipedia says about Messiaen:

“Messiaen found birdsong fascinating; he believed birds to be the greatest musicians and considered himself as much an ornithologist as a composer. He notated birdsongs worldwide, and he incorporated birdsong transcriptions into a majority of his music. His innovative use of colour, his personal conception of the relationship between time and music, his use of birdsong, and his intent to express profound religious ideas, all combine to make it almost impossible to mistake a composition by Messiaen for the work of any other western composer.”
