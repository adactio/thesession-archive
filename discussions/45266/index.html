Re: Help…new neighbors have destroyed my ability to practice whistle at home

Feeling like you’re bothering others certainly does change the practice dynamic, and when the practice dynamic is affected, so is everything else. If you feel like you can’t practice in your own home, talking to your neighbors is the best thing you can do. At best, you figure out something that works for both of you and maybe even make some new friends. At worst, you learn about your neighbors, and possibly their schedules and routines, or situations that may give you an idea of why they have an issue with your practicing.

One of the most intimidating things I ever did was introduce myself to all of my adjacent neighbors and check in with them about the noise, because I lived in a small studio apartment and I played an acoustic piano(yikes!). I only ended up meeting two of them, and the apartment on the non-piano side was vacant. Neither of the neighbors I met had any issues with me practicing, and one was glad to meet me. To be thorough, I also checked in with my landlord every now and then, and she always gave me a warm smile and a, “There have been no noise complaints.”

I know this looks bad, especially after such negative experiences, but there are a number of possible reasons why you may be able to practice at home after all.

1. Your neighbors may work or routinely be away during your convenient ours. If they’re friendly and understanding, you may be able to establish a relationship with them that draws their support and cooperation.

2. They may not actually be bothered by you, or hear you at all. Thin walls are real, but so is aural space, and their aural space may not be affected by you due to their own noise. I know this may not be the case for you, but it may not be as bad as it looks.

3. At best, they like the music and want to hear you. It’s a “perfect world” scenario but it happens.

Of course, there are also plenty of reasons why neighbors may have a problem with you practicing. Young children, working for home, they sleep during the day/work at night etc. But you will never know if you don’t talk to them.

If talking to them is absolutely out of the question, do some research and learn about your instruments sound control options. Discovering heavy-metallic mutes for the fiddle was both shocking and timely for me, because I was a beginner and didn’t realize they even existed. With that mute, I could practice at virtually anytime during the day, and even into the evening.

↳ Posted by tradlad on August 10th, 2020.

Quick & easy method to make a quiet practice whistle.

All the methods I’ve seen, (including link above) suggest various ways of covering the fipple / top sound hole. The geometry of this is critical to the tone and tuning and that seems to be easily ruined by adding anything into or over this.
I found an easier and more reliable way is simply to use pvc electrical tape (the cheap stretchy stuff that comes in rolls and various colours) to restrict the airflow into the whistle, where you blow in, (NOT the top sound hole).
Stick two pieces the tape so as to leave a small vertical ‘slot’, centred on the inlet. I’ve just measured what width gap works for me; it’s about 1.5mm.
2 mm softens the sound a bit, but you can go narrower. By 1 mm it’s getting too narrow, and hard to blow into the second octave.
I recommend any whistle player gives this a try. Most will have occasions when quieter practicing is appropriate.

“… more a myth than anything. They say it deflects a bit of the sound so reduces reverb …”

Yes, that is a myth - or misconception. Eggboxes on walls are a cheap and fairly effective way of reducing echo in a room; it was formerly a popular method of deadening the acoustics in low budget recording and broadcasting studios etc. - which has been misinterpreted as soundproofing. The only way to reduce sound transmission is to have thicker or denser walls.

It may not have been a particularly warm acknowledgment, but I’ve always thought of eye contact as an acknowledgement. Some people don’t talk to strangers, so if someone is approaching them, they wait for them to follow through with the interaction they initiated.

With that being said, it’s perfectly understandable and reasonable to be intimidated to meet your neighbors. They very well could be mean-spirited or dangerous people. I just wouldn’t make judgements that far based on a blank stare and a possible misunderstanding.

Here is the loose script I would use if approaching a stranger who was one of my neighbors about this issue. Note: I wouldn’t use this script in the moment of your practicing, in case they are hostile towards you.

ーー
“Excuse me, may I have a moment?” (reveals their disposition towards you and if they are open with talking to you.)

“How are you today?”(reveals their mood and if it’s a good time to talk to them or not at present.)

“I’m so sorry to bother you, my name is […] we’re neighbors.”(introduces your intention, and reveals whether or not they may already know you.)

“I’m a [musician] and sometimes I practice when I’m home. This is an attempt to be conscious of our shared space. Does the sound bother you at all? Or is it really loud?” (this expresses acknowledgment of a possible issue and an interest in their comfort.).
ーー

Hopefully, this gets the conversation started and lays the issue out in a constructive way that can be sorted out civilly. This doesn’t guarantee a positive interaction, but it does give you the peace of mind of knowing that you tried and were willing to sort out the issue. It also may reveal a number of things that would be good to know in the future.

I hope something works out for you and that you find some way to practice comfortably and in peace. Note: Playing music in the park can be a very enriching and therapeutic experience. I go out to the local square for concertina tunes all the time. Though, I wouldn’t recommend practicing where others can here you unless you’re trying to get over performance anxiety.

Try putting a terry-cloth stretchy pony-tail band over the fipple opening. You can grade the volume level, depending on how much or how little you cover the air outlet.
