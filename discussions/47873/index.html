What makes Irish music sound Irish

I think it’s a mixture of modality (Mostly Dorian and Mixolydian), pastoralism, and the pentatonic scale. If you listen to composers like Seán Ó Riada, Charles Villier Stanford, and Archibald Rosenthal for example, their music is steeped in pastoral themes, use of pentatonic scales and modal scales. Now I can’t really describe in detail what constitutes a pastoral theme yet, I just know that it “sounds like” the countryside. It sounds open and free. Like a breath of fresh air. The pentatonic scale is just a given with rare use of the 7ths and 4ths. Usually the 7th comes at the end to create a sense of resolution, and the 4th, 6th scale degree represent tension. Each tune is split into parts that create contrast making use of sequencing (When a tune repeats the opening idea with a different underlying harmony) a lot of the classic tunes don’t sequence though. The language is spoken in tiny period structures (Use contrasting material after the first idea instead of sequencing) Then instead of sequencing it repeats the first idea with cadence material. (This is known as a hybrid, but I’m afraid I’m just starting to recognize those) and occasional sentence structure (Makes use of heavy sequencing). 8 bars per phrase each split up into 2 bars per idea or 4 bars per idea. The sense of resolution or cadence material comes within the last two bars of a sentence creating a sense of closure or, to create lift, a sense of non-closure that leads back to the beginning and/or to the next sentence or period (the B part). This is all for dance tunes btw. Airs and songs are a whole different ballgame that I have yet to understand.

The rhythmic recipes for the different tune types are as follows:

Double Jig: 6/8 with use of 8th notes to make up the melody can be concluded on a quarter note followed by 8th notes to create lift.

Hop Jig: 9/8 with use of a mix of quarter notes and 8th notes to make up the melody.

Reels: 4/4 with use of 8th notes to make up the melody sometimes ending on quarter notes

Irish Polkas: 2/4 with use of dotted 8th notes to create what I call an elastic feel. Where it stretches and shoots forwards and 16th notes

Hornpipes: 4/4 with use of 8th notes, triplets and quarter notes. Also includes a subtle to sometimes heavy swing.

Slip Jigs: 9/8 with use of 8th notes ending on quarter notes.

Hop Jigs: 9/8 with use of quarter notes and 8th notes to create that “elastic” feeling.

Slides: 12/8 With the use of quarter notes and 8th notes dispersed in between each other.

Re: What makes Irish music sound Irish

Kellie, I can’t help but wonder if much “compositional technique” has actually gone into the most traditional-sounding tunes.

What I mean is that the vast majority of traditional tunes probably grew not out of composition theory or a prescriptive sense of how to write a tune, but much more straight from a musician’s immersion in this music, in the vocabulary and idioms found across the body of trad tunes. And then other people play the tune and make little changes, and the tune keeps being tweaked this way, until it’s not so much “composed” as planted, watered, and nurtured by the trad music community.

Some trad “composers” might pen tunes with theory in mind, but at least some of those tunes then sound overly “composed”—like intellectual exercises rather than something that sprouted from the tradition.

It would be interesting to ask Charlie Lennon how he gets his tunes. Could be wrong but I’ve a hunch he hears an idea in his head that’s just a bit different from every other tune he’s played over his life, and then he follows it to see where it might go. Not thinking of 7ths and 4ths or modes, but just feeling it out.

And as Ailin suggests, the music’s in the playing of it. Likely a great many tunes grew out of twiddling about on one’s fiddle or whistle or flute, etc., building a tune out of what’s possible under the fingers.

Re: What makes Irish music sound Irish

This will come across as unduly harsh and critical, but Kellie, I really intend nothing more than to help clear a path toward your own insights into this music.

Looking for patterns is fine, but be wary of too quickly assigning labels to them and assuming you’ve captured the gist of thing. The labels “mixolydian” and “dorian” aren’t doing you any favors. Yes, there are mix and dor tunes in this music. But what mode would you assign to the reels Dinny O’Brien’s or The Old Bush? We might slot them into a mode in order to type them into the tune archive here, but that doesn’t mean the tunes actually are strictly in one mode or another. What do you do with a tune that has sharped *and* natural 7ths *in the same measure*?! In fact, that’s as much a structural element of many Irish trad tunes as any specific “mode” label. What do you do with a tune like the jig Paddy Fahy shaped mostly in G major but that’s now as often played in G dorian (https://thesession.org/tunes/532)? Does one sound “more Irish” than the other? What about the composer’s intent? What about the player’s interpretation? Is altering mode also an element of this music?

Beware general labels. You might as well be saying “Irish music features jigs and reels.” Well, so does Scottish music, and Breton music, and Metis music, and baroque, and….

Re: What makes Irish music sound Irish

Keltie, the nationality of music doesn’t come from a set of ‘rules’, they only affects how you write it down. The difference comes from language - you play as you speak. When a composer sits down to write a tune his brain is atuned to the rhythms and cadences of his native language and dialect, that is what sounds right and natural to his ear, so that’s what he writes. When you come to learn the tune you’ll lilt it or sing it in your head using words or sounds from your own language, and that is how you’ll play it. Listen to people in everyday speech: listen to an Irishman and you’ll hear jigs and reels in the rhythm of the words, listen to a Doric speaker you’ll hear strathspeys, from a Jamaican you’ll hear calypso and reggae.
