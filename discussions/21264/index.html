“Traditional, arranged by…………. ”

In a recently published volume of tunes “for Scottish fiddlers”, several traditional tunes have been included. However, each of them have been credited as “Traditional, arranged by….”.

In a few cases, the arrangement is by a particular musician while the remainder have been credited to the author of the book.

From what I can see, very few of these arrangements are significantly different from what I or many other musicians would actually play in a session or a performance.

Of course, if a player has made a conscious effort to rearrange a particular tune, eg add a variation, compose an extra part etc, then I agree that he/she should receive some credit for this.

However, just transcribing a traditional tune and publishing it in a book is hardly your own “arrangement” even if one or two notes might differ. After all, there are several different settings of traditional tunes.

Of course, I can understand that it’s another possible source of “royalties”. However, would performers now be always expected to ensure that their version of a “traditional” tune doesn’t have a copyrighted “arrangement” somewhere?

To an extent, credit is already given to other musicians on frequent occasions, i.e. “I learned this from the playing of……” .

So, is copyrighting arrangements just formalising this practice or, on the other hand, just being a “bit naughty”?

What do you think?

Re: “Traditional, arranged by…………. ”

Another facet of this discussion is that sometimes “Trad. Arr by” is used to note the fact that several tunes have been combined into a medley by a particular person or band. Working musicians put a lot of energy into their medleys of tunes, and if one is going to play a set of tunes learned “from the playing of” (which can be a dodgy proposition, depending on the context) it is only good form to credit that as well.

Similarly, with traditional song lyrics, a singer might put a lot of creative time into combining different versions of a song, making their own setting of it. The song is traditional, but their version of it is unique. Again, best practice is that if you are going to “cover” their version of it, you don’t just say “Trad.”- you credit their version of the song.

Re: “Traditional, arranged by…………. ”

For traditional tunes or songs being discussed here, the ownership is most likely in the public domain as the original copywrite will have expired (as all copywrites do). So, all versions recorded by those artists, other than the original artist, are protected material (“Trad. Arr.”) however, it is only the sound recording that is protected. This means that ‘unauthorised’ playing (in the commercial and legal sense) is prohibited; meaning no other entity can ‘play’ such a protected recording without the usual permission and / or licenses; it is the ‘recording’ itself (i.e. the recording artist’s CD) which is protected and it is nothing to do with other artists who may also have ‘protected’ their own ‘recording’ of the same public domain work which will be very common for popular traditional music.

Re: “Traditional, arranged by…………. ”

There are two separate sets of rights involved in a recording: The copyright, which protects the tune and lyrics on behalf of the composer(s), and the mechanical rights which protect the band and producers rights to the actual recording. Arrangers rights don’t only apply to the recording, if someone has made an arrangement of a song or tune that differs significantly from the original they can hold copyright on their arrangement, which covers anyone using that arrangement either live or in recording.

The “trad. Arr.” credit you see on medleys and stuff doesn’t indicate anyone’s right to anything, it is only a credit. To find out who owns the rights to a particular track, somewhere on the CD label there will be a C in a circle, which tells you who owns the copyright or license for all the music CD, and a P in a circle which tells you who owns the mechanical rights. You then have to contact those rights holders to find out who holds the rights to an individual track.

As for what is and isn’t traditional you might be surprised, I think there may be a lot of truly traditional stuff getting recorded in Irish circles, but here in Scotland most of what gets recorded by trad musicians these days is still in copyright. The old tunes still get played a lot, but they are so well known that people look for more unusual stuff to record. I recently did the rights for a CD by a small local band, the track list they gave me listed only three composers out of twelve tracks, the rest were marked as trad. When I checked the registrations, it turned out only two tracks were trad, all the rest were still in copyright.
