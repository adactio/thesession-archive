↳ Posted by LHMark on May 13th, 2015.

Re: What are the fundamental differences betwixt a bouzouki and an octave mandolin?

Scale length (vibrating length of the string/the distance from nut to bridge), tuning, and style. Bouzoukis have a longer scale length (in the 24-26+ inch range) and are usually tuned to open or semi-open tunings like GDAD or ADAD. Octaves on the other hand usually have a scale length around 20-23 inches and are usually tuned an octzave below a mandolin in GDAE. Bouzoukis tend to be used more for accompaniment while octaves tend to be used for melody. This is how I see it but there really aren’t any hard and fast rules for distinguishing them and they may vary from player to player and region to region (for instance these are American terms in Ireland one might see an instrument with a 22 inch scale length called a short scale bouzouki, one with a 25 inch scale called a long scale bouzouki, and something with a 20 inch scale called a mandola (occasionally the word “octave” comes before that). I guess it depends on who made it and who is playing it.

The question you should be asking yourself is do you want to play tunes on it or back them? For the former an octave provides an easier reach on the left hand for the latter a bouzouki has a more resonant sound.

Re: What are the fundamental differences betwixt a bouzouki and an octave mandolin?

Primarily scale length and tuning, which leads to slightly different applications. It breaks down something like this:

Bouzoukis are usually longer scale instruments with lighter gauge strings than Octave Mandolins, and a resulting “zingier” tone. Very good if you mainly play accompaniment, and usually tuned GDAD. The body design is most often a flat top and flat back with an oval soundhole, although there are archtop versions. As a design, it’s a bit more formalized and predictable than Octave Mandolins, which can vary quite a bit.

Octave mandolins are usually somewhere in the 20“-22” scale range, which makes them easier for playing melody as well as accompaniment without a capo. An OM player focusing mainly on accompaniment might use GDAD tuning, while someone playing a mix of melody and accompaniment might use GDAE like a mandolin or fiddle, an octave down. That’s how I tune my OM, since I’m mainly interested in melody. I play guitar for accompaniment.

The body style of OMs can vary quite a bit. There are “Irish” style OMs with flat tops and backs, built like a shorter-scale Bouzouki. There are OMs designed as large versions of Gibson mandolins with carved archtops, and either oval holes or f-holes (that’s what I play). There is also something often called a GBOM, or “guitar-based octave mandolin,” with a large guitar body and double course strings tuned in GDAE or GDAD. It can be confusing, and there are some major tonal differences between these different body styles, but they’re all considered Octave Mandolins.

Cross-posted with Why Bother? above, but it sounds like we’re on the same page.

Re: What are the fundamental differences betwixt a bouzouki and an octave mandolin?

The mandolin family has been around for a while, and is pretty well defined, following the nomculture of the violin family (except that, just to add a little confusion, the instrument we call a tenor mandola isn’t really a tenor, it is pitched as an alto, matching the viola, and the instrument we call an octave mandola is really a tenor, matching the tenor violin)

Mandolin - scale length about 14", tuned GDAE
Tenor mandola - scale length 16“-17” (but sometimes a bit longer, as happens with violas) tuned CGDA
Octave mandola - scale length 19“ - 22” tuned GDAE an 8ve below the mandolin
Mandocello - scale length 24“ - 26” tuned CGDA, an 8ve below the mandola.

All mandolin family instruments have four courses, tuned in unison. Obviously, if you change the tuning of one or two strings it doesn’t suddenly stop being a mandola, it is the pitch range that is important, not the actual tuning.

The Irish bouzouki is a fairly new instrument, and much less clearly defined, but generally it is something tuned in the same pitch range as the octave mandolin, but with a substantially longer neck and lighter stringing, either in unison or octaves. There is no confusion between bouzouki and mandocello because although they have similar scale lengths, they are tuned to different pitch ranges (and the mandocello has a wider fingerboard to accommodate the heavier strings).

In the case of christy taylor’s instrument with a 23" scale, if it has 8ve strung lower courses then calling it a short scale bouzouki is quite legitimate, but if it was strung in unison it would just be a slightly oversized octave mandola.

‘Cittern’ was originally coined by Stefan Sobell for an instrument he ‘invented’, but these days it is used specifically to denote a mandolin family instrument with five courses.

@ jeff_lindqvist:
The scale lengths for Mandola and Mandocello are pretty well locked down, at least on this (USA) side of the Atlantic, because those were instruments built in relatively large numbers in the early years of the 20th Century for mandolin orchestras, playing string quartet and other orchestra arrangements for the traditional violin family. Which is where the names came from. This pre-dates, by a long shot, the adoption of the Greek bouzouki in Irish music (although of course we’re talking about different music, for these instruments).

There is even a surviving example of a 22" scale Octave Mandolin made by the Gibson company in 1904:

http://www.mandolincafe.com/forum/showthread.php?64364-1904-Gibson-Octave-Mandolin

Scale length is 21 1/4“, very close to what many might consider a more-or-less standard 22” scale Octave Mandolin in the present day. Because it’s just one example, and because it wouldn’t have fit into the conventional Mandolin Orchestra concept back then (being halfway between a Mandola and Mandocello), it may be that this was a one-off, custom instrument. But at least someone was thinking about it, and willing to pay for it, 111 years ago.

Re: What are the fundamental differences betwixt a bouzouki and an octave mandolin?

“Either instrument can be tuned in unison pairs or high/low octaves like a 12-string guitar, usually on just the lower two string courses.”

Yes, they can. But if you octave string an OM it ceases to be an OM and becomes a short bouzouki. I think it might be helpful to think about the origins of the instruments and their intended purpose. The mandola came to ITM from the European classical arsenal, as a melody instrument for picking tunes (forget about American genres where the mandolin is used as a soprano guitar, that is a separate evolutionary branch, and has little bearing on what happened in Ireland and Europe). On a melody instrument 8ve stringing is not much use - you land up with a bizarre tonal change as you cross from the 8ve D course to the unison A, so a mandola is tuned in unison.

The bouzouki came into ITM from a completely different direction, and for a different purpose - it was brought in specifically as a chordal accompaniment instrument, for which 8ve stringing is ideal as it gives a much fuller chord. Unfortunately, in its original form the Irish Bouzouki performs its intended task well, but isn’t particularly versatile. To try to overcome that people are experimenting with unison stringing, shorter necks etc, sometimes blurring the distinction from the OM. The Irish bouzouki hasn’t yet found a definitive form, it is still evolving, so if you look at an instrument and think “Is this a bouzouki?” you might not be able to answer. But if you approach it from the other direction and ask “Is this an octave mandola?” that instrument is well defined so you should be able give a definitive answer. And if it isn’t a mandola, then it’s probably a bouzouki.

Re: What are the fundamental differences betwixt a bouzouki and an octave mandolin?

o‘muirgheasain, The first thing to do is look at/measure the strings that were supplied with the instrument, and use the D’Addario tables or an online tension calculator to work out the tensions. If you are playing about with different tunings and gauges it is always safe to go to a lower tension, but as christy has pointed out, it’s not safe to go to higher tensions - decent instruments are like racing cars, they are built to be just strong enough and no more.

In general lighter strings will give less volume and sustain. From a purely tonal point of view instruments give their best with the heaviest strings they can handle. Makers generally understand their instruments, so if you stick with the gauges they recommend/supply you won’t go far wrong. The main reason for fitting lighter strings would be to make the instrument easier to play, but you are compromising the sound to do it - far better to learn to deal with the heavier strings.

The other problem you may encounter is that if the instrument was originally set up well, then lighter strings will buzz - lighter strings have a wider swing and require a higher action, so you may need a higher bridge.

Switching between octave and unison stringing generally isn’t a problem in terms of tension - although the unison string is much heavier the gauges are chosen so that the tension is about the same in both.
