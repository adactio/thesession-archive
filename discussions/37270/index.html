Losing the music

Has anyone ever totally lost the will to play? Does it ever come back?

I’ve still enjoyed the odd time I have played in a session, but I don’t do that very much these days. Otherwise, the inclination to play has vanished. It’s a bit sad but not so much so that I’m picking up the pipes. Instead I’m eyeing them mournfully, thinking, ‘I really ought to play you, but don’t feel like it.’

Re: Losing the music

Famous story: Paddy Keenan was about to throw his pipes into a dumpster in London, thirty or forty years ago. He was stopped by a friend and we have all suffered ever since.
“During this time [c. 1970], Keenan didn’t play his pipes, even trying at one point to sell them at a pawn shop, but had no takers…. They were Crowley pipes with a custom Rowsome chanter, and the Crowley part of the set had been owned by the Honorable Garret Browne of the Guinness family. Keenan walked out of the pawn shop and made to throw his pipes into a trash bin. A friend stopped him and told him he’d keep the pipes for him.”
So take heart. You may or may not play the pipes again. There is life after pipes.

Re: Losing the music

For me, yes and yes. I know exactly how you are feeling. After playing piano for decades the fire just burned out in me. The only time I touched a piano was to tune one for hire. It didn’t happen all at once. I dropped and picked it back up several times before finally calling it total quits. I went about a decade without playing. But the itch to play again slowly came back. But did I want to play the piano again? Heck no, after that amount of time I’d lost all my technique and endurance and I didn’t feel like spending the time and frustration of building that back up, it would only discourage me again. So last year, on a whim I picked up a used hammered dulcimer off Ebay. I took a chance not only on buying a twenty year old instrument sight unseen, but also of not knowing if I would like it, or could even play it. On the latter part, I figured it was a good bet as I’ve always like hammered dulcimer music since being introduced to it back in the 80’s. In any event, if I didn’t like playing it I could always resell it or give it away. This time took a different approach to my music. With no college theory classes and performance jury’s to attend, I decided on playing something entirely different from all the classical stuff I learned. One year and three dulcimers later, I’m playing most every day and even performing where I can. I now play ITM, church hymns and some popular music, stuff I literally rarely ever did. And I enjoy the challenge of making my own arrangements, pulling out my rusty knowledge of music theory from long, long ago. I guess the bottom line in all this is: maybe you’re not burned out with playing music, just with playing the same instrument and/or the same music. So try picking up an entirely new (to you) instrument and learn it, or maybe try out some new music styles on your pipes that’s completely foreign to you, something you thought you’d never play. Try composing or arranging. Anything that’s new, different, and exciting. Ten years is a long time to be stuck in a rut, with no direction to go but backwards.

David E.

Re: Losing the music

You obviously haven’t completely lost the love - if you had you wouldn’t be here posting. you’d have put your pipes in the cupboard and walked away. You’ve probably just gone stale because you don’t feel you’re progressing or acheiving anything.

Step one: love your instrument. Give them a good clean, fettle the reeds (or whatever pipers do), get them into tip-top condition so that they are a joy to own and play.

Step two: challenge yourself. Dig out a tune or a technique that you’ve alway shied away from because you think it’s too difficult, then master it. The feeling of achievement will almost certainly get you going again.

Step three: stay fresh. Don’t get caught in a rut of just going to your regular session, playing the same old tunes with the same old people from one year end to the next. Find different tunes, different people to play with,,different situations to play in.

Re: Losing the music

I learned classical piano growing up and made it to grade 8. I worked hard at it playing 1-2 hours a day but I never felt I had “got there” yet - the problem with the grades system I think - and I would not have described myself as a musician.

Life and a job and things happened and it gradually fizzled out until 15 yrs of not playing went by. Then I suddenly found I was missing making music - it had been such a big part of me. Aged 40 I treated myself to a viola on the back of 18 months of violin lessons in primary school and felt as if the other half of me had come alive again. At first I just made sound on the instrument, long notes, freestyle. I took a few lessons, then I discovered ITM and later a fiddle and have loved the challenge of learning this whole new genre which was unknown to me. Now I play fiddle, viola, whistles, am learning pipes and church organ (nod to the piano there).

If the music is there, it will come and get you again, one way or the other. Keep the pipes but don’t stress; just let it be. You could stroke them occasionally, if you wanted. And if you feel a hankering for another instrument, a new challenge, go out and do it. Just for the fun of, the love of the music. Not for exams or grades or competitions, just for you. That’s what I think.

And before anyone leaps in, I am not, repeat not, dissing any instrument whatsoever, especially whistles! I think they’re all grand!

‘Some great advice on here, so I won’t repeat it other than to add my two penn’orth.

Sessions can be great, mediocre or downright awful. That’s why I keep going, as just once in while, something clicks into place and the combined effort is way better than the sum of the parts…. and then it falls apart again. It’s those rare moments of magic that keep me going to them. (But not too often, as they often do my head in, and not as my only source of contact with other musicians. ) Another benefit of sessions is you get to meet other musicians and maybe if you like them and how they play, suggest meeting for a house session and then the exciting stuff can really happen, when you learn from each other, develop something and hone your skills. I have found a need to balance out the Russian Roulette of sessions with time elsewhere playing with other musicians at a similar level, or better (if they will tolerate me- it helps me to up my game).

Sometimes that doesn’t always work out, and there is a fallow patch- I reckon our creativity is like a bank account, you can only keep paying out for so long before it gets overdrawn and needs some filling up again. This could be something directly to do with your creative practice as a musician, like going to a concert, listening to CDs, or something completely unrelated- an art exhibition, a visit to some gardens, going to the cinema or just hanging out with your friends, having a laugh. Whatever makes you feel joyful.

Just don’t worry about it- if it’s not a joy, just put those pipes away, where they can’t look at you reproachfully, and concentrate on bringing some joy back into life generally. Just do what makes you feel good. Feed your soul and the music may flow again. Or you will feel inspired to do something else. I’m having a few years-long break from being a painter, which is the profession I trained for, and from playing the piano for which I had lessons as a child for ten years or so, but am having a great time now making leathergoods and playing the fiddle.
