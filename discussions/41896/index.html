Tuneworks

A turgid and hollow approximation of a tradition for a dislocated society that no longer has any real worth in its arts and culture or a valuable resource?

I’m struggling to decide. Part of me really detests these false, mocked up and stripped down tune books for pensioners , late learners and the encouraged young. Another part of me knows all too well that in a fractured world very few of us are lucky enough to actually be part of any folk tradition. These Tuneworks type efforts help create an ersatz approximation of such and so perpetuate some kind of folk culture I guess. My problem is the way works like this create a kind of homogenized subculture that pervades in a circuit of folk festivals and sessions creating the kind of culture that reminds me so much of school and church clubs. Is stuff like this another “magic book” to prop the socially awkward and disconnected up or a genuine reflection of a folk tradition?

Re: Tuneworks

“…tunebook-based ‘sessions’ at local folk festivals … workshops for new learners.”

That partly much describes Tuneworks. There is, if I’m not mistaken, a kind of two-tier system - i. tune learning workshops, for learning tunes from scratch and ii. session for those that have either already learned the tunes or are more confident sightreaders or ear-players. I think they are of some use to classical players interested in playing traditional music, in that they bridge the gap between orchestra and session, still offering the ‘crutches’ of sheet music and an element of direction - and perhaps to the beginner musician, in that it gives them an opportunity to try their repertore ‘in anger’, but withtout fear of being judged for their fluffed notes, wobbly timing etc.

But having seen quite a few of these sessions at folk festivals in England (and even participated in them as an anchor musician a couple of times), I notice that some people keep turning up year after year, without graduating to ‘real’ sessions - thus creating a kind of ‘tradition’ of their own. Ultimately, it is their choice - they are in their comfort zone and who can blame them for wanting to remain there? What saddens me is seeing young kids placed in that environment in order supposedly to introduce them to traditional music, when they would be much better served sitting on the periphery of a ‘real’ session, seeing and hearing musicians in full flight.

I think a lot of the ‘perpetual beginners’ that you get at slow sessions probably go as much for a social occasion as anything else.

@SteveT:

That last point deserves more attention, I think. We do need a wide range of players to sustain a market for specialized trad instruments. A fiddle is a fiddle, but some of the other instruments commonly played in sessions are modern recreations and interpretations, that might not exist if the market was smaller.

For myself, I was able to buy a modern interpretation of a 19th Century flute to play this music, instead of having to hunt down a wobbly (and probably very costly) antique, or adapt to playing a Boehm silver flute. I don’t think something like this would exist, without a wide market of players of all levels to support the few dedicated makers who are targeting the “Irish traditional” market. Players of box, bouzouki, bodhran, and pipes would be in a similar boat. At least Bluegrass, OldTime, and 50’s Rock musicians have no trouble finding instruments to play!

I do realize that there is a trap that some beginners fall into with “comfort zone” classes, where they never advance past playing off sheet music at a slow tempo. I’ve seen it with one local class. These are people that will never join a local pub session because they’re happy where they are. But hey, if this kind of thing helps sustain a market for trad instruments, I don’t see the harm. Also, as Colman O’B pointed out above, slow sessions and classes like this act as a safety valve, so the local higher-end sessions don’t get overrun with perpetual beginners. That alone is valuable.

Re: Tuneworks

I’m lucky enough to know the folks who started Tuneworks & they helped me when I was starting out.

Firstly (as others have said) it’s designed for people starting out, the tunes are well known & relatively easy. As far as I’m aware, there was no “meeting to decide” what standard tunes were used. It’s just tunes that most people would have heard of (and I think at the time, could get recordings of). It’s a tool, to help people get going. They’re not designed to be an alternative to getting to a good session regularly. Nothing can compete with that.

And hopefully, some of those who start will end up being able to play at sessions. I did. Caveat that I may be a bit of an outlier. I had a mission to learn, knew a lock of tunes already (it helps to have a family of players), and could already play music. For others, perhaps the vast majority, they might never get past the stage of pulling out a tunebook and having a rattle at it. And there’s no harm in that.

As for the people who started Tuneworks. I know for a fact that they don’t lay any claim to purity of tradition or mastery of style. They’re simply good players, a few rungs up a ladder, and willing to help other people get onto it. I’ve caught up with them the odd time at a session since, and I appreciate that helping hand at the start.

Though I’ve not been to a folk fest in a few years, I know that attendances at the workshops have boomed. But I also know that there are (and always have been) decent “proper” sessions at in and around the festivals, with seriously great players. Of course one outweighs the other, by virtue of the fact that fewer people can play to the standard of a decent session. Whereas everyone was a beginner.

I would probably class myself as one of your somewhat despised ‘latecomers’ who have found Tuneworks to be a useful step forward. Coming from a non-musical family and tradition I never developed the listening skills required to pick tunes up by ear, having taught myself to read music with an old recorder and a tutor book. That was the only music I had. I then played guitar for a few years, and tried violin but that was never going to happen, too ‘classical’ and sounded terrible.
40 years later I picked up a fiddle again, encouraged by an adult learners group, and much of the stuff we played came from Tuneworks. Yes, the tunes are a bit dumbed down maybe, but I found an opening into the kind of music I really always wanted to play. I now play the fiddle, getting much more proficient, and seek out for myself better/different settings of the tunes I particularly like.
And by playing the simply arranged stuff I am developing my listening skills, from non-existent to certainly-beginning-to-happen. I still can’t pick up very fast and complex stuff but from not being able to put three notes together without the dots I am well on the way to playing by ear now.
I was rather offended by what I read in the original post. Latecomers to music may need a different approach to those who have been lucky enough to be surrounded by traditional music for a large portion of their lives, but we can still be classed as musicians. If Tuneworks gives people a step up to playing music, in any way, I can’t see any harm in it. If somebody turns up their nose at a Tuneworks version of something I play, then I don’t value their opinion, but if they kindly invite me to play a little differently then I am ‘all ears’.

Steve T: “There is a section on the Tuneworks site where someone is showcasing local tunes on a piano accordion. Playing from the dots by the looks”

I assume you mean the Shropshire tune archive.

Firstly, it is a 3-row button accordion (apparently something like what Jimmy Shand played, with bisonoric RH and Stradella LH - although probably differently tuned on the RH).

Secondly, that ‘someone’ is John Kirkpatrick, who, whether or not you like his style or the tunes he plays, is a highly respected traditional musician who plays in a authentically English style and most definitely has plenty of tunes under his fingers. He might be sight reading them to ensure he plays the particular setting that is written down since, like any good traditional player, he does not naturally play a tune the same way twice. (I will admit that he seems uncomfortable playing the tunes just with the right hand, but they come to life when he adds the basses.)

In my opinion, this tune archive the best section of the site since it represents a real tradition, if a partly revived one. Even if English music is not your thing (which I imagine it is not for a lot of people that frequent this site - it is a relatively small ‘thing’ for me), I think you could get more out of these videos than you could from learning a mishmash of English, Scottish, Shetland, Irish and American tunes in a generic tunebook session in a beer tent.
