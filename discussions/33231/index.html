↳ Posted by GreySatyr on January 31st, 2014.

Re: Session Question

At the sessions I have attended, and others with more experience may have had a different experience, but someone will suggest a set, and typically the person who suggests the set is the one who leads the set. She or he will play the A part through once, and then those who know the tune will join in. At my session, the guitars and bouzoukis handle accompaniment. The rest of us, the fiddlers/(flute players/floutists/flutists)/concertina players/dulcimer players, stick to the melody, with ornaments here and there.

I don’t think that ITM sessions are about allowing people to “shine,” really. Not that there is anything wrong with that aspect of bluegrass; it’s just different.

Re: Session Question

A session is a VERY different experience than a bluegrass jam. I remember once playing at a party that included Irish, Bluegrass and Old Timey players. I was in a room full of bluegrass and old-timey fiddlers. I thought I’d start a cross-over tune everyone would know, so I launched into Red-Haired Boy. We were standing as we played and as we were coming around to the A part, the fiddlers gathered around me (on flute) and just went into a pizzicato accompaniment, with only me carrying the melody. Caught me quite off guard, that did. That would never happen in an Irish session. In an Irish session, typically, someone starts a tune where each part is repeated and the entire tune is played three times over. Then, whomever started the tune launches into another tune, or if it is apparent he or she did not have one in mind, someone else might jump in with a tune the segues well from the first tune. Typically, this goes on for a total of two or three tunes, but there is no real limit. There are no solos or “breaks,” and absolutely no improvisation beyond some variations of the tune each time through that does not collide with others who aren’t playing it. It’s a toss-up as to whether there will be instruments playing in accompaniment. It is not necessary, and among some, not appreciated. Final thought: If you don’t know the tune (or learn new ones REALLY quickly), sit out. Noodling (as it is known) is heavily frowned upon.

Re: Session Question

Not every session has or needs a leader. Sometimes there might be an implicit leader, to whom everyone defers on account of their greater experience, standard of playing or repertoire. In other cases, there is a designated leader or leaders, who may or may not be receiving payment for playing. How the designated leader(s) execute(s) their role depends very much on the individual(s) and on the other musicians; some may choose and start all the tune sets whilst others may actively offer each of the other participants a turn to start a set. Other ‘leaders’ might take a more relaxed stance, letting others start most of the tunes and only starting a set when there is a silence, their main role being to provide continuity and a certain standard of music throughout.

“I don’t think that ITM sessions are about allowing people to ”shine,“ really.”

It may not be what Irish sessions are *about*, exactly, but opportunities to shine do exist. For example, when someone is asked to start a tune, others might sit out and listen to the person’s playing for a couple of times round before joining in.

I think one concept you need to grasp, GreySatyr, is that, whilst in Bluegrass there are certain instruments that are essential to any band or session (banjo, mandolin, guitar, fiddle, bass), an Irish traditional session is a lot more flexible in this regard. A session *could* include pipes, fiddle, flute, whistle, accordion, concertina, banjo, mandolin, guitar, bouzouki and harp; on the other hand, it could just be two concertinas and a flute or a banjo and uillean pipes - or two dozen fiddles.

Re: Session Question

The answer is “it depends.” However, here are some of my observations and experiences. There are exceptions to all of them, which other commenters will surely point out.

Traditional Irish sessions are not like bluegrass or old-time American sessions, where the a single tune is played endlessly, with instrumentalists taking solo “breaks,” and with a lot of backup. In Irish sessions, it is customary for melody instruments to play the melody of the tune all the way through rather than backing up a soloist. Provided they know it; otherwise it is a good idea to listen, rather than noodle or try to back up. In Irish traditional music, especially the instrumental kind, the melody (tune) is most important, and backup/rhythm is minimal (or at least tasteful), or sometimes absent altogether. This is less the case than years before, but I did play in a session with 20-30 melody players and four backup instrumentalists, only two of whom (an Irish bouzouki player and me) could back up by ear and play any of the tunes. (Not that anyone could hear us with all those fiddles, whistles, flutes and pipes. And not that they cared.) You will sometimes find sessions that are overwhelmed by guitars, chopping backup mandolins, shaky eggs, spoons, and the like. These are less than optimal experiences for musicians and listeners.

In traditional Irish sessions, it is common to play medleys of three or more tunes, with each tune usually played three times, though this can vary depending on the number of sections to the tune. A great many tunes have two or three sections, and are played thrice, but some have five or six sections, and might only be played twice. Sometimes the tunes in a medley (or set) are all the same type (all jigs, all reels, all polkas, etc.) and sometimes they aren’t. Sometimes they’re all in the same key, sometimes they aren’t. The same goes for modes. Sometimes the tunes are announced at the beginning of the set, sometimes someone calls them out just before the change, and sometimes you just gotta figure it out on the fly. Since all the melody instruments are playing at the same time, it is not a good idea to just add your own ornamentation unless you are confident it is the same, nearly the same, or consistent with the lead/stronger players ornamentation.

For some reason, it has become common in the US to call both songs and tunes “songs.” Don’t do this. Tunes are instrumental, without words. Songs have words. However, songs are sung to tunes (or airs), but if no one is singing, it’s a tune. Don’t worry, though. The trad police will not haul you away. Not usually.

If you are within driving distance of Athens, GA, please come down to our sessions at the Globe on Sundays. We’re friendly, even to guitarists, and not stuffy at all. Everyone gets a chance to call a set or a tune or song. We even sneak in an occasional bluegrass tune. If I’m there, you’ll get to hear a harper, playing tunes with accompaniment or backing up.

Re: Session Question

Everyone plays the melody. Chordal backup by guitar/bouzouki/mandolin is fairly recent (within the last 60 years) and not “traditional” at all. There is a fair amount of ornamentation (following certain guidelines), so it’s good to learn what the ornaments are and how to use them:

(fiddle) Left Hand:
a) the cut
b) the turnaround
c) the trill
d) the slide
e) double stops

Right Hand:
a) the treble (sometimes called triplet)
b) the chop
c) double stops

In some sessions harmonies are allowed - it’s certainly genre appropriate (see De Dannan, Solas, Lunasa for examples), but little or no improvisation and certainly no “soloing”, although in a band with a limited number of melodic players, it appears that one or another may take over the melody while the others play more quietly for a time (this is something De Dannan does a lot, so does Solas and Lunasa).

“I have been told that a roll is not quite the same as a ”turn“ in classical music—but I couldn’t say, myself.”

You are correct. In a turn on flute, when you play the note above the note you will end on, you uncover that note. In a roll, you don’t. In a turn, the intention is to hear each note of the turn, whereas in a roll, the sound of the grace notes is more subdued.

Guitar playing was first recorded as backing for Irish music in the 1930s, I think - Louis Flanagan strummed a tenor banjo on the sides he and his brother made in the 20s, and I can’t recall a side from that era with guitar backing. PJ Conlon made a few crazy sides in the 20s with a fellow strumming the bajeezus out of a tenor banjo - triplet backing, very exciting and given that PJ played a box with 6 or 8 voices it must have been louder than loud too.

Guitar shows up behind Irish players in the Swing Era of jazz in the mid 30s, or occasionally a little before. It’s interesting how backing on traditional music follows popular tastes - in the 50s Jackie Roche and Cape Bretoner Winston Fitzgerald had very jazzy guitar sidemen, guys who were obviously big fans of Barney Kessel and the like, whose music was very popular then. The goofy cuts I’ve posted here from James Keane and Paddy Glackin also are very much part of their time (late 60s/early 80s).

Re: Session Question

celticagent: “In some sessions harmonies are allowed”

Sorry C. Gent - Perhaps it was not intended thus, but it gives the impression that every session has its ‘house rules’, set out in a rule book for all newcomers to read before taking their seat in the session circle. :-) (Perhaps such sessions do exist, but I’d hate to find myself at one of them.)

A session is nothing more than a collection of individuals, with similar, but not necessarily identical tastes. The music that gets played is a consensus between the tastes of these individuals. As a visitor to a session, it is your responsibility to be sensitive to the way the other musicians play, and try your best to fit in: if they play at slowish tempos, it is probably not appropriate to start a tune at breakneck speed; if you don’t hear others playing harmonies, it is probably safe to assume that those present don’t particularly care for them.

“… (harmonies are) certainly genre appropriate (see De Dannan, Solas, Lunasa for examples)…”

I think it is best to treat band arrangements as a separate sub-genre of traditional music from sessions. Of course, the two overlap, but bands (of the kind cited) fine-tune their playing with a view to communicating with a captive audience, whilst session playing has mutual communication between musicians as its primary end. This does not mean that playing harmonies in a session is *never* appropriate; assuming that something is OK in a session because you’ve heard it on a CD is, however, misunderstanding the nature of the music.
