Singers who take the mick?

Added by TheHappyCamper on November 17th, 2023

Singers who take the mick?

If I went to a session (for the sake of argument, not my local) and as a tune player started a set of tunes, then another set of tunes, then another set of tunes, making sure nobody had time to start anything in the interim, then my guess would be somewhere around the 3rd set, i’d be politely asked to wind my neck in. And that’s in a session where the aim is to collectively play tunes (not songs) with like-minded people.

So why is it a singer (for the sake of argument, not a regular) can come and sing song after song after song in a tune session and nobody can object? I imagine the singer’s implicit defence is ‘I’m singing songs that everyone wants to hear, *i’m* the one being inclusive, you tune-players are the ones being exclusive.’ And everyone hushes the punters who would shout over tune players, the singers get maximum deference. Third song-in-a-row or not.

While i’m on the subject, there’s crashing through someone’s attempt to start a set of tunes, either with a song or just as likely a set of tunes…

Cat, meet pigeons. Discuss.

Re: Singers who take the mick?

The situation you describe isn’t “taking the mick”, it’s being self-indulgent, and downright rude and disrespectful to the musicians who come to play music together, presumably on a regular basis.
No problem at all with the odd song in the course of an evening - there was a recent discussion on this - but one or more singers imposing song after song in an instrumental session is not something I’ve come across very often. Most singers have more sense.
Two solutions - you don’t need to immediately start a set of tunes yourself once the singing stops. Pass the word round the table, “as soon as the song stops, ”Bucks Of Oranmore“ 6 times”, or similar. This may only be a temporary solution, though, depending on whether or not the hint is taken.
If the singer is so dumb as not to see he/she is overstepping the mark, it needs to be explained politely but forcefully that the session was created by instrumental musicians for them to play music together, and songs are only tolerated at their sufferance.
In the session described above, it should not be the musicians who have to leave, but the presumably unwanted insensitive singer.

Maybe the pedestal thing is because it’s a different relationship. You are playing with people, s/he is singing for people. When there’s a performance going on, folk often will respond by keeping the noise down out of courtesy, even if they’re not really listening.

That’s not an excuse, just an explanation. I like hearing good singers and good players, but I don’t enjoy having them alternating at the same event. It’d be fine if it were an agreed structure, where the tunesters took a break to stretch their shoulders and get a fresh pint while the singers took a turn, but it doesn’t sound as if it was. That’s where you need a leader with decent people skills, who can pick up that the group’s getting restive, stand up and thank the singer publicly, and then firmly invite a regular player to start a set.

I don’t think that singers in the situations described are consciously taking the mick. Rather, they don’t grasp the purpose and format of the traditional music session. I have the impression that many people who only sing and don’t play melody regard themselves as a cut (or several) above the instrumentalists. So they sing a song as something that (they think) will improve the offering, and if they get applause they will sing another. The problem is that some of them don’t know when to stop. Because instrumentalists have to play with others, they know that that there has to be give and take and they can’t expect to impose their own repertoire on the entire evening. Some singers, alas, come along along expecting to be able to sing all their favourites …

Re: Singers who take the mick?

I don’t want to sound like one of the singers who thinks they’re the bee’s knees or anything, but I do have a degree specifically in singing, for what it’s worth. Not that you need one to be a good or great singer, mind you (in fact, some would say that you need to NOT have one!). I am often asked to sing, and if asked I will oblige. I will RARELY sing otherwise, and am perfectly content to play a long session of tunes without singing a note. Sometimes, if I’m at a new-to-me session and have been asked already to start a set, if they ask me again I’ll ask if a song would be OK instead. That’s about the only time I’d start singing without some explicitly asking me.

Most of that is due to realizing that the session is not a forum for individual performance, and when one person commandeers it the rest of the people can be left out. I often try to sing songs that can be played along with (McAlpine’s Fusiliers is a personal favorite), so that there’s still a “session” vibe to the proceedings. I also avoid the sorts of slow, morose songs that some seem to gravitate to. Many are quite pretty, but there’s a time and a place! Overall, I think people appreciate it when I do sing, evidenced by the fact that I am still asked to do it.

All of which to say, there’s a way to sing at a session AND not piss everyone else off. It just takes a bit more tact than some have.
