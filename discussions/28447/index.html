Instrument Difficulties. Having doubts…

At some point, i became interested in the idea of some instruments being harder than others. This probably came from so many people telling me how hard piano was to them, and me trying to explain how “easy” it really was. Soon i began investigating, and from my conversations with Choir and Band directors, private music teachers, other soloist and bands, i came up with this list, from easiest to hardest…

Drums
Keyboards(including free reeds)
Strings
Reeds
Pipes
Horns

Eventually(while learning violin) i came up with the idea that no instrument is actually easy. That they are all hard in their own little way. Piano because it uses Bass and Treble clef, Chords and Melodies, Playing notes with both hands. But easy because Intonation was not a factor at all, and Tone not as much a factor as with the other instruments. And that one fundamental frequency was in one place. But there is one trick. They say it is the hardest instrument to master.

The biggest rumor I’ve heard, is that French Horn is the hardest instrument to learn. But this was blown out of the water by one of my friends. She told me that it was EASY to HER. 6th grade, first chair, could blow louder than all the 8th graders. I also met a girl the other they that mentioned that Clarinet was easy to her. This is also rumored to be a very very difficult instrument.

So now I’m at this idea.

Maybe it’s not the instruments that are hard. Maybe the difficulty has more to do with the fact that we either understand them or don’t. What are your ideas?

This is the information that i’ve gathered over time from others(and some from my own experience.)

Most drums, melody and harmony is not a factor at all, so learning how to use all your limbs to do different things, while staying on rhythm, seem to be the only issue(Have tried playing drums, and several different types. They are really fun)

Free Reeds, some have buttons that play two different notes, which would make it harder. But to me, they are just like the keyboards(Has had the opportunity to play a button accordion, plan on picking it up one day).

Fretted Strings, tone begins to be more significant, which makes them harder. Learned this while picking up guitar and playing around with mandolins(So far, the majority has agreed that guitar is definitely a HARD instrument. I think it’s because of the 6 strings, chords, and hand coordination factors, and the same fundamental frequency being in more than one spot). They also need to be tuned frequently because of changes in weather. Bass guitar and Ukulele were super easy(Most have agreed that these are easy instruments.)

Non-Fretted strings, tone becomes really significant and more practice/play time is needed to develop good tone. Intonation also becomes a factor. Different types of skills required, i.e. Vibrato, having a really good ear. Also need to be frequently tuned, especially travelers.

(These next types, i haven’t played enough, so i can only go by what i’ve heard.)

Pipes(Flute) I’ve heard by many different ages, levels of experience, and types of people, that Flute is one of the hardest instruments to learn. Tone is definitely a factor. Not sure about tuning and intonation. Looks to me like they are also more physically demanding. All i know is, when i tried Flute, i could barely make a sound, and when i did make a sound, i couldn’t get it to change -.-

I don’t know anything about the reeds. But i’ve heard more than once that the Saxophone is not only *easy, but also very very easy to *play badly*. Sounds funny, and also kind of intimidating. Also learned that some Saxophones aren’t concert pitch. Not sure if this makes it harder to play with others or not. They say that clarinet is really hard though. Played duets with an out of practice clarinet player and he frequently needed breaks. I’m sure this would be the case with most out of practice Pipe/Reed/and Horn players.

Horns, they say that French Horn is so hard because the notes are so close together. I’ve also heard that Trumpet requires some intense mouth and breath training. Trombone with that crazy looking slide…. i have no idea how that works, but it looks and sounds very hard.

So that’s how all of my crazy ideas have developed. Learning from my own experiences and listening to others’ ideas, and opinions, from their experiences. Would love to hear what you all think.

Re: Instrument Difficulties. Having doubts…

I think the hard part for most people isn’t in the techincalities of a particular instrument, it is in actually playing music rather than just a series of notes. Learning to first of all hear, and then to reproduce all the subtle nuances is far harder than learning how to control a particular instrument. That is why learning a second instrument is always far easier than learning your first, even if the instruments are from completely different families.

It’s interesting that when non-musicians start asking questions they always focus on the notes (how do you know where to put your fingers?) they never appreciate the importance of dynamics. Yet with almost every instrument getting the right note is the easy bit, the bit that takes years to master is the dynamic control - right hand with stringed instruments or breath control with wind.

I think the music role of a particular instrument is also a major factor in how difficult it is percieved to be - people generally consider the viola and cello to be easier than violin, even though the technique is pretty much identical. Presumably that is because music or parts for viola and cello tend to be simpler than for violin.

Re: Instrument Difficulties. Having doubts…

You can over analyze this stuff. I think the difficulty has more to do with how much expertise/effort/skill it takes to accomplish what you are trying to accomplish. That depends on a lot of factors -- including the type of music and the desires of the performer. For example, folk guitar to back simple folk songs can be relatively simple, but one can try to accomplish some rather complex things even in doing that. On the other end of the spectrum, the subtlety demanded in certain classical guitar pieces can only be done well by a small handful of performers who dedicate their lives to the craft. It is very simplistic to think bowing is more difficult than plucking in stringed instruments. The difficulty of controlling tone is much more complicated when you pluck since you only get an instant to put the energy into the string. Again, consider the very subtle effects that can be done in classical and jazz guitar. But the challenges of controlling a bow are also not to be underestimated.

Bottom line, none of them is easier than any of the others. All of them can be pushed to significant limits and beyond.

Re: Instrument Difficulties. Having doubts…

No, I think Ian is probably talking about sound.

The irish mandolin and the American mandolin are completely different animals.

The Irish mandolin is derived from the traditional European mandolin, with a low bridge height and a thin, flat (or sometimes lightly carved) top, which gives it good attack and high frequency response, making it ideal for picking melody.

American mandolins (i.e. F and A styles) were an invention of the Gibson corporation. Although they happen to share the stringing of the mandolin they were designed to fulfil the role of a soprano guitar. The thick, heavily carved top and high bridge give them a lot of volume, but without the hard attack and high frequency presence of an Irish Mandolin, making them ideal for strumming accompaniment in old-timey and bluegrass music, but not much use in ITM.

Will, I think you and several others have missed the point I was making in my original post. The important difference between American (ie Gibson) style mandolins and Irish or European instruments isn’t whether the top is carved or planed, but in the geometry of the instrument. Yes, Sobell and a few others make Irish mandolins with carved tops. But they are derived from European flat tops - the arching is very low (which is why some people think they are flat tops), the neck is set flat, and the bridge is low. All those factors add up to give a low break angle over the bridge and consequently low downforce, which allows the use of a tin soundboard and light bracing. Gibson mandolins have the neck set back and a high bridge. The resulting high break angle and downforce produces a much louder instrument. But to withstand that downforce requires high arching,and a thicker soundboard. And the thicker soundboard does not reproduce the high overtones.

The end result is that Gibson mandolins are, overall, louder than European and Irish models (whether flat or carved top). BUT almost all the sound energy they produce is in the sub 2.5 Khz range - the same part of the spectrum that all the fiddles, flutes, guitars and boxes are using, so they have to be loud to be heard. In contrast the European mandolin is quieter overall, but much more of the energy goes into the high overtones, in an area of the spectrum where there is little else happening. The end result is that, although it is quieter, it can still usually be heard, often more clearly than a Gibson, because it is not competing with other instruments.

“But does that weird looking scrolly thing on the left hand side do anything to the sound?”

I don’t think so, and I believe that’s the general consensus.

There *may* be a hair, just a hair more sustain and/or volume due to the significantly larger headstock on an F-style, but I’m not sure that’s ever been proven. It’s like those claims that adding clamp-on weights to an electric guitar headstock will improve the tone.

It does make a very nice strap hanger, which people laugh at (me too), but it’s at the natural balance point of the instrument and keeps the strap out of the way.

There’s another consideration here. If you’re going for the tone of a high-end, handmade archtop mandolin, it’s kind of hard to avoid F-styles with the scroll. It’s the flagship instrument for builders like Gilchrist, Kimble, Ellis, etc. That whole market is much more driven by Bluegrass players, who like the F-style ‘cause it’s what ’ol Bill played, than by the small niche of Irish trad players.

These ultra high-end builders do make A-style models, but most of the ones you’ll find readily available at retail stores, or for sale used, will be F-style mandolins. There’s also a consideration of resale value, with something like an F-style Gilchrist probably being easier to move and holding value better than his A-style, just due to overall market factors.

Finally, there is a family resemblance for archtop, handmade mandolins, but the tone can shift a lot of ways between builders, with the woods used, with the strings chosen by the player, and with the player’s own technique. I play an F-style, but it has a redwood top, and I use somewhat darker-sounding wound strings than a Bluegrass player would.
