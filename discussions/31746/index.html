Re: Would like input on learning a second instrument.

Tin whistle’s a given. I’ve only come across two people in my entire life who I think wouldn’t benefit from learning & playing at least a few tunes on whistle. Having said that I wouldn’t rule out flute. It’s a fairly basic instrument. If you can get a sound out of blowing across the top of a coke bottle you have the beginnings of your embouchure. With a little bit of practice you can get a good sound on the notes in the middle of the first octave. Then you’re on your way to playing a slow jig or two at a moderate pace. A little more work on the high & low end, getting comfortable with the instrument, a few cuts & taps, taking your time at first ..
If you’re interested in playing flute it does take time to develop your embouchure, even holding the instrument, & getting everything up to speed. But for someone who is ‘interested’ the basics are very straightforward.

What a shame. Still, if you are only interested in “melody”, then there’s maybe no need change over from the fiddle. It does have other possibilities which the fiddle doesn’t, of course, and vice versa.

Some people have mentioned the banjo(presumably they mean a tenor) but I’m not sure why you would appreciate this instrument any more so than the mandolin. The same would go for octave mandolin, mandola, or bouzouki type instruments. They are all quite similar although there are obviously a few subtle differences in terms of technique, possible ornamentation etc.
However, I’d suggest that the best stepping stone from fiddle to these other instruments is actually via the mandolin.

Then, of course, you might wish to try something completely different although it generally makes more sense to try another instrument which is related in some way to what you already play. Still, you say that you are a quick learner.

The suggestion try a whistle is good and it can lead on to the flute, pipes etc. What’s more, it’s cheap and if you don’t like it, there’s nothing lost.

There’s always the bodhran, of course, which always goes down very well in Tuesday night sessions in Edinburgh’s Forrest Road along with a couple of twenty verse ballads. The human voice is an instrument too. :-)

Re: Would like input on learning a second instrument.

There are three things that I’d consider before learning a new instrument:

1 Price - A tin whistle is the cheapest instrument you’ll come across. Make sure to get one in D.

2 Learning curve - Mandolin/tenor banjo would be easiest for you since the fingering is the same. Learning to play with pick instead of a bow will be the major hurdle as well as the obvious gain (if you’d like to try out guitar or bouzouki). Any other instrument would demand more of you as a learner.

3 Usefulness - Maybe the session lacks a certain sound, maybe you’ll gain something from learning a certain instrument, maybe you can use it other contexts. As Piece says, a backing instrument can be a good choice.

In any case, it’s good to know the basics of another instrument in order to understand what can and can’t be done, how those musicians think etc.

Re: Would like input on learning a second instrument.

One of the biggest factors in choosing a new instrument is what you want out of it. You may want something with a slightly different sound but similar playing, or you may want something very different. I personally like a lot of variety, that’s why I picked up whistle/flute. They play differently than anything else I had studied at that point, and they have a very different sound, which I really liked.

The biggest advantage that I’ve found to playing both fiddle and whistle/flute, is that if a tune doesn’t fit well on one, it almost indefinitely will fit well on the other, even if it takes a bit of getting used to. For instance, the tune The Moving Cloud is terrible on fiddle, but a breeze on flute, Hand Me Down The Tackle is great on the fiddle but annoying on the flute. Then there’s tunes like the Silver Spear etc. that fit well on both. :)

Re: Would like input on learning a second instrument.

You may want to see what’s available locally for starters. Inexpensive D or C whistles are typically under $15-$20. Some music stores will carry Generations, Feadógs, Clarkes (conical shape, with a wooden block), or Oaks .. See if any of your friends has a whistle to loan you. It’d be good to play a bit on a whistle before buying. Some stores let you play their instruments, but ask first.

Certainly you want a good instrument. The only problem is unless you can play, or at least hear, different whistles in person you won’t know what you’re getting until it arrives. All I’m saying is don’t rush immediately into buying what seems to be the best whistle ; unless you’re lucky enough something pops up that you don’t want to pass up.

Check out the Irish Flute Store < http://shop.irishflutestore.com/Irish-Whistles_c4.htm >
Chiff & Fipple’s Used Instrument Exchange < http://forums.chiffandfipple.com/viewforum.php?f=35&sid=828c83f2a3ec95b2efb67cc80e0b59fb >
Jerry Freeman < http://myworld.ebay.com/freemanwhistles/ >
These are tweaked whistles. The advantage is you don’t have to play every low priced whistle in the lot to find the right one & you only pay a little more.
Michael Burke < http://www.burkewhistles.com/home.php?cat=265 > Good design for playing in tune through low & high range.
Misha Somerville < http://mkwhistles.com/mkshop/shop-online > Low whistles, but probably the best & relatively expensive.
Colin Goldie < http://www.colingoldie.de/whistles.html > My favourite! ;)
Paul Busman < http://www.busmanwhistles.com/busman_price.htm > He learned the trade from Glenn Schultz.
& many others which may be a little more difficult to find .. John Sindt, Glenn Schultz (Thin Weasel), Pat O’Riordan, Mitch Smith (Oz whistle) ..

Re: Would like input on learning a second instrument.

As I have said many times in other discussions, you don’t need to go overboard with your first whistle. While some of the cheapest whistles do suffer from quality control problems, the player is far more important than the instrument. I myself have been perfectly happy for years with plain, unmodified Clarke whistles. My last one held up for over a decade, pretty good for something that cost less than $10. If you want a whistle that stands out from the crowd, a Susato is a good, inexpensive choice. But I don’t recommend playing that from the start. When you are starting out, standing out from the crowd is not always a good thing!
Whistle is a fun instrument, so jump in and enjoy yourself!

In my opinion, if you can already play an instrument fairly well you can pick up a wide variety of instruments and be able to play a basic tune on it. It’s getting the speed and ornamentation down that takes time - regardless of what you pick up. I’d say the concertina is probably the easiest to pick up. Pull or squeeze, press a button and there you go. I’ve seen lots of fiddlers switch to the mando or tenor banjo because, as mentioned, it’s the same tuning.

You can pick up a decent banjo or mando cheaper than you can pick up a decent concertina so if you have a budget that may be a factor. If you wanna try a whistle to see if you like it and then proceed to get something a bit fancier try Oak whistles. $11 and they’re great. I use mine in sessions, gigs, camping, etc.
