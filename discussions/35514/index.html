It’s music for dancing to… So they say!

In the aim of fostering an understanding:

I’ve never understood the rationale behind saying that jigs and reels should be played “as fast as it is possible to dance to them.”

There are two reasons this popular comment confuses me:

1. One can dance to any speed of music
2. If the suggestion is that: ‘we must always play the music for that specific context of formal dancing, even if the context is not there’ - isn’t that just silly? (Ie rules for rules sake)

I may just be quite laid back about the music but it really does baffle me when people say the music must not be faster than dancing speed… Please help me understand what exactly people mean by that…

Cheers!

Re: It’s music for dancing to… So they say!

I’ve never heard anyone say anything about playing dance tunes “as fast as it is possible to dance to them.” I have heard (and expressed) the opinion that they should not be played so fast that the tune is obliterated in a torrent of notes, or at a speed that is beyond the capabilities of the musicians. A good rule of thumb is to play dance tunes at speeds appropriate for the dances, until one has the technique, understanding of the music, and musical judgement to play the tunes at another, not necessarily danceable tempo. Or not. (That’s the understanding and judgement part.) A familiarity with the dances will improve understanding of dance tunes.

Have I ever played dance tunes too fast or too slow to dance to? Sure, for purely instrumental purposes. How do I know that the speed is too fast or too slow to dance to? I learned the dances, and watched dancers much better than I was. If one is dancing randomly, without using specific steps, jumps, postured, patterns, and choreography, one may certainly dance to any music at any speed. Jigs, reels, and other Irish dances are anything but random. Also, when dancing a dance that uses specific steps, jumps, postures, patterns, and choreography, speed will be limited, both slow and fast, by gravity.

Also, I hate to break it to anyone, but sometimes saying “you’re playing too fast to dance to” is a tactful way of saying, " you don’t have the chops to play that fast, you don’t have a clue about
Irish traditional music, and it’s a complete train wreck." Don’t be that person.

Re: It’s music for dancing to… So they say!

“You can dance to any sped of music” might hold true if you are just bopping about at a disco, but not if you are performing fixed steps to traditional dances. The rhythm of the music has to fit the rhythm of your feet.

Even if you don’t dance yourself try this: skip round the room, then run round the room, then jog on the spot. In each case listen to the rhythm of your feet, you’ll find that skipping is fairly slow, running is faster and jogging on the spot is fastest. Those equate to the way people move when they are dancing - country dancers skip, ceilidh dancers run, and step dancers jog on the spot. So if you play the same reel for all three you will need to play it at three different tempos otherwise they can’t dance to it - if it is too slow they can’t stay in the air long enough on each step, it it is too fast they have to shorten their steps and don’t get to their required destination by the end of the phrase.

If no one is dancing you have more latitude, but you still need to stay within that range of tempos - that is how the tunes were written, and where they feel comfortable. Your audience will be able to comfortably tap their foot to the beat. If you speed it up too much the foot tapping turns into headbanging, and everyone except the teenagers goes home.

Re: It’s music for dancing to… So they say!

I am a dancer and there’s a problem if the tune is too fast - one can’t complete the figures in time, and the melody sometimes gets blurred which makes phrasing one’s steps harder - or if it is too slow, when you’re liable to wobble or fall over while setting.

Yes.

A tune is simply a gathering of notes in a specific sequence, and when
put on paper (dots), there may be some mention of a recommended tempo.
The individual musician can, naturally, ignore all of that completely and do
what they bloody well please. But just do not call it that which it no longer is.
You are either trying to play traditional music in a traditional manner, or you
are doing something else. And neither one is wrong nor right - just very different.

I agree with the comment that many sessions are just about impressing with high speed, faultlessly confident, playing. The subtlety and character of a tune may get lost in the onward rush. I think that there is often a best pace for many tunes to showcase them. The disparaging ‘Diddly-dee’ reference really comes from not ITM-peoples perception of the music being an unintelligible continuum.

I would draw a parallel with birdsong. As all the birds are familiar with what they are singing, they can hear all the notes even though they are being whistled at high speed. To us human bystanders it can sound more like noise, even if pleasant noise. Us ‘session birds’ hear things differently to casual bystanders. So I guess that is why a session primarily for the performers should sound different to one for the ‘general public’, particularly if they wish to dance to it.

Re: It’s music for dancing to… So they say!

>… The music far pre-dates that of the formalised dances we see today …

I’m not sure how ancient you think “the music” is, but most Irish traditional music isn’t all that old. Mostly 19th-20th century, with a bit of 18th century here and there. There is some music collected in the late 18th and early 19th century that could be ~100 years older. Or not.

The earliest formalized European (not Irish) dances we have instructions for are 15th century. Formalized dance existed for centuries before then, but we don’t have instructions, just descriptions and artwork. Formalized Itish dance also predates the 15th century, but again we don’t have instructions or even good descriptions. Or documentable music. The formalized Irish dances we see today solidified in the 19th century, with antecedents in the 17th and 18th. They have continued to develop, of course. For any of these dances, and therefor the dance tunes, there is a range of danceable speeds. For example, contemporary championship-level step dancing choreography is sometimes so complex that the tune must be played at a funereal tempo, while the same tune played for energetic young ceili dancers would be considerably peppier.

While there was surely a time when ancient people in Ireland danced non-formalized dances to equally ancient music, it was so many centuries ago that it does not apply to Irish traditional music or dance. People have never stopped dancing in a non-formalized way, and it is still possible to see them do so at concerts and sessions. (Just watch the drunks and one day a year Oirish this St. Patrick’s a Day.)
