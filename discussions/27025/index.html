Adjusting to new fiddle strings

I’ve read through several past posts around strings. What types of strings, how often you should change strings, etc. But not sure I found anything specifically on adjusting to new strings.

I just put new strings on my fiddle about 2 weeks ago.

Have had the instrument for about 3 1/2 years, but only really been playing it (what I would consider seriously playing) since this past December. And had never even thought to replace strings and would not have done it now if my teacher hadn’t suggested it. (you may laugh at that… but I thought, if they aren’t broke why replace… obviously now I do hear a difference)

Thing is, with these new strings I feel like I am learning all over again. Bow slides more, I squeak often going from the A to E string which I didn’t do before and in general I find playing more difficult. I have also noticed a difference visually in the behavior of the new strings. They vibrate a lot more than the previous strings ever did.

So is the new difficulty playing a mental thing? Or have others had a similar experience after new strings?

Any tips or thoughts?

Re: Adjusting to new fiddle strings

New strings always take a while to settle in - a few days perhaps, or even more, depending how much and _how_ they’re played*. The tone and their behavior changes over that period, partly because you, the player, are getting used to them. Dominants (and some others) are known to have a metallic sound when new, but after a few days playing that goes away and they sound great.

* A tip I’ve found that helps is to play new strings quite strongly for a couple of hours each day. It helps the settling-in process - and is good for the player, anyway.

Re: Adjusting to new fiddle strings

The good news is that you noticed the difference! If you hadn’t, then I’d be worried. :-)

If you would, please tell us what strings you bought and installed. To help inform our responses.

Steel core strings tend to settle in faster than synthetic cores. And if your old strings were steel and now you’ve gone to synth, the new strings will likely feel a touch mushy. You may be overplaying them (leaning in too hard with the bow).

Also, even among the high-end expensive strings, the Es tend to be problematic. Lots of players don’t buy a complete set, preferring a different E. Myself included. I play on either Thomastik Vision Solos or Larsen Tzigane’s for the G, D, and A strings, and then always use a Thomastik Superflexible No 8 for the E string. It makes for a huge improvement in tone, responsiveness, and playability.

So let us know what you’ve strung up your fiddle with.

Re: Adjusting to new fiddle strings

My take on rosin is two-fold: most violinists tend to use too much of the stuff. You only need to apply a little once in a while. For me that is once a week, and I do a lot of playing. If I can see rosin appear on clean strings when I start playing then I know I don’t need to put any more on the bow just yet. Too much rosin will in fact kill some of the tone and adversely affect your bowing.

Secondly, in my view there is rather too much snake-oil in the violin rosin market - I won’t be more specific, but just look around at the prices, the additives, and the wonderful promises in the adverts, and draw your own conclusions.

I have two cakes of fairly basic rosin that are several years old. One is light-colored for warm weather, and the other is dark, for the cold weather (but I’m still not convinced there’s a significant difference between the two). I also play the cello, and use the violin rosin for both instruments.

↳ Posted by Moondyne Fiddler on March 13th, 2011.

Re: Adjusting to new fiddle strings

I use Eudoxas on my orchestral violin, with a Hill E, and I must say they stay in tune remarkably well. In a 2-3 hour rehearsal they need less tweaking than almost all other strings I’ve used, with the exception of Obligatos (synthetic). If I had problems with them I wouldn’t use them for orchestral playing. Many orchestral professionals use them because of their tone and dynamic range.

Don’t forget that gut strings are already stretched during the manufacturing process so they don’t stretch all that much more from new. What can happen, and this goes for other strings, is they can slip and settle on the pegs if care isn’t taken in the initial winding on of the strings. It looks and feels like stretching but it mostly isn’t.

Because gut strings are slightly fatter than many other strings some attention should be made to the grooves in the nut and the bridge to make sure a string doesn’t stick in its groove (another principal cause of going out of tune unexpectedly). Liberal application of 4B pencil lead to the grooves is usually all that is required, but if you have a history of strings fraying or breaking in the region of the grooves then a visit to the luthier is indicated. It’s a simple enough problem for him to correct but he knows how to do it and has the right tools.

Re: Adjusting to new fiddle strings

“Don’t even consider using a set of Eudoxas unless you are experienced.” - Interesting comment by Moondyne.

What I think is meant by “experienced” in that comment is not years of playing and knowing loads of tunes. It is knowing how to get the tone and dynamics out of gut strings, a slightly different bowing technique, which isn’t difficult to learn. Basically, you use a lighter pressure and more bow speed, and play closer to the bridge. If you “dig in” with gut strings in order to play louder you’ll kill the tone and in fact produce less volume.

Years ago, before the coming of synthetic core strings (I think Dominants were the first in the ’60s), everyone played on gut-core or plain gut strings and was taught the bowing technique that goes with it. As a young cellist I played on gut strings for the first 6 or 7 years, so using gut violin strings for the first time only a very few years ago was no problem.

Re: Adjusting to new fiddle strings

Huh. Michael, I have to disagree with you here. Dominant strings don’t at all have a wide dynamic range and aren’t particularly responsive--at least not when compared to better strings. Seriously, if you like Dominants, give the Visions a try. They’re similar in feel and in how they require that subtlety with the bow, but are much more responsive, more interesting tone, and a far wider dynamic range. Plus they last a lot longer, even the A strings (it’s *not* just the repertoire--Dominant As come unwrapped quicker than any other string I’ve played and nearly every decent fiddler I’ve talked to says the same).

And the Larsen Tzigane’s are even better, in all respects.

If you move from steel-core to synth strings, it will take some time to adjust to the softer feel. They *do* want a more subtle, sensitive bow hand. But a good synth string can be pushed and driven, with just as much volume and grit as any steel string if the player knows what they’re doing.

I don’t do “Frankenstein” sets of strings, but I have yet to find an E string as consistently great as the Thomastik Superflexible No. 8. I like them and my fiddle likes them. Some phenomenal fiddlers agree (e.g., James Kelly, Calvin Vollrath). If you read the feedback on strings from top-notch players, it’s not at all unusual for the Es standard to a set to be panned in favor of a Hill, Goldbrokat, or Superflexible E. I don’t worry much about what the manufacturer thinks--it’s the *playing* that matters.

Re: Adjusting to new fiddle strings

@Wendy - I think your Dominants will have well settled in by next Saturday.

Dominants are the ‘industry standard reference’ strings, and many makers and sellers will fit their violins with these as standard practice.

Regarding rosin, I don’t really believe the brand makes much of a difference. Some are what you might call ‘grippier’ or ‘stickier’, but if the bow hair is in good condition, that is by far the most important thing.

I have tried loads of brands of rosin over the last 40 years, and I can’t honestly say that one is better than another. That aspect is much over-rated, and rosin is generally over-used, evidenced by the thousands of fiddles suffering from frostbite, and bows which are so caked with it that you can almost see the crystallization.

I’m using Jade Opera at the moment. It happens to be green in colour (big deal…). If you want a rosin that’s easy to apply, try d‘Addario Natural Rosin. It comes in a ’matchbox’ type case, with a little holster, so you don’t get your fingers all sticky :)
