↳ Posted by Kenny on August 31st, 2023.

Re: Is there any session etiquette concerning drones?

Droning and chording are two different ways of accompanying melody and they don’t always fit together. If you have a chordal accompanist who is experienced enough to choose the right chords to fit with the drone all is well and good. But in a typical session where the guitars and bouzoukis are just banging out I IV V behind everything there are inevitably going to be clashes. The same thing happens with fiddle tunes that use ringing strings and double stops - in sessions you usually find you need to just play the bare melody.

Interesting gulf between “what is” and “what should be”

I think that Will is correct in saying that the etiquette is technically “anything goes” - but of course any number of variations on that are possible based on regional preferences, how vocal the host(s) may be, etc.

And in many other open social music settings (particularly jazz and blues), there is DEFINITELY an unwritten rule that if you’re playing so loud you can’t hear the person next to you, you’re playing too loud. Same with picking appropriate volume for the room, although that is very much a problem for those playing with amplifiers.

As a piano box player, I’ve definitely gotten some glares for daring to use the left hand. Sometimes they were earned, sometimes not. As uncomfortable as it was at the time, I appreciated that bit of directness.

So, @Boyen, I think that you are probably correct that there are some contexts where playing without sensitivity to one’s own volume would be worth a mention. I’m not sure Irish sessions are that context - and so maybe there is nothing in the overall umbrella of “session etiquette” that you could appeal to in order to justify asking the piper at your sessions to not use drones.

Doesn’t mean that you’re wrong for feeling annoyed about it, but maybe then it’s worth trying to problemsolve, with that musician or with the hosts, as to a way that you would feel less annoyed about it. Your personal sense of etiquette may see the piper’s choices as unforgivable here, but it’s possible it never crossed their mind that it was a problem, and maybe they (like me) have some trouble picking up subtle social cues.

Unfortunately I found this discussion a bit late. But since I have a sneaking suspicions I’m actually the piper in question let me add some thoughts:

First we need to clear some things up: I mostly play Scottish small pipes at sessions. As regards to their loudness: Even tough I play quite a loud set for Small pipes standards (Ian Kinnear) they are a lot quieter then most uilleann pipes you would ever come across. That also counts for the drones. I put an effort in making sure I have a nice balance in drone and chanter sound.

Also I definitely put in an effort to tune my drones at sessions. Admitted this can be hard since our pub can be super noisy but I don’t like the sound of my instrument when the drones are way out of tune either. Some patience for pipers trying to tune their drones for the benefit of everybody is always appreciated. Drones go out of tune again (especially when we just start playing) so after a bit we really do have to retune them. Short off topic note: Please don’t “Give pipers an A” Im trying to tune my drones to the A on my chanter and am listening to lock them in. An A from a different instrument that is likely slightly different makes that infinitely more difficult. I appreciate people are trying to help but it makes my life so much harder.

Second: I think Uilleann pipes and Scottish pipes are a bit different. In a way the drone sound is a bit more fundamental to the sound of the Scottish pipes (Weather they are small pipes border pipes Highland pipes or whatever). To clarify: I also play a half set of Donnacha Dwyer’s but I don’t consider myself an expert uilleann piper. To illustrate my point: I think almost all sets of uilleann pipes would come with a drone switch. Indicating that the drones are seen as more optional. I think only about 30% of small pipes and border pipes would come with a drone switch. (Highland pipes never do). This demonstrates that with these pipes drones are seen more as a fundamental part of the sound of the instrument. I definitely prefer playing with my drones on. I feel the sounds of the pipes is “naked” or “incomplete” without them. In my opinion they are a fundamental part of what makes the pipes what they are. Pipes without drones are just an overcomplicated limited range oboe. :P

Now: That does not mean I think pipers should always have their drones going no matter what. Most small pipes are in the key of A. That means that most sets have a drone setup with a tenor and a bass drone in A and a Baritone E. For playing in they key of A that’s beautiful. For the key of D not so much. So I choose to mostly have my baritone drone off at sessions. (Yes Boyen they could still be louder :P). This way the drones are both in A so they should not cause to much friction with tunes in A or D. Of course there is the odd tune in a different key. Sometimes I get a tune in Bmin for example. (Although not super common since the limited A mix range of the small pipes does not help trying to play in other keys). In that case I would make the decision to turn the drones off since they would clash to much with both the tune and any cords someone is trying to play.

With all this in mind I think there are a couple of thing that could cause somones “annoyance”. First of all: The drones are in the key if A. That makes them a bit different in an Irish session context. It might mean they don’t always harmonize as easy with the tune you are playing. You might play some left hand stuff that resolves and harmonize nicely with the melody of the tune but clashes a bit with the drones. Usually this can be accounted for in different accompaniment but I also understand that in a session context this is maybe asking a bit much.

Also: The drones of the small pipes stick up in the air next to my ears. (And often the ears of my right side neighbor). I do sympathize with my neighbor’s a little bit. But like a said I think the drones are a key part of the sound of the instrument. So if its really that annoying don’t sit directly to my right side and it should be alright. Like I said the drones are really not that loud. Uilleann pipes drones are lower to the ground so less “in your face” in that sense.

As a last thought: I think drone sound might be a bit like coffee or beer in the sense that some people might need to learn to appreciate it and some people might just never develop a taste for it. That of course is totally fine.

Tuning pipes is a whole topic on itself. But for people who are not too familiar with it: Tuning pipes is one of those “more about the journey not the destinations things”. Unfortunately its not as easy as sticking on a tuner and turning some pegs.

Basically I make sure my chanter is in tune when I leave the house. I usually try to account for the fact that the pub is often hot. So I tend to be a bit flat at home so it will come up at the session. Then at the sessions the chanter is usually pretty good. Trying to tune a chanter at a session (Apart from maybe a bit of fine tuning) is hard to do. So I make sure I come prepared and decently tuned.

That can mean the chanter is maybe a tiny bit off. But never in a way that would detract from the music. For drones to sound nice on a set of pipes they do need to be VERY in tune with the chanter tough. So its more important to tune the drones to the chanter. Then they are going to be alright with the rest of the musicians because I made sure the chanter is tuned before going to the session. If other musicians give me an A that is slightly of it is basically impossible to hear when my pipes “lock in” and the drones will sound crap with the pipes.

Pipes are usually just intonation.
