Sean nós and airs

This morning I was walking to my car and listening to Denis Murphy. “Caoine Uí Dhonaill” off of The Star Above The Garter came up and I was, as usual, transfixed by it. When I got home I played, without much thought to it, some Joe Heaney-- “Barbary Ellen” to be specific.
That got me thinking (again) about the relationship of singing and isntrumental music, specifically sean nós and airs. I know that sessions are oriented towards faster music (I’ve actually never heard an air played at a session! Though I have heard a few songs.) Do any air players here listen to much sean nós or take many cues from it?

Re: Sean nós and airs

If you have a taste for them, airs can absolutely move you in a way that the dance music just can’t. I LOVE my airs and will drop the odd one in at a session - if the session is right for it and then usually just the one.

They tend to be “solo” spots so its handy if the session seems to be flagging a bit, It lets the others have a bit of a break. I find that they always go down well with other musicians and punters alike. A change is as good as a rest afterall.

The airs are an art form in their own right and in many ways are much harder to play than the dance music where there is little room to express sensetivity.

I’ve always said that you play airs with you guts and ears and let your fingers follow where they taje you.

Re: Sean nós and airs

Here is a good example, I think, of the importance of phrasing in sean nos, coming from the Irish language. A lot of what the song is about derives from the ‘sound of the structure’ of the lyric, not just the actually meaning, I think.

The OP, I think, asked whether people learn English or Irish versions of sean nos songs. I would think it very much depends on the song, especially how old it might be.

Taimse im’chodladh is quite an ancient tune. You can hear the phrasing of the Irish lyric as sung by Iarla O’Lionaird in the link below. Imagine trying to sing the English translation in that sort of timing…it just wouldn’t fit, it wouldn’t rhyme in the English literary fashion. The rhythm, structure, phrasing and everything else about this sean nos song (and many others) comes from the Irish language. I do not think this song has ever been sung (or at least recorded, but I suspect even sung) in English. It just doesn’t go and loses just about everything if that is done to it.

If you like Irish music, and this song doesn’t want to make you really listen and even learn some of the language, even for the rhythmic understanding, to benefit your playing, it makes for a review, I think, of what one is looking for or trying to do in the music.

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=leG3XiZmuXc

I’m not sure that the lyric that O’Lionaird is singing is the exact translation of the English version below. It isn’t the point anyway. Phrasing and timing is, and what we can learn from that for playing the music.

(“Táimse im’chodladh” is an allegorical lyric, with a beautiful lady personifying Ireland appearing to the writer in a vision, beseeching him to arise and take arms against the invader. It thus falls in the Asiling, or ‘vision’, tradition of Irish poetry and song. It was composed in the 18th century in Munster. A translation by Dr. Eoin O’Neill of the Irish lyric, goes:
***
As I was abroad late one evening
-I am asleep and don’t waken me-
It happened that I noticed by my side a beautiful apparition
-I am asleep and don’t waken me-
Her curly, ringleted, cascading surplus of tresses fell over her trembling limbs,
As she launched the arrows that pierced me in the side.
-I am sleep and don’t waken me-
***
Arise my loyal family and take up your weapons
-I am asleep and don’t waken me-
And level to the ground every English clown.
-I am asleep and don’t waken me-
If only three survive, let there be shouts of triumph in all your towns;
From Carrick-on-Suir west to the banks of Dingle
Raise your blades and give the English their own treachery;
-I am asleep and don’t waken me-
***
The melody was a part of the old Irish harp tradition, and was also absorbed into piping tradition. Brian McNamara (2004) calls it one “of the great airs associated with the Uilleann pipes.” Source for notated version: Bunting noted the melody from Hempson the harper at Magilligan in 1792.)"

(excerpt from: http://www.ibiblio.org/fiddlers/I.htm)
