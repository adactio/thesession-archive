“A Proper Charlie” or what?

Our future King(though we might still miss a generation) has hit the headlines today over his “dated” views on education.

He says
"What is wrong with everyone nowadays?

"Why do they all seem to think they are qualified to do things far beyond their technical capabilities?

"This is to do with the learning culture in schools as a consequence of a child-centred system which admits no failure.

"People think they can all be pop stars, high court judges, brilliant TV personalities or infinitely more competent heads of state without ever putting in the necessary work or having natural ability.

“This is the result of social utopianism which believes humanity can be genetically and socially engineered to contradict the lessons of history.”

How do these views relate to learning and participating in traditional music? Personally, I believe that it(trad music) should be for all or, at least, the opportunity to learn and play(in appropriate circumstances) it. However, we probably *do* have many people on the scene with ideas “above their station” who will never become good musicians. That shouldn’t matter as long as they realise their place and not disrupt things for other people, eg assuming they have the right to play or perform in every situation, session, and venue etc. Is this part of the reason why we have so many strummers, goatwhackers, egg shakers etc because they wish to the part of the scene and “recognised” as being a traditional musician without having the talent or putting in the work?

Sorry, if you think I’m a bit harsh. I certainly don’t want to be seen to be agreeing with The Prince of Wales and, as far as my own musical abilities are concerned, I certainly know my place and limitations. Also, I’d argue that everybody with a genuine interest should be encouraged to learn and participate in music just as long as they do not have unrealistic or unreasonable expectations.

Re: “A Proper Charlie” or what?

While clearly running the risk of being flamed off this discussion group, I feel duty-bound to state that, within limits, I actually agree with ol’ jug ears and have always suspected that he’s not as daft as he looks . . .
Irrespective of whether or not Charlie’s observation can be applied to ITM, I do agree with the general thrust of his point. My wife is a teacher here in the UK, and daily she comes home disgusted with the state of the nation’s education system; a system which has become so dumbed-down that children literally cannot fail their GCSE exams - every grade, no matter how poor (and we’re talking as poor as 10%), is considered a pass. In fact, teachers are actively discouraged from telling a child that he/she is wrong, even when the answer given is patently incorrect. Consequently, even the least gifted emerge from the system with a full complement of qualifications, therefore expecting to be welcomed into third-level education. And in order to accommodate the larger numbers of prospective university students, we now have countless new ‘universities’ offering degrees in subjects that amount to little more than watching TV or reading the tabloids . . . How then are employers to make the distinction between truly gifted graduates and those who, in less dumbed-down times, would never even have been offered a university place, let alone given a degree?
In another arena of endeavour, we now have a plethora of pop stars who have come up via the Pop Idol/Fame Academy route. Consequently, there’s a horde of Music Academies/Brit Schools that have sprung up to take the influx of children who have seen these wannabes transformed overnight into celebrities and who therefore have come to believe that pop success can be achieved simply by doing a short course. As a session bass player, I’ve had the dubious pleasure of working with a number of such ‘artistes’ and am often (with a few notable exceptions) appalled by how little they seem to know about the work that goes into a live show, by how rudimentary their skills on their chosen instrument often are, and by their attitude, which seems to run, ‘I’ve done the course, I’ve got the certificate, now give me the career - I deserve it’.
Of course, it’s not in the interests of the Fame Academies/Brit Schools/new universities etc to tell any of their students that they don’t have the talent to make it - the talentless are paying for the privelege of being there, and it’s money that keeps these places going, after all. So it just goes on, and we’re now at the point where a talentless child can apparently go through the school/higher education system without ever being told that he/she doesn’t have the talent to make it in their chosen profession.
With my English reserve, of course, I wouldn’t dream of saying anything, but sometimes I find myself thinking, ‘Somebody really needs to tell this individual that he can’t sing/play/speak French/spell/do long division/etc’. Then perhaps the individual in question might go away and spend a couple more years practising/studying or might realise that they don’t actually have a natural gift for the particular field of endeavour they’re flogging away at . . .
Thankfully, we haven’t yet seen an ‘ITM Idol’ and the subsequent appearance of ITM Academies up and down the country, but I’d be prepared to bet that you’ll look back on Charlie’s observation with a rather more sympathetic eye if and when that day ever arrives . . .

K.
