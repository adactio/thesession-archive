Is the term “Celtic music” inappropriate?

The term “Celtic music” has been widely used worldwide as a collective term for traditional music in Celtic-speaking regions, including Ireland, Scotland, Wales, and Brittany and so on, with festivals like the Inter Celtic Music Festival in Lorient, France, having a history of 50 years.

While the historical connection between ancient Celts and modern Celtic music remains unclear, the linguistic relationship of Celtic languages in these regions has never been denied. Therefore, I believe that the term “Celtic music” is appropriate for describing the contemporary traditional music played in these Celtic language regions.

However, there are arguments in Japan claiming that the term “Celtic music” is inappropriate for the following reasons:

1.It evokes a connection between Celtic music and ancient Celtic people.
2.It may lead to racism and extreme right-wing exclusionism.
3.It is seen as a commercial term.

I personally believe that Celtic music is a concept nurtured within the revival movements of oppressed ethnic cultures and endangered languages, such as those suppressed by imperial powers like Britain, France, and Spain. Denying this concept raises ethical concerns about denying ethnic identities.

For example, just as no one considers Bossa Nova, a type of Latin music, to be the music of ancient Latin people, the validity of the term “Celtic music” remains unchanged even if modern Irish music and ancient Celtic music were unrelated.

I would like to hear your opinions on the use of the term “Celtic music.”

It’s a useful term for semi-related types of music, mostly from Scotland, Wales, and Ireland, but also Galicia and Brittany, and places like Cape Breton and Newfoundland, with music heavily influenced by Scottish immigrants.

It’s also useless because it implies ‘woo,’ like Enya and other New Age waffle, which has very little to do with jigs, reels, and hornpipes. I have not personally come across the white supremacist co-opting of the word ‘Celtic,’ but I read about it somewhere, and I can totally believe it.

It’s very slippery, changing meaning depending on context. Don’t think you can say that it’s problematic, nor can you say that it’s unproblematic.

Thanks for all the comments. I have read all of them. You all have helped me to deepen my thinking and I enjoyed some humorous comments :)

I believe that the term “Celtic music” should be judged in context as to its correctness.
However, I have doubts about the conclusion that it should be abolished because it has no historical or genetic legitimacy, or because simply it is commercial.
This is not coming from my own position, as Tamiko points out, that I own a music store named “Celtic Flute”, but because of the established aspect of Celtic music as a cultural activity of a rare language and marginalized region, and to deny this fact would be a denial of cultural identity, which leads to be an ethical issue.
I personally use “Celtic music” for referring to the music of Celtic regions generally but prefer to use more specifically “Irish traditional music” or “Breton dance tune”.

In addition, I believe that there are some problems with the unreflective abuse of “Celtic music”.

1.Cultural generalization: the fact that what Celtic music refers to is too broad. Celtic music is not limited to Ireland, but can include a wide range of musical genres, including popular and healing music as well as traditional music from Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, and Brittany. Because different musical styles and traditions exist in each of these regions, using a single term to describe these various types of music can be a generalization that ignores cultural diversity. For accuracy when identifying musical genres, more specific terms such as “Irish traditional music” or “Irish folk rock” should be used.

2.Lack of historical accuracy: The term “Celtic music” is relatively new and has been popularized to refer to Irish music. However, Irish music does not derive solely from Celtic culture. Irish music also includes other cultures and influences, such as Norman, Viking, and English. Therefore, simply labeling Irish music as “Celtic music” is not historically accurate.

3.Cultural Attribution and Ownership Issues: The term “Celtic music” is used not only in Ireland but also elsewhere. However, if we are dealing with Irish music, it is important to respect the culture and its origins and traditions. To collectively represent Irish music as if other regions or cultures have a proprietary interest in it may diminish the uniqueness of Irish culture.

4.The problem from the perspective of cultural consumption: since Celtic music is a designation of peripheral ethnic music from the perspective of mainstream Western music, it is prone to distortion and overproduction by third parties, and there is a danger of leading people to believe that the essence of the creation is far removed from the reality of traditional music. Therefore, students of traditional music are likely to misunderstand the essence of the subject if they see it vaguely as “Celtic music,” and need to study more regional, more traditional styles and in more depth.

Re: Is the term “Celtic music” inappropriate?

I think what you have completely missed is the link with language. Celtic music is music from areas which do (or did until recent times) speak Celtic languages. Any discussion of who the Celts were and where they came from way back in the bronze age is completely irrelevant because the music simply isn’t that old, almost everything we play was written within the last 500 years. What counts, and what sets Celtic music apart from other forms, is the language spoken by the composer and those around him. There is an inextricable link between language and music, not only throught the obvious crossover in song, but also because the brain gets attuned to the speech you are constantly hearing around you: the rhythm, lilt, patterns and cadences of the language inform your composition. Even where a language has died out and been superceded, the people of the area still speak the new language in a dialect that embraces the speech patterns of the old, so it still informs their composition.

The Celtic languages of Ireland, Scotland, Cornwall, Brittany etc. are different, but they have some common characteristics that distinguish them from Germanic and other languages. Those common characteristics can be heard in both the language and the music, and they are sufficiently strong for it to make sence to group them together as ‘Celtic music’.

The problem we have today, which has devalued the term ‘Celtic’, is that we have another breed of ‘Celtic’ music - stuff that is written by people who haven’t been raised in the Celtic areas and don’t have the innate ‘feel’ for the language and music. They ‘borrow’ some of the characteristic traits to produce something that sounds vaguely ‘Celtic’, but couldn’t be identified as coming from any one specific tradition. It’s like speaking some nonsense words in a way that sounds Gaelic, but no Gaelic speaker would be able to understand what you are saying.

Borderer states - “As several people have pointed out, the earliest evidence for the traditional music we play comes roughly from the period 1650-1800”

Which music exactly are these several people referring to? 1650 is an arbitrary starting point that has no basis in fact.

The music of the Irish harpers dates many centuries before this. During the Tudor conquest of the 12th Century, the British sent a man called Gerald of Wales to survey Ireland and the only good thing he had to say about us in his Topographia Hibernica referenced our harp music. It is well known that Irish harpers and Irish monks spread musical knowledge throughout Europe long before 1650.

The earliest notated Irish music comes from the late 1500’s, so, given the prevalence of aural traditions before this, we can assume that a native Irish music related to our common airs existed in non-notated form long before the 1500’s.

Irish music is really ancient and the characteristics it developed many centuries ago undoubtedly influenced the 1650-1800 period being referred to. Irish melodic styles may have morphed into jigs, reels, hornpipes and other such tunes in that era, but to suggest the Irish music before 1650 bears no relation to that which came afterwards discounts a large amount of historical evidence.

Going back to the notion of ‘Celtic’ music, the ancient ‘Celts’ of Ireland had numerous musical instruments which have been discovered and dated to thousands of years ago. Would anyone be brave enough to dismiss the possibility that the music they played bears no relation to the traditional Irish music we play today? There may not be notated evidence of it, but the evidence is all there within written descriptions of Irish music through the ages, for example this from the medieval book of Irish laws, Crith Gablach

"The various officials who surrounded the Ard Rig Erind Conaire Mor… there were three royal druids and jugglers, three poets, three judges, nine harpers, nine pipe players”

Nine harpers and nine pipe players in a medieval Irish royal court would certainly suggest an ancient Celtic music that might have some relation to the music we play today!

Traditional Irish music didn’t just suddenly appear out of nowhere in 1650, it developed through a constant evolution over thousands of years that continues to this day. The Celts obviously had a huge influence on this, as indeed did many other peoples who settled in Ireland, or indeed people who just passed through, sharing their music with us.

There are many false narratives dating back centuries which dismiss the sophistication of native Irish people. These have roots in the numerous attempts to conquer and repress our people. The idea that our native dance music only took shape between 1650-1800 is but one of these tropes that unfortunately many people have fallen into the trap of believing! We have a wonderfully deep history of music dating back millennia, all this music is connected and the ancient nature of it should be celebrated, regardless of whether it is labelled ‘Celtic’, ‘Irish’, ‘folk’, ‘traditional’ or ‘flying spaghetti monster’ music……

I think music/notation should be differentiated from sound/style, with word description coming even later to those. Trying to classify something as alive as music is quite dismal, but I think nescessary not only for clear communication but to counter false perceptions that are purposefully created sometimes.

An interesting example of those differences that I was looking at recently was attempts at reconstructing medieval music from the dots, this by very studied enthusiasts. In short, it could not be said how a medieval score was played with certainty, and so various versions were offered. Each sounded quite different, and no-one could say for sure how it really sounded in the day.

Without say the examples of Irish playing Irish music where would we be ? I very much doubt that many would be able to pick up a score and having not heard Irish played music still be able to make it sound anything close. So much of the music is in nuance or fine detail, and that nuance makes a vast amount of difference. I also think that that ability or character is ancient, that links to the distant past are not broken. Where else does all we know or understand come from otherwise ?

Attempts at reinventing identities are a modern trait of certain countries which lack their own, or are political machinations designed to neuter independent or local directions. More innocent are those looking for self meaning when their surroundings offer none.

Even now with plenty of access to local Irish music, to me at least it is usually very clear when, of the wider genre of music, it is coming from Ireland as compared to the continent or the US. I don’t say this as a value judgement but only to try to defend from “Irish” being misrepresented, or even worse being stiffled by a reverse flow of outside influences that just are not part of existing traditional local Irish culture or society. All societies in the world are under increasing amounts of outside pressure currently, so I think it is at least fair to help elaborate the distinctions.

The British are a good example, they are still very “Celtic” of descent, but have adopted or had imposed continental culture over centuries and look at the result. Few play close to how the Irish do even when they try. It just isn’t there, but instead present their own aesthetic. Many musicians are able to discern these differences.
