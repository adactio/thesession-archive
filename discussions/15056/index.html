An experienced musician will often praise the playing of a beginner/intermediate player, with the intention of encouraging someone who has, for them, just undergone a traumatic experience. The quality of the playing, from an absolute standpoint, is irrelevant; what matters is that the player has done his/her best, and any put-down comment would be thoughtless and cruel.
On the other hand, if Mick, an experienced player in the ceili band, plays like crap, then other members of the band are likely to point this out to him in no uncertain terms - their way of encouraging him to pull himself together pdq.

Re: Did I sound ok ?

I would think that every player on this site that has played in a session has asked that question, at least of themselves.

Unless it is a teaching situation (like being invited into the session by your teacher, or something similar), actually asking the question can come across as “fishing for compliments”. Whether it actually is or not.

The only time I ever actually vocalized the question, I was asking my wife, and was expecting an honest answer. All I ever got was encouraging remarks.

In fact, I think the first thread I ever started on thesession was complaining about all the encouragement. People would say “you sounded great!” as a way of offering encouragement, but I was there, I had heard how horrible I had been, and so it always felt patronizing to me.

So now, when I give encouragement, I try to do it in a way that is honest, and not patronizing. Like “Good job! It was good to see you playing on those tunes you just learned” or something similar.

Re: Did I sound ok ?

I second (er…third) the “if you need feedback, pay a teacher” comment. At my lessons, I want my teacher to scrutinize my technique. I want him to criticize my playing. I want him to offer suggestions and tell me what I need to work on. And he does all of these, expertly, offering me constructive feedback that is far more valuable than the yes or no answer I’d get to the question, “Did I sound ok?” (which I can answer as well as the next person).

At sessions, though, I want to enjoy playing tunes, and in order to enjoy playing tunes, I can’t be inhibited by self-doubt.

Incidentally, I’ve found the experienced musicians to be nothing but encouraging: although I’ve never asked “did I sound ok?” I’ve on occasion explained that I’m a beginner, in order to offer the others an out if they wanted, for instance, to retract their invitation for me to lead a tune. But that only served to make them encourage me further! It makes sense: encouraging a beginner to keep playing will help them improve, because we improve by playing, especially in groups; you can’t humiliate someone (“you sounded awful”) into improving.

Re: Did I sound ok ?

If you have to ask someone else, then you’re not listening properly to your own playing. And that’s the most imporant skill to develop.

If you really want some honest, constructive feedback, consider asking more specific questions (and ask someone likely to give a helpful answer):

- I feel comfortable with jigs, but my reel rhythm just doesn’t have the pulse or lift I want. Can you hear what I’m missing? What can I do to improve that?

- I don’t have much confidence in my intonation in the higher range. Am I off enough to bother you? Any tips on how to make my intonation better?

- You’ve got a great, singing tone even on the uptempo tunes. How do you do that? My tone seems to go all thin and weak when the pace picks up.

Well, you get the idea.

In my experience, the better musicians are the ones who are acutely aware of their own shortcomings. In public, they play to their strengths, but at home they work on whatever needs improvement (till it becomes a strength).

Re: Did I sound ok ?

Danny, I would never suggest being “constantly” self-critical. That’s why I said the better musicians play to their strengths in public, and work on improving at home.

I’ll add that I think this is a good mindset to have at any stage in your progress as a musician. Beginners too often dwell on all the things they’re doing “wrong,” cringing at every bum note or fumbled phrase. In my experience, it’s far more constructive to find whatever it is you’re doing well and build on that.

This works even to address weaknesses in your playing. For instance, newbie fiddlers often punish themselves for rarely getting the intonation right on the high b on the e string. It’s good to notice that, and to want to correct it. Chances are, however, that they are consistently hitting it flat. In fact, most of the people I’ve taught hit the same flat pitch time after time. The bad news is that it’s not b. The good news is that they’re actually very accurate in their finger placement. Once they realize that, their confidence grows, and then they can work on hearing the right pitch in their head so they’ll land on it (time after time).

I’ve had other players ask me to critique their playing. It really is the musical equivalent of “Do these pants make my butt look fat?” as Johnny J points out above. I usually reply with a compliment on some specifc aspect of their playing that they really are doing well. And then I tell them that they’re much closer than they realize to sounding as good as what they aspire to, and if the timing seems ripe, I’ll offer a tip to help them on the way.

But more often than not, the best tip is “Focus on what you’re already doing well. Use that awareness to improve whatever you think needs improving.”

Re: Did I sound ok ?

I wonder if what some of us are reacting to here is the implied criticism in the way Eddie frames the question.

I don’t go to my local session to listen critically to my friends. I go to drink beer and play tunes. Some nights we sound crap. Other nights everything clicks and the music soars. Sometimes even the best players have off nights. Some times a beginner flubs the sweetest part of a tune. In either case, I don’t hold it against them. After all, it’s not like I bought a ticket to hear them perform.

About once every 6 months, I launch into Ashokan Farewell, with feeling. The emotion builds--such a lovely, poignant waltz. People stop their conversations to listen. And when I get to the end of the A part, I purposefully land on D sharp.

The effect is akin to listening to your lover’s heartbeat lull you to sleep--only to be snapped awake by a chimp stomping a chord on an untuned accordion.

People groan and laugh even harder when I hit the D sharp again at the end of the B part.

It’s a healthy reminder that we’re here to have fun.
