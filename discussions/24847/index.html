Your abilities to play low or high whistle will give you a familiarity with the tunes and the fingerings, which is helpful. But the irish flute requires a whole new and transcendent level of ability, namely of course, with the embouchure, that the whistle, high or low, obviously can’t give you.

I, too, find the low whistle much harder to play than the flute. I don’t think it’s a transition instrument at all. It’s not going to make playing the flute any easier. Playing the flute certainly doesn’t make playing a low whistle easier. The two are hardly even close to the same instrument. They might not even have the same fingering (for the C natural). Re: Low D Whistle: Legit or Transitional Instrument?

If my low whistle is harder to play that a flute, then I’m laughin.

But it depends on the whistle. My whistle is a black wood thing with a reverse conical bore so it’s a lot closer to a flute than your average low whistle. I’m not saying in terms of playability, I’m saying in terms of articulation. For example, it barks like a flute so you build that into you phrasing. It also has a hard bottom D like a flute. And there is a lot more dynamic range than the metal cylindrical whistles. No where near as much as a flute, but it gives you an idea of how that helps with phrasing. The other thing it does really well is straddle the octaves … you know, that thing where flute players have that sound where they push into the second octave, but there’s stil a kind of growl of the low octave there too. I love that flute sound. Good flute players use that in their phrasing a lot, but it doesn’t work on a metal low whistle, you have to play them really clearly.

There was mention of pipers liking low whistles. This has nothing to do with the techniques used to play it, but rather the instrument’s lack of dynamics. Pipers are used to playing with no dynamics and have ways of their own to compensate in their phrasing.

Re: Low D Whistle: Legit or Transitional Instrument?

I have a somewhat different perspective than many, because my first teacher, back in the late 1970’s, played Low D whistle as his primary instrument (he also played flute, box, and banjo). His was one of the first Low D’s there was, an early Overton.

So from my first introduction to Irish music I accepted the Low D whistle as a legitimate Irish instrument on par with flute, box, pipes, fiddle, or banjo.

Things have turned full circle and now, after playing Irish flute for over 30 years, I have switched to Low D whistle as MY primary instrument. It’s the only thing I bring to sessions.

I was forced to switch from flute to Low D whistle due to persistant hand-cramping caused by the position the flute is held in.

So to me, in a strange way, the flute was the “transitional” instrument and the Low D was the final destination.

Yes indeed a Low D is not a flute and I’ve had to change my approach to playing somewhat. I tongue more on the Low D than I did on the flute. My style is less rythmic, because I can’t “push” notes on the Low D whistle like I could on the flute.

I find it much harder to play in tune on a Low D whistle, or indeed any whistle, than on the flute. For whatever reason I could always just pick up the flute and play it in tune, but on the whistle it’s a constant process of listening while I’m playing, trying to get used to how to blow the thing in tune. Of course eventually it will become automatic like it was on the flute.

This is in spite of the fact that my whistle is, in itself, right in tune. It’s my blowing of it that needs work. I can’t blame the instrument.

I have to face the fact that even the best Low D I can find, in my case the MK, can’t do what a flute can do. On the flute you can play the high notes as softly as you please and play the low notes as loud as you please. Even though the MK has quite a bit of scope for “pushing” the notes, still the underlying volume of each note is fixed, and the low notes are a bit too soft and the high notes are a bit too loud.
