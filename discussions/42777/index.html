Re: Seems a long time since any discussion of playing the bodhran

IMHO, I would suggest that any really good bodhran playing would have to be informed by knowing the tune being played. The melody players all made the effort to know the tune. It seems obvious that very few serious collaborative musical efforts could benefit from participation of a player who doesn’t actually know the tune being played.

In this music, the melody is sacred, the prime directive, the raison d’etre. The focus of all the players should be on elevating the melody, creating lift, and getting dancers psyched, even if there aren’t any dancers. If any accompanist doesn’t know the melody, then whatever they’re playing (improvising) is almost certainly going to detract from the quality of the music and possibly be disruptive for the other players.

Short version: learn the tunes before joining in.

↳ Posted by greenman on October 16th, 2018.

Re: Seems a long time since any discussion of playing the bodhran

I want to second the point TomB-R made above, about how the bodhran’s role in this music isn’t to “lay down an underlying beat,” the way percussion is used in Western/Pop music. To my mind, a bodhran player should always be listening very closely to the melody players, and follow what they’re doing with the rythm pulse of the tune. Support the rhythm pulse that’s already there in the melody being played. Don’t try to lead the rhythm or be a human metronome.

This is where the idea of “knowing the tunes” gets problematic. Even if you already know the tune -- maybe you play it on a melody instrument -- you can’t just play what you know. You have to follow what the melody players are doing with the inherent rhythm of the tune. That might be different from how you’d play the melody on another instrument, especially in terms of tempo. If your ears are sensitive enough, and you’ve listened to enough of this music, you should be able to follow and support any tune at a session that you’ve never heard before.

That’s how I think of it, anyway, and it applies equally to guitar players backing the tunes (at least in terms of rhythm strum, not choice of chords, which actually does require knowing the tune in advance). Backing on guitar is where I first learned the concept of “follow, not lead” in this music, before getting more into the melody side with mandolin and flute.

Re: Seems a long time since any discussion of playing the bodhran

Maybe I’m just splitting hairs here or conducting an exercise in semantics here. I find much to agree and disagree with on this subject. First I’ll be clear that a drummer is included in the vast category of “backer” those who accompany the tune, those who give the melody a solid place upon which to stand. Next, I don’t like the term “follow” the melody here. Follow, by definition, means being behind and I can’t think of any time where that’s a good thing. Of coarse the melody is most important. It’s kind of self-aggrandizing to think that Irish trad is somehow unique. Nothing special here. The melody leads in any kind of music. We can put that to bed. Let’s dispose of the notion that a backer (drummer, “zouk, guitar, jaw harp, nose whistle, box player’s left hand, piano, whatever) should be able to play a melody instrument. Let’s instead say that the backer should have a substantial idea of the melody and, as important, a ”feel“, studied or instinctive, for what lifts a melody and what interferes with it. Add to that the idea that a backer has to be intimately connected to his own instrument in such a way that he can create the feeling on demand. When those things come together the backer is playing side-by-side, in a wonderful connection to the melody instead of playing ”catch-up“. It’s not easy, not at all. I suspect that someone who believes that he or she can pound away on a 4/4 drum beat, or a few block chords would contribute much to the diss-appreciation of backers. A skilled backer, including a drummer, is golden. Personally I’ve played, and thoroughly enjoyed, a whole lot of porch music over my years. As much as I’ve loved every moment (well almost) I wouldn’t go see the show or download the CD. For sure there are many who cling to the simple ”Melody is All“ approach and anything else is ”not my cup-o-tea“ , even ”pure shite“. I respect that and I’ll happily play with you anytime. I’ll even buy the tea, pint, or dram! Still it seems like the ”crab-basket" approach to music. I believe that the connection we achieve among skilled melody players is a wonderful thing, just not greater, or less, than the connection we achieve when playing with a skilled backer. Can you imagination the joy we can feel when both come together? I can.

Re: Seems a long time since any discussion of playing the bodhran

Lots of comments from those who don’t play drum. The problem is that they are trying to diagnose what they don’t like about bodhran players who have mucked up their session. In so doing, they are barking up the wrong tree. I guarantee, it is not about knowing the tunes, it’s about knowing the drum. Playing to Irish music is dead easy if you know the form and you know the drum. Tunes are very predictable and there is a lot of repetition. It’s hard to go wrong keeping the beat steady, but after sitting out the first time through on the A part (which I recommend no matter how well you know the tune), one can pick up the groove. After that, you can gradually vary the pattern, add syncopation and/or emphasize certain beats as good taste allows. You are going to hear the same phrases at least six times each before the tune changes, so if you can’t develop what y0u are playing during the course of the tune, not knowing the tune beforehand is the least of your concerns. Rare is the tune that has somehow caught me by surprise, meaning what I was playing was not in keeping with what I was hearing. It’s just not that hard, but you have to be at least competent on the drum, and many are not, so I feel your pain, especially since I am first and foremost a flute player. However, I would happily busk with a decent bodhran player who didn’t know a single tune I played.

All gone quiet on here. Fair enough. Maybe there’s only so much to say about playing the bodhrán.

But just an observation/discovery for any bodhrán players here - in my original post I commented on not being able to play really fast when the fiddle-players were cranking it up, and that other musicians had often commented on how we needed to slow the tunes down to let them “breathe” more.

From looking closely at players on YouTube I’ve changed the way I hold my tipper. For 20+ years with different sessions and band I’ve held the tipper with thumb on top and three fingers underneath - a solid and powerful grip. But changing to a grip more like holding a pen (mainly between thumb and first finger, with middle finger supporting underneath) is a different thing. I can keep up with faster tunes now and still be articulate. The tipper’s closer to the skin and top and bottom can bounce quicker. Still working on it but looking forward to playing in session next week with new drum and new approach \())
