Re: Irreconcilable viewpoints

Ok….this is just my opinion, based on decades of musical experience,in multiple genres, but still just a sample size of one. And here’s a qualifier(also IMO). I don’t know anything about DADGAD, or as I’m fond of saying,GODDAM, tuning. Another assumption is that you know something about rhythm…at least the difference between Alligator and Crocodile. If you don’t, you’ll never be welcome in sessions.

Irish music structure just isn’t that complex. I’t’s kinda Music Theory 101. Pick a key, pick a mode. You’ve got six chords to choose from. You gotta figure out where to use them, but there’s still only 6 and most often no more than 4. C’mon, when was the last time you used a Dom 7 #9, a sus 4, or even a minor 9? For the most part, because chords overlap in some keys. you can play in D, G, A, C with just 16 chords total. You can do more and many good backers do. But at least if you can move freely between the 6 you won’t suck. As pointed out before, backers should wait it out until they have some notion of where a tune goes…just like melody players should wait it out until they can play it. The ability to pick up a melody on the fly is a rare talent indeed. Backers have the edge here though, They only have to play the chord (rhythmically of course) not capture the melodic figure. As mentioned, the best backers know the melody and how to give it a place to stand and a place to breath. Still, it’s pretty basic to be adequate. Sadly, even that often takes more effort than some are willing to give it.

So as to the question of jumping in to tunes you don’t know…well, sometimes you can, sometimes you can’t. Ya gotta know when you can and when you can’t. If you need to be told (and it’s no different for backers than melody players) that what you’re doing isn’t working, you can’t. I know it sounds a bit harsh. It’s all part of the dues you pay to join the club. That said, even a modest backer with a good ear and good manners won’t ruin a session, a talented backer can bring it to life. As a lifelong bassist, I’m not limiting myself to ITM here, I would never, never, play anything but the most basic bass part without being comfortable with the melody first.

Re: Irreconcilable viewpoints

Any sweeping generalisation or rule anybody comes up with won’t mean much, because every musician is different.

I’ve played with guitarists who could, on the fly, do a good job accompanying tunes which were new to them- they had a good feeling of where tunes tended to go, and if a tune had an odd harmonic twist it would catch them off guard the first time it appeared, but not afterwards.

One guy, equally fluent in regular tuning or DADGAD, was exceptionally good at “winging” backup. He used to say, though, that “you can’t wing Beatles, or O Carolan.” He had to be familiar with the melody to appropriately back up O Carolan tunes. Reels, jigs, etc rarely caught him off guard.

Then there have been people who were excellent guitarists but who were clueless when trying to back up tunes new to them. They would make more wrong guesses than correct ones. They seemed neither to have a good feel for the typical patterns, nor have a good ear which enabled them to quickly respond to what was happening in the tune. But if they knew the tune it was lovely.

Re: Irreconcilable viewpoints

Precisely my point. I’ll bet that the good one’s have at least a working understanding of chord theory or at best have studied it. Pick a key, say “D Major”. The chords are gonna be DM, Em, F#m, GM, AM, Bm (the 7th is just about never used in most genres). Most of the time you only use 2 to 4 of them. There’s a pattern to it. Yeah, this generalization isn’t ALWAYS true, but if you follow it as a backer, and have a sense of rhythm, along with a good ear, you’ll soon get the reputation as a guy who can back almost anything. It’s a useful three legged stool… ear, rhythm, and knowledge. If ya have all three to even a modest degree you “get it”. If you lack even one, well, like the stool, ya fall over. It doesn’t mean you can’t get to be a good backer. It just means that there’s going to be a lot more memorization (and patience) involved. Now I don’t want to get into a pissin’ contest about whether
backing should be used at all. That’s been argued to death on the site before. I just suggest that a little bit of theory is often what separates good, great, and poor backing. The best backing, no matter how sophisticated, must serve to give a lovely melody a firm place to stand. Otherwise it’s just noise.

Many of the (many) discussions about backing/accompanying here focus on whether the guitarist knows the chords. And of course, that is important. But more important (and usually where learning guitarists fall short) is with the rhythm. As a very good guitarist once told me, the real challenge in playing the guitar is in the right hand.

In other words, if a guitarist doesn’t know what chords to play but has a good right hand, (s)he could simply mute the strings and play rhythm. Not the ideal, but in my view, far preferable to making good chord choices but not having spot on rhythm. As a melody player, I really want to hear eighth notes and accents--pretty harmonies are icing on the cake.

Well now, Richard, I guess it depends at its most fundamental on whether you see a session as a gig at which the assembled maestros can display their talents, or a practise at which those with less confidence and experience can learn some stuff and find out how to do it without the pressure of public performance, or somewhere in between. It’s quite surprising how much ego can become invested in a pub session (of any kind of music). Personally, I don’t like that, because it can become ‘exclusive’ in the worst sense of the word - again, most of us have encountered the syndrome that goes ‘here’s a tune only the four of us know - we practised it at home, and we can play it fast enough you won’t pick it up’. This is the other side of the ‘incompetent backer’ syndrome‘, and makes me think ’why don’t you guys go and get a gig someplace and let us keep this loose and fun?‘. On the other hand, I was once at a mixed Irish/Scottish/whatever session in the James Joyce bar in Brussels, where an unfortunate bohdranist went ’Boom!‘ on the first beat of every bar of the lament ’Neil Gow’s Lament for his Brother Donald’, and slightly out of time. The fine Scottish fiddle player leading the session (Eddie S., from Aberdeenshire) put down his fiddle in the middle of the second time through and said quietly but firmly “I cannae play with you making that noise.” The recalcitrant percussionist left without a word, never to return, and the poor chap was dead within three months. Whether or not the two events were connected is not clear, but the moral is - we might sometimes wish some people would go off and have a session somewhere else, but don’t say it…

Re: Irreconcilable viewpoints

I guess this thread just touches on some buttons for me.

A session is a session…a social gathering of musicians with similar interest. A gig is a rehearsed event featuring a select group of players. Neither is a practice…that’s something you do solo or as a group, in preparation for something else. During a session less experienced players join as able and hopefully learn something. True for melody players and backers alike. Just as important, better players need to make a hole for others…just as what somebody else, at one time or another, did for them. Everybody has an obligation to lend a hand and level the field. Sometimes somebody’s ego gets in the way of that. I suggest they find a group, an agent, and a studio. I once attended an “open” session that was hijacked by A-list players (and I mean A-list…oft recorded, oft quoted) who made the session another performance. I left without a word, they likely didn’t miss me. Just as session players don’t enjoy a clueless backer, or melody free tooter…we don’t enjoy sitting through a “fill-in-name and fill-in-name show” when we came to play. Hire a hall and put on a show if you’re that good…I may even buy a ticket. But I don’t give a rat’sass how good you are or who you are if you don’t have good session manners. Other side of the coin, get some chops so you can join in more often,.. be patient, you’ll get there. To the rest…be patient, they’ll get there.

By the way, kudos to Kevin Crawford, and the guys from Lunasa. They added a very welcoming atmosphere to a bunch of modest players a couple of years ago after a local performance.
