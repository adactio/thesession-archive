Challenge Values: The Significance of Intellectual Courage.

The last several weeks, I’ve been comparing the things that have had the most impact on my musical learning experience. Undeniably, one of those things is specifically my being willing to work through aural challenges, for the sake of expanding my aural vocabulary. What is an aural challenge? An aural challenge is anything that you haven’t learned or don’t aurally understand. Some examples of aural challenges are learning how to recognize certain accidentals, tonal modulations, or new chords/chord progressions in general. (This essay can also apply to non-aural challenges, like techniques and skills. An example of these would be learning a piece that requires you to learn a new key or work on new motor skills exclusive to your chosen instrument. But since I’m a piano player, I’ll be mostly talking about tonal modulations.)

To elaborate: I’m not talking about learning what a D Major chord sounds like. I’m talking about learning what a Major Two Chord(II - D Major in the key of C Major) sounds like in a piece of music where the Two Chord is naturally minor(any piece of music using a simple major or minor scale). I’m not talking about learning what a minor chord sounds like. I’m talking about learning how to recognize a minor two(ii), minor three(iii), or minor six(vi), when all you’ve ever heard is Major One(I), Major Four(IV) and Major Five(V).

Now that that is out of the way, what am I talking about when I say “Challenge Value”? In short, the challenge value of a piece of music is how much you will learn from the piece if you actually figure it out. Because musical understanding stacks like math, you don’t necessarily “learn” from every new song or tune you pick up. If the new song or tune doesn’t feature any new information, then you’re only reinforcing your understanding of things you already know. But if the new song uses a few modulations you don’t recognize, or a new tune uses an arpeggio or accidental you’ve never heard before, then those pieces of music have “challenge value”, for learning by ear.

For me, I determine the challenge value of something by assessing how difficult it is compared to how much “style” I can cultivate from it(pretty much how useful the new information is/or simply how much I like it). For example, if a song uses a lot of modulations that I don’t recognize, and it is a song that is in a style that I favor, it has a high challenge value, especially if those modulations are exclusive to the style. I can then use that information to learn other songs in that style, or other songs that use the same information. If a song uses a lot of modulations, but I don’t like them, or the style of the song, then it has a low challenge value. Not only is the information near useless, but the music itself has no lasting value and I won’t even be motivated enough to work through it. Of course, if you don’t like the piece of music as a whole, but you like something it does, you can isolate the challenge and learn it independently. I’ve done it a few times myself. Remember, the point is to expand your aural vocabulary, and you don’t have to learn an entire piece of music you don’t like for one or two unrecognizable phrases. Just learn the phrases.

But what about the music that you love, that you adore even, that just happens to be extremely difficult? The truth of the matter is, the harder the better. These have the highest challenge value. This is why intellectual courage is important. Sometimes we want to run away from the things that challenge us, even if they are very good for us. But if we take a step back, sit down, sit with the music, and work through it, there is so much to be gained. With every new thing you learn, everything else that uses the same thing becomes easier, in a sense. If you’re a composer like me, every learned accidental and modulation is invaluable. Everything you learn cuts the learning curve and eventually, you reach levels of stylistic and conceptual mastery. But we can’t do this if we’re afraid to get uncomfortable and do the hard work. Or, if we don’t have the confidence to even try. Sometimes we’ll even be surprised by how much we have learned. You’ll find things easily that you thought were far beyond you, or learn things quickly that you thought would take… goodness… too long.

After working through several high-value pieces over the last few months, I realized a pretty deep musical secret just this last week. A lot of modulations are simple changes in the natural chords’ value. Like changing the naturally minor iii chord(e minor in the key of C Major), to a Major III chord(E Major in the key of C Major), or to a diminished iii* chord(e diminished in the key of C Major). I’ve been playing piano for 19 years and this concept never even crossed my mind. I realized it after I began cataloging all of the modulations I’ve learned over the years. Some are more complicated to figure out, like mixed chords, but those tend to have a certain richness about them that would at least reveal that they are indeed mixed.

I can’t say that I’ve learned all of my favorite music, or that I’ve learned all of my favorite modulations. What I can say after having learned the lot of them; I am not afraid to try. I guess one reason why I take my time going through “the challenges” is because of that fear of not having anything left to do if I just happen to complete them all. I don’t know if that is a rational fear, but there is so much joy and magic in finally solving the mystery after so long. Those “aha” moments. I guess I don’t want to run out of opportunities for those moments. At the least, I am much more capable than I was 9 years ago. 2 years ago even. And also, Music is fun, and it would be boring if it were any easier.

Re: Challenge Values: The Significance of Intellectual Courage.

Ben, the way I use the term “modulation” in this context is in a simpler sense. I’m not referring to complete key changes, I’m referring to using alternative chords from a key that is outside of the natural scale system. This is in context of harmony. In the context of melody, it would be called an accidental. For example, in the key of C, the note “F” is natural. If you # the F, it is now an accidental. That F# harmonically changes(*modulates*) the natural d minor chord into a D Major chord. It would also change the natural F Major chord to an f#diminished chord, and the natural b diminished chord to a b minor chord. If the tonic is C, and the F was sharpened permanently, you would be playing in the mode C Mixolydian, whose relative major is G Major. Does that clarify things?

I do apologize for the confusion of words, as I did look for a more fitting word but couldn’t find one. Note, the article is definitely an extension of the conversation of the usefulness of modulations. Though, I hope that the many other posters and lurkers understand, like DonaldK, that the main idea is to learn how to do things you don’t know how to do in general. I just used modulations as my example because learning them expands your aural vocabulary, and that’s what learning by ear is all about to me.

Intellectual Courage.

I’ve given a lot of thought to my use of the term “Intellectual Courage” and have decided that I should clarify the context of my understanding of the term.

A couple of years ago, I discovered the term “intellectual honesty”. Apparently, it’s your ability to accept new information contrary to your beliefs; your ability to accept that you don’t know something; your ability to accept that you are wrong. I was hoping to use the term “intellectual courage” similarly, as; your capacity to seek new information contrary to your beliefs; your ability to understand when you don’t know something; your capacity to correct yourself rather than staying wrong.

I’ve realized that when I use to term, I’m referring to having it in the context of a prior experience. Deciding to try again after you’ve failed. Going back to something that you couldn’t figure out before. Not really giving up on something and shutting the book, but leaving a note to come back to it after you hit the next learning curve.
