Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?

What was your experience of taking up a second instrument like?

What are the instruments?

Why did you do it?

Why did you choose the second instrument?

Bonus.
Fiddle is my first instrument but I got a bit fed up (i.e. I stopped practicing) so I picked up the concertina (in my mid thirties) as it was very different from the fiddle (and always in tune). I tried to practice an hour a day for the first three years, mainly learning new tunes rather than recycling the ones I knew. Turns out I was more disciplined and methodical in my learning-process than when I started the fiddle in my late teens….
As a result of this I became a much better fiddle player although I played it less. I recon I started to think about the music I play in a different way, or just started to be more conscious about both the tune and the playing of it.

I have now taken on my first pupil on the concertina, which also forces me to express thoughts about playing, which is very healthy.

Re: Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?


Transitioning from a keyboard to a stringed instrument was arguably one of the most humbling and humiliating experiences of my life. As a pianist, musical concepts like intonation and tonality weren’t even in my awareness; Whereas, when it comes to violin, they are essential. I was baffled by how little I could refer to the piano for adjacent support when learning fiddle.

I’ve always appreciated the music associated with fiddle playing. However, when I was growing up, there were no orchestras for me to learn violin, and folk musicians and fiddle players didn’t exist in my world. All of the “grown-ups” were either church musicians(like, literally every single one of my piano teachers) or school teachers. I have no memories of live folk music from my childhood.

It’s documented history that I owned a violin before I started learning Irish music, so I can say with certainty that I was interested specifically in the instrument itself. Hearing Irish tunes made me realize that I wanted to learn folk music. To this day, it has been the most rewarding and fulfilling decision I’ve ever made.

Consequently, I’ve taken up a 2nd “keyboard”(concertina) and a 2nd stringed(guitar) instrument.

Re: Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?

A few thoughts…

It is interesting that the vast majority of Irish kids start young on whistle and then pick their ‘real’ instrument. So they all get ‘second instrument’ benefits, if these exist.

My theory is two-fold:

As an adult, that first few years on the first instrument you have to both learn ‘the music’ and the technique so a lot of tune recall and retention alongside learning touch, mechanics etc etc. It was a slog. For me anyway.

The second instrument comes in with a base of general Irish musicianship already established.

And there is something else, something in making the brain and body re-engage with the music from a new angle though a different physical means of producing it.

So I think there is a wisdom in the madness of all the instruments available - they fit certain people better.

Re: Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?

I started playing Irish trad on mandolin, did that for enough years to reach what I’d call “journeyman” level -- never a master, but decent enough for playing in local sessions and a few side gigs.

At some point -- maybe it was fiddle envy or listening to pipers -- I began to feel that I was missing out on a wider range of expression through ornamentation, the distinctive sound of Irish trad. The standard ornament for mandolin is the banjo style “treble” because it’s loud enough to be heard in a session. And that’s about it. You can simulate a cut or roll with hammer-ons and pull-offs, but the very short string decay and intrinsic quiet nature of the instrument makes that only practical when playing solo. And in a quiet room.

So I decided to try learning a sustaining instrument with wider ornamentation potential, and ended up with flute. It’s been a journey. The first years were rough, and I still haven’t transferred over all the tunes I supposedly “know” on mandolin. But I’m reasonably happy with it now as my main trad instrument. I also use a low Bb flute for slower tunes like airs and “slow reels.”

I still play some mandolin for tunes that have essential notes on a fiddle’s G string, which is the the only real annoyance with flute because I don’t like “folding” notes up an octave or leaving them out. I play octave mandolin at home for slower pieces where it’s nice to hear a deeper range sometimes. And then… I played guitar for 30-odd years before getting into ITM later in life. So I still play a few tunes on guitar, or accompany my fiddler S.O. on guitar. I can’t seem to completely give up the multi-instrument thing. But flute is it now, for the main focus.

Re: Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?

I’ve always enjoyed learning new instruments. My first was actually harmonica, which I picked up from a little book called “Country and Blues Harmonica For the Musically Hopeless” - an excellent way to start learning the instrument, I still think. After a while, when I started playing music with other kids in my school, I realized that the harmonica player doesn’t usually have a lot to do in a band setting, so I learned a little guitar, just enough to strum along with the band. After a bit, I came across the mandolin, and learned some things on that. At some point in there I discovered Irish music, and started picking the tunes on the mandolin, eventually that led to the whistle and the banjo. After experimenting with playing tunes on the harmonica (standard 10-hole Richter-tuned D harmonica works great for lots of tunes!) I got curious about the box, so I got hold of one and learned that, and when a friend of mine was upgrading her flute, I ended up with two of her old ones, which I still play. These days when I go to a session I’ve always got a flute and a whistle and usually a box or a banjo as well.

I’ve found that after the second instrument, learning a new instrument is really not much of a thing. As Brendan says above, the hard part of learning your first traditional instrument is that you’re trying to do two tricks at once - maybe three. You’re trying to learn the mechanics of the instrument, and you’re also trying to learn the style. And if it’s your first time playing an instrument, you’re also trying to get used to the experience of actually making a sequence of pitches come out in tune and in time, which is itself a hard enough task. The second instrument, you’re mostly dealing with the weirdness of playing music, but not on the instrument you already know. This is similar to learning a second language - you’re trying to say “The cat sat on the mat” in, say, Finnish, but you already know how to do that in English, and the English interferes. Same thing with music - you try to play the Banshee on the banjo, but you already know how to play it on the mandolin, and your fingers get confused. With the third instrument, you’ve managed to separate the tune from the finger motions that produce it on one particular instrument, and now it’s easy. You’ve got the tunes, you just need to find them on the new instrument - which is just fun!

Brendan makes a good point:
> there is something else, something in making the brain and body re-engage with the music from a new angle though a different physical means of producing it.

In my experience, learning an old tune on a new instrument changes the way I understand the tune and it usually teaches me something new about the tune that carries back to other instruments. If I know a tune only on one instrument, it’s easy for my thinking about the tune itself to get tied up with the mechanics of playing it on that instrument. If I know it on several instruments, that helps to ensure that I abstract the tune itself from the tune as played on the box, the whistle, the banjo, or whatever. So learning a tune on a new instrument, I think, helps me hear new things in the tune, and helps me play it better in general.

Re: Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?

learning the pipes as a second instrument helped me a lot. I had been playing classical flute before I switched to the wooden flute. I did what many of people with a precious classical background and some proficiency on their instrument did: I played too fast, didn’t focus on more subtle aspects of the music.

I think learning pipes gave me more understanding about Irish music (suddenly the emphasis on the low D made much more sense for example!) and made me much more humble and willing to play slower.

Also I can’t stress out how much fun playing the pipes is. Playing the d myxo pipe tunes (Connaught Heifers, Humours of Ballyloughling etc.) is so much fun and has such an archaic feel.
This also helped me with understanding more, that some tunes are just more fun on some instruments than on others - even if this doesn’t mean that they are harder to play on the less-fun instruments! I think many classical trained musicians lack this understanding and try to play the most complicated tunes when they start with Irish music. And tend to blatantly dismiss “easy” traditional tunes.

Over the last year or two I started with the regulators and it enhanced the music experience a lot more for me.
You also shouldn’t underestimate how handy it can be to switch between two instruments if you have the feel that one instrument is already well overrepresented (I like sessions with a lot of flutes…but when there are too many flutes with not so good tuning I like to switch to the pipes).

Re: Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?

Definitely bonus!

I picked ups the concertina after some 20 years of fiddle, because it was hot summer, my irish-music-loving pals were all in Ireland for festivals and I was barricaded in my house to stay away from the scorching sun…
And so I did an impulse purchase :)
In any case, I had been eyeing the concertina for a few years.

Learning a second instrument when you’re already familiar with the music makes it a whole different experience. I focused straightaway on the important parts (articulation and rhythm) and I progressed really fast.

Another advantage is that, playing the fiddle already, I was in no hurry to learn: I could take my fiddle to sessions, and take it easy with the concertina. So different from when I started on the fiddle, and I could not wait to play fast and join sessions!

As someone else said in this thread, learning music with different mechanics has a lot of advantages.
You can focus on other aspect of music that you had neglected with your first instrument.
The fiddle is a devil, on an off day it’s hard to be satisfied with your music, you feel like you’re constantly out of tune (and maybe you are). The concertina has not such difficulties, so you can put your attention on other aspects: tempo, rhythm, etc. So I I got much better at tempo and articulation.
I honestly believe the concertina has made me a much better fiddler.

Also, when I learn a new tune on the concertina I can transfer it to the fiddle almost immediately; the opposite way around does not work this well, yet. Still, it’s another chance to widen your repertoire.

I have other second-ish instruments (whistle, mandolin, guitar) but the concertina is the only one I am really focusing on.

I have a weird brain.

These days, in sessions I’m mostly playing Uilleann pipes, B/C box, Anglo Concertina, and whistle. I play all of them at a fairly high level, but of course, there is no end to the work, which is what I love about this music.

I do enjoy playing backup on GDAD bouzouki, but in the sessions I host/co-host, there’s other regular backup players on guitar/bouzouki and I need to keep the tunes going, so I’ll only do backing if someone else is hosting, there isn’t a regular backup player, and there are other strong melody players.

I think all of this is good for my now 64-year old brain, hopefully will help me avoid future dementia.

Re: Second Instrument - Bonus or Burden?

As a longtime fiddler, I also dabbled in whistle. That led to keyless flute, which felt intuitive and mechanically straightforward coming from decades of lashing fretless strings with horse tail. So flute came up to snuff fairly quickly, playing in sessions within 6 months. Of course, I already had a raft of tunes and the pulse and a sense of phrasing.

Fluting is much like singing in that you have to suss out where to breathe in a way that enhances the music, and that in turn opened windows into phrasing, punctuation, and the use of silence that might never have occurred to me with a bow in my hand. Playing flute made me a better fiddler, a better musician. It also helps in understanding the guardrails that fluters, whistlers, and pipers play within, so when you’re on fiddle you can make choices—in what tunes to play, what keys, what ornamentation, etc.—that lift the wind players at your session.

I also enjoyed how easy it was to pack a keyless delrin flute anywhere you go, which kept tunes in the head and fingers from going stale on, say, long travels and rough trips where a fiddle might’ve been an inconvenience.

A bonus, without a doubt.

I learned mandolin and whistle more or less concurrently, with petiodically shifting focus. Why dud I choose those instruments? I didn’t really, we just happened to have one of each in the house (I did not grow up in a traditional music or ‘folkie’ household - they had both just been picked up as bargains by my mother) - so when I became interested in traditional music in my late teens/early twenties, it was obvious which instruments to go for. I also played around a bit with trad tunes on piano and guitar (piano was my ‘original’ first instrument and learning guitar was de rigeur as a teenager).

Realising that the mandolin was a somewhat peripheral instrument to Irish trad, I felt that the whistle gave me a bit more insight into the music and, ultimately, stylistically informed my mandolin playing - whilst having a less trad-centred instrument also allowed me to ‘think outside the box’ a bit when playing the whistle.

The caveat - not so much for a *second* instrument but for playing multiple instruments - is that there is a point at which the benefits start to be offset by the rarefication of time and energy to allocate to each instrument. My focus has shifted to fiddle over the last 20 years, and the whistle receives very little attention (much as piano and guitar took a back seat once I was focusing on mandolin and whistle). No doubt, the number of instruments one can play concurrently to a consummate level depends on the individual but, for me, the fall-off is definitely noticeable beyond three instruments. (I also play piano accordion in a klezmer band and keyboards in a garage rock band, neither of them to any more than the bare minimum standard required).

Look it’s not magic but I think there is a very positive effect in taking on a second instrument.

My experience - 3 and a bit years on banjo and 5/6 odd months on the C#/D box - has been that on balance banjo playing has benefitted even with a lot less attention.
