It’s not just about playing fast or even getting the notes correctly. You have to get into “the groove” of that particular session.
That, for me, has always been the most difficult part. Some musicians can adapt much more easily than others, of course. However, if the style of music is slightly different to what you are used to playing and/or there are subtle(or not so) variations of some tunes it can be quite easy to get thrown off track.

So, practice and patience seems to be the answer and familiarisation with what happens in a particular session is important. Far better musicians than us can still feel out of place in some sessions and are happy to “sit out ” certain sets.

I should conclude that instruments such as the banjo, mandolin etc are actually harder to play well at speed than the flute and fiddle(I can speak for the latter) once you get past the earlier stages. Again, it involves adapting… I tend to introduce more “shorter” notes without changing the tune as such or the feel of it. e.g. splitting one long note, adding triplets and so on. Sometimes, I do the reverse with the fiddle and actually make longer notes. It all depends.

Re: How can I improve my speed playing on banjo?

You’ve only been playing for a year?!! It sounds as if you’re simply trying to play much too fast for your level of banjo-bility! This is potentially quite problematic as you will take find yourself taking shortcuts, to the detriment of your musicianship. Take a step back and play within yourself. Your ideal tempo is (and will always be, by the way!) the one at which you can play the tune accurately and fluently, with enough time and space to add your own ornamentation. If you stick at it and practice regularly (20 minutes every day is better than 5 hours once a week) you will speed up. Set a metronome at your comfortable tempo (see above) and GRADUALLY increase it, week by week. Don’t try and RUSH to these mad speeds: it won’t work.
Never try and measure yourself against others: music is not a competative sport and tastes vary (as you can see from this thread!). If the other musicians at the session are too fast for you, you will be happier in the long term, as well as the short, if you find a slower session.

Re: How can I improve my speed playing on banjo?

There’s a saying that comes in handy in a lot of situations: slow is smooth, and smooth is fast.

What this means in this music is that you can’t play a tune well fast until you can play it well, period, and that means playing it mindfully, at a moderate pace, and really listening to what you’re doing as you’re doing it. Importantly, this isn’t about improving your manual dexterity. The problem isn’t in your fingers, it’s in your mind.

One thing to think about - can you play the tunes back in your head? What do they sound like in there? Now, can you play them in your head at the tempos you’re talking about? If you can picture every note, in real time, at whatever tempo your whistle players are going at, then you’ve got a reasonable chance of executing that on the banjo. If not, then there’s no hope that your fingers will be able to go faster than your brain.

Another consideration: on the banjo, it’s pretty easy to ignore phrasing. I hear this all the time, players will simply keep a sewing-machine rhythm without dynamics or breath. This makes it very hard to play with others, particularly with whistlers or flute players, who have to pay attention to phrasing. You can’t lock in to someone else’s playing if you’re ignoring the pulse of their playing. One way to get a sense of this is to pay attention to your breathing as you play. When you breathe in, there’s a phrase around there somewhere. Find it and bring it out in your playing.
Another way to get this sense, of course, is just to pick up a whistle and learn a few tunes on it.

↳ Posted by Will Evans on November 17th, 2025.

Re: How can I improve my speed playing on banjo?

As others have said, the common BPM measurement for reels is two beats to a bar even though they’re more often written in 4/4 than 2/2. So dance tempo is between 113-120 counted in 2 (sometimes up to over 120 if set dancers are really insistent).

Playing reels at 110 is still no small feat on any instrument though, but pretty common in sessions. For banjo, focus on consistent, economical picking, with minimal excess motion. Leave out triplets until they fall under your pick comfortably. Make sure you can play the tune cleanly slowly, with minimal excess motion, and pick up the metronome pace.

Perhaps even more importantly, building speed comes from THINKING the tunes as fast as you want to play them. I recommend students sing/lilt a tune, getting it up to speed in their brains through their voices first.

Hope this helps!

Re: How can I improve my speed playing on banjo?

Relax more. Especially through the tricky bits. Tension is the enemy.

Work on economy of motion. In both hands.

Slow down. The path to high tempos runs through slow tempos first. Skip this step, and your high tempo playing will be garbage. Build a sound foundation first. It’s noticeable when players have done this. Concentrate on executing the basics to perfection.

Picking with the fingers and wrist is correct. The elbow should be pretty stable and relaxed.

You might need a different pick. Experiment.

You might need a different pick angle of attack.

Don’t worry about triplets. Concentrate on time and swing. Good, simple, danceable playing with the right amount of swing is much better than bad triplets.

Master relaxation first. Then you’ll be ready for the triplets. Again work up from slow tempos.

(a) over time I get more fluent so even though day-by-day it doesn’t always feel as if I am getting anywhere, if I stand back then month-by-month, year-by-year, I am. Don’t over practice. (e) experiment with picks/plectrums. I started playing mandolin initially and I used a very soft bendy pick (guitar strum type) and it was like playing with a piece of lettuce. I was encouraged to try harder and thicker picks and gradually worked my way up to medium, hard, very hard and jazz brick picks. I became convinced that a really hard pick was the way to go for speed - somebody leading a workshop even said so - I ordered special Wegen picks - lovely! Then at another workshop a bluegrass mandolin player said you need a much softer pick to play at speed and demonstrated it. So I seemed to be getting conflicting advice from different mandolin players of equal credibility. Being a simple person, I could no longer work out which way to go - does not compute, does not compute - so I went back to experimenting and I’m currently somewhere around Hard to XHard for guitar (melody) but can use a Medium quite happily. Whatever happened to those thumb quill picks that Sully used to use for banjo?

(f) it is worth ‘analysing’ a tune - I find this for guitar certainly - to figure which fret positions, fingers etc I am best to use and to work out tricky bits. So for banjo this will definitely be the case unless you have extra long fingers. Re: How can I improve my speed playing on banjo?

Two more thoughts regarding the left hand.
1. Are you playing it like a fiddle, such that the ring finger lands on the 5th fret? or the 4 finger way where the pinky lands on the 5th? Unless you have big flexible hands the 4 finger way is faster.

2. Since you’re fairly new to this I’m going to guess you have a 19 fret banjo. These are really designed to be tuned CGDA. Screwing them up to GDAE makes for very tight strings which is why the Irish banjo typically has 17 frets. If you tune it to FCBD and capo the second fret, you’ll drop the string tension a bit making it easier on the right hand and the frets will be closer together making it easier for the left.

Gerry O’Connor recommends the Jim Dunlop .6 nylon plectrum (light grey one).

>> But some are saying the 19-fret ones are prefered.

In chatting with Chuck Ogsbury at Ome banjos 20 years ago, he mentioned that he didn’t make 17 fret banjos because the longer strings on a 19 fret banjo resonate better and the entire instrument sounds better. (That might change now that Chuck is retired, and Ome got bought out by Gold Tone). I have had many banjos of both lengths, and in general, I would say that Chuck was right about that, although, I can’t say that I’ve ever played a real high end 17 fret banjo, so that might be a factor.

gilmo789 talks a bit about fret spacing slowing a player down, and string tension being an issue. First off, I use heavier gauge strings on a 17 fret banjo than I do on a 19 fret banjo specifically to get the same tension that I am used to… I like about 17 pounds of tension on each string, and to get that tension at a shorter scale, you need heavier strings. As far as fret spacing between the two lengths goes, in first position, the difference is a bit negligible. The spacing between the nut and the first fret would be about 32.8mm on a 19 fret, and 29.93mm on a 17 fret. So that’s a difference of about 9%, which sounds significant, but in reality, it’s not even 3mm.

I find that if you have good left hand positioning, the scale length difference isn’t really noticeable. Part of it depends on how you’re fingering the banjo (4 finger vs 3 finger). If you’re using 4 finger style, you’re generally going to shift your hand when you’re playing above the 5th fret, and the difference between shifting a couple millimeters further isn’t likely to slow you down. If you’re playing 3 finger “fiddle fingering”, then it can feel like you’re stretching a bit further, but if you have your fingers pointing toward the bridge a bit, instead of perpendicular to the neck, then you’re reaching forward, instead of stretching sideways. And if your thumb is in a good position, you can pivot your hand on the thumb to reach the 7th fret (or higher), without losing your positional awareness.

FWIW, I play 3 finger style, and my biggest problem playing on a 17 fret banjo is that I overreach the 7th fret when I’m jumping up to the B from down at the F or G. (If I am coming to the B from the A, I don’t miss it, just when the melody makes a bigger jump up to the B). But that’s just because I am used to longer scale banjos. I actually played a session last weekend on a borrowed 18 fret banjo, as I was visiting my in-laws, and I was overreaching a bit even on that instrument, but only reaching the B… Otherwise I couldn’t really detect any difference in scale length. To be fair, I have big hands, but with proper positioning, it just doesn’t make much difference, even if you have smaller hands…

There are also different schools of thought about picking and speed. Jon Kiparsky mentions “pick the tune instead of the pattern…” That can be helpful to get rid of the relentless sewing machine rhythm, but back when I was struggling with tempos and steadiness, a lesson with a great player convinced me that playing with more strict picking patterns was what I needed to be able to play faster and more steadily. The problem I was having back then was that certain parts of tunes felt difficult, and would mess up my tempo and rhythm. The teacher watched my playing and immediately noticed that I was losing my rhythm because all of a sudden I was picking in the opposite direction of what I was doing for most of the rest of the tune. So while I was picking a more efficient direction (meaning since my hand was moving from one string to another, I was picking in that same direction, no matter what, in the name of “economy of motion”), it was messing up the flow of my hand rhythm, and causing a blip in my overall rhythm and timing. So I started playing my tunes with pretty strict picking patterns (DUDU for reels, DUD DUD for jigs). That has an extra bonus for me in being able to play variations. Even if I choose to play notes on a different string, I am going to pick it the same direction, so I am never fighting any “muscle memory” that plays a tune a specific way… And then my phrasing of the melody is what keeps me from being a relentless sewing machine of notes…

Ultimately, ensuring that I was using fairly strict picking patterns was really one of the things that got me over the hump with faster tempos! (YMMV)

“Practice every day” might also be phrased as “play every day”. It’s not such a hard job as all that, really, just play tunes you like, a lot. But either way you say it, yes, you learn to play by playing, and not any other way.

And again, the best way to play them fast is to play them slow. If you take a tune that you _can_ play at a brisk pace and play it with lift and rhythm at about 80% of that speed, that’s the way to really get inside the tune. If you can find the lift in a tune at a slow pace, you’ve learned something real and deep about the tune, and that’ll carry through when you’re playing it faster.

You can also learn something about a tune by playing it well out of your comfort zone from time to time, of course. When you do this, though, you should pay attention to what you’re leaving out, and how that affects the tune itself. If you play it faster than you can listen, that’s probably too fast and I imagine you won’t learn much from the exercise, except for any mistakes that you train into your fingers along the way.

> 2. Since you’re fairly new to this I’m going to guess you have a 19 fret banjo. These are really designed to be tuned CGDA. Screwing them up to GDAE makes for very tight strings which is why the Irish banjo typically has 17 frets. If you tune it to FCBD and capo the second fret, you’ll drop the string tension a bit making it easier on the right hand and the frets will be closer together making it easier for the left.

I’m sorry, @gilmo789, but this almost entirely inaccurate. Yes, most vintage tenors would have been tuned CGDA originally, but that’s CGDA a fourth ABOVE the usual GDAE, not a fifth below. So GDAE is tuning DOWN, not UP, and the strings are actually going to be less tight. Remember, the tenor banjo is tuned an octave DOWN from a fiddle, not the same octave.

Most players compensate by using thicker strings, but string tension is always a consideration as the lower tension in GDAE can result in a loss in tone and tuning problems when fretting. Which is why the majority of Irish tenor banjo players prefer *19* fret tenors over 17 fret tenors, which have a shorter reach but often don’t have the ability to get a really nice G and D string tone due to the shorter scale length.

And even if you did decide to tune it down a step, which would result in even LESS tension on the strings, the tuning would be FCGD, not FCBD.
