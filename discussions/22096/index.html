Guys, it’s not about frets or the right string. It’s about the position
of the bow in relation to the bridge. It need to be parallel through
the entire stroke and at the right distance from the bridge for the
pitch range. It takes a while to get this right; if you haven’t spent
time working on this then you may not have it right yet.

Re: Beginning Fiddler Vision Woes

Hup said, “It [the bow] needs to be … at the right distance from the bridge for the pitch range.”

There is a simple way to do this visually. Tighten the bow as normal and place the hairs flat on the A or D string (don’t tilt the bow). If you can see all of the end of the fingerboard framed between the bow hairs and the bow stick then the bow is in the right position on the string for the pitch range - which for Irish trad playing is the first position.

If you move the bow towards the bridge, which you may want to do to brighten the tone or make it louder, or if you’re playing in the higher positions (a la J Scott Skinner’s tunes!), then your view of the end of the fingerboard will start to disappear behind the bow stick.

Conversely, if you move the bowing position towards the fingerboard - which tends to give a weak wuffly tone that is really mostly intended for special effects in classical playing - then the end of the fingerboard will start to disappear from view behind the bow hairs.

“It [the bow] needs to be parallel [to the bridge] through the entire stroke …”

This is rather more difficult to monitor visually (you need a full-length mirror - or a video cam and monitor) - although the straws in the f-holes isn’t a bad idea. There are two approaches. The first, which I consider essential for the learner, is a good teacher (or an experienced player who knows how to do it) who guides the pupil through the subtle right arm, wrist, hand and finger movements that control the bow, starting with the bow hold and general playing posture, including not allowing the fiddle to drop below the horizontal.

The second approach, for the advanced player, is auto-monitoring by the feel of the bow if it drifts from parallel and by the ears noticing changes in tonal quality.

Why is having the bow parallel to the bridge important? If it isn’t parallel then it will start to slide and skate on the string towards either the bridge or the fingerboard, and the tone goes weak and “glassy”. This effect is most pronounced at the ends of the bow. If you let the fiddle droop below the horizontal then you alter the arm-bow-fiddle geometry and the bow will skate more easily. However, if you’re usually in a playing environment where you have the fiddle pointing down at an angle (very common in sessions) then perhaps you should modify your personal arm-bow-fiddle geometry to retain the optimal parallelity of the bow on the strings.

I agree with previous posters that you shouldn’t be watching your fingers on the strings - develop your hearing and finger-feel to monitor what’s happening. Use your eyes to watch other players (or audience), or to read the sheet music if you’re in that situation. If you need to look at the fiddle it should be no more than a quick check on the bow, and a sideways flick of the eyes is all that is necessary.

Re: Beginning Fiddler Vision Woes

My cello teacher told me not to look at my fingers when playing, but to use my ears. Marker strips of paper on the fingerboard were very definitely not allowed! Interestingly, he didn’t teach the “positions” as such. You know the sort of thing - a few months learning all the notes in the first position and then, on the big day you move to the second position. Months later, if you’re a good pupil, then it’s the third. And so on.

He threw that method of teaching out of the window and instead got me exploring the whole of the fingerboard from the word go, like a game. Later on, he’d get me doing exercises like relaxing my left arm by my side, closing my yes, and then he’d say something like “play the second D on the A string with your first finger”, or “play the E-flat on the G string with your third finger”. And then I’d try to hit that note with the required finger with my eyes shut. Not easy, but after a while it becomes a bit zen-like - you mentally “hear” the note and your finger goes straight to it without conscious thought.

Orchestral conductors use this method of getting their violinists to mentally “hear” a very high note they’ve got to hit without the opportunity to prepare it first. I can assure you it works.

Re: Beginning Fiddler Vision Woes

The reason for not looking at one’s fingers, left hand, etc… as it was explained to me, is that in most people, vision is the dominant sense. When one is looking at their fingers, vision is predominant, touch and hearing become less so because the dominant sense overrides the other senses. If one wants to develop muscle memory, fine tune their ears, then it would be advantageous to NOT look at one’s fingers, strings, or whatever.

Oh fer cripes sakes. Her “vision problem” is just the normal workings of binocular vision. When an object gets too close, the perspective from each eye is too different for the brain to resolve. That’s why you close one eye and just use the other, when peering at something really close up--otherwise you see double.

Re: Beginning Fiddler Vision Woes

You don’t give an age for “The Baroness” but this sounds like a case of Presbyopia. Simply put, as we age our eyes don’t like to focus up close (like reading a book or looking at violin strings). They especially don’t like to change focus rapidly like focusing on a music stand and then back to the strings and then back to the music stand, and so on…

A visit to the eye doctor can confirm this to be certain. In the interim, a set of bi-focals or just some simple reading glasses propped on the end of the nose (yes, it looks highly academic…) may be of some help.
