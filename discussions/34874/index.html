Re: Superparticular intervals survey

@ipat-e
“tune your g note to 432mhz and tune remaining strings”
I imagine “mhz” was a typo for Hz, and in that event, if the G of the fiddle were tuned to 432Hz (it’s usually 196Hz, btw) then the D, A and E would respectively be tuned to 648, 972, and 1458Hz. Bearing in mind that string tension increases dramatically as the string is tuned higher, I reckon the tension of the strings in this case would be about 4 to 5 times the norm. In the remote eventuality that such tunings could be achieved without the instrument collapsing, I just wouldn’t want to be in the same room when tuning is in progress :-)

“Atonal music has always been around”
Has it? I think the answer depends on how “atonality” is defined. The modern definition (basically “no tonal centre”) dates back to the beginning of the 20th century when the first experiments in serial music were being made, but broader definitions might include some very early music which didn’t necessarily use scales or modes quite as we we know them today.

Re: Superparticular intervals survey

And, at the risk of having my tongue too far from my cheek: one of the things I like in trad music is the way it tends toward or evokes what in twentieth-century music is called pandiatonicism: in that sense, if you stay in one diatonic scale but are free with the notes within that it *can* be the case that all notes sound sort of ok. (Unlike, say, playing the M3 in the 5th bar of a blues, as is often pointed out.) In some free-and-easy sessions, there can be a sense of that: the sense of chord progression comes through but with some “differences of opinion” (or what some would call mistakes) and also in various takes on the tune (also termed heterophony), there is the identity of the tune but sometimes blurred a bit by a wash of the scale/key/harmony.

@Mack Garner: You might be interested to know: Bernstein’s contention that the diatonic and pentatonic scales are more natural does not really hold water. IIRC, he bases it on acoustics more than physiology, but the thing is that in most musics—at least the ones he knows—the temperament is adjusted from the overtone series and this is not “natural” at all. Not to mention he focuses on Charles Ives (who composed “The Unanswered Question”), who jumbled things up quite cacophonously, even in that piece, though LB’s take on it is interesting. And according to Bernstein’s ideology (which it is), not only so-called “atonal” music but blues would be unnatural. Maybe you know all this, but while I am at it: there was such tension and discord (so to speak) at that time over atonality, and composers like LB who composed in more accessible styles were put down by the so-called establishment. I find it interesting that he endeavored, like they did, to use a sense of system (rather than of culture or invention) to legitimize his preference. (LB’s UQ has been said to be influenced by Noam Chomsky’s linguistic research.) Also of interest culturally: many of these composers writing more consonant music [another term that does not really apply] were gay men [or non-straight like LB], and there was a real division/subculture etc. (I’ll leave out for now LB’s hitting on young male students . . . ) Anyway, I like UQ too, and I groove on diatonicism too, though his presentation is part of and informed by a certain historical and cultural context—not simply “true” or false. (The book and video are based on or documenting the Norton Lectures at Harvard.)

Debussy “fooled around with”? Ow! I know what you mean—but there is some interesting harmonic shaping in his WT music. And different pentatonic scales have different characters. It’s often said that gamelan music has no harmony and is heterophonic. And while the tuning is very carefully done, there can be some variations in tuning. Dissonant? Or shimmering? :-)
