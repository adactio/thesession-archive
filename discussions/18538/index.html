Guitar Chords for Tunes in the Dorian Mode

I maintain that many Dorian mode tunes should be accompanied using just two chords (the actual chords would of course depend on the keynote of the tune in question).

For example (to cite two well-known tunes: Rakes of Kildare and Ballydesmond Polkas) Both are in the Dorian mode with the keynote of A, and I believe that the accompaniment chords should be restricted to Am and G for these particular tunes.

Anyone agree? Anyone disagree?

If you agree, can you explain to me why many guitarists at sessions don’t adopt this seemingly simplist approach to such tunes, but like to “enhance” them by using different or additional chords? To my ear, it spoils the melody when they do this. It’s even worse when there are several guitarists present, all using different chord accompaniments.

I would like to make it quite clear that I’m not against the use of guitars for ITM (or other TM). Quite the reverse, in fact - I think that a good guitarist can add body and impart “lift” to a tune set - but only if the accompaniment is appropriate.

Hi wyogal
I would agree with you that for some tunes alternate chords are appropriate. For example, for a tune in D-major, you might be able to play a D chord at one point on the first time around, and a Bm on the second. However, in my post, I was not referring to tunes in general, but specifically referring to those tunes using the Dorian scale. You say in your profile that you are classically trained, and are thus clearly totally familiar with all the standard harmonic scales (major, harmonic minor). But you also say that you are “just learning Irish style”, and so may not yet be familiar with the old melodic scales, which are common in ITM. Apologies in advance if you know this stuff already, but much Irish music is modal and thus uses different scales to the classically-taught ones. For example, the Dorian mode scale is equivalent to the white notes of the piano, starting from D. This can of course be transposed into any key, so the Dorian mode scale transposed to the key of A is thus: A B C D E F# G A. These modal scales were never intended to be harmonised, so if they are chorded by a guitarist in are in a modern setting, it needs to be sympathetic and appropriate! In default of having a DADGAD-tuned guitar, the chords that I would use for one of the examples that I gave: “The Rakes of Kildare”, would be:

|: Am Am | Am Am| G G | G G|
|AmAm | Am Am| G G| Am Am:|
|:Am Am | Am Am| G G| G G|
|Am Am| Am Am| G G | Am Am:|

Re: Guitar Chords for Tunes in the Dorian Mode

Hi chuneboi - continue to enjoy the red whine!

Regarding chords, I certainly wasn’t suggesting that a guitarist should be only using two chords during the course of a session. The ceilidh band in which I play has a repertoire of 600+ tunes. All traditional, (although not all Irish). If you were the guitarist with us, you would need to know the following chords in order to be able to play all of our material:

Major Chords: A B C D E F G

Seventh Chords: A7 B7 C7 D7 E7 F#7 G7

Minor Chords: Am Bm Dm Em F#m Gm

I make that 20 chords – double the number that you were suggesting might be needed!

Regarding modes, there are four modal scales that occur in Irish music: Ionian, Dorian, Mixolydian and Æolian (or natural minor). Ionian is exactly the same as the modern major scale, which always has semitone intervals between the third/fourth and the seventh/eighth notes of the scale (regardless of key) – the rest of the intervals in the scale being whole tones. Even the Ionian scale tunes in ITM though are sometimes quite difficult to harmonise, as they tend to progress upwards and downwards by semitones. For example, what chord do you use for an ABA triplet in a jig, without being discordant with either the A or the B?. Regarding the other three scales, they may sound “minor”, but they are not, as all they use different intervals to the ones used in the minor scale. Tunes in standard (i.e. harmonic) minor keys just don’t exist in ITM.

The following link contains an interesting discussion on this topic …

http://www.standingstones.com/modeharm.html

.. and there’s loads of other discussion about it, both within the session.org and elsewhere on the Net

Re: Guitar Chords for Tunes in the Dorian Mode

chuneboi, here’s what I was talking about with the 4ths….

take our example of D dorian…

a stack of 4ths built on D is DGCF. From D to F is a minor 10th.

so now let’s look at the 10ths in the dorian mode

D-F, E-G, F-A, G- B, A- C, B- D, and C- E

the minor 10ths exist on the 1st, 2nd, 5th, and 6th scale degrees. If you were to substitute a stack of 4ths for any one of those 10ths on those scale degrees, all of the notes in your chord would be in the mode

DGCF (1st degree)
EADG (2nd degree)
ADGC (5th degree)
BEAD (6th degree)

Furthermore, a stack of 4ths based on the other scale degrees would give you notes OUTSIDE the mode

F-Bb-Eb-Ab (3rd degree)
G-C-F-Bb (4th degree)
C-F-Bb-Eb (7th degree)

what’s more, in each of these cases you will clash with the raised sixth scale degree of dorian mode.

so the interval outlined by the stack of 4ths is how you can effectively “substitute” one for the other, and how you can figure out which scale degrees are safe in other modes.

you can take this concept further by saying that your “jumping off point” and your point of resolution when breaking down traditional harmony can be found in the same way, but those ideas are more from jazz, and not really idiomatic to Irish music.

but if you like to play 10ths in your backing, this substitution business with 4ths is worth a good ponder.

A lot of folks tend to lump minor and dorian tunes into the “Minor” category and give them the three chord approach, for example, using Em, D and C. Hi mactavish - for my answers to most of your questions, please see my recent post ( Hi Al Brown).

Regarding your “OR” statement, none of these tunes were originally intended to have any harmony. All ITM was pretty much solo playing until the 1930s, when the word “ceilidh” was used to indicate several musicians gathering to play in an ensemble. And guitars didn’t come in to it until about 1970.

I’m no “tablets of stone” traditionalist though, and sure, changes, new ideas etc. are always needed to keep the whole thing going. And I’m all in favour of having guitars at sessions - as long as they are playing appropriately, and not clashing with each other or killing the melody.

When making changes to something that’s established though, you have to be subtle. If you are, you will enhance the tradition - not detract from it. If you’re not, you’ll end up turning the whole thing into jazz, or some other different genre.

If someone says to you “A sort of a D, but it’s weird” either (a) they don’t really understand ITM , or (b) they do, but don’t think that you wouldn’t understand if they explained it properly.

True, there are some tunes in ITM that modulate (change key within the tune) and there are some that used gapped scales. But such tunes are comparatively rare. Music as a subject in general is unique, in that it spans the gap between art and science. I have been playing guitar in sessions and bands etc for eighteen
years and I used to accompany tunes in the way you say but it got so boring that I eventually got fed up with it as do most guitarists who play ITM. The most useful information I have been taught on the subject of Chord substitution is the 1for 3 or six, 2 for 4 and 5 for 7 rule. By this what I mean is if you look at your seven diatonic chords in the key of D for example ; DMaj, Em, F#m, GMaj, A7, Bm, C#m7b5, DMaj7 and use numbers for them instead such 1,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. So you can sub a D or 1 chord for F#m or Bm, a G or 4 chord for an Em and a A7 for a C#m7b5.
This works in any mode in modal music.
Once I learnt this simple rule I was able to enjoy playing guitar in ITM again and felt I was actually able to improvise with my playing a lot more and instead of painting in just Red, yellow and blue I could also use purple, green and orange.
I do believe that the tune is God however and accompaniment is there to make the tune sound good and sometimes just playing one chord or even dropping out compleatly is what best serves the tune. I think the key is to have as many tricks up your sleave as posible but to use them when appropriate rather than showing off.

Basics are good things, and basics can sound just fine, in fact some prefer it kept simple and basic, others like a backer with lots of complexities going on if kept subtle, it’s a taste issue and also how much work one wants to put into it or not.

For those who are obsessive compuslive and driven to know more, they can throw a few of the notes in here and there that are shown in the dots, leading up or down to the next chord after mastering the basic three chords. That would be considered a walk , run or lead in perhaps.

Good to possibly, for those who can read the dots and are interested, pick the tune out slowly as a melody, record it and practice backing to the recording. it’s a good way to drum in the structure of the melody. Or they can of course use the midi files, but there would possibly be more value if they learned to note out the tunes themselves slowly, and back themselves, this way they will understand what it’s all about much better, it will sink in faster. Then practice it to CDs or a tolerant melody player, who can then bring in other suggestions as to stress notes, moods and feelings that could make it all flow together and sound like real music. Accompaniment on any level really is work, just like melody playing. I have had a few people come for lessons and say they can’t play melody so they’ll try guitar, saying it’s easy, then they tend to scurry off. It’s really not so easy…simple enough to form and strum chords, but it’s the putting it together with the music and understanding the music and making it all gel that is the hard part. That’s the real work. Anyone can strum chords in a day, as any aspiring melody player can string together a few bits of a tune in a day, it’s the putting it all together, getting the flow that makes it sound like music where the real work lies.

Hmmm, I don’t see the harp as all that strongly connected to the Irish tune tradition that dominates this forum. It’s really a different branch of the vine. And the lute and viol? They both nearly died out after the High Baroque and, if they did happen to be involved with the early days of our tune tradition, their style of accompaniment would not have been strumming or sawing chords, two to the measure, but playing a continuo with moving parts constantly passing through a variety of chords.

The branch we celebrate here seems to have had a long tradition of unaccompanied playing before the pianos and guitars were invited to join the party (or did they crash?). :-)

But,… Who knows for sure? My point is that the “correctness” of simple triads, no extended or diminished chords, no sus4 chords, etc. derives from as much from conditioning as to any correctness deriveable from the tunes, themselves. If you listen to the entire history of recorded ITM, you hear the tunes played pretty much the same way, but the backing varies depending on what was “correct” in the context of the times.
