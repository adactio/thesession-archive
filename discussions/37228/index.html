In most traditional music there is an instrument which sets the sound and is to some degree (not slavishly) copied by all other instruments playing in that tradition. Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

Ha, while I was composing the following long screed people have said some of the same things. But here is the said ramble anyway:

You haven’t been at this caper for long so take heart. Here are a few thoughts that may help unlock the mysteries and help you to play with an Irish accent.

It’s really all about “the flow”. When I was at about your stage as an enthusiastic wannabe fiddle player in London several decades ago, I got a lot of encouragement, kindness and advice, some of it rather cryptic, from a great fiddle player, Danny Meehan. He would indulgently listen to me massacring a tune and give little hints, the most frequent of which was, “Keep working on the flow, Steve, the flow.”

I’m not sure I knew at the time exactly what he meant but I have learned since and I’m sure he would give you the same advice. For my taste your playing is a little too staccato - the notes don’t flow into each other enough. But even staccato can still flow more. The flow has much to do with subtle play of emphasis and note lengths. Are you listening mainly or only to box players? If so, start listening very intently to fiddle players, pipers, flute players, lilters.

And phrasing. You have to hear the tune as a tune, a melody, with its resting places - and spots where you jump up and land on the floor with both feet. You have to hear the tune as a tune and you have to make the listener hear it as a tune, a tune that is going somewhere, that is saying something, as simple as that might be in traditional music.

For that, you’ve heard it before I’m sure, but there is just no shortcut. Listen. You have to do an awful lot of listening - maybe of a kind you’re not doing yet - and exposing yourself to osmosis. Go to Ireland for the summer and soak it all up. Find good solo recordings of instruments other than your own and listen to them all the time, when you’re doing the washing up, walking the dog, waiting for the bus - until you know them by heart, every little note and nuance.

And: learn to lilt.

A couple of technical points. When you have two repeated notes, the second of which is a quarter note (crotchet) in a stressed place - as in your Gravel Walk (ea A2) - you have to do something with that long A to keep the tune flowing instead of playing it bare, which pulls us up with a jolt. It doesn’t have to be anything fancy like a treble or a short roll (although it could be) - just a little pulse with the bellows, or play A {d}AA, instead of A A2, or a little triplet: A (3AdA.

The other thing you could do is use more of the optional outer row notes (F# in this tune, C# in others) to smooth things out, like those gfg passages. And: a few grace notes please.

But keep at it, don’t give up, and “keep working on the flow.” :-)

Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

The problem is pretty easily identified - it sounds like a midi recording because the phrasing is so even. There is a jauntiness and pulse that is completely absent, and those are the elements that are most characteristic of the traditional style. If you cannot hear the difference, you can not possibly play it. If you can hear the difference, you know what I’m talking about. The two tunes you are playing characterize the best of Irish music, so listen to a few recordings of each tune and it should be apparent why your version does not sound Irish. The more you listen, the more the proper rhythm will come to you.

I’m going to take a risk of re-introducing an old argument here, but you must play reels in cut time (2/2), not in 4/4. If you play in 4/4, you will get exactly the sound you have now and be bewildered as to why it doesn’t sound Irish. If you have a good enough ear, you will eventually get the right sound simply because your ear will finally lead you to it, but if you continue to play in strict 4/4, you will never get it right. There are two pulses in cut time, whereas there are four in 4/4. Even if you add accents, counting in four will always lead you towards either a choppy sound or one where the notes have such even emphasis that there is no lift to it at all.

Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

Let me add a few points in anticipation to the debate that may ensue: I do not believe anyone would play like the OP without prior musical training. Someone just starting out by ear would simply imitate what they hear. I think the OP is trying to reconcile what he hears against what his musical training tells him. This assumes he has worked from sheet music improperly written in 4/4. He is trying to play as written and is not getting what he hears others do and cannot understand why.

Bear in mind that a person who plays a reel in cut time (having never heard a reel before) will not sound very Irish (more likely Baroque), but he or she will come much closer to the mark than they would playing in 4/4.

One last point: A person can appear to be tapping their foot in 4/4 for either a reel or a jig, but they are simply tapping their foot; they are not counting measures. They are playing memorized music in a genre they know, so why count? But mentally, they are putting the pulse where it belongs because they know how it should sound. In this case, tapping the foot is more about maintaining the tempo than it is about rhythm or time.

Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

An afterthought: Boyen, you might find it easier to concentrate on the lift and the Irishness if you spent time learning to get the most out of ordinary, basic, common-or-garden tunes instead of “big” tunes such as the two you posted here, and technically challenging pieces like Music for a FH which I have heard you tackle.

If you can make Wind that Shakes the Barley, Rolling in the Ryegrass, Dunmore Lasses or the Boys of Bluehill and other simpler tunes sound Irish, you’ll have a good chance with Boys of Malin and the like. If you can’t, well you shouldn’t tackle these more demanding and complex tunes.

In the book of tunes by the late Chris Langan (piper, pipemaker and teacher), he points out what he saw as a regrettable phenomenon - of learners wanting to run with all the big and cool tunes before they had learned to walk with the simple old ones, getting them “under the fingers” - i.e. mastering the style. Those simple tunes are an ideal vehicle for learning phrasing, ornamentation and variation.

When you do go to Ireland to soak it all up, and some point you will find yourself in the company or the house of a traditional musician, who will say to you, “Play us a tune.” They will respect you far more if you play something like The Boyne Hunt nicely and simply, with lift, than if you attempt Jenny’s Welcome to Charlie or Road to Errogie and don’t quite pull it off.

Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

Boyen, speaking as someone who is just coming up to one year on the accordion I think you are doing great. I’ve come to it from having played the fiddle for many years but I’ve found the box to be really tough to play with any sort of fluency in the early days.

Anyway, to this point:

“… it’s really hard to get all the notes in and still have this rhythm with the correct emphasis …”

The stock answer is to slow down, but you can only take this so far as you seem to recognize:

“… and preferably also at a certain speed.”

Kevin Burke in his workshops makes the point that if you slow down, for example, a reel too much then it ceases to be a reel. That doesn’t matter if you are just finding the notes and getting them up to speed but it does matter when you are working on the “feel”. For a reel I would say the limit is probably about 92bpm but you can make up your own mind on that. Now, if you are struggling to play the whole tune at that speed then Kevin’s solution is to break it down into chunks.

So you might have a process that goes something like this:

1. Find a recording of The Boys of Malin that you would like to emulate. Preferably a solo performance with or without a backer, but it doesn’t need to be a box player.

2. Use a slowdowner to isolate the first two bars of the tune and bring the speed down accordingly.

3. Play the recording of those two bars.

4. Play those two bars yourself trying to make it sound as close as you can get it.

5. Repeat 3 and 4 ad nauseam.

6. Then try the next two bars.

Hope that helps.

Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

Stepping back from philosophy, and setting aside the staccato/legato question, which is probably worthy of its own thread, and completely disregarding the 2/2 vs. 4/4 red herring, I’d like to make two points about your playing, Boyen. First, the places where you stumble seem frequently to be places where you rush the beat. I’m not sure whether the rushing is a product of nervousness in knowing you’re coming to a tricky spot, or whether the rushing is causing the stumbles, but either way it’s something to be aware of.
Secondly, on the subject of phrasing: I don’t think I’ve ever seen anyone here discuss this, at least not in these terms-the issue of “inner” and “outer” phrasing. This is a subject I became acutely aware of when studying the music of J.S. Bach. In Bach’s music, you frequently find long phrases of two or even four bars of running 16th notes. However within each phrase are groups of notes, usually from two to five, which divide the long phrase up. If these inner phrases are ignored, the music has the quality of a run-on sentence. If the inner phrases are too regular in length, the playing sounds square and equally monotonous. Irish dance tunes have a similar construction- the outer phrases give the call and response structure while the inner phrases, especially in reels, create accents in unpredictable places, which impart lift and musical interest. In your playing, Boyen, the inner phrases tend all to be four notes long, which makes it hard to impart the constantly shifting accents so important to the music. Try switching this up a bit and see what happens.

“the places where you stumble seem frequently to be places where you rush the beat. I’m not sure whether the rushing is a product of nervousness in knowing you’re coming to a tricky spot, or whether the rushing is causing the stumbles”

It’s very common with people learning to speed up on the easy bits and slow down on the difficult bits. Human nature, I guess.

Whether it’s a matter of getting good enough on the instrument so that there are no difficult bits, or having a rock-steady sense of rhythm to force the difficult bits through at the same tempo as everything else, but playing with other musicians and playing for dancers both requires steady rhythm.

For me, and for many, what helps tremendously is to practice with a metronome. When I do so my technique improves because all phrases, the easy and the difficult, are played exactly the same. Also my “flow” improves greatly.

About suggestions to learn steady timing by playing for dancers, the dancers would probably appreciate it if you learned steady timing at home before you play for them. But yes, if one’s timing is already good enough to provide a steady beat for the dancers it will only get better when you spend a lot of time playing for them.

About phrasing, I notice that on many early flute recordings the phrasing is blocky, each phrase having a clear ending before going onto the next, the opposite of the smooth flow going over the phrase-endings which one usually hears today. I’m not saying one is better than the other, just different.

Setting aside philosophy for a while, here’s an exercise I enjoy. First you need some kind of an electronic device with abundant drum beats programmed in…I use Garage Band or the rhythm tracks on an electronic keyboard.
Then I pick a tune and play it over as many rhythms as I can or pick a beat and play as several tunes over it.

What do I learn…maybe nothing at all about Irish music specifically. I do have a LOT of fun with it and after a while I found something about how rhythm and melody work together. Approach, attack, phrasing, ornamentation all find new ways to be expressed. Try the Kerfunten Shuffle, the Wind That Blows the Barley Street Beat. It’s almost as much as it is to play reels as jigs and vise-versa. My theory is that the better I get at relating a melody to a rhythm the better I get at playing an Irish melody to an Irish rhythm. The exercise is probably best done alone to avoid the dread “eye-roll”, but it is a good way to hold off the inevitable boredom that comes from playing too much alone.

Try it, the worst that can happen is you won’t like it and then you can stop. Otherwise…enjoy.

This is fascinating. Do you do things like pick a reel, and play it over not only 4/4 and 2/4 beats but also 3/4 and 6/8?

This is very common, a cliché in fact, in Highland piping. They love to take a jig and play it in 4/4, or take a reel and play it as a waltz, or what have you.

There are many Scottish songs and slow instrumental pieces which are commonly played both in 3/4 and 4/4, and one gets quite used to shifting any tune back and forth. In Irish music the tune that comes to mind is Star Of The County Down, commonly heard in waltz time and in 4/4.

Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

>> “inner” and “outer” phrasing.

That’s an interesting turn of phrase. I sometimes talk when I’m teaching about “fractal” rhythmic balance. Take the first beat of a 2/4 march. The notes in it have to “balance” each other, but they have a slight “lean” to them, which is picked up by the next beat. But that whole bar also needs to be balanced, which is what happens with the next bar. Then you build up the 2-bar phrases, which depend on each other, through to entire parts of the tune.

Re: You know the notes, but it ain’t Irish

These comments are all well and good, but the only remedy I’ve discovered for music that doesn’t sound quite right is to do as much listening as possible and then as much imitating as possible. Find a player or several that you like, listen as extensively as possible to his or her playing, and then practice with that sound in mind, preferably with the recording right next to you so that you can get as much of the sound in your head as possible.

The other part to this is that you need to be strict with yourself on your tempo and rhythm. Dance music is built on these things. Get a metronome. One of the biggest mistakes I made as a more novice player was neglecting the metronome in favor of simply imitating what I heard, and as a result it took me much longer to achieve a solid and idiomatic rhythm to my playing.

Peter Carberry is a box player with a lovely simple style that could serve as a stepping point if you don’t have anything else in mind. Best of luck to you with your playing!

That’s because you don’t understand your own playing well enough to be able to explain to someone else why their playing is not the same as yours. Which in fairness is the same problem most traditional musicians in most oral traditions have.

Your determination that I don’t understand what I’m doing aside, can you honestly say that listening is not an important part of the learning process for any music? A strict mathematical delineation of how long to play each nother can be helpful to some, but can’t even begin to give an accurate portrait of specifically how music is to be played, even for western classical or ghb which are both very reliant on sheet music.

A more novice musician asking for advice saying “I know it isn’t right but I don’t know why” can often be remedied simply by creating a more full concept of what sound he wants to achieve, which can only be done by listening. The suggestion that listening and determining on your
own what sounds you want to recreate is a lower form of learning as opposed to being taught one specific rhythmic pattern for each type of tune is rubbish.

Take a look at western classical music for instance, where a piece will have markings for interpretation to aid the performer more accurately recreate the composer’s intended sound. These marking are never “play the first eight note in the group longer than the rest” or “slightly accent the end of the note to create the feeling of frenzied movement.” They will simply say cantabile, or vigorously, or with movement or any other number of things, because every player is going to approach them differently. Everyone has different methods of teaching and likewise learns in different ways. My having a philosophy of learning and teaching that differs from yours hardly does not invalidate either.

My thoughts exactly. Maybe you’re no John Williams but for a year and a half you’re playing ITM better than most with that much experience! I’d say you just need to work on smoothing it out and then learning the phrasing. You do need to learn some basic music theory. Invest in some theory lessons or a basic theory book. Understanding the mathematics of music, and the chord progression and how each cord fits in the tunes will help you play better. Don’t be intimidated, it’s not rocket science.

Also learn the phrasing. Phrasing is something that’s difficult to teach with written words, but… To start with, think of the phrasing as a question and answer session. Your “basic” ITM dance tune is in two 8-measure parts (A and B), with each part itself divided into two sub-parts. Each sub-part has a question in the first two measures and an answer in the next two. So each part has two 4-measure question/answers. Often one answer of the A part is written the same as an answer in the B part, but don’t hesitate to change things a bit, with ITM the notes are not written in stone. Once you understand the way a “typical” ITM tune is constructed, you can begin to understand the phrasing. Now mentally (or physically with a pencil) draw a line through each note in a sub-part, sort of a connect the dots thing. See how that line goes somewhere? It flows gently up and down, or jumps up and down in jagged peaks and valleys. That line has a beginning, middle, and final destination and you need to play it that way. Note the “high point” in each phrase or literally the highest note on the staff. You want to emphasize that note in some way, make it the most prominent. With instruments with a wide dynamic range, you can accent that note; with instruments with little or no dynamic range you can lengthen that time on that note (making up for it by shortening the following note). The last, or destination note of the answer phrase, is another one that sometimes need emphasis, just go with what sounds and feels good to you.

Also, there are a few spots where it sounds like you are trying to perform beyond your technical abilities. You certainly want to do that in practice, push yourself to get stronger and better. But when performing and recording choose the tempo and level of simplicity that allows you to play it well. (As I like to say, people like to hear tunes played fast, but not half-fast!)

I hope this helps.
