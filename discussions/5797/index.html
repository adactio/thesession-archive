Re: Ethnomusicology - what is it?

I don’t see ethnomusicology as useless. I think of it as a *modifier* to a base set of skills and therefore a potential differentiator. It’s a specialization when done at the Masters level and therefore I don’t think of it in isolation. So, for example, if I were a school principal looking to hire a music teacher, and I had two candidates with Masters - one generic masters of music ed and one with masters in ethnom., I would greatly favor the latter. Think of all the contributions that such an individual could bring to multiple classes. When schools teach history to kids, it’s great to have the art teacher engage in relevant activities. Why not music? When kids learn geography - wouldn’t it be great to have the music teacher relate, talk about and teach about regional music? I love interdisciplinary studies for these reasons. Granted - the application is not always obvious, and you won’t see many job ads with the right wording, but the satisfaction is grand when you can show people how much you can contribute and have them light up.

There are other work opportunities for ethnomusicologists. This was just one example.

Re: Ethnomusicology - what is it?

Poor tiny. (Sorry guys, I had to skim the tail end of the debate there). I don’t know who you should talk to, other than an ethnomusicoligist. There aren’t very many of them around, and there aren’t many universities (in Canada) that have the program.

“Yes”, you take classes, “yes” you do research, and “yes” you play music. A pretty big chunk of your classes would be about anthropology, though. A huge chunk, in fact, because your “job” as an ethnomusicologist would be to research and try to understand the influence a culture’s history and ethos influence the way they express themselves musically.

You will end up being an “expert” in a music-culture relationship of your choice and be able to have your opinions taken seriously on most music-culture realtionships on which you are *not* and expert.

Beware of deciding to go into the field because you have a passion for ITM and everything about it though. The further you advance in the academic system, the greater will be the pressure to find an “original” field of study or perspective. You’re more likely to research the influence the mating call of a llama has on the music of Tuvian throat singers for your thesis than you are to end up in a cozy Donegal pub with a Guinness in one hand and a dictophone in the other.

It’s also widely considered by anthropologists to be next to impossible to be objective about your own culture.

Let me put it this way: If you were born and raised in Ireland, you have a passion for Irish music and you want to know everything there is to know about it, then you’re just an ordinary Irish person. If you have a driving desire to know everything there is to know about Latvia, the people that live there, and especially the music they play, you are an “ethnomusicologist.”

According to the British Forum for Ethnomusicology (http://www.shef.ac.uk/music/staff/js/BFE.html):

“Ethnomusicologists seek to understand the human processes within which music is imagined, discussed and made, and to relate specific musical sounds, behaviours and ideas to their broader social, cultural and political contexts. Re: Ethnomusicology - what is it?

It is becoming increasingly common for anthropologists, ethnologists, folklorists and ethnomusicologists to study their own cultures. Obviously, studying other related and contrasting cultures will help them with this.

Mick Maloney is but one case in point. In Scotland, another would be Margaret Bennett (mother of the late Martin Bennett and a wonderful Gaelic singer who came from Skye).

I can understand the feeling that such pursuits are useless, but without them quite a few traditional songs, tunes and dances which we now take for granted as part of the tradition (again) would have been lost were it not for traditional music revivalists of the 50s, 60s and 70s trawling the collections made by people in these disciplines.

In a perfect world these disciplines might be unnecessary. We would all value and preserve our cultural heritage and would not need anyone to archive or interpret it. But this isn’t a perfect world.

Ethnomusicology Test

I’m a late coming to this discussion, but I just couldn’t resist. Here’s the deal: If you can make it the end of this post, and are not bored out of your mind, you know that there’s at least a possibility of your being interested in it as an academic specialty. (That’s my excuse for the length. That, and the fact that it’s not often an opportunity to talk about such topics comes along. ) OK? Here goes.

If your primary goal is to be a performer, even a professional performer, of ITM, it’s certainly not necessary to have a degree in anything. There is a growing number of professional ITM players who do have advanced degrees in some aspect of Irish history, ethnomusicology, or folklore, including Grainne Hambly, Maire ni Chathasaigh, and Matt Cranitch, but it is still the exception rather than the rule, and most all of them that I am aware of were all professional-quality players before entering academia.

The Masters in traditional music is a performance program, so it’s obviously not the same thing. It might be a great experience for some people, but I haven’t been that impressed with what I’ve heard about it from the people who’ve come through it. If you come in a fabulous player, that’s how you’ll leave; if someone else is a decent player, that’s how they’ll leave. The reason for this is that the discipline of teaching Irish music, as opposed to performing, has really not been developed. In the past, ITM was transmitted, not taught. But this has been discussed elsewhere.

In my experience a lot of people seem to want to study ITM in college because they just love it, and want to do it all the time, but they (or their parents) have always assumed that they will to go to college. They figure, if they have to study something, and ITM is available as a course of study somewhere in some way, shape, or form, that’s for them. Once they do some research and find out how it all works, most probably do something else, while a few dive in and never look back. Traditional musicians who do end up doing something related to ITM in college choose many different paths, as well, including language, history, cultural anthropology, literature, ethnomusicology, interdisciplinary programs with names like Irish Studies, Celtic Studies, and Celtic Civilization, and a host of other options. But if you’re going to commit to an academic program like that, it should be because that’s really what you’re interested in, as others have said. It shouldn’t be your second choice.

I know of several very highly regarded young ITM players, people who already have several All-Ireland wins to their credit, who go to regular conservatories that understand and support their goals and interests in ITM to one extent or another; one studies music composition, one studies music theory, and still another is a jazz major, with the low whistle as the primary instrument. If a conventional school is willing to work with you and be receptive to your goals, and you feel that they could help you grow as a musician and give you the skills you want, that can definitely be an option. There definitely seems to be a trend among my generation of musicians in particular to want to pursue regular western music training as part of our education. But if you’re not already a world-class player, there doesn’t seem to be any college program in existence that can train you to be one. It all gets back to teaching, and philosophies of teaching…

But more on that later; I promise, I’m going somewhere with this. First of all, there is definitely a difference between musicology and ethnomusicology. Musicology encompasses a range of topics and approaches, from music history to theory to performance practice, and musicologists may specialize in the music of a particular genre, period, and/or composer. There are a million directions that one can go in, from comparing the function of sonata form in the works of Haydn and Liszt to theorizing that the aesthetic of twentieth-century music--serialism, atonal music, and mathematical perfection as beauty—is actually behind the entire modern trend towards a rigid interpretation of performance practice, not only in what is typically thought of as “early” music, but also for classicism and even the Romantic era. (When I read that article, I thought, you could totally make the same argument about performance practice in Irish music…but that’s another thread.)

This is all real, honest-to-goodness geek stuff, in other words. I find it totally fascinating and exciting, and I could probably even explain why. But if you are not totally fascinated and excited by stuff like this, without me trying to explain why, you should be looking at another career. If you are not waiting with baited breath to find out how the contents of that article could possibly relate to Irish music, then you’ve got all the information you need to know it’s not for you.

Ethnomusicology gets more into the realm of cultural anthropology, which is a social science. However, this is a blurry line, complicated by the evolution of a new branch of musicology, called the New Musicology, where culture and cultural influences on music, particularly politics and pop culture, are explored at length. An example would be Susan McClary’s work; her most famous book is probably Feminine Endings: Music, Gender, and Sexuality. Additionally, performance practice is considered a branch of musicology, but it is obviously influenced by social and cultural factors, so it’s another case of the line being blurred.

In the past, the activity most associated with ethnomusicologists is probably going out and collecting and transcribing music. Bartok and Kodaly were some of the first to do this, with the music of various eastern European cultures. Seamus Ennis is one of many who did a similar thing in Ireland, traveling around collecting tunes and songs. Nowadays, there is almost no musicology being done on Irish music; it’s all ethnomusicology. In fact, at least one of the best-known scholars of Irish music is in fact a PhD in cultural anthropology, not music (which sometimes leads to some interesting (mis)uses of technical musical terms that they insist on using.)

Maybe this will help illustrate the difference. Analyzing and investigating the music itself or its performance in a specific, quantifiable way is musicology. The method of note-frequency analysis and theories of alternative modality described in Tomas O Canainn’s book, attributed to Sean O’Riada, falls into the category of musicology. On the other hand, social dynamics of sessions as musical communities seems to be a popular topic amongst ethnomusicologists in the last couple of years. My tuning survey is closer to ethnomusicology; my rhythm analysis is musicology.

In general, I’m not nearly as interested in the stuff that most ethnomusicologists pursue as musicological research on ITM, but somebody must be, as there is a heck of a lot of it out there. My personal suspicion is that ideas that are collectively ingrained in much of the ITM community make it almost taboo to study the music itself seriously and at any length. The reason there’s no functional musicology of ITM is the same reason that Limerick is not turning out fabulous players in droves. It’s that there is a fear of analyzing this music, persistence in believing that it’s impossible, that, for example, the lift or the swing is magical and ethereal, and that it is dangerous to the tradition to believe otherwise. I’ve already addressed this as it relates to teaching and learning music here:

https://thesession.org/discussions/5608#comment119713

As I said before, the point for me is that this music is beautiful, and I want to understand it, and I want to share that with people. I enjoy investigating it, especially because so little has really been done on this. I like the challenge of figuring out what makes it tick, what makes it work, since it’s extremely simple and extremely complex at the same time. It can be a source for dialogue among musicians about what we are doing and why, dialogue that has real practical significance for how the music is played, taught, heard, and enjoyed. And it can significantly impact people’s approach to music making, making it clearer and simpler than they thought possible, because it’s based on a really trying to understand.

So what’s the problem? It’s related to the question of whether people can be objective about their own culture. That can certainly be hard, and is part of it, but one doesn’t have to be a member of a particular culture or ethnic group to have an agenda that they want to push motivating their research—in other words, they already know the results before they do anything. Like many other things, the converts are some of the truest believers. Regardless, there is less interest in real research, and more in propaganda. This starts at the top, in the highest echelons of academia and Comhaltas, and is conveniently self-propagating: many people who buy the agenda believe they know the “truth” about ITM, and become passionately dedicated to spreading it, becoming propagandists in their own right, no research required. There are a lot of interesting and even controversial topics directly concerning the music itself, how it is played and taught and why, that I would like to see addressed. Very few people, whether in academia, performance, or pedagogy, want to address them, because they’re afraid of the answers. Maybe I’ll make up a little informal survey of my own and see what different takes people here have on some of these, and release the results. That would give a much better explanation of what’s involved here than I seem to be able to now. J

Finally, when I was looking for the thread that I linked to above, I noticed some comments on another thread I passed through in the process that I had missed.

https://thesession.org/discussions/5552#comment117458#comment117458

It’s funny, I never made the connection that my comments could be chalked up to a vested commercial interest in teaching. True, that’s how I make my living, but I never really planned it that way—it just happened. I haven’t really had time to take any more students since a year or two ago, but I have a terrible time saying no. It’s not the money that motivates me at all—that’s just the factor I use to convince my partner that I really do have time to take one more. J My classical teacher thinks I don’t charge nearly enough, anyway. But some of my best students, really incredibly talented people, are far from wealthy. They don’t own their own instruments and they pay what they can afford for lessons. Seeing them succeed is the best payment I could ever have, even though that’s not what pays the rent.

Anyway, the reason I have to be forcibly restrained from taking everyone who ever contacts me is the same exact thing I mentioned above; as Maire ni Chathasaigh says, “I have always felt a mission to explain Irish music, not just to non-Irish people, but also to other Irish people. Given half a chance, I will expound my theories to anybody…I try to explain why Irish musicians play the way they do and what the actual aesthetic of the music is. A lot of people involved in Irish music play, but they can’t really explain why they do what they do, very well. So, I feel it is necessary to explain. If somebody asks me I simply launch into it.” (http://www.alternatemusicpress.com/features/maire_ni_chathasaigh.html)

(It’s probably obvious by now that Ms. Ni Chathasaigh has been an enormous influence on my music and my way of thinking about the music, and this sort of approach to teaching is part of that.)

Ironically, my whole goal as a teacher is to make it simple, because once you know it, it is simple. When someone who knows explains it, it becomes simple to someone else. If everyone who ever learns it has to re-invent the wheel, it’s not simple. I’ve often wondered if there is a desire to keep the “secrets” under wraps, or a feeling that people should have to learn it the hard way to really earn it, or something, behind some people’s insistence that so much of this is ephemeral and can’t be taught. Of course the majority who believe this probably believe it as a result of the self-propagating propaganda I mentioned earlier, nothing more. However, where do the ideas come from in the first place? Whether it’s simple but sincere misunderstanding, a product of the general trends in 20th century performance practice that I alluded to way back up there, or something more isolationist and sinister, it’s an enormous presence in academia, performance, and most of all, the world of amateur music. And it will certainly continue to be. In my opinion though, there absolutely has to be room for more.

OK. So, if you made it this far and are still interested, congratulations, you might just want to be a musicologist when you grow up. If you quit reading a long time ago, you can be fairly assured that the answer is no…
