Piper’s Grip – used at what whistle pitch?

I’ve got the standard high whistles: D, C & Bb. Because I mainly play by solo, and enjoy the pitch of lower whistles, I play my Bb whistle most often. I’d like to purchase an A, G or F whistle next. From what I’ve read whistles can be broken down by range: The A whistle some characterize as a high whistle (lowest of the high whistles), while others characterize it as a low whistle. The G whistle is almost universally recognized as the highest of the low whistles. Classified in recorder-like fashion A, G and F whistles have been referred to as alto, mezzo soprano, or middle voice whistles; for that matter Bb is also thought of in similar terms. I enjoy the pitch of these mid-range instruments – not too high / not too low.

Question 1: At what pitch (Bb, A or G) do most whistle players begin to use the piper’s grip? I imagine this has more to do with one’s finger span / hand anatomy than it does the pitch of the whistle.

Question 2: a) Do few whistlers use a piper’s grip when playing a Bb whistle? b) Do most whistlers use a piper’s grip to play A or G whistles?

In advance, thanks for sharing your knowledge!

I use a piper’s grip to play all of my whistles in any key, but I’m a piper. It’s more comfortable and quicker than using finger tips, regardless of the key. The only difference for me between the different keys is which pad on which finger seems to lay the easiest on which hole - on the higher keys I will use mostly the pads of the first joints, and as the keys get lower and the whistles longer I will shift some to the pad of the middle joint.

Re: Piper’s Grip – used at what whistle pitch?

The rationale for “piper’s grip” is not that it enables unwieldy instruments to be played. Its selling point is that it allows all the movement to come from the base joint of the finger, which improves articulation speed.

The tradeoff is that the pads of the fingers are a lot less sensitive than the tips, and so newcomers to this style of hold can struggle to learn to cover holes effectively, which can lead to other problems like an over-tight grip. This isn’t helped when you first try to learn it on an instrument with a wide spread.

Re: Piper’s Grip – used at what whistle pitch?

First I should point out that the term “piper’s grip” can be used by different people to refer to different things, and may or may not be the same grip as somebody uses on pipes.

To be specific, each finger has three soft pads on the bottom side.

I play Highland pipes and Uilleann pipes and on both of those my lower-hand grip uses the middle pads of fingers 1, 2, and 3 and the end-pad of finger 4. I have never used this grip on low whistles; on them I use the middle pads of fingers 1 and 2 and the end-pad of finger 3.

On both Highland pipes and Uilleann pipes my upper-hand grip uses the end-pads of fingers 1, 2, and 3. I know that this is not correct Uilleann fingering, but I came to the Uilleann pipe from the Highland pipes and I (incorrectly) transferred my grip.

Most Uilleann pipers, I think, use the middle-pads of fingers 1 and 2 and the end-pad of finger 3 on their upper hands. This is what I use on Low Whistles and I suspect that most Uilleann pipers do too. So, this upperhand grip can accurately be called a "piper’s grip’.

Anyhow I don’t think there’s a hard-and-fast rule about which key whistles use what grips. I play little whistles using the end-pads of all fingers and use the Low Whistle “piper’s grip” on Low D, Low Eb, and Low E, and perhaps on Low F.

Re: Piper’s Grip – used at what whistle pitch?

I’m with you, I prefer the holes in-line, and I’ve not rotated the Hole 6 on my Burkes.

What I would like (though it may sound odd) is to have a joint like that between Hole 3 and Hole 4, in other words to be able to have each hand at its own angle.

About Reyburns v other makers in regards fingerhole spacing, it’s very strange, because one Reyburn Low D I had had the three upper-hand holes more spread out that usual, while another Reyburn Low D had the three upper-hand holes a tad more closely spaced than usual.

Comparing a number of makes of Low D, it was interesting to see various makers have different approaches to hole-spacing (all of them being tubes of more or less the same diameter) with one maker having the closest spacing of the group on one hand but the widest spacing of the group on the other hand. You would think that a maker would have an approach/theory to hole-spacing that they would apply equally to both hands.

Of course the more widely spaced the holes the closer they are to the acoustically correct places leading to more uniform hole sizes (the Boehm flute being the ultimate) and the more narrowly spaced the holes the further they are from the acoustically correct places leading to more variety in hole sizes.

With Low Whistles it’s always a compromise between the demands of acoustics and the limitations of the human hand.

To An Draighean : who wrote “I use a piper’s grip to play all of my whistles in any key…”

At what key whistle do you personally begin to shift from playing with the pads of your first joints to using pads of your middle joints? I Imagine with higher key whistles the fingers of both of your hands are pretty much at a 90 degree angle to the vertical whistle pipe. The lower the whistle key the more your hands are slanted downward at an approximate 45 degree angle.

The piper’s grip that I’m most familiar with is: top hand - fingers 1 & 2 - pads of middle joint, finger 3 - pad of 1st joint, bottom hand –fingers 4 & 5 – pads of middle joint, finger 6 – pad of 1st joint.

…though I have heard of variations on this theme.
