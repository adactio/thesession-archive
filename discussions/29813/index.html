You’re coming on nicely, Jerone.
The main criticism I would offer is that you are still tending to bow every note seperately, most noticeably in the reels and the air. In reels it is usual to have a strong down bow on the third beat of the four, whereas you don’t seem to have any particular direction, and often end on an up-bow.

In the air, you never used the frog half of the bow at all. Try this for an exercise:- play it again, but start right at the frog. Let the weight of the bow work for you, and play until you reach the middle or before, then go right back to the frog. Do it again using all of the bow. Think of the bow as a providing a continuous flow of energy, like a wheel, or a reservoir of sound to draw on, rather than a hammer to strike out each note. If you want to add colour you can vary the ‘amount’ of energy by carefully changing the pressure and speed, but it might be better to concentrate mostly on getting a long, steady note over the whole length
.
Well done, mate. I look forward to your first cd.

Good stuff Jerone. Definitely slow down. As Michael says, half that speed will do nicely.

Don’t be put off playing bowed triplets in jigs. They’re a lot of fun, and they do have a place. Not everybody plays them a lot, but it’s a stylistic thing. Listen to north Connaught and Ulster fiddlers to get a sense of crisp bowed triplets, and Munster fiddlers for the typically more scrunched up, less clearly articulated triplets, that can be played in jigs. But it comes down to having space and freedom to move inside the tune, and you can’t achieve that without pace, and pace first comes from playing, you got it, at a nice steady speed…

Jerone, sounding good!

Ditto Michael’s comments, especially about slowing way down and finding the groove.

Do check out Portal Irish Music Week in Arizona this coming October. http://portalmusicweek.com/

And also the O’Flaherty’s camp in Texas a week after Portal.

Another idea that might help is to loosen up your wrist and hand. Right now, nearly all of your bow motion is coming from your arm, opening and closing at the elbow. That’s fine for long bows, but for shorter bow strokes you’ll get a much more relaxed, fluid feel and sound by leaving your elbow quiet and moving your hand and fingers instead.

The easiest way I know to understand this is to lay your forearm on a table or countertop with your bow hand dangling over the edge. Then play a bunch of slow short bow strokes by simply letting your hand drop and raise from the wrist. Don’t worry if it sounds scratchy or thin--the idea is to focus on how it *feels* to move from the hand rather than the elbow. Eventually, you want to move the motion down into just your thumb and index finger, “as close to the stick” as possible (as Brian Conway says).

Keep on keeping on!

Re: My progress - 1 year later

“One main issue that I do have, is I get really clumsy when I slow down … So I’m more comfortable playing really fast and don’t feel clumsy”

This is not right. What is actually happening is that you are no more or less clumsy when you play fast or slow … it’s just that you notice it more when you play slower. It’s a common problem. When you play fast, the clumsy bits go by so quickly you may not notice them as much, think about it.

I think you have this feeling that playing tunes is like riding a bike … where you wobble about a lot when you try to ride as slow as you can, but when you speed up, the forward momentum makes it much easier to balance. The forward momentum irons out the wobble.

But this is not case. It’s not like riding a bike at all. Think of it more like a tongue twister:

“Sinful Caesar sipped his snifter, seized his knees and sneezed”

If you say it slowly not only can you articulate the phrase and give the words meaning, you are also much less likely to make a mistake. But you can only do this if you are already an accomplished talker of course. If you are not an accomplished talker you’ll struggle with it at any speed, but when you try it fast it can develop a sort of rhythm of its own and although wrong, is much more fun.

I just tried this experiment with my 4-year old and he did exactly that. He could say each individual word by repeating them after me, but lost it completely when attempting the whole phrase, even very slowly. (Interestingly, he was much better at the “seized his knees and sneezed” because he already knew those words, but he really struggled with “sipped his snifter”, because, I’m surmising, he didn’t understand the words.) And when he tried it fast it was less accurate, but he enjoyed it and it made him laugh. He was comfortable.

So … the important point. When you learn to ride a bike, it’s counterintuitive to imagine that it’s easier if you go faster. But when you learn a tongue twister, you’ll never in a million years be able to articulate it correctly with meaning at speed until you can master it slowly.

There’s just one small but very important thing in your bowing. Not so much that it’s not parallel to the bridge (many players don’t bother too much about this), but it’s the change of angle on each bow change. Because the bowing is often not maintaining a straight line on each direction change, you are missing the bite on the first note after each change of direction. Not every single time, but often enough for it to be noticeable.

That aside, well done! … and I’m sure you will continue to do well :)
