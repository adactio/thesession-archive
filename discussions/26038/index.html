Strange Songs in Repertoires

Strange probably being a bad word, but what is your opinion about sessions or Trad bands having non-Irish or even non-European Folk derived songs in their repertoires? Folked up Rock? Tradded up Soul?

Danu recorded a Bob Dylan song and Planxty did a Norman Blake, but is there room for more experimental stuff (Frankie Gavin’s Bohemian Rhapsody not withstanding) or is even that mild cross-folk pollination pushing it?

Should a session or Trad band have only ‘those’ types of songs (if they have songs) and none others? What do you like? Mind a few Tradded or Folked up odd song choices?

Re: Strange Songs in Repertoires

A good song’s a good song. Gaughan sings Phil Ochs and Si Kahn, and it sounds great - as I said the other day, the great thing about No More Forever (and the coal miners’ benefit album as well, the live one) is that when he sings a song, it doesn’t sound ike a “new” song or an “old” song - it’s just a song. The John MacLean March goes right with the Three Healths, it’s all one thing.

Thje main thing that makes a song work, for me, is honesty. A singer should pick songs that they think are great and that they can inhabit entirely, and sing those the way they hear them. A band should pick a singer whose songs and singing suit the band. There’s not much worse than hearing someone try to sing a song because they think people will like it, when they don’t feel it themselves, or hearing someone try to doctor up a song into sounding the way they think their audience will prefer it instead of just singing it as they hear it.
But when the singer sings it as it’s meant to be - for that singer - and the band supports the singer well or gets out of the way, then it’s probably going to be pretty great, or at least a little great.

Re: Strange Songs in Repertoires

I have to agree with Jon; a good song is a good song simple as that. Also, every singer is going to have a different interpretation of a song based on their own musical tastes. If someone who has a background in trad wants to sing a Dylan song or whatever it is going to be influenced by their trad background and have elements from that idiom.

The idea of ‘cross-folk’ is a little bit ridiculous. Many songs in the American folk tradition were inspired by Irish and British (meaning England, Scotland, Wales, and NI) songs. The whole Old-Time tradition came out of Ulster emigrants to Appalachia who brought along their fiddles. Then in during the 60s folk revolution many people who we would consider the best of the Irish and British folk singers (i.e. Andy Irvine, Luke Kelly, and Christy Moore) were inspired by American folk musicians to get into folk music. Andy cites Woody Guthrie as one of his biggest inspirations. After that they were inspired to look into the music from their own countries. The music from America, Ireland, and Britain have been influencing each other for years.

Personally, I wouldn’t be a fan of most pop music but hey if a person wants to sing a version of a pop song at a session who am I to stop them? As long as they have respect for the other musicians playing I don’t see a problem.

At the end of the day it is all music and if it sounds good why should it matter what genre it is part of?

I am an old 70year old retired singer, and although I did sing a lot of traditional Irish songs, usually of the humorous variety, I used to throw in some modern songs like: Eric Bogle’s “Silly Slang Song” & Bill Watkins Ballad that was put to music by Andy M Stewart & Gerry O‘Beirne called the “The Young Apprentice”. I also used to sing songs from that great family of song writer’s that of “The Sands’ Family”, and that of the Fermanagh song writer living in Listowel, Kerry, named Mickey McConnell. I found if I could bring a laugh to a session, it helped others that were listening a sense of fulfilment.
Although my Irish is not very good, I would never try to sing in the “Sean Nos” style, not that I have anything against it, it just isn’t me!
I think any song that is sung well will go down a treat with any audience, as I believe tradition has to move forward with the times that we live in.
