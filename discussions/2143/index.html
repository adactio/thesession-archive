New to Session Music

I have been involved with the Soutwest Celtic Music Association (http://www.scmatx.org/main.htm) for years as a dancer (mostly Scottish with a bit of Irish) but age and injurys have encouraged me to seek out my love for this type of music.

I played the silver flute in school, but don’t know much about how to get the style down. I have a high D whistle with a flute head and recently joined a slow session in Dallas, TX.

Questions:
* How do you learn the basics? I am not tied to sheet music, but when you don’t know where to start, it really helps. My slow session strongly discourages sheet music and only publishes a tune list on the web, they don’t announce what tune they are playing and I feel like I am breaching ettiquite if I ask the name of a tune.
* Are there any sources for learning style? Specifically, cuts and rolls.

Thanks for your input.

Re: New to Session Music

I started my trad (whole music) career with a whistle tutor book and CD that went with it - can’t remember what the hell it was called though! I prefer to play by ear, get a handful of CDs such as Altan, The Cheiftans and Arcady - all are fairly handy for learning off, they tend not to put too much variation from how they are likely to be played at sessions. Once you can pick tunes up by ear from a CD you will be able to do it at a session. I find it easiest to learn of CDs with an uileann piper - it is easier to pick the tune out and where pipers put in grace notes, cuts and rolls is generally where whistle players will put them in.

You tend to find different tunes played at different sessions so there is no harm in asking names. Jim will tell you - I spent a couple of years at one session asking the names of the same tunes over and over - eventually turned in to a bit of a joke! I suggest to avoid this take a pen and paper!! If you ask permission before the session you will probabaly be allowed to record the session, take it home find the sheet music / CD / recording you have made and learn the tunes from there. It can also be handy to ask for the sources as well as the names of the tunes. O’Neills and Anthony Sullivan tunebooks are excellent collections of the majority of tunes you will need.

In terms of where to start a tune at sessions - the easiest in the first instance is follow another whistle / flute / pipers fingers, gives you a good gist of where to start and I found it a reassuring to check that I was playing the right thing in the early days. Again no harm in asking ‘where does that tune start’ ‘what key is it in’ etc. I would be very surprised if people arent’ prepared to help.

Good luck………..

Re: New to Session Music

Hmmm. What slow session is this, and who is the leader of the session? If you have questions about the particular etiquette of the session (what’s up with feeling like you’re crossing session etiquette by asking after a tune name?), ask the session leader how you find out what the tune names are for each tune. Also, at any slow session (in fact, normally any session at all), the use of a tape recorder or other recording device is usually quite welcome, just ask. Be sure to ask after the tune titles *while* the tape is running so you know which is which.

Anyway, the first, second, third, and fourth requirements to playing this stuff is to listen, listen, listen, and listen to the music. Don’t just have it on in the background, you’ve been dancing to it for quite some time (though mainly Scottish, and here we’re mainly concerned with Irish), you know what it sounds like, but you need to find out what goes into it now. Get as many recordings as you can possibly afford, beg, borrow, or take out from the library. Put it on, sit down, and listen to it, figure out where the players vary the tune, what ornaments they use when. Get a tune firmly in your head, to the point where you can lilt it, before trying it on your instrument. “If you can sing it, you can play it,” Shannon Heaton always taught me, and she’s quite right. Try and find different versions of the same tune and see how other players play it.

You’ll hear different styles within the tradition. You’ll hear tunes so wildly variated that they sound like different tunes. (Sometimes they *are* different tunes.) We’re a wildly different bunch roundabout these parts, and opinionated about it too, which you’ll quickly learn if you hang out on this board for very long, but part of the point of this is that we’re all part of the tradition, which is big enough to encompass most all of it.

Most of all, have fun with it. If you’re not having any fun, why do it? :)

Good luck!

Zina

Re: New to Session Music

“Discouraging anything that helps to bring people to the music is an absurd practise.”

Well, my first reaction was to say “horsesh*t” but then I thought that might be seen as a little confrontive, so let me see if I can adapt my response:

“Bringing people to the music” is perhaps a bit of a loaded phrase. If by “bringing people to the music” you mean “encouraging people with a real commitment to the music to seek excellence in participation” then I agree with you.

If on the other hand you mean “sheet music is okay because it ‘brings people to the music’,” or “creating ‘inventive’ countermelodies and harmonies is okay because it ‘brings people to the music’”, or “playing left-handed sewer-flute is okay if it ‘brings people to the music’”, then I don’t agree with you.

This music is the way that it is because a lot of people over several centuries made a commitment to playing it as well as they possibly could, and some of those made a commitment to teaching it to others. The music works well on certain instruments and poorly on others because its very style and aesthetics were formed by those instruments. Someone attempting to incorporate a new instrument does so *only if* s/he has made a commitment *first* to playing solidly within the tradition.

This music takes dedication, commitment, and patience, and those who lose heart after only a little bit of rough going are probably people who are not going to stick it out in the long run. Why would someone who *has* made a commitment break his/her/their neck to encourage a beginner whose commitment seems shaky? In the longer run, if they’re going to get discouraged and drop out, better they do it quickly and save everyone suffering.

Back to the original questions:

jbwalker99, a couple of suggestions, in addition to the good ones made above (especially about listening):

1) Find a teacher, an advanced-or-better player who is a lineage holder and learned directly from other lineage holders. A teacher of your specific instrument who is immersed in the tradition can expedite your progress a lot, and save you error. This is true even if you have to travel to do it.

2) Look for Grey Larsen’s magnum opus “Irish Flute and Tune Whistle” (due any day from Mel Bay), a mammoth study of all aspects of traditional playing, “ornamentation” (articulation), style, repertoire, and seminal players. See also Hammy Hamilton’s and Fintan Vallely’s books.

3) Learn the tunes your local plays. There are a lot of tunes in the tradition; it’s necessary to be patient and to accept that they can’t all be learned immediately.

4) Asking the names of tunes is fine. Timing when you ask is smart.

5) If you really feel flummoxed about session behavior, seek out Barry Foy’s “The Irish Session Handbook.” Mixed with some good sarcastic Dublin humor, it gives good pointers.

6) If you’re still unsatisfied with your local resources, get your butt up to Lubbock. We’ve got a teaching session, a pub session, concerts, seasonal events, a Celtic association, a good Irish dance school (both step- and set-dance), and a lot of good energy. You’d be welcome.

chris smith
