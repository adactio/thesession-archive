Re: How to plan a trip to Ireland?

Jerone, first thing is get a passport if you haven’t already. It can take 2-3 months by normal process, although you can expedite it but it will cost you.

You don’t need to shell out for a full-blown tour unless you like travelling in a group--you may get more in-depth commentary but I think it isolates you from the locals by creating your ready-made society of fellow tourists. Really all you need is a 2 week itinerary that you can fill in yourself. Identify the places you’d like to see, google accommodations, and then connect the dots. Start big and ambitious, and edit yourself down as time and budget demands. Remind yourself that you will be back--you don’t need to see EVERYTHING in 2 weeks.

As far as tolerance, I think it might be way too easy for me as an American middle class male of European descent to say there’s hardly any issues. But Ireland simply does not have our US history of slavery, civil rights and continuing racism. They have had (and continue to have) their own struggles for equality, but in other areas—gender, religion, national origin, not with people who have a different skin color. I think it will blow your mind. But you should probably not take my word for it, I’m sure there are better informed folks who may have a different perspective.

Re: How to plan a trip to Ireland?

The poor guy wants some serious input and all he gets is “mustard” (sigh)… Having visited in March myself a couple of years ago (I was in charge of a group of 17 people) and having set the trip up myself, here are my recommendations: fly into Dublin - you really have to see some of the “touristy” stuff like Kilmainham Gaol, Trinity College, the Book of Kells, the Guinness factory (of course), and Grafton Street, but only spend a couple of days there. March is well before the big tourist season, so most things aren’t crowded. The nights should be devoted to pubs - I highly recommend O’Donohughs (sp) right by St Stephen’s Green - and pub grub. Only spend a couple of days in Dublin, though… Then go west - we stayed in Doolin at a big, modern B&B, but there are lots of places to stay in the area. If I could do it again, I would probably make Galway my base and make day trips all over the place. The only real problem (aside from the March weather) is that, since the tourist season hasn’t really begun yet, you may find a lot of places closed. The pubs and music should be going, but probably not as much as they would be in June/July.

I really wish someone from the area (i.e. the West) would chime in to give you some real advice instead of adding to the “mustard humor” - oh well…

Have fun and good luck!

Re: How to plan a trip to Ireland?

As mentioned earlier, get a passport as soon as you can, the process can take a while.

Save up a good chunk of money, as Ireland is one of the most expensive, if not THE most expensive country in Europe.

It would be hard to suggest an itinerary without knowing how long you have to spend. If you don’t have a lot of time to spend, then I say go west, don’t waste your time in Dublin, I think it’s one of the most overrated cities in the world…. (But then, maybe I’m biased, being from the Wesht).

By and large race shouldn’t be an issue. However, there are idiots every where, and Ireland is certainly not immune to that. I can’t garuntee that you won’t run into a drunken idiot(s) outside a chipper at 2:30am.

Transportation can be an issue without a car. Ireland does not have very extensive public transportation network. There are buses, but you will be limited to where you can go. If you can swing it, rent a car, it gives you exponentially more options and freedom of movement and schedule.

If the purpose of the trip if mostly musical in nature, you might consider visiting in the summer, when the some of the Summer schools are going on, Willie week, Mooney school, south Sligo etc. Attending one of those might blow you mind and give you a different appreciation of the music and culture.

Re: How to plan a trip to Ireland?

Your current mobile phone may not work in Ireland. I think that the USA and Europe have different frequencies and/or communication protocols for mobile ’phones. You may be able to buy a cheap Pay-as-you-go telephone in Ireland if you need one.
Avoid Tipperary. I went there and it was full of 4-star hotels, coach parties, and shrink-wrapped copies of the “typical Irish experience”. Ugh.
Take waterproof clothing.
You may find that you need to hire a car. It’ll have stick-shift - and you’ll be driving on the left, so go carefully - it may be a good idea to have a co-driver to keep telling you to “drive on the left”.
Electricity is 240volt, and has UK-type sockets. If you plug a 110v device in you may fry it.
I suggest you stay in Bed & breakfast houses. You can find them on the internet, and you’ll get a breakfast that’ll last you the day!
Take an audio recorder with you. You can use it to keep a log of what you’ve done, as well as any music you come across.

Re: How to plan a trip to Ireland?

I’d second what Arkle and BigScotia say above more or less, re phone (buy a cheap pay-as-you-go phone or SIM card), public transport, summer schools etc. If going on a tight budget, just head for one area and explore possibilities in that district - so go to Donegal - Ardara, Glenties etc or go to Clare or to Cork. If travelling with a friend, of course you’ll have to take their possibly non musical wishes into the mix.

Keep in mind that trad music is a minority interest in most of Ireland. You won’t find a session going in most pubs you might drop into. You need to seek out opportunities - either by going to touristy areas where sessions are run regularly for visitors, places where the music is popular and there are established regular, usually evening/ night time sessions or ‘summer schools’ & festivals where people will meet to play and there are usually music workshops/ tuition etc.

Racial issues - a changing society but roots are in a conservative, Catholic and white culture. Steady immigration in recent years from eastern Europe and some African states, good few Nigerian families. Most people are decent and respectful like everywhere but travel in the expectation of the odd derogatory comment and don’t be too put off by it. Be careful late at night in any of the larger cities and towns - get a taxi/ lift rather than walk.

Jerone,

You might want to have a look at this recent thread - https://thesession.org/discussions/32391#comment692999.

Firstly, bear in mind that in March Ireland’s weather can be pretty raw.

Next, as others have suggested, if you really want to have a close encounter of the Irish kind, you’d be best to consider just a small area of the country for exploration. However, as others have also pointed out, many sessions in rural areas of Ireland won’t be functioning in March.

My own suggestion would be for you to spend a couple of days in Dublin getting to grips with the country, but also seeing some of its major attractions (as has been pointed out above), then getting a bus up to Donegal (www.buseireann.ie), seeing Sliabh Liag and visiting Glencolmcille. Then get the Feda O’Donnell bus from Donegal town to Sligo - http://www.feda.ie/site/?page_id=20 - (time it right and you might catch a good session in the town) and Galway. That’s only five days used up so far (or six if you spend a night in Sligo).

After that. the world is your lobster. Clare is easily accessible from Galway (e.g. http://www.buseireann.ie/pdf/1284376006-423.pdf), but bear in mind that bus services are few and far between in March.

On Dublin, the city itself has a population of around 1.1 million, according to the 2011 Census. That would put it on between San Jose and Dallas. However, for Greater Dublin, including all the suburban sprawl, it’s 1.8 million persons (almost 40% of the entire Irish population). Re: How to plan a trip to Ireland?

My interpretation of summer school ‘levels’ in Ireland - probably varies in practice according to size of festival, number & age of students, egos of students, capabilities of tutors etc.

Beginner: can play a few tunes, maybe not very well, usually relies on notation

Intermediate: has a good few tunes, ready to learn more technique, might like notation but can learn by ear

Advanced: good handle on range of tunes, good handle on ornamentation, can learn quickly and pick up new tunes by ear

You can usually switch from one to another if you find it too easy or too hard, though often something to be said for persevering.
