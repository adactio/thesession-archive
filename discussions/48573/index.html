“How much can be changed before it’s a different tune?”

One of the oldest questions there is, which will never have a definite answer. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_Theseus

If you’re truly interested in building “theories”, not only read all the music-theoretical material that was, in fact, provided to you in the previous thread, but start reading about philosophy of science. I agree with all others that you should primarily be listening to the music as much as you can to generate true understanding, but if you believe what you’re doing is a worthwhile endeavour, you need to realise that “analysis” to understand the world is something that humans have discussed for millennia. It’s not just you, believe it or not. To this end, read Karl Popper as well as Roland Barthes, for a postmodern understanding of “analysis”, to scrape just the tip of the massive iceberg.

Also remember that the world is complex and things extremely rarely, if ever, have a singular explanation, to reiterate what dozens and dozens of people on here have already suggested. Take “accents” and “lift”. What is an “accent”? What is “lift”? (Don’t answer here, my question is rhetorical). Learn about the operationalisation of concepts, because without it, you can never achieve the analysis you presumably want.

Re: Disdain for analysis?

Specific answers to specific questions, hurrah!

“All I wish to answer is what note hierarchy exists for variations to be created.”

None whatsoever. Variations are either rehearsed or improvised. They work or they don’t. A bad variation runs the risk of throwing a) other people off the tune in question or b) throwing yourself into a different tune entirely.

Some rehearsed variations (e.g. Sean McGuire’s version/variations of the Mason’s Apron https://thesession.org/tunes/74) become part of the tradition. Others do not. Improvised variations are largely ephemeral unless recorded. (See my comments re. Lord Gordon’s in a previous discussion.) Most competent musicians won’t think of it in terms of tune vs variations, they simply play the tune the way it sounds to them in that moment, which means they almost certainly never play it the same way twice. That only comes from experience.

The most effective way to learn to do either of these things is to listen to as many different ways to play a tune as you can find.

“How much can be changed before it’s a different tune?”

Depends on the tune. Off the top of my head I can think of at least a couple of dozen examples of tunes that regularly get confused with other extremely similar tunes in sessions. One particular rabbit hole is the Belles of Tipperary/New Policeman debate which on this forum is like dropping the “Han shot first” bomb into a Star Wars debate. The more tunes you learn, the more examples you’ll run up against.

“What in the playing creates lift. It’s my theory that lift is achieved through accents.”

Yes, but not only through accents. See my previous comments re. Coleman. Expression in ITM is multi-faceted and too complex to fully describe. What you (rather flippantly, although you are in some ways correct) dismissed as being unrecognisable to the average listener is still ENTIRELY THE POINT. The nuance is everything, and only by listening and playing can you really begin to tackle this particular monolith. There isn’t a way to accurately notate the way that ITM is actually played. Again, go check out that Coleman link on YouTube.

“What makes a good B part?”

A good A part.

I jest… any answer to this question will be entirely subjective. There are no rules, only conventions, and all of them have exceptions. It comes down to taste, and that relies on having a large repertoire under one’s belt in order to make judgements like that.

Hope that helps.

Re: Disdain for analysis?

Kellie, you say there is a mentality that trad music cannot be analysed. It can, but it isn’t helpful - you don’t need to be able to explain how an engine works to drive a car.

If you really want to understand trad and how it relates to art music it might be better to look at the history and evolution rather than the individual notes. In the beginning, up to the early baroque, instrumental music was voice-lead (horizontal harmony): each instrument would take one voice of a choir and play their own contrapuntal melody line, the melody lines interact to produce chords, but those chords are coincidental. Then along came the harpsichord. It was a big, expensive, non-portable instrument that was only to be found in the big houses, so had little influence on the music of the common people. The harpsichord allowed harmony to be structured in a different way: with a melody line played over a chord progression (vertical harmony). That approach made it very quick and easy to generate harmony, but for it to work some quite restrictive rules had to be applied: gone were the seven modes and various gapped scales, replaced by major and minor diatonic scales. This is the music that modern theory really relates to. Yes, you can analyse trad music (or voice lead baroque music like Bach or Mozart), but because it doesn’t follow ‘the rules’ that analysis becomes very complex and not particularly helpful. And if you are hoping to find some theory that explains lift, that’s like looking for a recipe for fairy dust.

Re: Disdain for analysis?

Learning about music may include some “analysis” but enthusiastic learning doesn’t stop with left-brain categorizing. There are many tools in the musical tool box, but the tools are not the music. Real curiosity about the music will involve finding out how it works, and a rational level of discovering the components, patterns, ingredients, should lead to more enjoyment, more confidence in playing and listening to music. Jazz players go through this all the time. Always learning, never wanting to get stuck on anything.
But analysis can lead to roads obsessed with either 1) cold, detached librarianism, basically a deadened experience, or 2) encourage and facilitate ongoing creativity and enjoyment. So can “critical thinking”, so popular in universities, “oh, you are getting a degree here, you’ll be so smart”, yet critical thinking is so often entirely reactionary and stagnant. What you want is to learn to be alive, to build on understanding, to elevate engagement and enjoyment, not get trained to live in a fabricated zoo cage or work as a librarian at Dullsville Music Library Corporation. You can always find people who will congratulate you for making friends in Dullsville, they have a large water cooler to chat around, have very regular coffee breaks with sugar donuts and a very fine MUSAK system.

So, it depends on the person doing the learning. Be aware of the process you’re using. Is the person really curious about something and what are they going to do with what they learn? Hopefully, it’s not too predictable. Experiment. Explore. Try things out. Get to the frontier experience OFTEN!

Re: Disdain for analysis?

There is nothing wrong with analysis, and it can be a very important part of growing as a musician in a tradition. The best players in this tradition might not let on, but they have done plenty of thinking about the elements of their music, or the elements of the music as they seek to play it.

The issue is when you reductively mistake analysis for the be-all, end-all or the biggest or only factor in gaining mastery in a traditional style. You must also live with the music for years even beyond Seamus Ennis’ “seven years of learning seven years of practice, seven years of listening,” in a way that occurs at an almost autonomic level that is beyond analysis.

But to take an analytical glance a few elements that contribute to lift or swing:

Accents can be ONE variable that contributes to lift or swing. Accents that create or contribute to syncopation are known as “agogic” accents. Accents are often disavowed by teachers of ITM, but yes, ITM players do “lean in” to a note at times, but very, very subtly and only in certain spots.

Lift and swing ALSO come from duration--how much you shorten or speed up some notes, versus how much you lengthen or draw out others, to get the syncopation idiomatic of the style you are playing. A flamenco habanera does this much differently from the Tulla Ceili Band. But they are both doing it. In Irish music, how this is apportioned can differ from one region, county, or parish to another.

Lift and swing can ALSO come from the staccato-vs-legato balance: Where you “break the line” of a phrase, or “come off” rather than slur. This is very, very important to lift, and something that classical players sometimes take a long time to grasp when coming into a dance-based traditional music form. This is an element where your “seven years of listening” is crucial.

Annnnnd . . . the melody of the tune itself, can ALSO contribute to lift or swing. The paths taken by the notes in a traditional dance tune actually create syncopation and movement in themselves. That is why they are called “dance tunes.” My teacher referred “dance reels.” You can really see this in polkas, slides, and slip jigs, but also in the classic dance reels. This music is not always, but often, more composed of arpeggiated intervals and the tunes themselves help fling the dancers around the floor, or around the house and mind the dresser.

That is a few variables. But you can not reduce swing, lift, or variations (I didn’t get into that last one) to an analytical formula. You can think about these things, and your musings will change over the years as you gain more exposure and understanding. You also must live with it and “get it in ya.”

Re: Disdain for analysis?

I would second that, Tim. Research is never to be discouraged. Knowing more about music in general (including a little theory) won’t hurt. As I said in the original discussion on B-parts, doing exactly the sort of analysis that Kellie is talking about can unlock things in the music and one’s understanding of it. All good. I don’t have anything against it, I’ve done it myself, it can be useful.

Full on classical analysis of the music, though… it’s a highly academic route which will likely take years if not decades to undertake. (Somehow, I fear this may be as unpalatable as the “total immersion” method that many of us have been pushing.)

Here’s the thing. I came at this music having been exposed to it from an early age. I started out learning classical violin, and music theory. I began learning traditional tunes by ear around age 12, and carried on developing a repertoire at a fairly brisk rate until my early twenties. (Three or more tunes a week for ten years or so). The theory part really kicks in at age 14, as I had to sit an exam to progress in practical grades. I took music as an option at High school, including arrangement, improvisation, composition. I was literally immersed in thinking about how music is built, ground up, for YEARS.

(Incidentally, I fundamentally disagree that written music can properly describe traditional music. Try teaching a computer to play a roll, not as a classical ~ ‘turn’ but as it should be played. I have. Nope. Very nearly impossible.)

In contrast, my experience of learning trad music was largely as that oral/aural tradition. Unsurprisingly, theory has little or no place in the vast majority of trad teaching. A bit more in Scottish than Irish, but then I mostly played Irish, so it’s a moot point. Technique, phrasing, expression, nuance. Practical skill over theory. Every. Single. Time. As such, when learning from other musicians, the prized skill is being able to hear what is being played, to imitate and then adapt to make it your own. Much of the process requires little or no talking. The music HAS to speak for itself. The analysis is in the ear, in subjective perception of the music as played, which is intrinsic to the fact that it is and should always be an oral/aural tradition.

In my experience, theory was useful as a retrospective tool to understand the complexity of some of what I was hearing, and it unlocked possibilities in my own playing that are now part of the way I play.

This is fundamentally opposite to the approach that Kellie seems to be suggesting - that by creating a theoretical model for how the music works using a repertoire of some 100 tunes, he can extrapolate what it is that makes all Irish tunes sound Irish.

That’s why there’s so much resistance. That’s why I keep taking the bait. The sheer arrogance of it.

Even if such a prescriptive/predictive model were possible, which I and others doubt, the scale of the task as I have said before would likely take longer than one lifespan. That’s not what Kellie wants to hear, of course, but it is the truth, no matter how many times those more experienced in the tradition are dismissed as we try to point this out. It is still the truth: theoretical and academic, or traditional and immersive; there are no shortcuts.

These thoughts may not be new.

"A man convinced against his will

Left brain: analysis. Right brain: music. Musicmaking: both.

Living things are predisposed to be acutely aware of minute differences. Humans are aware of numerous differences in each other, in animals, in things, in art. We perceive animal breeds, tree species, vehicle makes and models, beverages, accents.

What is perceived by us as a national music? and the various cultural genres, and personal styles within it? and mixtures of these genres and/or styles? Why can we immediately exclaim: “I don’t know the tune, but obviously that’s ____ playing, and they are mixing _____ and _____ styles.”

When is a tune not a tune? We perceive variations in the context of hearing a tune, even tho they stray completely off the melody.

Differentiation of features makes a tune unique. Tunes and variations are infinite. So we cannot catalog every difference. But our memory does a good job. Memory is retained input.

Therefore, someone close to a style can differentiate more deeply within it. How do we differentiate examples? Thru experience, especially by immersion such as while growing up, and/or purposeful study. It is a rare person who can (or would) articulate these differences. The complete task is humanly impossible.

A YouTuber speaks a foreign language better than many native speakers, but lacks the depth of cultural references.
Composed “Irish tunes” in films can be awful. Why?

AI is probably already making “Irish music”, and will improve. How? By having “learned” “how it goes” from massive data input.
It may be interesting and fun to analyze, but this AI will leapfrog Kellie’s analysis, without any explanation needed.
