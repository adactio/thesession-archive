How to listen without a record

In 2002 I bought a Sony minidisc recorder for use recording sessions, workshops, concerts; any & all traditional music. The transition from cassette to minidisc was unprecedented. I was able to lay in track numbers & instantly select any place in the recording. The minidisc recorder now looks like a dinosaur. In 2010 I am thinking there is a high probability that all who are new to traditional music will eventually decide to record sessions. Presumably to learn the tunes. Let’s discuss!

Here is my own humble opinion. If someone wants to record a session it is best to ask. Here is a twist, I have no problem whatsoever for someone to record a session without asking, under two circumstance. If the recording is not at all disruptive to the session & the recording will only be used by the person doing the recording for the purpose of learning the tunes.

About the title ~ I have great respect for anyone who takes the time to listen to a session making music, really listen, & notices how individual players (in the session) play the tunes. These days I want to hear everything 1st hand (getting how tunes sound live in my head). So, if you want to record, be sure to listen first.

Re: How to listen without a record

Ages ago on this board there was a discussion - ah, here it is - https://thesession.org/discussions/23209 - about session etiquette; “don’t interrupt the flow of the music” was highlighted. That’s stuck with me, in no small part because it’s been my experience at sessions that when the flow of music IS interrupted, nine times out of ten there’s a digital recorder involved. Sometimes we’ll chat for a bit between sets, other times someone will lead a new set a few seconds - or not even - after the last one finishes - whatever the circumstances seem to call for at the time.

But on occasion, someone with an iPhone or whatnot will cut in (usually after a set finishes, but not always) - “what was that second tune? great, now how about the third? can you repeat that?” - and then they’ll loudly enunciate the name into the mic (and would the rest of us please keep it down a bit so that the tune name gets recorded ok?) because they need to know RIGHT THEN, so that the tune name can go into the iPhone right after the tune.

In one particularly memorable case, when some of the core musicians had gone to the bar, I found that a new fiddler turned out to know an obscure tune that I’d learned some time ago. Excited, we took advantage of the break to reacquaint ourselves with it. We hadn’t even gotten to the B part when a third musician approached, tapped me on the shoulder, and said, “Hey, can you play that later? I want [musician X] to play that tune that he just played an hour ago - I didn’t get a chance to tape it.” !! (If anyone in town heard an irate fiddler yell, “Jesus Christ, I’m PLAYING A BLOODY TUNE” six months ago or thereabouts - that was me.) And so the flow or music, or conversation, has been interrupted to accommodate the recorder. Not cool.

I don’t bring recorders to local sessions; I don’t even ask for tune names. If a tune catches my ear, I’ll try to pick up a bar or two and then look it up later. If I can’t find it, I’ll try again the next time I hear the tune. Infinite postponement of concentration -I like that one. I’ll bring a recorder to out-of-town sessions, where I’ll often hear tunes I don’t know if I’ll hear again for months; but I’ll keep it on my lap and only turn it on for a handful of particularly appealing tunes, which I can look up later.

@ligg:

As I said earlier, if I’m going to record, I record the whole session as a single file. It’s not fecking stupid, but you are right I’m never going to listen to the whole lot.

Recording the whole thing isn’t stupid because it means all I have to do is turn the recorder on when I arrive, completely forget about it while I enjoy the session, then turn it off when I leave, I don’t have to fiddle about with it between sets and annoy people.

When I get home, I open the file with Audacity - without even playing it the quiet spells between sets are obvious, so I can wind to the beginning of each set and listen to a couple of bars -

‘That’s one I know’ - bin
‘That’s one I don’t know, but I recorded it last week’ - bin
‘THAT’S the one I want to learn’ - copy it into its own file.

In ten minutes or so I’ve got separate files of the sets I want to learn, and the original 4 hour file goes in the bin.

In an ideal world, yes, it would be nice to learn tunes just from hearing them live. But I’m not good at picking up tunes - I might have to hear a tune a dozen times before I get it. If it comes out in session once a month that would mean I’d take a year to learn it. Doing it my way I can hear a tune for the first time one week, and have it good enough to play along the next.

Brief linguistics digression: the genitive case in every language I’ve come across denotes “pertaining to”, with “possession” as a special case, rather than the reverse - despite the descriptions offered by grammarians and grade-school teachers. That is, “my car” is mine, in that it pertains to me, because I own it, and can sell it, lend it, give it away, or paint it pink if I like. “My mother” pertains to me by familial relations, and therefore I don’t have any of the permissions of ownership.* These are two sorts of “pertaining”, there are others, including the usage in question here.
“Pat’s tune” is Pat’s, not because he has some ownership of it, but because it is locally associated with him, perhaps because he introduced it to the session or plays it often, or plays it exceptionally well, or for some other reason. This is typically a non-exclusive relationship, and you can say “that’s one of Pat’s tunes” without saying “and therefore you can’t or shouldn’t start it”.

I hope that clarifies the ownership question - it’s a simple misinterpretation of the genitive that’s caused the confusion.

* More on this: A possession relationship can only be one to many - if I own the car, you do not. (In a case of collective ownership, of course, the object belongs to the collective, not solely to any member). A familial relation is typically many to one - for any given mother, there may be several people who call her “my mother” - or many to many - for any given cousin, there may be many people who have this cousin, among others.

“Point being, I STILL think giving this guy the cold shoulder was a bit harsh. Is it that hard to play along and then say, ”Well, I was just d*cking around with the tune there. That’s not the usual way people play it. But there’s this fantastic recording of it on (insert album name here).“ ” (TheSilverSpear)

I agree with this. Definite mentoring opportunity here, and it takes so little time and effort. If you want the newbies at your session or in your locality to learn the ropes (or at least learn not to offend you), you have to show them the ropes.

And I am confused by something that feels like a real contradiction here.

It sounds like people are criticizing the poor kid for recording and learning the first guys whacked out version of a standard tune. But I’m always “hearing” on these boards that it’s not pure to learn from dots, it’s not pure to learn from midi files, it’s not pure to learn from studio recordings, and it’s much more pure and preferable to learn from someone’s actual playing (of which a live recording is about as close as you’ll get in this day and age where getting someone to sit down and teach you a tune note by note is almost impossible).

Yet I see a contradictory trend beginning here towards recommending that people seek out the “standard” version of tunes - meaning what, the dot version, the midi version? - and hold that above whatever live interpretation came out of their local session.

I know this is an age-old debate, but I really don’t think you can have it both ways.

Now I myself find the “standard” versions to be versatile, a common language among session players, but this has only been made possible by the advent of recordings and the Internet and globalization and all that…

Anyway, I’d be way bummed to be the kid who busted his butt learning the “wrong version.” I’d be even more bummed if I learned it hoping a particular guy would play along (it being what he started the week before) and he brushed me off. And I would be exceedingly grateful if a kind soul gave me a few words of encouragement to soften the rude guy’s blow. But that’s me.

Random said: “Something can change each time a tune is played. Recordings & sheet music are not flexible in that sense.”

Which is why (in response to worthy’s worries) it’s best to learn from another human being, not a recording of another human being. Used to be how all the tunes were swapped, person to person. Imagine that. Picking up tunes this way is a skill like any other. If you think you need a recording to learn the tunes, then you’ve probably let your aural skills and retention and recall capacities atrophy. The more you do it, the easier it becomes. Think of all the money you save not needing electric gadgets.
