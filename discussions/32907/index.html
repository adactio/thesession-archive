Can you read ABC?

I’ve never gotten the hang of reading ABC right off the page, although with transcribing things more these days I seem to be getting better at it. For whatever reason sheet music is just much more easier to “grok” as they say - I can take in the whole score at a glance, with ABC I still find myself going note-by-note. Does this go away with practice? Some of you can read ABC as easy as any notated score so there’s hope. I’m surprised it’s taking so long, no doubt it helped that I learned to read sheet music as a youngster.

I keep meaning to knuckle down and just practice reading ABCs while I play, forcing myself to get the hang of it. A hopeful sign is that lately I’m not clicking on the “sheet music” button here so much, I can tell basically what I’m looking at from bar to bar.

Re: Can you read ABC?

@ Sebastian -- at the risk of belabouring the point, it has nothing to do with ‘some vectors in space’. An analogue clock face contains all the information that a digital clock face has, ie the numbers, and in addition offers a visual representation of a day divided into portions. If you take away the numbers and just leave the pointers, you can still tell roughly what time it is by the relative positions of the pointers and the sizes of the areas defined.
Similarly, staff notation shows immediately the positions of notes relative to each other, which ABC does not.
In notation, a single mark gives you note, octave, duration, and pitch relative to the other notes, whereas in ABC you have to read a separate mark for each of note, octave, and duration, and the relative pitch can only be deduced by referring to a known scale. Granted, all these things only take a very small fraction of time and a minuscule amount of effort; but the fact remains that one is easier than the other.
@ Theirlandais, I understand what you are saying, I am not stupid. I just disagree with you. If you feel you need to spend a lot of time explaining, perhaps you are wrong.

↳ Posted by jeff_lindqvist on December 7th, 2013.

Now I know my ABCs… 8-) ( for gam - unrestrained! - sometimes if not always… )

In the early pre-computer uses of ABC notation there were differences, and a few varieties of ABC notation too. Some people put in the bar lines, I did, others just did the note groupings, but often in four bar phrases. Some would add a bar arching over a group of notes, with a double bar signifying 16th notes. A line under a note lengthened it, and two lines lengthened it further, and three lines… Instead of the comma following the letter for a note to signify it was an octave lower (B,) a similar mark was put just under the letter of the note, and instead of an apostrophe to show the higher octave (c‘) a similar mark was made right above the note. In some ABC notation, and there were others, the starting note for capital letters was the D just under the staff, so that the rise to the lower case letters went so ~ D E F G A B C d e f g a b c, instead of as others had it - starting on middle C. Instead of ’~’ for a roll, thee was a half moon mark made under the letter of the note to roll. Sometimes the key was given as a letter, and at other times it was given as sharps (#) and flats (b) before the sequence of letters for the melody, and sometimes the repeat signs were also given, but that could be minimalist ( : ), or with a bar line ( |: ~ :| ).

Instead of using > & <, for some forms of the earlier notation, a sequence in 2/4 like |: AB cd | would have the first note of a pair underlined to show the swing, or the second note of a pair underlined to show a snap… But there were a number of variations on everything. The adaptation of it to ascii code has lead to standardization and greater clarity as a result, and increased functionality… I’ve had to reteach myself more than once over the years. I see those changes as a challenge but as a welcomed and good thing. Some of the older forms of ABC notation that I’ve been given over time are not exactly clear on all points and could be quite confusing, just as I know for some who were familiar with the older systems have found the newer developments, at least initially, difficult and confusing, not what they were used to. I know this for a fact because I’ve helped a number of people to understand and master those changes, just as I have also taught both dotted and ABC notation to others. I have respect for both…

Looking over older ways with the ABCs I have also found the use of > & <…

Mr C - I don’t see any comments of a derogatory nature toward the use of ABC in this thread.

There’s a very, very good reason why people don’t bother to learn (to read) and become familiar with ABC, and that’s because many can read standard notation pretty well. That’s not a useful opinion, it’s a fact.

ABC is great as a portability tool (that is blindingly obvious), and I have no doubt at all that it is also a great tool for those who can’t / won’t read standard notation, for whatever reason.

If one is fluent in both, then maybe that is a good thing also.

Just for the record : ‘self-imposed learning disability’ is quite an emotive phrase. To the best of my knowledge it was first coined by the late great Christopher Hitchens in one of his debates. The full phrase was ‘religious belief is a self-imposed learning disability’.
