I find it helps to home in on the ‘important’ notes in a sequence, usually where the beat falls, and think of the others as fillers or lead-ins. You have to make absolutely sure in your own mind which note you are aiming for, and pick it out in the sequence, rather like running across a long line of stepping stones. Know which one you are aiming for just before you get to it, hit it dead on, and move on to the next, leaping over the requisite number of stones in the process.

Hello Rose,
Probably I am in the minority here with the following advice. One of the happiest moments
in the learning process for me was abandoning the metronome and attempts to count out the
rhythm in my head. This came when I realized that by listening to the tune being played while
playing along with a recording of the tune (with a slow down app initially), I didn’t need to think about
note time value: That is, by knowing the tune, the brain can handle the note value without conscious effort.
I had previously studiously studied note time value with serious intention and a good computerized
learning program so this was not some frivolous decision to go down the road this road.

If you know a song, you can sing along without attempt to assign note time value. I found that it is also
true with playing tunes on my fiddle. I know this advise will be scorned by many here. Nevertheless,
it worked for me and I have been a serious student of the learning process for my last 4000 hours of
Irish fiddle playing (reading the lit, keeping notes of what has worked and what has not). I found that examining the way the brain learns music is as fascinating as fiddling. Today one can even get a PhD in the specific area of how the brain learns music.

I apologize in advance to those who think who find the metronome and counting essential. The above thoughts may be the result of irrational and unconventional thought processes of an all to independent thinker.

Re: Trouble keeping time…

Metronomes are fantastic for learning to keep time, and there’s another (often overlooked) benefit which is that they teach you how to play to a beat that’s external to yourself, which is precisely the skill one needs to learn to play with other musicians. You have to listen to the click, and match it, which develops your skill at listening to a beat.

Better yet is all the slow-down software that’s available nowadays. You can take a recording of a great player and slow it down to a speed that you’re comfortable with and just play along. Then you’re developing many more skills, not only listening for the beat but also hearing the rhythm and pulse and phrasing, and besides hearing pitch to develop that part of a well-rounded musician’s ear.

↳ Posted by Jim Dorans, JustAFiddler on April 23rd, 2014.

Re: Trouble keeping time…

Since you already know that you can’t count time on your own, I don’t think ignoring the metronome and counting will help.

This is what I want you to try, i’ll try to explain it the best I can…

First, let me explain to you subdivision. Subdivision is how 2 eighth notes = 1 quarter note, how 2 quarter notes = 1 half note, how 2 half notes = 1 whole note. See a pattern? Even though the note values are different, the numbers are the same because the note values are subjective. Tempo dictates the actual timw value of the notes. A half note at 70bpm is longer than a half note at 90bpm because 70bpm is slower than 90bpm. All the note value tells you is how many beats the note is held for.

I hope that made sense.

Now, set your metronome to 60(This exercise only works at this tempo beside a stopwatch.) Every beat you hear is a quarter note in 4/4 time(an accent on the first beat helps you hear where every measure starts.)

Try to feel the space between the beats as you clap on the clicks. Since the metronome is set to 60, it’s like counting a second a click, so you’ll be clapping once every second. Those are your quarter notes.

Now try to clap twice between beats, one of the claps being on the beat and the second clap being in the middle of the two beats. You should be evenly clapping once on the click and once during the silence in between the clicks. At this tempo, the points that you clap is at .00 and .50 seconds. Those are your 8th notes.

Now try to clap 4 times evenly in between the clicks. The first clap will be on the click and the following three claps should come before the second click. These claps happen at .00 .25, .50, .75, Those are your 16th notes.

Does any of that make a little sense?

Re: Trouble keeping time…

“Playing with a metronome will make you play like a metronome, and then only when you’re playing with the metronome. Real human-being music doesn’t sound like a metronome.”

This has some truths twisted around with false ideas in a strange way.
1) “Playing with a metronome will make you play like a metronome.” If we take that to mean playing with steady rhythm yes! We should all be so lucky to be able to play as steady as a metronome. Many people are born with an extremely accurate internal metronome, lucky for them! They ALWAYS are playing to a metronome but we just can’t see it. Other people aren’t this lucky. The good news for them is that most people who weren’t born with a great internal metronome can improve the one they have by regular practice playing in time to a steady outside beat (whether this is provided by a metronome, or by playing along with good musicians).
2) “then only when you’re playing along with the metronome.” Yes there are some people with hopelessly bad internal metronomes that no amount of practice will improve. But most people, if they diligently practice to a metronome (or other provider of a steady beat such as other musicians) can improve their internal metronome. If this wasn’t so, music teachers wouldn’t encourage (or require) their students to practice with a metronome.
3) “real human being music doesn’t sound like a metronome.” If you mean a series of clicks, well a human can produce a series of clicks too, if he wants. If you mean playing with a steady rhythm, metronomes and all good musicians sound exactly alike in this regard. It’s the BAD musicians who sound nothing like a metronome in the matter of steady rhythm.

Most people don’t realize that most of the professionally recorded music they hear in ‘the media’ such as TV and movie soundtracks, pop songs, etc are all done to a click track. Yes, sorry to say, real “human being” music is in effect done to a metronome. And it can sound VERY human and VERY musical.

Obviously a steady rhythm is necessary for group playing, unless there’s a conductor to speed up and slow down everybody together, or the musicians have worked out all the places to speed up or slow down in advance. If each musician in a group is varying the tempo in his own personal way the result is chaotic noise.

An example of a group deciding in advance how to vary a tempo is the way many good Highland pipers play Amazing Grace, in 3/4 time but with the 3rd beat in every bar lengthened throughout the tune. A pipe band can play this tightly together because everybody ‘feels’ the tune the same way. Strict metronome time with that tune doesn’t sound as nice.

Then there’s the dance aspect… if a musician’s timing is all over the map, how can dancers anticipate where the beat is going to fall?

“We should all be so lucky to be able to play as steady as a metronome.”

No we shouldn’t. We should all be so lucky as to be endowed with the skill to listen and respond to the musicians playing alongside us. You can’t interact with a metronome because it isn’t listening to you. Playing music is all about interacting, not reacting. For the same reason, playing along with a CD is a pretty poor substitute for playing along with real people. That CD has been edited, spliced, corrected, boosted, compressed, etc., and is not real live music any more. Which is not to say it can’t be enjoyed (I’m of the Glenn Gould school of thought here), but it is not a suitable vehicle for teaching yourself good, natural rhythm. Now I’m an honest sort of fellow, and when I say I had the same issues as the original poster I mean it. I have two metronomes and they were a totally disastrous waste of time when I tried to use them to put myself right. I likened it to being unhappy with the way I ride my bike, therefore I put stabilisers back on. Useless. One day you have to take them off again (aka ditching the metronome). Playing with other people who are half-decent is the very best thing you can do. The next best thing is to record yourself and listen back. If you can hear what’s up, you’ve cracked it and you can work on it. If you can’t hear what’s up, whereas your mates can, you’re stuffed. Learn pottery instead.

Re: Trouble keeping time…

Playing with a metronome has its place, but I don’t think that by itself it will help with the OP’s issue which is sub-dividing the beat. I f you don’t want to clap the rhythm, then there are a couple of possible solutions. One is a programmable drum machine which you can program to play each rhythm in question. You can then start at a slow tempo and play along, gradually increasing the speed until you reach the point where your motor skills will not allow you to keep up. Then back off a bit and practice at that speed until you have internalized the rhythm. Another, less mechanical approach would be to get used to counting every 16th not(semiquaver) : one-e-and-a two-e-and-a etc. Going slow will let you instill both the rhythm and the finger movements.
One last point: I think for a lot of people the main problem with sub-dividing is simple panic. They see those notes all bunched together with big black beams, and they freak out. If this happens even for a split second it can be enough to interrupt the flow of the rhythm, which in turn creates a negative feedback loop, or self fulfilling prophecy.. Once again slow practice is invaluable here.
