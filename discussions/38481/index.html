Re: Overt body (foot) signals

In one of our local groups the raised foot means a change is coming or the set is ending. It is often acknowledged with another foot raise. If anyone wants to continue the tune/set he/she raises BOTH feet, heels together with toes at a 45 degree angle in a Vee shape. Thus the message “vee-toe” and we go another round!

Seriously though I’m too old to go around sticking my foot in the air (although it seems to wind up in my mouth a lot). Most of us are content with paying enough attention to each other and what we’re doing to know what’s coming next. A glance, eye contact, a nod, is usually enough, or, the occasional “hup” or a call out of the next key, is enough. Or, as argued in a recent post, we just know that the one who took the lead for the set is entrenched in the 2 (or 3) times through and be done with it. Personally I’ve never raised a foot as a signal although I may sometimes do it to facilitate the passing of gas and only then outdoors with a strong headwind. That said, I’ve also never gotten my Underoos wadded up when someone else does.

Re: Overt body (foot) signals

The foot raising is certainly more prevalent in the US than elsewhere I’ve seen. This is probably because of the fact that it’s a very common practice in old-time. But it doesn’t happen much in my sessions. With a tight knit group of players, all the changes and endings can be seamlessly communicated with much more subtle cues. The glance, or raised eyebrow, or re-positioned instrument, etc.

Both the raised foot and the subtle cues can be missed if someone has their eyes closed. But people are pretty good at sensing the flow of the set, and opening their eyes to see if it’s time to change. Occasionally, someone won’t appear to be paying attention, and then an audible cue can be useful. Sometimes just re-positioning my instrument (banjo) so that it points toward the person will be enough. But often times, a word becomes useful.

We certainly use “hup” and “again”, or “one more” at times. Especially when the group is larger or there are people less familiar with the session. And my sessions don’t have a set number of times to play a tune, so the “one more” gets used a lot.

I have a major pet peeve for the use of “hup” to end a set. “Hup” means going to another tune. When you use it for the end of a set, the music just stops bluntly because everyone is waiting to hear the next tune. If I started a set, I never tell people when it’s going to end other than with my music. I will use a chord or some sort of flourish, sometimes accompanied by a deliberate slowing of the tune to signal that we’re ending. And everyone picks up on it, and ends with a proper ending, instead of just stopping.
