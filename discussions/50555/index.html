The Peeler Polka - time signature?

Was just listening to this crooked polka, ‘The Peeler Polka’, on the excellent Paddy O‘Brien album ’The Sailor’s Cravat’ and thinking of having a go at it. It seems to use unequal bar lengths.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nTGcpIqLD78&list=RDnTGcpIqLD78&start_radio=1

If anyone does play it I wonder if they’d agree with me on the time signature - click the video link above to hear it.

The A part seems to be: two bars of 3/4 plus one bar of 2/4 (that’s how I count it, but I suppose you could just as well say it’s one bar of 3/4 plus one bar of 5/4), repeated. So you can count: 1-2-3, 1-2-3, 1-2.

The B part uses the same unit - two bars of 3/4 plus one bar of 2/4 - only it adds two bars of straight 4/4 to the end of it. So the count would be: 1-2-3, 1-2-3, 1-2, 1-2-3-4, 1-2-3-4.

It reminds me a lot of Tolka Polka - in that everything adds up to multiples of four, but you can’t count it straight like that. I’d be interested to know who wrote it - I presume it’s a relatively recent composition? Trouble with streaming is of course you don’t get to read the sleeve notes…

Re: The Peeler’s Polka - time signature?

3/2 hornpipes are fine, but this tune is not played as a 3/2 on the recording at all, just a plain ’ole 2/4 polka! %) What may be throwing you off is the sort-of syncopated pattern of the tune, an effect produced purely by the combination of long and short notes. Think Britches Full of Stitches or Joe Bane’s polka which similarly lean into syncopation. The musicians, while not in any way overdoing the emphasis on this pattern, are playing it fairly straight without the characteristic pulse / lift common in places like Sliabh Luachra. The result is that you’re shifting your attention between the “syncopated” and “non-syncopated” bits. Had this track had an oom-pah backing, you probably wouldn’t have had issue with it :-)

A small thing that may be of aid in balancing the tune’s structure and the polka rhythm is the use of a pickup beat / anacrusis. It may sound like a completely optional thing (and for the most part it is – when played for listening) but I once had a very long talk with an older-generation musician about the importance of the pickup beat in the old style of playing for the dances, in the same vein as Denis Murphy & Julia Clifford used to play in Sliabh Luachra. If you listen to Denis & Julia’s recording of the classic Ballydesmond set, you’ll immediately notice how strikingly different it feels to some “renditions” you’d hear in sessions around the world – not sped up to the max but with an infectious rhythm that suits the sets. One part of that picture is an apt placing of some pickup notes to make the beat “jump out” as opposed to landing with a thud. Hope that makes sense :-D

Re: The Peeler’s Polka - time signature?

Well, I disagree that it would be “horrible” to back this tune without syncopation – both backing styles are valid, one would perhaps suit a modern-sounding band playing for listening, the other a céilí band playing for the dances or a solo recording with a subtle piano backing. Of course they could also be combined to a degree, it’s a matter of taste, and it’s a good thing we have artistic freedom of choice.

Horrible things happen when the backing is slapped on a tune while completely ignoring the context and the sensibilities of the musicians playing. That’s happened to some of the most respected figures of the traditional music world, sometimes due to a third party’s desire to make the recording sound more “marketable”. Ceol as Sliabh Luachra is an infamous example of that – the musicians involved were all masters of their craft but the combination produced one the worst arrangements of Sliabh Luachra music ever. The backer is a fantastic musician who uses plenty of syncopation… which made it so much worse in that particular case.

Re: The Peeler’s Polka - time signature?

A gavotte you say? Kudenn ebet! (Excuse my brezhoneg)

You can indeed make a Ton Simple out of this tune, or rather from Part A. Notably, whereas a syncopated rhythm for backing polkas is largely an artistic choice and may not be of help to the dancers, Breton gavottes canonically have a 3-3-2 or 6/8 + 6/8 + 2/4 rhythm with fitting dance steps.

That does not mean the tune is really a gavotte or was necessarily made out of one – but it’s a good excuse to bring up the contemporary Breton approach to melody playing and accompaniment that states that they can co-exist independently, not being a perfect match, as long as both fit the steps. Specifically, both the singer / musician and the backer(s) can interpret the gavotte to be either in duple or a triple time – with all the possible combinations of the two considered valid.

Here’s a great explanation from a Breton musician with examples of singing, playing and staff notation (you can turn on YouTube subtitles and set to auto-translate them into English):
https://youtu.be/ZLaBTnntuVg?si=vSb1Sxb4sgoQzk8C&t=198

There’s something really cool about that approach: you can be as sassy and experimental or old-school and subtle as you like but whatever you do, it *must* fit the rhythm of the dancers’ feet – or else it’s not really a gavotte. Anyway, here’s the same tune after getting a gavotte treatment (polkas getting a taste of their own medicine?:-D)

X: 1
T: The Peeler’s Gavotte
T: Gavotte Ton Simple
R: gavotte
M: 4/4
L: 1/8
Q: 1/4=150
K: Ador
|:A>B cB AG E2|AB c/B/A d2 d2:||
|:A>B cB AG E2|A/B/c BG A2 A2:||

or

X: 1
T: The Peeler’s Gavotte
T: Gavotte Ton Simple
R: gavotte
M: 12/8
L: 1/8
Q: 3/8=150
K: Ador
|:A2B c2B A2G E3|A2B cBA d3 d3:||
|:A2B c2B A2G E3|ABc B2G A3 A3:||

Re: The Peeler’s Polka - time signature?

@brailsford, absolutely, it’s all in the head! There really needs to be a common reference point for the melody and the accompaniment to agree. This used to be the dancing, now it’s more about finding intersections between each other’s listening experience, such as favourite musicians and bands. When the rhythm of the dancers’ feet is taken away, there’s always a chance that the tunes get shaped into something else, up to a point of them becoming abstract echoes of their former selves. One example of that would be polka rhythm getting skewed in favour of a backing pattern to the point of the backer becoming the lead. It can be fun to listen to – but this music is no longer danceable and would be a torture for a musician out of Sliabh Luachra to play along. On the other hand, if played with a proper lift, the tunes you used to think were shapeless and crooked suddenly snap into place!
