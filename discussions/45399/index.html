Re: Becoming an Irish traditional musician

With print-on-demand services churning out books of reasonable quality (and in the case of Ingram Spark, quite high quality), there’s no need for scaling. It doesn’t matter whether you sell 50 books or 1 million. The price of printing is that *one* book, and you come up with a retail price reflecting that. I suspect that Jess doesn’t have control over that if the book is with a traditional academic publisher, but still, that old school model is increasingly BS with print-on-demand improving all the time.

I got out of academia shortly after I worked my arse off for zero money to get a piece published in a peer reviewed journal. It would cost you guys quite a lot to read my article (and the degree to which you would not care is high) unless you’re attached to an institution with a subscription. But does any of the money paid by universities or individuals go to me? Nope. The journal’s publisher rakes in all the dough. I realised I could go into journalism and fiction writing and get paid for working not quite as hard.

It’s a nasty system. You have to get stuff published in peer reviewed journals to get/keep a job. But publishing is by no means guaranteed to land you a job. So you have to work hard, for free, for a very theoretical job. And if your discipline is history, you’re more likely to see a yeti (not the car… I own one of those) than aforesaid theoretical job.

I would be keen to read Jess’ book but it would have to be a sane, reasonable price.

Re: Becoming an Irish traditional musician

Jessie, in the earlier discussions did I bring up the book

May It Fill Your Soul: Experiencing Bulgarian Music
by Timothy Rice

Dr Rice is in the ethnomusicology department at University of California Los Angeles.

The book is a very good read. It’s aimed at a general audience and covers a wide variety of topics concerning Bulgarian traditional music from the pre-WWII period through today. He has mini-bios of some of the musicians that have been the major caretakers and transmitters of the tradition, the people who embody the tradition as your book’s title puts it.

He does drift into academic speech at times, especially when the topic is his own journey of attempting to understand the tradition and learn to play the music in a traditional manner. He speaks a number of times of the emic/etic dichotomy, of his forever being an outsider and being denied experiencing the music as Bulgarians do.

Many years ago I was going around teaching Irish flute and I always emphasised my own status as an outsider, that I did not embody the tradition but could only observe it as closely as I could. Or as it was put to me, back when I was starting out, by a very good American player who had lived in Ireland for a time:

“We Americans are always searching for ‘the sound’. The Irish players HAVE ‘the sound’.”

When I taught it was never “I do it this way” but “I’ve heard THEM to it this way”. It’s not for me to put my own playing forward as being part of the tradition, only to point beginners to the players who are the tradition, and what I have observed them doing.

Re: Becoming an Irish traditional musician

Helen O’Shea refers to “authenticity” as a mirage in the desert, that continually recedes as you approach it. Like a mirage, it’s not real, and you’ll never catch it.

In group/out group dynamics are always present in any activity. But when they get larded up with nationalism they become especially troubling. Irish music has always circulated internationally and back to Ireland from musicians of Irish descent (or not) who played in the US, England, and Australia. It lived in cities as well as on mountaney farms.

For me playing Irish music is pretty strictly a solo, meditative thing. It’s way to easy to offend or be offended otherwise.

Re: Becoming an Irish traditional musician

This topic prods my interest every time there’s a discussion involving ‘authenticity’. Briefly, I sort of regard a tradition in music pretty much akin to a tradition in spoken language. That is, if you’re not brought up in it as a native speaker, you might be an enthusiastic learner and achieve great proficiency, but the chances of speaking with a genuine accent, or of becoming creative and fluent in idiomatic and colloquial expression, are very slight. Attained by very few, I’d say.

I didn’t have that sort of immersive early experience, so I make do with what I’ve picked up from various sources and influences along the way. I’d like to be a better player of Irish music (and English, and Swedish, and Shetland . . . ) but there’s no point in beating oneself up about it. I’m with Richard D on this: the thing is to do as well as you are able, keep trying to learn and improve, enjoy sharing what you can, and avoid pretentions about your own place in the scheme of things. The real tradition-bearers wouldn’t have to make such claims: they just do it.

The book is an over 250-page hardback book (also peer-reviewed by other scholars before editing). In short, publishers need to make their money back so they don’t loose money, so they basically gouge the price to make their money off of library sales. For context, my friend wrote an academic book and he tells me the income it generates is pretty much enough to cover a nice meal and a weekend break away. Re: Becoming an Irish traditional musician

Jessie you might not have the time to do such, but I urge you to write a “popular” book on this topic.

There are excellent books written by ethnomusicologists (whether or not they have a “doctor” in front of their name) which are reasonably priced and available to all. Yes they’re not bestsellers but they get into the hands of people interested in the traditions.

I mentioned the excellent

May It Fill Your Soul: Experiencing Bulgarian Music by Timothy Rice.

Also excellent is

Play Of A Fiddle*: Traditional Music, Dance, and Folklore in West Virginia by Gerald Milnes.

Both books transport the reader back to a different time, long gone, immerse us in the culture of isolated rural villages and the place and function of musicians in it.

They then bring us forward into modern times, when the music is “discovered” by the outside world, commercialised, distorted, and taken up by enthusiastic outsiders who love the music but cannot know the music’s traditional role, cannot experience the music as a native.

Something like that about Irish traditional music would be welcome on my bookshelf!
