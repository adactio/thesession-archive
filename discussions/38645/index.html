Is anyone playing “new-old” Irish folk songs?

Or perchance would traditional players shy away from a new tune—at least until it’s had a hundred years to “settle into the collective consciousness,” acquire the patina of age, and be spun off into a dozen variations? :-)

As I’ve been listening to what I presume are fairly old, standard Irish tunes, I’ve been surprised by the extent to which accidentals are used—I’m talking, for example, about a piece in DMajor where an ascending line will go up D-E F#-G, then come right back down as G-F(natural)-E-D. That’s pretty sophisticated harmonically, and is actually very similar to (as one example) Magyar folk music (as epitomized by Bela Bartok), which is constantly shifting notes a half step going one way and changing coming the other.

I guess my point is that from what little I’ve seen so far, the “rules” appear to be pretty flexible. :-)

Scott, accidentals are relatively rare in dance tunes. They occur, of course, and sometimes there will also be key changes within a tune. However, I am concerned by your statement about how often you think this happens. The overwhelming majority of tunes played in sessions do not contain more than the occasional G#. Sometimes a Cnat and C# will exist in the same tune. Most of us here can name off those tunes, primarily because they are so few compared to the vast repertoire where no accidentals occur.

I learned a popular tune called Rakish Paddy with a G#. When I mentioned it here, literally everyone told me the version they knew did not have it. Jenny’s Chickens is played with and without it. That leads me to believe that the versions with it may have not started life that way. G# and Fnat are very difficult to play on a keyless flute or tin whistle, so that may tell us something, as well.

Re: Is anyone playing “new-old” Irish folk songs?

Fnat is actually quite easy to play on keyless flutes, whistles, and pipes, and it’s common enough as a standard feature of a tune, and as a way of varying the tune, that I for one could not easily name all of them. Similarly, but moreso, with Cnat.

I don’t tend to learn tunes with the G/G# alternating thing, but since the Fnat/F# alternation happens often in Am and Amix tunes, I’d expect a similar frequency in Bminor/Bmix tunes, granting that the latter is more rare.

That said, I think that the alternating Fnat/F# and Cnat/C# thing is partly influenced by fixed-pitch instruments trying to make sense of the in-between or “neutral” tuning used by many old-school players - Paddy Fahey did this with his Bs, which is why his common jig is played both in G and in Gminor, neither the B natural nor the Bb being quite what he played.

Re: Is anyone playing “new-old” Irish folk songs?

Perhaps simple-system flute players steer away from tunes with accidentals? In my experience they aren’t all that rare at all. The c-nat / c# ambiguity is very common.

You get the odd surprise g# in D-major tunes, esp. as played by Sligo-style fiddlers. The Providence is an example.

The g# in Jenny’s Chickens isn’t an accidental though - the tune hovers between the keys of F#m and Bdorian - g nats in the piece would be about as annoying as, well, gnats.

[Nico has said almost exactly this next bit nicely while I was talking on the phone, but here goes anyway] A good chunk of accidentals probably derive from attempts by players of fixed-pitch instruments to get close to the sound of non-ET F#s and Cs played on pipes and fiddles. For example the Bb in the Paddy Fahy’s jig that was discussed recently - on a recording I have heard of Paddy F himself playing it, the B is clearly a natural, flattened to sound sweet with the G, and not a Bb.

The same is true of the Fs in e.g. the turn of the Chicago Reel or Sean Reid’s, which sound utterly ghastly to my ears when played as stark F naturals on, say, concertina. Sometimes though a tune really mutates along these lines - e.g. Chief O’Neill’s Favourite, in which the Fs that start the second part have really grown roots as F naturals, and attempting to play them as anything else (such as a just F#) will earn you funny looks.

There’s lots more that could be said. I’m sure someone has addressed the question of accidentals in a PhD thesis somewhere. Meanwhile Scott needs to listen to a few hundred recordings esp. of the old fellas. :-)

Re: Is anyone playing “new-old” Irish folk songs?

@Scott Wilkinson:

You mentioned Lydian-mode tunes, wondering if they were much used in ITM.

My answer would be, “No. If they are in the repertoire, they must be pretty rare…” That is to say, true Lydian mode tunes whose scale, in other respects a straightforward major scale, has an unambiguous sharpened fourth - as opposed to tunes with movable or ambiguous intervals in the playing, of which (as shown in posts above) there are many.

I’ve learned and heard a great many Irish tunes, and have yet to register one in the Lydian mode as I have defined it. (Not that I claim prowess either in knowledge of tunes or in listening attentively all the time…) Indeed, the only trad tune I know that is in the Lydian mode, or most generally set in it, is something of a one-off - a Shetland tune called The Greenland Man’s Tune:

https://thesession.org/tunes/4680

Furthermore, either the tune or words that went with it, or both, have been claimed to be ‘Yakki’/‘Yaqqi’ - i.e. from a Greenland native culture - Inuit, I presume. So it’s some sea-miles away from Irish trad.

@Nico—excellent points all! And (as I sit here thinking about it), I’d say that no—a formal music education isn’t relevant to traditional music at all. (That is, one could be a fantastic performer of traditional music with encyclopedic knowledge of it without a formal music degree.)

It’s possible that a formal degree might help someone pick up traditional music faster? But maybe not.
