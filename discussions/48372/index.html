Re: Access to Seamus Tansey essay

In 1974, I spent 7 months in England, Ireland and Scotland, researching where traditional music was going and what messages that might have for us in Australia. Three months or so of that was in Ireland. I attended a lot of events all around Ireland, listening to talks, interviewed a lot of movers and shakers, and spent a lot of time musing over what I heard.

At that time, many people were apprehensive about where traditional music was going. I particularly remember one Comhaltus event where three or four senior figures lamented that the music had seen its best days, and its remaining days were clearly numbered. That’s nearly 50 years ago, and history doesn’t appear to have borne out their worst fears. We’re still here. In larger numbers, in a widening diaspora, with improved support and resources. Convince me I’m wrong.

I think Seamus’ essay falls into the same trap, but he’s a bit more pugilistic than they were. They were resigned to their fate, he seems to have been in the mood to put up a fight. But fight whom? Lay in wait for Mícheál Ó Súilleabháin after a gig and hit him over the fingers with your flute? “Mind the keys, lads, mind the keys!”

I think it comes down to misunderstanding how traditions work. They are never closeted, safeguarded against change. They live daily with change. At the core, they have the stalwarts, those of us content with the tradition, only wanting to get deeper into it and enjoy it even more. At the edges they have the progressives, always wanting to try out something new. And the Show-ponies. Faster, more aggressive, sleazier, slower, new instruments, crossovers, etc, etc - we’ve seen it all go by.

Mostly these new-fangled fashions rise and fall, and leave not a trace. Occasionally, one of them will gain a foot-hold, and the entire body of the tradition will bend a degree or two to take on some aspects of the change. Nothing lost, something gained. That’s not a fail - that’s how traditions grow and adapt. You either adapt or die.

So, I reckon take Seamus’ essay as a bold statement of how really important the music is to him and his circle. We can all agree with that. But take his call-to-arms as over-reaction. Well intended, but unnecessary and unproductive. If a tradition can’t stand on its own feet, it’s lost. No amount of Cardio-Pulmonary-Resuscitation will bring it back.

Re: Access to Seamus Tansey essay

Tansey’s irritation with Mícheál Ó Súilleabháin is understandable in that it must have grated to hear an orchestral pianist opine so much, so academically, on traditional music. And then you have Ó Súilleabháin’s overwrought arrangement of tunes. Never mind the Irish Music Academy and acclaim that fell to Ó Súilleabháin.

But if that’s where Tansey was coming from, was he misreading Ó Súilleabháin’s own sense of his place in the music. A 1995 interview quotes Ó Súilleabháin as saying, “I said to myself ‘here am I stuck with the wrong instruments for this music that I love. I don’t play the pipes or the whistle. I can play the odd tune, but no matter how long I stick with them I’ll never master them. I’ll always be a piano player.’”

If that’s how Ó Súilleabháin really felt, then he wasn’t trying to change the music as much as he was playing the music that he loved with the tools he had. In that light, Tansey’s criticisms come off as overly harsh.

The full orchestrations of trad tunes, whether by Ó Súilleabháin or Lunasa or Martin Hayes, etc., don’t strike me as threats to or replacements of the tradition. They come across not as appeals to tradition but as attempts to reach an audience that might never hear a slip jig in its natural habitat. They’re more likely to woo some classicist over to the dark side than to dilute traditional music.

Over my lifetime as a fiddler, I’ve lost count of the violinists who’ve approached me and expressed a strong interest in Irish traditional music. Some of them went all in and no longer play orchestral music, just trad. And at least three that I briefly mentored have since graduated from the (forgive the multi-level oxymoron) trad performance program at Limerick.

Several fine violinists that now play only trad have told me that they were drawn to three primary elements of this music: (1) the danceable pulse and lift, (2) the beauty and emotion in the melodies, and (3) the simultaneously personal and social environment this music lives in. As long as these are the basis of the music and the tradition, it will live on.

"Seamus Tansey

Irish Traditional Music, the melody of Ireland’s soul; it’s evolution from the environment, land and people

Irish Traditional Music, for me as a flute player is and always has been a very emotional and fundamental subject. For the past 25 years of my life it has been part and parcel of my immortal soul. During that time I have tried to the best of my ability to emulate and captivate this melody. I have from time to time experimented with piano and guitar backing in the hope of uplifting and enhancing this melody and the powerful message it’s supposed to give to the people as a whole and this from time memorial. Sometime I have been pleased with the outcome and more times not. My concern is always with the suitable chords which either authenticate or they do not. My concern is also with the balance of harmonic backing in relation to the melody so as it will be at all times allowed to give its melodic message to history and from history. Irish traditional music is, therefore, essentially melodic and the harmony has to be at all times submissive and supportive to helping that melody to deliver its true message to the ears of the listener, past, present or yet to come. From the sound of the pipes, the sacred tabernacle of that music, to the humble tin whistle, the fiddle, accordion and flute, this law or rule applies equally and just as essentially to-day as much as at any other time in history.

Why should this be so we might ask? What has laid down the law in that respect? The short answer to that is the laws of nature and the law of the land which in this case is the island of Ireland. The laws of nature are the environment and the creatures of that environment since Ireland first began. The singing of the birds, the ancient chants of our forefathers the calls of the wild animals in the lonely countryside, the drone of the bees, the galloping hooves of the wild horses. The law of the land is the geographical location of the countryside be it mountain, valley, forest or plain. The wind the rain, the flowing rivers that shaped the mind and passion of our ancient forefathers, inspiring them to harness together all those sounds of animal, mineral, bird and insect so as it moulded itself into a melody which is and always will be. If we listen carefully to the pipes, the tin whistle, flute, accordion and fiddle even though all of them are international instruments with international origins, we can hear as clear as amber, unless we are tone deaf, musically stupid or plainly infantile, all the essence, evidence and influences of that environment.

If not, then where in the name of Christ in heaven did we Irish traditional musicians leam the technical know-how so as to emulate this native music as it has been for centuries? Where did all of us leam the ability to roll a note, to treble a note, to double, to cut and tip, to back stitch notes, to trill, to bark, pop, run and cran? Where did we leam to sound a vibrato as the pipers did? Our primitive forefathers had no records, they had no televisions or cassettes or CDs that we take for granted in Irish traditional music to-day. I put it to you therefore that it had to come from the throats of the birds, the wild animals, the ancient chants of our forefathers, the hum of the bees and the mighty rhythms of the galloping hooves of the wild horses all moulded together by the native genius of our forefathers who were deeply influenced by the geographical and regional terrain about them, the sun, the moon, and the stars did the rest. From darkest Africa to the wild prairies of America, from the land of the midnight sun, to the South American pampas. From Russia to Greenland, this law olds good and each tribe, nation and country derive their music also from their different environments, their different geographical locations and ethos.

That does not mean however, that each environmental or geographical message will be the same and the music moulded from those different environments and geographical locations the same either. They are quite different and meant to be different just like each nation’s culture, dress, history and language is different. You can’t mix them therefore, or any of them, or else you have a mongrel representing nothing or speaking nothing, just a noise or an obscene sound that should never be heard. In the case of Irish traditional music if you dilute, impose, cross-pollinate or orchestrate foreign cultures with that which is native and part of the soul of this country you will kill or else you will smother the very essence of our music and the environmental source from which it sprung and the telepathic message our fathers sent to us through history and we to further generations. You can’t impose jazz chords, therefore, the essence of Africa, on to the melodic structures of Irish traditional music and still retain its true message, its true meaning. You can’t impose bluegrass or country & western, or classical for the same reason as you can’t impose jazz. Each have a different message to give, a different history, environment, geography, a different bird structure, animal structure, different everything. Country and western emulates the call of the wolf, the sound of trains going through the lonely prairies. All beautiful when you hear it authentically. The cry of an eagle or buzzard in the darkness. Box Carl Willie, Johnny Cash and many others, even Elvis Presley proved that. The same is true for bluegrass. All of them a mixture of different musical cultures. That however, was a different kettle of fish altogether because the new nation of America was being bom even though it be a mongrel nation. The fiddle music of Sweden, Germany, Hungary and Scotland, the guitar from Spain, the brass instruments left over after the civil war played by the African Americans who emulated the music of the jungle. All a different thing because as I said before America is a mongrel nation. I say this without prejudice.

Nevertheless it is a fact and because of this her musical culture is also a mongrel. Philip King took far more back home than we gave to the American continent. The Irish have this tendency to exaggerate that wherever they are, or whatever they do, it is only we, the Irish, who have influence on finishing the great railroads or building the great dams and all the highways. We don’t think about all the other peoples who have been there as well from all over the world. It appears it is the same with “Bringing It All Back Home” and Philip King and company ran away with the idea that all this music he heard in the far west let it be country and western, blue grass or whatever that it all authentically belonged to Ireland. I was watching those programmes with interest. There was a very small input of Irish traditional music in this series of “Bringing It All Back Home”. There was far more Scottish influence, far more German and Swedish influence, Spanish influence and the Irish of course had their place who went out during the Famine. But I think we took a large big chunk home of what did not belong to us. The sound of the music and the fiddles was not authentically Irish, it was Swedish and German and Hungarian and Scottish, all mixed together. Anyway, the standard of fiddle playing in Ireland before the Famine and during it was not that high anyway to have any great influence on America. The pipes and tin whistles of course, they were a different matter and most of the pipers did not emigrate. In “Bringing It All Back Home”, therefore, we have been allowed to get away with murder and we should leave it all back where it belongs. It gives us a licence if you like, to think that we can copulate and mongrelise with every culture under the sun. What bullshit! A nation of bullshitters are we to think that. Put together by people who know sweet damn all about Irish traditional music and its authentic roots for that matter.

Now it is quite obvious there are an awful lot of people in Ireland, especially from music academies with classical background, who are academics, not necessarily of Irish traditional music, who are not satisfied with the existing perimeters of our culture and music. I would ask those people instead of remaining inside the family of Irish traditional musicians, they should leave. There needs to be a split. Go your separate ways. Form your own Celtic twilight sub-culture as an appendage to the Irish traditional music that we know and do what you like as long as you do not associate yourself with the music that we know and love and that has come down to us for generations and from the very environment of this country. I think that is the only solution to it. Tradition versus Change. If that is change i.e. the mongrelisation, the bastardisation, the cross-pollination, the copulation of our ancient traditional music, with other cultures, then I say we want none of it and I know I speak for thousands of traditional musicians, followers and music lovers up and down this country to-day. I wish the Micheal 6 Suilleabh£ins, the radical progressives, the stereotype revivalists, the best of luck in the future. Let them get on with it I say and we’ll get on with our programme and in the next century let future generations pass judgement on us today and let future generations at least have a free choice and not be robbed of their heritage and the music handed down to us by our ancient forefathers. From the pipe music of Patsy Touhey and Johnny Doran and many more travelling pipers of old. From the music of Kipeen Scanlon, Jim Coleman, Paddy Killoran and Jimmy Morrison who turned this music into an art form when everyone of us was just a twinkle in our father’s eye. If we can’t enhance what those people handed down to us with such incredibly high standard, let us not compromise it just because it is over all our heads and it is over all our heads at this moment in time and has yet to be developed and bettered by us. I would sooner see our music die completely in this generation than see it drip-fed by outside cultures treading the length and breadth of this land in the century to come. I was bom, brought up and came to manhood in the Coleman Country, the Coleman Country which is the Nashville of Irish traditional music, the mighty region of the whole of Co. Sligo, south Leitrim, north Roscommon and north Mayo which taught the whole of Ireland. It taught you all. Don’t ever forget it. In that region Irish traditional music was a sacred cult or religion. It still is by its musicians and music followers. Not a harmless bloody recreation for the amusement of infantile peasants buck-leaping and clod-hopping around a cross-roads. With that and on that I rest my case. This music of our immortal souls, and the music of that soul will never die when it echoes live through other souls eternally."
