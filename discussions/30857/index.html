Re: Does the many outweigh the few, or the one?

You won’t ‘gain fluency’ by playing lots of tunes.
6 weeks to master a tune is pretty good going, at least for me. I’m still finding things hidden away in tunes I supposedly learned forty years ago. I wouldn’t try to learn more tunes than you absolutely need to avoid so much repetition that you get sick of playing. Make a list, or arrange your tunes into sets, however small, and play them all with equal regularity, gradually adding tunes that you really like. If you don’t like a tune, stick it in a list of rejects, which you can go back to every few months to have another go.
The most important thing, I think, is to play as slowly as you need to in order to play without making a mistake. Increase the speed by a tiny fraction, and repeat. You will probably find as you speed up that all sorts of weird things happen to the rhythm, the swing, the mood, the dynamics and the phrasing, all of which is part of learning the tune -- which does not consist of a string of notes.
Above all, enjoy what you are doing, even if it is difficult.

Re: Does the many outweigh the few, or the one?

I think it’s better to focus on one tune at a time and learning it from a couple of different sources, preferably recordings. There’s no exact time limit though, some tunes are simple and can be learned in 10 minutes others can take months or even years, it depends on the tune and the technique it takes to play it. Go at your own pace,

The problem with trying to bolster your rep by learning everything as quickly and cursory as possible is that you end up with half-assed once through settings that could be useless outside of your regular circle of session friends.

Take your time and enjoy the experience.

↳ Posted by Mark Huppert on November 13th, 2012.

Re: Does the many outweigh the few, or the one?

I am of the philosophy that when you’re starting out, you should just learn the tunes that “speak to you”. If you’re learning tunes solely because they’re popular, or because you feel like you need to know as many tunes as possible so that you can “fit in”, you’re not necessarily learning them for the right reasons. (The worry that you don’t know enough tunes never goes away, just so you know).

If you learn the tunes you love, you will spend more time with them. If you love every tune you’ve ever heard, then just keep listening to them and learning the ones you feel like learning, and eventually the tunes will start to sink in, and you’ll be playing tunes that you never sat down and figured out how to play.

But if all you do is play a million tunes poorly, you’re not going to be one of the fun people to play with. If you go to a session, and only play 5 tunes all night, people will still be happy that you were there if you played those tunes well.

I learn tunes quickly, but I really only get familiar with them enough to really express them eloquently when they’re the tunes I love, the tunes that just automatically pop out when I am playing because they’re in my head. Otherwise, they’re just the tunes I picked up in sessions and couldn’t even think of starting on my own at home, without having some outside stimulus to remind me of the tune. And a few of the comments above are correct. You may “master” a tune, and be able to do all sorts of fun things with it, but 5 years from now, you might be playing it completely differently, because your own maturity with the music will have changed, and you may interpret the melody in a completely different way. (And that never stops either…)

BTW, a great way for me to find out how well I “know” a tune is to see how fluently I can play it in another key that I’ve never played it in before (even really slowly). If the consternation of playing it in a key where you can’t rely on the finger memory as an aid makes you get lost in the middle, then you don’t necessarily have a great grasp of the tune. Besides, that’s a real great mental exercise, and I highly recommend it. :-)

Re: Does the many outweigh the few, or the one?

There are some tunes that you just won’t like, ever.
There are some tunes that you might not like at first, but a happy insight reveals new aspects.
There are tunes that you love at first, but over time lose their attraction as your tastes and abilities grow.
My advice is only to play what you like playing. If you play just for the sake of joining in, you will not be playing your heart out, which you should always do. There is little point in playing a tune if you are not immersing yourself in it totally -- and by that I don’t mean playing as fast and as loud as possible before rattling off the next tune and the next. Give each tune the attention it deserves, and savour every bit of it.

This thread has got me thinking a bit more about session tunes played on fiddle.

I would guess that the majority of session tunes fall into the ‘easy’ category - reels, jigs, polkas in the major & related keys, many with similar bowings and repeating l/h patterns.

Then the reels in the flat keys - a little bit more difficult, as you ‘lose’ a finger. Also, tunes often played in G because they are session-instrument friendly, when they really are more suited to the bright fiddle key of A.

Hornpipes - many are a bit trickier, on account of the more demanding bowing and string crossing.

Then the odd few which have a reputation of being demanding - like ‘Dr Gilbert’ for its bowing, ‘Reel Beatrice’ for the key changes / modulations …any more?

Re: Does the many outweigh the few, or the one?

A tip for Humours of Westport is to tune your high f note to the open A string as a double stop. This is a minor sixth, but it’s the tonic chord so it will sound major, really lean into the note with a heavy long roll with that droning A underneath it, and then when you get into the B get into the swing of the backbowing and practice the transition phrase to each part over and over, I find the scales moving in thirds can be difficult and it helps to just practice playing F major in thirds up and down over and over. Playing in F is also about playing a major third between your first and third finger, it should be very close to playing E and G# in A major or E major on the D string or B and d# in E major or B major on the A string.

Re: Does the many outweigh the few, or the one?

@Earl : Humours of Westport, I roll using the notes F,G,F,E,F, fingered 1-2-1-0-1. Slightly different from you.

fiddlelearner, which version are you working on? The O’Neill’s 1001 is in G (are you roughly using that version, transposed up to A?) The Krassen version is in A, but he’s changed the second part completely, so that the highest note is G.

If you are doing the O’Neill’s version, or any of the versions on here, after transposing to key of A, the highest note is a C#, so 2nd position is easiest. Fingering-wise, it’s only a semitone difference in fingering.

So, you’d play the high A with your 2nd finger (not your 3rd finger, as in 1st position). That leaves your 3rd and 4th fingers free to hit the high B and C#. When it modulates back to G, your first note is an open E, so not really any shifting as such.
