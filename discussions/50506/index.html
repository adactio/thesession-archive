The Meaning of Having a Large Repertoire

I’ve kept up with my Tune a Day uploads on YouTube, and the number has now passed 600 tunes.

Even so, it still isn’t enough to cover all the tunes I might encounter at sessions. At the same time, I sometimes wonder: what is the real meaning of having such a large repertoire?

It’s true that if you don’t know a tune, you can’t really take part in playing it.
But on the other hand, since a session is essentially a social event, one could just as well spend a lifetime playing only a hundred tunes.

Fifty years ago, recordings and tune books were not so widely available, and people didn’t travel as much, so such a vast repertoire probably wasn’t necessary.

However, nowadays every time I join a session, I come across tunes I don’t know, and I can’t keep up with learning them all.

Some people are very eager to learn new tunes, while others are not.

One memorable moment for me was about twenty years ago in Miltown Malbay, seeing Cathal McConnell with a tape recorder, trying to capture and learn the newly composed tunes played by young musicians.

By contrast, Martin Hayes once said in a podcast that he only wants to learn tunes that truly appeal to him.

https://open.spotify.com/episode/3SMaQJBytBKvXWL1C7mi7x?si=ggtvYxsDRrqAdLpErRn2fg

So, what do you think?
Is it always better to have as large a repertoire as possible?
Or do you feel that there’s no real need to make an effort to expand one’s repertoire?

Re: The Meaning of Having a Large Repertoire

Hatao. I’ve been playing this music for half a century, in sessions across many countries, and yet in Ireland I can know all the tunes in one session and none at a session in the next county over. This is despite the fact that at the drop of a hat, I can play somewhere north of 1,200 or 1,500 tunes, and can join in on many hundreds more if someone else launches them.

Some years ago, in a long, late night session where, as it happened, yer man Martin Hayes was next to me, I noticed he sat out a tune, one that in my mind is a common, widely played session tune. When I asked him about it later, he demurred, saying, “There are thousands of tunes I don’t know.” Which is true for any of us, no matter how steeped we are in this music.

It’s not just new compositions. There are old tunes I’ve somehow just never gotten around to learning, and this too holds true for even the most experienced players.

All that said, I’m always learning another half a dozen tunes, that many a week if I can. And brushing up the old familiar ones. And finding ways to make stale tunes fresh again, or moving them to another key. But I enjoy the learning process at least as much as I love playing the tunes I well know.

Everyone has their own preferences, their own approach. I’m happy to play with someone who knows 20 tunes, and I’ll play them again next week, but my local sessions tend to cycle through hundreds of tunes, different ones each week and month to month. That alone helps to keep old tunes from falling off the edge of the known world.

Re: The Meaning of Having a Large Repertoire

As a soloist, there are a number of reasons why a large repertoire may be of value, and one of those reasons may simply be novelty ーThe more tunes you know, the less likely you are to get bored of or outgrow tunes. Also, since all tunes are unique for one reason or another, the more that you learn *to standard* the more proficient you become at your instrument. Music is heavily linguistic, and more tunes equally translates to more fluency/more aptitude. Again, given that you’re really learning tunes at an appropriate standard rather than never improving or correcting flaws found in your playing.

As a dedicated session player(or dedicated folk musician), the more tunes you know means the more people you can play with and more experiences you can take part in. There’s music festivals, renaissance fairs, themed events, etc. You can play in more sessions. You could have more tunes in common with the local musicians, or you could even have more tunes in common with visitors to your session. But a really cool perk of having a large repertoire, is being able to lead sessions, teach regular classes, and build your own community.

So what does having a large repertoire really boil down to? Probably being better at your instrument and probably being able to play music with more people. But maybe that’s how I see it because those are the things that are important to me.

Re: The Meaning of Having a Large Repertoire

“Having a large repertoire isn’t a goal for me, it’s just a by-product of loving the music.”

That’s precisely it, Jim.

As for quantity vs. quality, it’s not an either/or situation, at least not after a few decades of playing this music. Perhaps in the early years we spend more time learning and polishing the mechanics of our instrument, the trucklyhow of the ornaments, pulse, and phrasing of this music, and developing our own style or voice. But after a time, those things do reach “a standard.” So as you add tunes to your repertoire, you’re not starting from scratch. Of course, there’s always room for improvement—I am a better musician than I was 20 years ago, than I was last year, than I was even last month.

Each tune is an opportunity to learn something new or hone some technique or skill, so over a lifetime, a large repertoire doesn’t detract from quality but rather enhances it. The key is to play with attention and intention, learning to listen in ever greater detail and depth to the nuances and character of the tunes and ways (your own and others’) of playing them.

Re: The Meaning of Having a Large Repertoire

I have a fairly large repertoire, with somewhere in the neighborhood of 1000 tunes. It’s really hard to tell anymore, because it’s a combination of tunes I was taught, tunes I sat and learned on my own, and tunes that I picked up over the years just from hearing.

In the first 10 years I was playing, my repertoire list was curated, controlled, and documented. But over time, things change:

You get better at learning by ear so you pick up tunes in sessions, you get more familiar with your instrument(s) so playing something that you have in your head becomes easier, and you listen to more music whether recorded or in sessions so your brain recognizes more tunes. Combining those three things, I started to lose what organization I had for my tunes! Add to that the fact that you become known as an “experienced player” and you start to feel even more pressure when someone plays a tune you don’t know, and the next thing you know, your repertoire is huge (and messy).

For me, there is a big spectrum of “knowing a tune”. Different points on that spectrum might include:

* I wrote the tune
* I have played the tune for more than 25 years
* I sat down and learned the tune (or had it taught to me)
* I learned that tune a long time ago, but haven’t played it in a while
* I picked that tune up in sessions, and we play it regularly
* I know the tune in my head but have no idea if I’ve ever played it before
* Play it one more time, I almost have it (until the next tune comes along, and then poof!)
* I’ve never heard that tune before

As with any spectrum, there is a lot of fuzziness between the different levels of knowing a tune. And the more tunes you know, the harder it is to keep all the names straight, which adds to the fuzziness. And there is also some fuzziness in how well I play any given tune, which roughly aligns with where it is on that knowing spectrum.

So what having a large repertoire means to me is that I can play the vast majority of tunes at the majority of sessions that I attend, either at home or in Ireland, and when I hear a tune I don’t know, it’s easier for me to learn it. The other thing to note is that a large repertoire is a bit of a self fulfilling prophecy. You don’t get good at learning by ear or more familiar with your instrument without learning a bunch of tunes, and the easier it all gets, the bigger your repertoire gets!

On the flip side, I miss the days when I knew the name of every tune I could play, and I could focus on playing them all as well as possible…

Re: The Meaning of Having a Large Repertoire

Reverend, your post got me to thinking about the various phases of “knowing” a tune.

For me, a large repertoire means that I have:

- A handful of new tunes I’m actively learning. At this point in my musical life, this phase is fairly short—minutes to an hour or so per tune. But it’s when I sift through settings to settle on a “default” way of playing the basic melody.
- A host of tunes that are relatively new to me that I’m internalizing through repetition on my fiddle (at home and in sessions) and lilting them in my mind’s ear when I’m doing other things.
- A long rotation of tunes that I keep active by playing them, both on fiddle and in my head. Some have tricky spots that I like to woodshed on now and then so that they’re comfortable whenever the tune happens to come up at a session. Some are tunes that for some reason aren’t as sticky for me as others.
- And finally, an endless roster of old tunes I revisit to find new ways of playing them—new phrasings, new variations, maybe moving them into other keys, or simply playing them with a different feel.

These phases overlap and blend together a lot—e.g., learning a new tune or rotating through tricky tunes may spark a fresh variation or interpretation for an old tune. So even though each tune is unique and special, with a distinct character, together they eddy, upwell, and stir like one big ocean of currents.

A large repertoire gives you relationships with many, many individual tunes, but also with a broad and deep body of the tradition. Every tune tumbles against the others, every tune adds to your sense of musical context.

Discussion over? Well, I think there’re still a couple more reasons to be learning new tunes that are worth mentioning.

As Hatao noted in the original post, sessions are social events at their core. A pub session may be a 20th-century invention but that doesn’t take away from the fact that they are about the community, be it local or at large – and about sharing. Many of us here would struggle to imagine a session where no new tunes are being shared and picked up, where no one even bothers to ask for the name of a tune and where it came from. But then again, I imagine that an average session-goer is likely to be familiar with a setting where most musicians have at least a few hundred tunes they can follow if not start.

The reality can be very different at sessions across the world. If any of you have experienced a mild depression over the same-old few dozen standards being repeated over and over again for years in one place, you may find the premise for this discussion to be quite riveting. Who would not want to have a player with 600+ tunes at their session??

I think it’s best practice to keep learning new tunes from others at the session even if they don’t sound like your average dream tune. It may sound obvious but sometimes it feels like an unlikely task for a participant to learn a new tune from someone else at the session because they don’t care for the playing of others and maybe prefer listening to that one cool band with tight arrangements as opposed to the unfiltered solo playing. Don’t be that guy! Learning a couple of tunes to support a fellow session goer who keeps starting their set alone may sound like an effort but it will always pay off in the long run. You don’t have to immediately fall in love with the tune, it may grow on you as you adapt it to your instrument and style, shape it and unfold it to make it your own. And when several people take part in that creative process, there’s always a chance something beautiful and new may come out of it. Supporting someone else’s tunes may mean a world to them – but regardless of that, it always involves creative work and adds to one’s experience.

Last but not least. Sometimes it feels like a very important aspect of traditional music is overlooked when the discussion focuses on the excitement of playing in sessions and the joy of collecting new popular tunes. Traditional music needs to be passed on – it wants to be passed on. Sometimes it requires a bit of work and persistence on one’s part to learn from the source and then share the music faithfully. But that’s part of a long game. An advanced mode if you like! Learn new – or some very old – tunes just because they need to be heard and maybe even given a new life, no matter where you are. That’s as fine a reason as any to be learning more tunes – even if you know a few thousand of them already.
