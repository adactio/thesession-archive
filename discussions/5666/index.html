Re: About playing tunes by their lonesome or in medleys…

I find this a really interesting question, Armand. Any musicologist types out there who actually have any idea of the truth?

Now you might argue that if you were playing for a set (dancing) you couldn’t possibly play one tune all that time. But of course you could, it’s just a strange idea to us. And if you did play one tune for a long time, you would probably begin to improvise, which would be even more interesting.

I’m gonna digress her, but stick with me. As I understand it from folklorist friends, Balkan music used to be played in such a way that musicians knew and traded many stock phrases of maybe 4 or 8 bars-ish) and they had a system of taking turns to intruduce these as they played for dances which might be quite long. There were phrases everybody knew, more regional ones, some parts of songs, some associated with individuals, people made up knew ones, etc.

Then with the rise of recording in the 20th century, and later the government funded “State Folk Ensembles” these more and more got organised into “tunes” with A and B (and usually in Balkan music C,D and E) parts and a “title”. Now most folk musicians perceive this as the norm and don’t question it - although improvisation is still important. Some older players remember how to do it the old way, but it’s probably dieing out.

Now I’m not suggesting that this is exactly what ever went on in Ireland because I don’t bl**dy know! But it does show how fast the “norm” can change, and I’ve often wondered at the number of “stock” turnings that keep showing up in many different tunes. Maybe there was once something a bit more akin to the Balkan approach.

It’s also interesting to note that in many other parts of Europe (including England) playing a tune for many repeats, perhaps the duration of a dance, is much more the norm. And also interesting to note that many English barn dance bands now copy the Scots/ Irish approach of many tunes per set, but this is quite a new approach. I think many continental trad bands are also beginning to copy the Scots/Irish “norm” of medleys because our music has influenced and inspired them.

But don’t get me started…

Dancing the music - - -

You were right Armand, whatever your source, in Cape Breton, as in Eire and elsewhere, one tune to one dance, as it is ‘dance music’, was the norm. The recording industry wanted tunes stacked, a kind of attitude of ‘more for your buck’, and this is likely the main influence that promoted set building, and even repetition limits, as well as one of the pushes influencing the rise in tempo.

Others have brought up ‘shared’ traditions with similar practices - Old Time music in North America, French, Breton, Scandinavian - - - and someone caught it before me, the ‘competitions’ in Scottish piping that lead to those type of sets, which also carried over to Cape Breton - stringing Strathspeys, reels, marches together.

The worst travesty I’ve come across, and I hate it, was where it was all sets, and a tune was only played once in a set, “See how many tunes I can string together in a given time!”

For dance, one of the most satisfying things is hearing the dancers leaving the floor or at the end of the night singing the tunes they’ve been dancing to. You need to get familiar with them for that to happen, and a few times I’ve had folks come up and request dance by the tune the exptected belonged to it - easier with Balkan or Breton dance - at least you’ve a fat chance of putting the tune to a dance.

However, in defense of sets, there are times when you can fit a change to a change in a given dance and it elevates the experience for the dancers, a bit like giving them an adrenalin rush jsut as they’ve started zen-out on you, as someone earlier mentioned. That is dead easy with 16 and 32 bar dances, for example, but can also screw up a dance by throwing the dancers off - “What the hell was that?” And, it can be done really badly, and yes, I know this personally, I’ve been guilty.

I remember often doing a good set of repeats and we were all in the swing and suddenly someone decided to change and if it were a bus changing lanes, we went off the bridge and crashed and burned. Once, at least 36 of us going and taking turns, we decided absolutely no sets and to take old tempos, like 130-135 for polkas, and everyone got high on the repeats, and the dancers and audience were even singing some of the parts, a bit like a rock concert where the musician turns the mike to the audience on the choruses. Now that was really in the groove and flying.

With some tunes tightly associated with a dance form or even a particular set of figures, the agreement between dance and music - when you’re playing them you can feel the moves of the dance, see the dancers when you close your eyes. It is definitely Zen to me…

Both can be fun, one at a time or in sets, but if a set I definitely prefer to get into the groove of a tune and with my fellow musicianers, to get down and comfortable with it, have fun with it, before making a move to take on something else for awhile…

Whew!

Sets - - - sets - - - sets - - -

disinformed - misinformed - uninformed -
Sometimes things are so quickly past over that we miss the connective tissues that make some semblance of sense. Without that ‘sense’, well, things can be open to all kinds of interpretation. Also, in the ‘writing’ we are without inflection or a face, as someone recently reminded me, and we can screw up, or not clearly get our intentions across.

Anyway, ‘set’ comes in to a lot of play, encompasses numerous use.

1.) - as met in the start of this thread, stringing different tunes together, like 32 bar jigs, or whatever, but also as brought into the discussion, the performance habit of putting different tune forms together, like following a strathspey with a reel, or as in contra dance music, following a 32 bar set of jigs with 32 bar reels, for what some call ‘excitement’, and a few do it the other way around - reel into jig time.

2.) - in dance - sets of figures, or quadrilles, as for a Cape Breton ‘square set’, made up of several figures with breaks in between them, as with that related type of ‘set dance’ in Eire/Ireland - or New England ‘Quadrilles’ - some types being ‘The Quadrilles’ / ‘The Lancers’ / ‘The Saratoga Lancers’ / ‘The Caledonian’…

3.) - sets of tunes for these dances… There were tunes so tightly wound up with these dances, ‘sets’ for sets, that every move was augmented by a corresponding move in the music, and not just the same number of beats to the shared phrase, but down to things like slowing down or speeding up or pauses in the music to complement the dance. Then new ‘sets’ came into vogue, names associated with a ‘new set’ of tunes to fit the dance, like the Lancers. Some names were for example ‘The Dublin Set’, ‘The Belfast Set’, ‘The Jenny Lind’, etc…

With each of these many sets of tunes for sets of quadrilles, it was one tune per figure. You’d do the first figure to its one melody, and then there would be a pause in the music an dance before the next set/figure of that particular set of quadrilles. The dance didn’t change, the set of tunes did, but still one tune per set.

Also in Cape Breton, in early descriptions of the step dance, influenced also by English and Irish dancers, not just Scottish, things tended to be referred to in the singular with older sources - “He danced a hornpipe.” Sometimes even the preferred tune would be mentioned. So even there, early on, there seems to have been a division, rather than the now common strathspey leading into a reel. Even in the early stages of that, it was more usually, from what ‘little’ I know, that what was preferred was one tune per form, so one particular favoured strathspey leading into one particularly favoured reel. The earlier dancers of this tradition also preferred the musicality of their contribution, like a good percussionist, to follow more than just filling in beats, to follow the phrase and complement the melody, while giving it some variation like a musician would want to do.

We lived in Cape Breton for a time, and attended a lot of dances all over the island, and music gatherings, as well as taking in several regular classes. While there are place names associated with the sets, they are also known, or were by the carriers of this tradition, by their earlier source names, like “The Lancers” and “The Saratoga Lancers”, to name two related sources, and those same sources for the sets in Eire, and for the evolution into square dance across North America.

The island was far from detached from the rest of the world, despite the fondness for focusing on the ‘Scots’ roots. There are many roots. The set that became popularly known as “The Down North Set:”, one that the fiddler Mike MacDougall played regularly for, was brought in via the rails. A lot of Cape Bretoners travelled all across North America for work, a few of them brought back Western Square Dancing, the early type, and that was adopted. That also happened with ‘steps’ for the sets, and such borrowing has never ceased. And then there’s the influence of Jerry Lee Lewis on the piano accompaniment. Hey, it’s a living tradition, not stagnant, though there is that fear now with the growth of mythology and all those various forms of ‘information’ mentioned earlier.

The sets were called, and many musicians, including Winston “Scotty” Fitzgerald, preferred that, liking that some figures did not get to rediculous lengths as happened as that traditions faded away. One of the things that killed of the ‘prompting’ of Cape Breton sets was ego. As I’d said, they were connected to the rest of the world, including close ties with the Carribbean islands. Well, as square dancing developed and the ‘patter’, what some saw as bullsh*t, grew, the words and the caller’s ego started to get in the way of the music. The ‘old’ style was just basic prompting, letting the dancers know what was to follow, reducing the words used, and then giving them up as the dancers got comfortable with things. It also helped to time the moves better to the phrases of the music, also preferred by many of the older musicians, and it helped things to round off neatly at the end of the figure and then the dance. I added to things rather than taking away, it originally helped communication. Sadly, the commercial got to Cape Breton, like the musicians’ union, and some callers got a bit too big for their britches.

Some musicians grew with and adapted to some of those changes. They had their share of input from the vinyl disks too, and ‘visitors’, as still happens. A lot of Irish and New England and Canadian Old Tyme, and others regularly visit the island, and have for a long time, and Cape Bretoners return from away, with all the other ‘come from awayers’ and things change, and sometimes the popular tales attached to it all.

Hornpipe or strathspey, the steps are now interchangeable, some came from English dancers, some from Irish, one dancer of the past was of Lebanese descent, others were Micmac - and there have been Quebecois fishermen and others from that realm visiting for a long time - and the Accadians from all over, and some French from France and elsewhere… It is really quite cosmopolitan…

Where did I learn the dances and music of Cape Breton, well, I hope this answer it some. I also did quite a lot of research, mostly preferring the oral variety, but also books and old newspapers and wherever I could find it… I agree, I am an imperfect filter, but I am still open and learning. I don’t tend to take it as gospel, so I can only offer how I’ve seen and found it, but realize I am imperfect, as are we all… I haven’t stopped learning…

Doubt frees us - ‘c’

For the old ‘tradition’, one thing that was true was that they didn’t see limitations like “That’s not Irish!” as of an value in limiting their love of dance and music. Many fine Irish musicians also dabbled in the dance band move, one old sod loved in Donegal also played sax in such a band, one badly beat up old sax too, but he had fun and like many of his ilk didn’t take himself so damned seriously. All this said, the ‘tradition’ was strong enough in an area to persist, at least in most part, despite any ‘foreign’ intervention or borrowing.

The crime in the modern sense, a bit like those bewailing the influence on cultures across the globe from the ‘commercial’ or big business, multi-nationals - is that the media proliferated by whatever means can tend to swamp some of the better parts of how things were done and considered previously, to overpower in part that ability to ‘survive’ in essence no matter what passes through. The pablam sold by the media is so persistant. So, if you are raised on a diet of just the session and competitions - you might miss the ‘dance’ in the ‘dance music’ you’re playing. If all you see is the TV version of anything, you might miss that it can’t be really an ‘objective’ view on the world about you - every word and image has ‘choice’ behind it, and someone making that choice for a reason.

Media and mythology can cover up or cloud our roots - our connection, understanding and appreciation of all that ‘tradition’ meant, good and bad - like one of the aspects I value most highly, humour. One of the best dancers I have known, was so because of his humour and heart. Stepwise and figure wise, well, we could laugh about it. It is nice that there are people like Armand discovering and appreciating those older ways, like with tempo and carrying a tune for a nice stretch to get familiar with it and have some fun with it and the others you might be sharing it with.

Our biases, preconceptions, expectations, preferences - are based on our experiences - those things that have influenced us in our ways. The sad part about most ‘collections’, recorded or printed, is that you can’t, or it is damned difficult, to instill it with those other precious elements when the content and intent is just an amassing of tunes or dances - they are limited. For some whose experience is limited to such things, their experience and expression of say ‘Irish tradition’ can be a bit too antisceptic or single-minded. While others, born completely out of context, can pick up those elements by the grace of their family and acquaintances - and breath that life into their ways in and around music.

Slowing back down, to take things in a bit more, as with a tune, also means you can start to listen to any others joining in, and listening is always a good thing. Next, the pauses in between, places to share a bit of yourself and experience something more of those others, and a laugh, the social ‘craic’. Music is just a really nice part of life, a part, as it is just a part of something larger in my scope that I think of as ‘tradition’ - as I’ve been fortunate enough to have experienced, on many levels and several places. It is nice to see it here too, even in the digital realm, with some I’ve had the pleasure of chatting with and laughing with, including old friends, and including having shared a few tears, confusion and ‘outbursts’…

Lonesome or in medleys…

Yes, I knew I was forgetting another step up. When the Dance Hall Act of the 30s came in, that bastard DeValera, anyway, the halls were built and in the countryside some of the larger set dances grew with the hall sizes, such as the “Paul Jones” mixers, couples all around the hall, and some progressive “Sicilian Circle” type dances, where instead of just up and down the hall, couple facing couple, or doubled as for the Seige of Ennis, you could go on for ages. Sets were called for. WIth some of these dances the musicians would focus on say two couple facing and play until they were back facing one another again. So a 32 bar dance for a 32 bar tune could go on for a long haul - and it was a welcome break to change into another gear/tune. For that sets became a God send.

In the smaller gatherings, such as the house dances, and for sets and couple dances and country dances, where there were say only four couple on average - the individual figures and dancing could do quite well with just the one tune each. As numbers grew so did the length of time for a single stretch of dancing and up went the repetitions. I’ve played quite a few large dances - six times through each tune in a set of four for example…

Go raibh ma‘agat ’Sharon the Flute‘ for helping the ’balance’…
