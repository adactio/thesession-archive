Versions of tunes ?

Just thinking…. while traditional musicians are generally happy to recognise a ‘Donegal’ version or an ‘East Clare’ version of a tune, does that equally apply to an individual’s version of a tune?

Say for talk sake Joe McBloggs came up with (and recorded) a version of Drowsy Maggie and the first half of the tune was the usual one but the second half differed dramatically, is that legitimate within the tradition and only to be judged by the quality of the music or not?

If I understand your question correctly, I’d say yes, it’s common—if not usual—for people to reference their source ie. “This is the Kane Sisters’ setting of a Paddy Fahey jig” or “This is the Gearoid O’hAllmhurain’s version of The Boys of Blue Hill.”

Or, “Let’s play Paddy Canny’s setting for The Cliffs of Moher in D dorian, and end on the A part.”

Or, “The Banshees Wail Over the Mangle Pit” which is really just Matin Hayes’ setting of “Young Tom Ennis”.

There’s Aggie Whyte’s Chattering Magpie, and Gillan’s Apples said to be from John Gillan’s version of Apples in Winter.

Re: Versions of tunes ?

I’m often a bit amused at discussions here or just around the table about the “correct‘ versions of tunes. Like all folk traditions what gets played in one place or by one player is a reflection of what someone thinks he remembers that he thinks he heard someone else play somewhere or sometime else. Or, in the case of noted music (ABC, or ”the dots“) it’s a transcription of the same. Of course there are many tunes that have been written by still active players and the respectful thing to do is to learn the tune from the original…if the writer remembers with precision what he wrote! It starts to get more complicated and diverse the farther we get from the original especially when the original may be decades to centuries old. Even the much touted O’Neill’s, I mean that with great respect, is a collection of memories and memory is both fragile and fallible. We all know that tune names are often a moving target and some of the time different versions are in fact completely different tunes. Add to that the deliberate adaptations of tunes for stage and recordings, from which many players get their ”version", and the complexity grows exponentially.

So…it’s certainly no wonder there are multiple “versions” of any tune in the Irish tradition just as there are multiple versions of any tune, or song, in any other genre. Irish musicians haven’t cornered that market. I just wish that when I hear someone say “there’s a lot of different versions of a tune” or “there are a lot of different ways to play that ”, the unspoken sub-text isn’t often “but MY way is the MOST RIGHT way”. From a historical or musicology perspective it could be fun to track a tune back to it’s roots but I doubt that most of us have the ability to do that. Maybe the best use of our time is to sit around the table together and play what we know in a way that makes us happy. The Universe won’t care.

In the bluegrass community there’s a joke. “How many banjo players does it take to change a light bulb?”

Just one…but there’s 8 more behind him saying’ “that’s not the way Earl woulda done it”.

Re: Versions of tunes ?

First of all, it’s possible that tunes can share short phrases without being related.

Lord Gordon’s Reel apparently was published in Ryan’s Mammoth Collection (1883) (source: http://www.tunearch.org/wiki/Lord_Gordon%27s_Reel ), and Salamanca in one of the early 20th century editions of O’Neill’s ( http://www.ceolas.org/cgi-bin/ht2/ht2-fc2/file=/tunes/fc2/fc.html&style=&refer=&abstract=&ftpstyle=&grab=&linemode=&max=250?isindex=salamanca ). Of course, this doesn’t necessarily mean that Lord Gordon’s is older than Salamanca, nor that they’re related at all.
