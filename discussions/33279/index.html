Re: DADGAD guitar accompaniment

you mean chord progressions? for the most part they should be the same but sometimes dadgad chords are neither major or minor. There may be other considerations like chord substitutions that you might use to avoid using a capo in less common keys. You might use a G chord DBDGBD in place of B minor for instance. B minor can be hard to reach without a capo, and if it is just a passing chord in the key of D it is often times easier to use that G chord, possibly just not strumming the G string if it stands out, in which case you are just playing a partial B minor chord. Or you can modify it by fretting at the third fret on that string (if your fingers can do it?).

Re: DADGAD guitar accompaniment

jeff, from wikepedia
DADGAD was popularised by British folk guitarist Davey Graham.[1] Inspired by hearing an oud player in Morocco, Graham experimented with detuning some of the guitar’s string from standard tuning (E2A2D3G3B3E4), arriving at D2A2D2G3A3D4 or “DADGAD.” He employed the tuning to great effect in his treatments of Celtic music, but also the folk music of India and Morocco. The first guitarists in Irish traditional music to use the tuning were Mícheál Ó Domhnaill and Dáithí Sproule; today it is a very common tuning in the genre. Other proponents of the tuning include Russian Circles, Rory Gallagher, Luka Bloom, Stan Rogers, Jimmy Page, Artie Traum, Pierre Bensusan,[2][3] Eric Roche, Midnight, Laurence Juber, Tony McManus, Bert Jansch, Richard Thompson, Dick Gaughan, Alistair Hulett, Soig Siberil, Gilles Le Bigot, Imaad Wasif, Jeff Tweedy, Masaaki Kishibe, Paul McSherry, Kotaro Oshio, Ben Chasny, Al Petteway, and Trey Anastasio. English folk musician Martin Carthy now mostly uses a related tuning, CGCDGA, whose explicit evolution from DADGAD he describes in his book.[4]

The suitability of DADGAD to Celtic music stems from the fact that it facilitates the use of a number of moveable chords which retain open strings.[5] These act as a drone on either the bass or treble strings, approximating the voicings used in traditional Scottish and Irish pipe music. Carthys comments on DADGAD are informative,“I found it confining as i could only play in D” Whereas in CGDA Icould play in 5 keys [CGFD]and possibly a minor, Carthys words not mine

Speaking about the 22000x chord (aka Em11), i could write it 22000* because the 1st string is not really muted,it’s simply let alone, not played. Pratically, I avoid it on down beats and I play it on up beats.

So on upbeats I avoid the 6th string (big D) and +/- the 5th string. I play the Em11 this way: **0000. It’s actually a G(add9) and a D sus4 6

Without moving a finger you have at your disposal a handful of shimmering notes to play with. Playing DADGAD tuning backing you may not think strong and full chords, you can think “irish bouzouki” tuned GDAD.
