Acidic Content in Lemons

Lemon juice is acidic because it contains citric acid, a tricarboxylic acid. Lemons are about 6 percent citric acid.
Citric Acid

Citric acid is formed through a cycle process. In the first step, biomolecules break down into smaller molecules. In step two, glycose and pyruvates produce CoA (carbon intermediates). In step 3, CoA gets transformed into carbon dioxide. Citric acid is produced as a byproduct.
Benefits and Risks

Although lemon juice has a high acidic content, it is beneficial to the body because it balances acidic and alkaline levels. When lemon juice enters the body, it is acidic. However, when it begins to break down, it raises the body’s alkaline level. Balancing the body’s pH level results in a healthier immune system that is able to function properly.

Citric acid can thin the blood over time if it’s consumed in large quantities. In addition, people who have a highly acidic stomach should avoid large quantities of citric acid in their diet because the body cannot break down the chemicals fast enough.

How Alkaline Foods Are Classified

Alkaline foods and acid foods are called alkaline or acid based upon the effect they have on the body AFTER being consumed. Anecdote time:

When I was quite a bit ill a few years ago the specialist in charge of my treatment gave me a bit of advice that I thought a odd. This bloke is a top scientist and doctor and we often had conversations about science and medicine. But he said to me, right at the beginning of my treatment, “Try to have a positive attitude.”

I laughed and scorned the science of such advice. But he went on to explain that while he knew the science of most of the complex concoctions of medicines he was prescribing me, there were some where there was little to no understanding and that his only real reason for the prescription was that statistically, they work. i.e. the evidence for their effectiveness went far beyond mere anecdote because the sample sizes were so large that it sat well within the range of scientific probability theory. He went on to explain that his job was not just about cellular microbiology, etc, but he also had to know a great deal about probability theory. And that his advice about having a positive attitude was not mere wishy washy feel good sentiment, but practical science based clinical practice. Statistically, after stripping out and accounting for many other variables (something one can only do when sample sizes are very large) those patients with a positive attitude survived longer than those with a negative attitude.

More anecdote time:

Yesterday (Saturday) I was playing in a big classical concert in Bristol. We had a 3-hour dress rehearsal, and a 2-hour break, and then the 2-hour concert. This particular orchestra has the policy of doing a concert (including yesterday’s which had an international soloist) on the basis of just three rehearsals shortly before. It’s a tough schedule but it works with the right conductor, saves a load of money on rehearsal time, but leaves precious little room for mistakes.

My desk partner and I together made a basic error: we accepted an invitation from three players in the brass section of the orchestra to go to a nearby pub for a haggis and a pint during the break. Now haggis is one of my favorite dishes, and I like a pint of ale as long as I’m not driving. I normally have one pint during a session, sometimes two, because I live close enough not to need the car. So I thought nothing of the pint I had with the haggis, neeps and tatties. The reality during the concert was that I was making occasional silly mistakes in fingering and bowing that I didn’t make during the rehearsal, though fortunately nothing obvious or embarrassing, and I felt more tired than I expected. My desk partner had his own set of problems arising out of forgetting to bring his reading glasses up from the green room and consequently having to push his chair back about two feet from the music stand in order to have any chance of reading the music. And at the end we sight-read Eric Coate’s fantastic “Dam Busters” March as an encore.

Moral: don’t accept pub invitations immediately before a concert from brass players; splendid fellows they doubtless are, but they know nothing about violin playing. In case you’re wondering, I’m well used to the 3+2 hours rehearsal+concert setup, so I’m sure that one pint beforehand was enough to tip the delicate balance. All this of course wouldn’t have really mattered in a session because sessions and concerts/gigs are as different as chalk and cheese anyway.
