noble savages?

I know that there have been many threads on reading sheet music versus playing by ear, but I haven’t seen one about whether the great players of the past were musically literate. Now, I know that a piece of music exists regardless of whether it’s notated anywhere or not, (“The map is not the territory”--S. I. Hayakawa) but I wonder if maybe the reading skills of some of the greats were perhaps under-reported, to make a better story. (“Gee, he plays so well, and yet he can’t read a note--how about that?”) Would some of you musical historians care to enlighten us on whether some of our heroes could read the dots or not?

Re: noble savages?

No music can be accurately notated with ink on paper. The dots are just a convention among people who understand what sounds the dots represent. Much like the written word, and most of us don’t parade our illiteracy as a “badge of pride.”

Much is made of Irish music being an aural tradition, but it’s not that simple. Yes, you have to listen to this music to understand how to replicate its sounds. But I can’t imagine a musical genre that isn’t also true for. And yes, the tunes are often and widely passed along from player to player by ear. But the music has also been passed along through numerous printed and published manuscripts and tune collections, some dating back almost 300 years, and also through the handwritten transcriptions of many players, both in dots and various abc and number-based systems. As a method for saving tunes from oblivion, writing them on paper has actually been a revered part of the tradition, practiced by some of the music’s most conservative and protective practitioners (such as Brendan Breathnach). In my mind, a “badge of pride” based on ignorance is better left hidden under your lapel.

Re: noble savages?

Mick, you’re an aspiring fiddler, right? You might also enjoy the book, The Northern Fiddler, a bit of a sociological/academic study of past generations of fiddlers from Northern Ireland, including the Byrne brothers, John and Simon Doherty, Con Cassidy, and other fine players. Take the sociological mutterings with a grain of salt, or skip over them entirely, and you’ll get a lot out of the interviews and storytelling in the fiddlers’ own words. The photos and drawings bring places close to life as well.

But even better than books are the stories today’s musicians will tell about their influences and mentors. You’re close enough to Seattle and Portland to touch base with any number of players living there who are immersed in the tradition. If you really want to play this music well, it’s important to get grounded in the community of traditional players and through them into the music of previous generations.

Re: noble savages?

I was just flipping through Blooming Meadows and noticed several points that may or may not be of interest. Lucy Farr, Mary Bergin, and Paddy Canny all read music but prefer/preferred to learn tunes aurally. The bio of Peadar O’Loughlin says that in his native Kilmaley-Connolly area of Clare (on the road between Ennis and Miltown Malbay), most of the tunes were learned from written manuscripts. (It doesn’t specify when or by whom.)

I also read or heard somewhere recently an interview with a student of Padraig O‘Keeffe (probably Denis Murphy, but I’m really blanking on this one - it’s getting mixed up in my mind with an interview I read with P.Joe Hayes who said something similar). The gist was that O’Keeffe taught tunes using an “unusual” notation on standard staff paper. The gaps between the five lines represented the four strings. Numbers in those gaps indicated which finger to put on that string. I recently met a beginning fiddle student in Rhode Island who was being taught using virtually identical notation, so it must not be so unusual after all.

As for modern players, most that I’ve met read music to some degree. I’ve had classes with Jackie Daly, Joe Derrane, Patty Furlong, John Nolan, and Billy McComiskey. Billy was the only one who didn’t hand out sheet music. I’ve heard that Liz Carroll can write the dots almost as fast as most people can read them. When I asked Josephine Marsh for a quick lesson on using the basses/chords, she wrote out a tune for me in what was essentially ABC.

In any case, I respect musicians who can read music but prefer not to, and musicians who can’t read music and never felt they needed to, but not those who proclaim that they’re better musicians because they can’t read music.

Well, like it or not, written music is firmly established in the tradition. We only argue about it because we, individually, have a choice whether to read or not.

It’s hard to imagine that anyone who likes the music enough to learn the tunes from the dots would not also seek out recordings and performances and thereby learn how it should sound. The choice of read/not_read is no more critical than the choice of listen_to_Gavin/listen_to_Burke.

Re: noble savages?

But think of all the music that has been lost because it wasn’t written down. We hvae very little music from before the 9th century that we can actually interpret. They wrote down the words, but not the music. Or they notated it in a form that is so obscure (to us) that we cannot interpret it.

Some of the ancient Greek music, like that of the Epitaph of Seikilos had musical notation that has been interpreted. Who knows if it’s how they meant it to be. They used a form that could be described as similar to ABC’s, representative letters, with slashes and lines to mark rhythm, but the chromaticism’s of the quarter tones are all guessed at, as is the tone of the music.

Yes, music is aural, and yes it is written down, the greatest thing is when the two are combined so that you can see it and hear it, and learn to play it to pass it on. I prefer to learn my fiddle music by ear, I learn best that way and many tunes I’ve never looked at notation for. Try doing that with a Mozart sonata. Langlais, a blind organist and composer learned Bach one line at a time by memory, but not all of us are capable of doing that. So notation is neccessary to assist in the learning of a tune, but even with my Mozart, I listen to lots and lots of recordings.
