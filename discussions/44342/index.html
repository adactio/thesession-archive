Re: Parts

I came from Highland piping where it’s “first part” and “second part”… except with the pipers who call parts “measures”. (Parts are called “measures” in the classic COP Green Book which has been the standard tutor for over a half-century.)

In orchestral scores etc letters A, B etc are used to designate different sections, though these sections can be of any length and often don’t correspond to the ITM concept of “parts”.

Seems to me the advantage of using letters for the parts is that you can use “1.” and “2.” to designate “first time” and “second time” without confusion. Not to mention you’re literally on the same page with orchestral players, if you find yourself amongst them.

@Mark M: Sure, we understand “bars 9 through 16”. I got the commonly used “A-part, B-part, C-part” jive from Leonard Bernstein. I believe he was considered a “proper musician”. Either way, we’ll understand you. Fewer keystrokes in “A-part”. Save energy, save the planet.

@Jim Dorans, Fiddleologist
Typically, Jig: strong ictus on 1, weaker on 4. BUFF-alo, Buff-alo
Typically, Reel: strong ictus on 1, weaker on 5. LOG-arhythm, Log-arhythm
Hornpipes get played a lot of ways, but essentially are a syncopated 4. Think New Orleans jazz, but not as much so. Lots of dots-and flags, deh-DAH deh-DAH, as opposed to a lot of Scots music, flags-and-dots, DAH deh, DAH-deh.
Add that to a bit of subtle rubato (unless you’re Martin Hayes, in which case, ladle the rubato on straight from your soul) and, if you care to, add a little backbeat (in this case, the same beat out of six or 8 gets empathized a tiny bit each bar or every other, to create a very subtle counter-rhythm. Leave this for later when it comes naturally. It gets overused by recording artists, and is something post-1960. But it’s cool.
*Finally, and most importantly, don’t emphasize notes VOLUMETRICALY, at least not at first and never a lot. This is historically and thus traditionally non-dynamic music, though these days it’s a bit more so than it used to be. Emphasize notes by STRETCHING THEM IN TIME. Just a tiny, tiny bit, not a real dot, more like a half-adot, max, and preferably a little less.
All this becomes apparent when you know the note sequence of a tune down pat, and then LISTEN LISTEN LISTEN to experienced players (not necessarily celebrities, though that’s OK too). Older, pure-drop reputation players are especially valuable for this, but any good player can be a useful model for “the nyah”, the combination of efficient tone and relaxed rhythm, regardless of tempo. The magic is not in the mechanics.

Re: Parts

One thing I didn’t mention about orchestral scores, the music used at studio gigs, “legit” music in general is that they try to avoid repeats and write things out fully, when possible.

So…a piece of music that Trad players would think of as being AABB, then play the whole thing three times

AABB x3
or
AABBAABBAABB

might well be written out in a “legit” score with sections labelled ABCDEF with A, C, and E identical and B, D, and F identical.

I have “lead sheets” written for accompanists for some of the pieces I play (just did a couple at a Christmas concert) and I’ve found that repeats can be confusing for organists and pianists. I’ve written the pieces out with as few repeats as possible, and trying to avoid first and second endings, tags, and so forth. They much prefer having things written out in full.

↳ Posted by trish santer on December 25th, 2019.

Re: Parts

For orchestral scores it’s easier to coordinate things if you avoid repeats. As Trish has pointed out, the full score for the conductor will have minimal repeats if any to avoid awkward page turns and, anyway, there may not be any repeats in the full score.
Problems arise when you extract the parts and try to condense those parts using repeats because that will mess up the bar numbers. I know this from experience. There’s a lot of extra effort in getting the numbers to match. Which is another reason for lettering sections.

Re: Parts

Nicatnite- I’m guessing the reason is the human ear likes repetition, and hearing something it’s just heard before (the reason so many pop songs are verse chorus verse chorus etc rather than a long stream of unrepeating parts) - even if you’re hearing a tune for the first time, you can enjoy hearing the repeat knowing how it goes. Certainly it took me years of not being particularly into classical music (which doesn’t tend to repeat the same) to realise I like it more if I listen to stuff I’ve heard a few times before rather than stuff new to me.
Another reason could be because repeating cuts down on the amount of music required for dances - without repeating parts or tunes, playing a ceilidh would require the band to know more and to be more on the ball for seeing what tune comes next. Also in a session, if someone goes into a tune during a set, it may take a few bars for folk to recognise what tune it is, so would be ashame for those bars not to come round again.
I have noticed second parts tend to be higher, and feel most of them sound like second parts, though that could just be out of habit of hearing them that way. Certainly when there’s a tune like My Cape Breton home, where different musicians vary on what part they start with, I can’t help but find it sounds backwards and weird the other way of playing it, but they’ll think the same about my way of playing it. And in that example I start with the higher part, and hear that much more as a first part.

Re: Parts

That reminds me, there’s a Highland pipe 4-part hornpipe that only has three parts!

They play A B C B, using the second part as the “turn” both of the first and third parts.

(In Highland pipe music it’s standard to play tunes only once so you don’t have the issue of remembering a long string of repeats.)

It’s composer laziness in my opinion. Couldn’t he think of anything for a 4th part? When I play that tune I do a variation of B to create a D part.

Same thing in O Farrell’s Welcome To Limerick: many people play the B and E (2nd and 5th) parts the same. I don’t. I play the 3rd beat of the first bar C for the 2nd/B part and D for the 5th/E part. I think the repeated hammering on D helps drive the tune home.
