“Have traditional musicians no ambition beyond the session?”

Some interesting reading for your edification. And some ideas worth discussing.

http://journalofmusic.com/focus/have-traditional-musicians-no-ambition-beyond-session

I have noticed in the last five or six years more and more sessions where I live becoming people’s little fiefdoms. Not all, but more than there used to be. Infrequent are days when I walk into a strange session and people are curious and friendly. “A new person…. I wonder what tunes they know. ” Now, more often than not (and I only speak for one city), regulars playing in their little cliques eye newcomers askance and “open session” really means mates of the session leaders. Or famous people.

I dunno. The scene here has changed and not for the better. People moan about the demise of certain sessions, which were at once good and famously welcoming to all and sundry, but that atmosphere can never be revived so long as musicians are territorial and prickly. I think Rossa, the whistle player who wrote that article, is onto something.

Re: “Have traditional musicians no ambition beyond the session?”

So a guitar player and bodhran player walk into a session (sounds like the start of a bad joke) and are upset because they weren’t welcomed immediately and warmly? They probably wouldn’t be welcomed in our sessions either. There are precious few accompanists who do credit to the music, IMHO, and fewer still who can walk into a strange session and make the music better.
Nor do I see what is so wrong with the “‘fit in or feck off’ attitude that permeates so many sessions…” A certain amount of “etiquette-driven, rigid experiences, full of unwritten rules and regulations,” is necessary in any social gathering. The strong players at a session don’t have to be rude, but encouraging a chancer to whack away at his chosen instrument can lead to the ruin of an otherwise pleasant evening.
While it’s true that the punters at the bar don’t often listen, and often drown out the music with loud chat, I am not there to play for them. I am there to pass a pleasant hour with my friends, as are the punters with their friends. We are playing tunes and exploring musical ideas, and they are talking and exchanging their ideas. Sometimes there is a connection between the two groups, and sometimes not. If I can make a few bob in the process, that’s great, but not essential. It’s up to all of us to make it fun. And if I don’t enjoy it I can go home and write an article decrying the state of the hostile world.
The writer finishes by saying that “If Ireland’s traditional music is to continue to thrive, musicians must broaden their range of performance settings …” The article was written in 2004. I think that Irish music has been doing just fine over the past 13 years, despite not being in great demand at funerals, weddings, and at times of rest.

Sorry if my post pointing out the date was unhelpful. The article seems to be about a different time, place and perspective than you describe.

Your assessment of the change in the scene may be objective, but I think the ‘outside looking in’ and ‘fantastic on the inside’ perspectives are appealing to our understanding of the subjective side of things. Most people have experienced them in some context and can empathise.

However, you seem to be missing some of your details: “infrequent are the days”, “people moan about the demise…”. So it’s not that it never happens and if other people moan it is probably not that the session scene is ‘full’ and you are the first one that came too late. Would just one session with a friendly face or two, familiar from the past or not, make a difference?

I occasionally think “why am I doing this?” and the first part of the answer is that I enjoy the music as a listener. I made various self-motivated and sometimes short-lived attempts to learn an instrument before playing with other people even occurred to me. So I guess I thought I was going to enjoy doing it. For airs, song tunes, Carolan, most waltzes and so on that I am unlikely to play with other people that is the case.

For dance tunes I am not so sure. Playing them on my own is usually ‘practicing’ with the aim of playing them better, or at all, with other people. There is also an element of it becoming a habit or part of a daily routine. I know that because sometimes it takes me a few days to get back into the dance tunes after a break from routine. And it may be an upcoming session that kicks me into action. So it probably is sessions that I am playing dance tunes for, mainly.

The linked article seems to relate to some sort of commercial aspect that has no interest to me other than a local pub welcoming us on a quiet night so that passers-by see something happening inside. When a “session tonight” advert goes up I get uneasy. I used to feel awkward about a few sandwiches or chips - until I noticed that they appeared on darts night or when people gathered to watch a match on the TV.

Re: “Have traditional musicians no ambition beyond the session?”

These days, I find that fewer sessions seem to suit me.

While I enjoy playing both Irish and Scottish music and some from beyond, I have a preference for the older repertoire although I also enjoy the better modern tunes. Also, although I’m not too snobbish about these things, I prefer sessions to be be slightly above the lowest common denominator in terms of standard and overplayed tunes. If I can’t play all the tunes in a session, that doesn’t really matter as long as I listen and learn for the next time.

As regards many sessions these days, I will exclude myself because of the repertoire. I also find that many of the younger musicians sessions will often comprise either newly composed or obscure material which is only familiar to the players who meet regularly. This is just a reflection of where the music seems to be headed these days. Generally, the musicians will be welcoming and friendly enough but there’s no real point in sitting down with them if you can’t cope with what’s going on in terms of repertoire, speed, and so on.

As for the more traditional sessions which might, on first impression, appear a little cliquish I think you just have to have a little bit of patience. I wouldn’t usually join a strange session unless one of the musicians asked me over or it was fairly obvious that plenty of players were “coming and going”. In some sessions, it may even be necessary to build up a little trust with the regular musicians, e.g. just going along to listen for the first few nights, making some conversation, and mentioning that you play a bit yourself. I’m sure an invitation will come in time as long as you don’t mention a guitar or bodhran… ;-)

Re: “Have traditional musicians no ambition beyond the session?”

I honestly think the cold shoulder approach is a sign of weak session leadership, and it’s unfortunately something you find a lot where I live. If you don’t want someone in your session, you can always take them aside and gently but firmly explain your reasons, all the while inviting them to listen or maybe directing them to a session better suited to their level/style. If they’re a learner on a melody instrument, throwing them a bone for a set of tunes will work wonders (I’m sure all of us have had the relief of suddenly knowing a few tunes in a strange session), and may make their night enough that they’ll happily sit in silence for the rest of it.

Just giving the evil eye and not talking to them makes the whole thing much worse. They don’t enjoy it, you don’t enjoy it, and everyone goes home unhappy. By taking the initiative, you may “hurt their feelings,” but you’re also ripping the bandaid off quickly. You’ll be hurting their feelings anyway by ignoring them.

For example, I asked to join in a session in Galway not long ago. The fiddler pointedly asked, “can you play the tunes?” and seemed wary until I named a couple that they had just played. A few sets later, I was a part of the group, and everything was grand (they even had me stay late for drinks with them after hours). But she did turn away a bodhran player and a harmonica player who just wanted to do his own little showpiece for the crowd. You could argue whether they should have let everyone and anyone in, but saying no straight out was probably better than the passive-aggressive approach.

I actually didn’t find the first paragraph objectionable as it relates to the paid session scene in Ireland. There are many other kinds of sessions, but the “pay a couple people to play a session every Tuesday” ones are generally a gambit to bring in tourists. That puts unique pressures on the musicians, who are now not playing for themselves but for an audience, and must keep a level of musicianship up. It’s a quasi-performance that means both making certain capitulations to commercialism (many have a guitarist who can belt out the “Wild Rover” or “Will Ye Go, Lassie, Go?”) and keeping the openness of a session a little tighter than normal. A lot of the comments here come from a different kind of session, one organized primarily for the musicians, but the article makes a lot more sense when viewed as a critique of the more narrowly defined, semi-professional session scene.

I thought from the headline that he was actually going to take a very different tack, and point out that there are tons of musicians for whom the session is their only musical output. I’ve noticed that a lot of musicians who are very capable session players are not great in a solo performative sense, which many would argue is the “purest” sort of traditional music playing. Things like tone and rhythmic drive get smoothed over in a session setting, and deficiencies in those areas can be hidden unless you also play by yourself a lot. I’m not saying that only playing sessions is bad, but I could see where some would take exception to it.

Re: “Have traditional musicians no ambition beyond the session?”

It’s pretty hard to have ambition outside of sessions when there is absolutely no public demand for this music. (at least in my area). On that end, having players join a session who really shouldn’t be playing in public, really doesn’t help the music’s reputation either.

It’s true what he says, “…but with everyone playing at once it confines itself to the one particular sound, meaning many will not get a chance to express their own musical imagination.” I’m not sure what he’s suggesting though. Are we supposed to start trad societies, where everyone signs up for a solo? Where’s the craic in that? It’s surely not going to do anything for the music’s popularity. Sharing tunes simultaneously with other people is the beauty, exhilaration, and craic of sessions. This experience of sharing this music, that moves me like no other, of being joined by other musicians playing our f-ing hearts out—that’s what gets me through my work week, through my life right now. For a some of us, it’s our church, our community, our “spirituality”.

If getting gigs, and making money at this was our ambition we’d all be miserable. It’s not popular. People want songs, they want words, they don’t listen to instrumental music unless it’s classical, and it’s not “dance music” without a DJ. They are disappointed if the music isn’t presented like River Dance, or Celtic Woman. I once heard a punter make this remark about session musicians, “They are all just way too serious, it’s a downer”. Well, the Music is serious business, and serious craic! Seriously, you can’t expect flute and whistle players to be trying to smile while they are playing? F***k the punters.

On the other hand, what’s really alarming is the diminishing number of public places willing to host sessions, and that is tied into the music’s popularity, and to whether it’s bringing in money (so maybe f**k the punters, isn’t the answer).

He might as well have asked whether we should have some ambitions outside of ITM altogether, rather than spending all our free time learning tunes, and going to sessions. But that would be like asking an addict, who has no intention of reform, what ambitions he has other than his next fix.

Re: “Have traditional musicians no ambition beyond the session?”

“‘Playing along’. This is often said in a disparaging fashion but surely, in most cases, all the musicians are doing just that with the exception of the person who is leading the tune?”

Personally, I make a distinction between ‘playing along’ and ‘playing *with*’: the former is simply playing the same tune at the same time (hopefully *in* time); the latter is where there is mutual communication between the musicians - irrespective of who has started the tune, each player responds to the other’s playing, subtly affecting the phrasing, melodic variation, ornamentation etc. It is relatively rare that such multilateral communication occurs between everyone in a session, except in smaller sessions (up to 5 players, say), but it can happen in larger sessions within smaller subgroups - and there are sometimes magical occasions even in larger sessions when *everyone* is on the same trip (so to speak). Some musicians are, I think, better than others at engendering this scenario (the late, great Peter Horan comes to mind). This distinction is, of course, highly subjective - you might feel like you have an intimate musical connection w ith noted musician X across the room, whilst noted musician X is completely unaware of your presence; but it is significant, nevertheless, since it changes the way you play.
