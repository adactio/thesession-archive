Can we still play all these tunes?

With this new system, I get notified when a member downloads a tune which I submitted sometime in the past.

Sometimes, I wonder if I actually ever knew the tune myself but when I have a quick look at the music I can usually heave a sigh of relief and say “Oh yes, that one!” I can also remember how to play it after two or three times.

Last night, I was looking through an an old folder(s) which contained loads of tunes which I played at one time or other and there are many more like that. Usually, they’ll come back to me very quickly but they just don’t seem to register on my radar on a daily basis.
To an extent, it depends a lot on what gets played in the sessions you visit, the tunes which are currently “in vogue” and on my own current favourites which change regularly.

A well known poster here once said “There are too many tunes already” and, maybe, your current repertoire is all you actually need at any one time(until it changes, of course) but I can’t help fretting sometimes over my “lost tunes”.
So, it’s always nice to welcome some of them back into the fold from time to time especially as there are some others (like family members) which can sometimes outstay their welcome.

Re: Can we still play all these tunes?

Something I started doing when I first started to play folk music and am still doing is that I keep a scrapbook of tunes that I like. When I first started, I was worried that I would lose track of all the tunes that I knew because I’d forget I’d even known them.
So now, every time I hear or learn a tune in a session or a recording or a performance, I find abcs for it or notate it myself, and stick it in my scrapbook with its name, who I learned it from and in what circumstances, who it’s by and where from if I know, and any other little anecdotes about it. It’s a mixture of tunes I know and don’t want to forget and tunes that I’d like to properly learn someday. And so whenever I need a tune, either for a band rehearsal or for practice by myself, I flick through my scrapbook and find one I feel like learning or relearning. And when I am in sessions, my brain is refreshed by remembering all those tunes I’d looked at earlier.
Sometimes I flick through and find an old tune that I used to know but don’t play any more, but I no longer worry about forgetting old tunes that I like because I’ve moved the contents of my brain, as it were, to something that doesn’t forget as easily. And the comments and stories make nice reminders:)

Re: Can we still play all these tunes?

I used to keep the tunes I learned in books, printed out using PrintMusic, now called Finale. Later I started keeping playlists of MP3s, made using Finale and Magix Software, which were just like glorified midi tracks but not so bland. Then along came ABC, and now I simply collect all the tunes I know into folders of about 45 minutes each, some arranged in sets of three, with the tunes playing three times through each, and others just collected under ‘reels’, ‘jigs’ or ‘hornpipes’, which I just loop until I’ve had enough, or in another folder entitled ‘learning’ which contains sub-folders according to the speed I can comfortably play the tunes at. As I learn a new tune, it moves through the various sub-folders (sometimes going backwards again) until it goes into one of the ‘learned’ folders. Every day I play along with as many of the folders as I want, cycling through all the tunes. including slow airs, and that way nothing gets forgotten (I think) and the tunes get associated with each other, the way the tracks of a record do, which helps when playing in a session. Whenever I hear a new tune I like, I write it out in ABC as I’m learning it, and Bob’s your uncle, as they say.

I do notice that a surprising amount of players are not confident and competent in this aspect of the music and sit out. I wonder why, too much book learning? IMO the most important thing is actual practice, so by not trying, people limit themselves. Of course some instruments are more forgiving in this regard ;quieter low octave yokes while some ;whistle pipes etc are not so easy to ‘fit in with’ .

My advice is to start at home with easy tunes, ie not trad, simple songs and the like and slowly progress to more complex faster tunes like jigs and slow marches. Listen to the tune your trying to pick up a lot , hum it catch single notes and build up from there.
The most Important thing IMO is being familiar with where the sounds you hear are found on yr instrument so ultimately the sound you hear is directly reproduced with millisecond delay on the instrument without conscious thought. Its like a direct line between fingers and ears that you need to develop.

good luck

Re: Can we still play all these tunes?

Firstly, Na éisc, bear in mind that I’m a grumpy old git, and Randy is right - I shouldn’t really have made such a sweeping statement. Even so, over the last ten years (say), having been to many sessions all over the UK, including Wales, most parts of England and Scotland, and also many sessions in other European countries and the odd excursion elsewhere, I’m frustrated to find that, at least in my experience, hardly a single one has remotely got the rhythm right, when any kid in most parts of Ireland will just … do it. And yes, it’s across all tune types. And it’s really really simple - it’s the pulse. People get so worked up about ornamentation and getting little bits of rhythmic nuances (but not the whole pattern), and being clever (oh, and things like how many tunes you know) … and they forget that the one thing that really matters is the pulse. Phrasing’s vital, sure, and other aspects of the music are of greater or lesser importance, but without the pulse you might as well pack up and go home.

Recently, I’ve had a particular thing about jigs. The old jig rhythm seems to be becoming a thing of the past, and it’s a shame. But hardly anyone can play polkas, it seems, hornpipes are either flashy or dull and featureless and even good old reels are being ironed out into something lacking in zest and life.

Anyway, don’t mind me. I’ll cheer up in a bit. Even grumpiness isn’t what it was.

Re: Can we still play all these tunes?

I don’t think the situation in the UK (or the USA for that matter) is as dire as Ethical makes out. There are plenty of good sessions if you know where to look, and there are plenty of crap ones too. A Weedgie even won the All-Ireland fiddle competition a few years back. It’s true that there are probably more good sessions in Ireland, but that’s because there are more sessions generally, and more people playing the music. They have their dross as well. I’ve found myself at more than one rubbish session in Ireland. But that’s the nature of sessions, isn’t it? Some of them, either tacitly or flagrantly, demand a certain standard and others are complete free-for-alls intended to give anyone and everyone the chance to participate. And I wouldn’t whinge about the free-for-alls. They are giving people the chance to play, folks who just want to dabble in the music and have a bit of craic, but haven’t time nor inclination to do the work and improve their standard of playing. And they’re not in your session. I’m not really into that kind of session, but I don’t begrudge the fact that they’re out there and people enjoy them.

At the end of the day, it’s okay to be sh i t at something and still enjoy it at your low standard. It’s easy to be snobby about those people who only play 25 tunes, but they probably have something better to do and don’t want to work that hard at Irish music. I’m a crap mountaineer, but when I’m not being terrified, I really enjoy it. No one ever says, “What’s the point if you don’t ever want to climb K2.” I have friends who are planning a Himalayan expedition, but they still speak to me. :-)

Like Randy said, I certainly wouldn’t tell the sessions in the Ivory or Sharkey’s, or Sandy Bells for that matter, that they don’t understand the rhythm of Irish music.

UK folk festivals, however, are probably not the best indication of the state of Irish music. You might get lucky and get a great session at one, but they to tend to attract the hangers-on, the goat-bashers, spoons clatterers, six-string devil blooooze botherers, all in one place. If you find the right quiet local session, you’ll get good music.
