Distortion?

Re: Distortion?

It’s a natural phenomenon. There’s a lot of high-frequency noise generated by the bow on the string, but fortunately it doesn’t carry more than two or three feet, but of course is very obvious to the player. This is why the fiddle always sounds better when heard from a little distance, and is one reason why the fiddle is one of the most difficult instruments to record in the studio or to use a live mic with. Also, some strings tend to produce more high frequency noise than others. I’m told that new dominants are notorious for this (I don’t use dominants).

Some of the frequencies produced by the fiddle tend to come out in directions specific to those frequencies, so if you have the mic too close it’s going to get an unbalanced mix of frequencies and therefore a distorted sound. This is why in a recording or broadcasting studio the mic will be some distance away. I remember seeing on tv a studio performance by Yehudi Menuhin of a Beethoven sonata. The microphone suspended above him was visible only in long-shot. The BBC, when broadcasting a symphony concert may well have just two spaced mics suspended high above the orchestra (plus perhaps another one for a soloist), and the sound is realistic and balanced.

BTW, an upside of growing older is that one loses a bit of high-frequency hearing, so bow hisses and scratches tend to be less obvious. I can attest to this :-)

Re: Distortion?

My ears are very sensitive to high frequencies. Unless I’m playing onstage with good monitor sound, I stuff cotton in my left ear when I fiddle. Not enough to deafen the ear, but enough to muffle the screech. It lets me hear myself in the mix much better and makes practice more satisfying.

This sensitivity is a nice benefit when I do audio engineering, because I can hear, and adjust, those high-frequency elements that have a subtle effect on listening pleasure. On the other hand, listening to a pipe organ recital can be painful. That instrument is capable of creating some really powerful high-frequency overtones that feel like needles in my inner ears.

Re: Distortion?

Upper-hearing loss doesn’t, from my own observation of elderly colleagues in orchestras, appear to present problems in listening or playing to music. As long as you can hear the top note on a piano (4KHz) or a bit above I don’t think you’ve got much to worry about as far as making music is concerned. Perception of pitch and dynamics aren’t usually an issue either.

Some years in my chamber orchestra we had an excellent leader of the violas in his eighties (now no longer with us, sadly) but he usually used a hearing aid. If you watched the good gentleman when the conductor addressed the viola section during rehearsal you’d notice that he would turn off his hearing aid and sit there nodding wisely to the conductor’s (inaudible) comments and instructions, and then, when the playing restarted he’d turn the hearing aid back on and it would be business as before.

There are financial advantages in not being able to hear high frequencies (above 8 - 10KHz, say). You don’t need expensive top-of-the-range audio equipment.

This could turn into a very existential discussion. Yesterday I made the mistake of recording myself on my laptop using the computer’s internal mike and Garageband software. Nothing fancy. And wow, was I dismayed. I didn’t think I sounded THAT bad. When I just play and listen to myself, my perception is that I sound better than what I heard when I played back the Garageband recording. In my despair I called a piper friend (who lives in Florida) and, through the wonders of speakerphone on my cellie, played for him. He did not think I sounded terribly off, though he was hearing me through the distortion of the cell phone.

So… the question is… how do I really sound? Do we ever know? Which is more accurate? What you hear, what someone over a cell phone hears, or what Garageband outputs? How accurate is the computer? Is it distorting something about the tone quality of the pipes? We’re hardly talking state of the art recording equipment here. Now, I’ve heard pipers better than me playing my chanter, and it doesn’t have that grim grim tone I heard on Garageband. But they’re also better than me. When I ask other people who are in the room, the answer I usually get is, “You sound fine.” Which might be true or might be them being nice.

What is reality, after all?

Re: Distortion?

A few months ago at a session I’d just been to the bar and was on my way back to my seat when I stopped to say something to one of our best fiddle players. I was right behind his left shoulder and quite close to his left ear, in fact our heads were almost side-by-side. And then someone started up a tune and he started playing. It was then I realised that I was hearing his playing as he was hearing it - I was that close - and it had far more sounds in it than were apparent to me a minute later when I returned to my seat about three feet in front of, and facing, him.
If I may be existential for a moment, some people could claim with some justification that the only place you can “hear” the best performance of music is in your head. I’d go as far as to say that what you play in the real world can only approach but never quite attain your mental audio image. Clearly, if there isn’t much of an audio image in your head then your real world performance isn’t going to be particularly convincing, no matter how good your technique. Conversely, you may have a superb, well thought audio image but your performance may be limited by technique (which may encourage you to improve your technique, btw) or by other factors beyond your immediate control.
I’ve known more than one musician who has claimed he gets more out of reading and mentally “playing” a music score than actually listening to a recording or even a live performance. If you think about it, this is what a good conductor has to be able to do before he confronts his players. And the greatest performers and composers have always done it.

Re: Distortion?

I hear sounds in my head all the time without any sound waves to stimulate them. My dreams usually have a sound track (all original music), and I go around all day with tunes reeling around in my head. I don’t have to hum or lilt or noodle them on an instrument. All it takes is neurons firing in the right sequence. This may be based on prior exposure to the sounds that stimulated the neural sequence in the first place, but my mind can recreate those sounds--and change the pitch, tonal qualities, and tempo--all without a single air molecule even shifting in its chair or scratching it’s little head.

I suspect lots of people’s brains are preoccupied this way, and nothing pleasant would ever come out of my fiddle if I didn’t imagine it first.

Re: Distortion?

Bob, I think the point is that it *is* possible, at least for some of us, to imagine better music than we can play. I’d go so far as to say that this is the primary way I improve--I imagine tone qualities and timing, etc., and adjust my playing techniques and feel to move closer to that imagined ideal. In fact, on variable pitched instruments such as fiddle and flute, this is the foundation for decent intonation. You *have* to have an accurate representation of the pitch in your head to be able to match it accurately on the instrument.
