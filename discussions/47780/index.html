Playing tunes you already know is quite a good idea but you often will find that you will be playing them differently(not necessarily wrongly, of course)!

I forced myself to learn reading “dots” when I first started going to fiddle societies.
I’d also found it useful at The ALP too because, although the official policy was to teach tunes “aurally”, many students who already had access to the music would “cheat” and ear players started to learn to read just to keep up!

Anyway, if you are already an ear player, it’s a good idea to play from the dots while still listening to the tune at the same time. The harder the tune the better(within reason) as it’s often the case that you will have already learned it by ear before you’ve worked out the dots. :-)

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

I learned to sightread by simply getting a big fat tune-book and sitting down with it on a rainy day and doodling through, tune by tune.

I pretty much knew how to finger all the notes, but all those little dots behind the notes, and bars under and over the notes, were mysteries.

I would tunelessly bumble along, but every now and then it would begin to dawn on me that it was a tune I’d heard!

And so, slowly, I began to get the feel for what those dots and bars meant in terms of how the tunes sounded.

Soon I could sightread a tune I’d never heard, pretty much at full speed.

That was in the 1970s when I was new to ITM. If I had been an experienced session player I think it might have hampered me, actually, because I would have recognised the titles and known straighaway how the tunes sounded. My process worked because I had heard only a fairly small number of ITM tunes and I didn’t know the titles of any of them.

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

I posted somewhere else the main technique I used to get sight reading quickly, but in general its a lot easier to start with a few notes, say pick D,E, F and G then go through a lot of tunes, just playing those 4 notes when ever you see them, ignoring rhythm until you instantly can go from seeing the note D to the fingers on the instrument. Then you just add more notes one by one over the course of a few days the basic linkage in memory occurs, then its just practice for speed and rhythm.

It’s more difficult to try and learn all the notes at once, as they don’t all fit in short term memory.

Another, maybe seemingly obvious, point….. It’s useful to know where the notes are on your instrument when starting to learn to read the dots.
Of course, even ear players will likely know the scales on their instruments and where/how each note can be found. However, it’s not something we necessarily think about consciously all the time either.

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

If you know what the dots, key signatures, etc mean you are a third of the way there. If you also know what finger positions on your instrument relate to the notes on the stave you are two thirds of the way there. If you don’t know that then you should be able to find info online. The other third is practice.

When I did this on the fiddle I had loads of tune books. I would play through a tune, once only, and go on to the next. At the end of one book I would move on to the next. I did thirty minutes a day and marked my place with a pencil and go on from there the next day. If you have never done this before maybe just ten or fifteen minutes at a time will be enough.

The point about doing a tune once through is important because if you repeat it you begin to learn it and stop actually reading the tune.

It can be a hard old slog at first, slow and unrewarding, so short sessions at first can be a good idea.

Something Ross mentioned but I didn’t see elsewhere in the thread that worked for me: WRITING music notation. I’ve been able to sight read very very slowly most of my life since we learned recorder in 4th grade (and maybe I knew a little before then since we had a piano in the house), but a couple years ago I discovered MuseScore, and it’s been a huge help for *reading* when I use it for *writing* my own melodies. A big reason is that you can have it play back what you’ve written down. At least that’s how it has worked for me.

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

There’s no secret. Buy any flute method book from a music store and go through it page by page. There will be minor fingering changes, but nothing consequential if you’re already playing a simple-system flute.

There are also good books on tin whistle that are methodical about reading music and simplify the process by teaching the same fingering as ss flute, with less emphasis on key signatures you seldom use for Irish.

When you say “sight read,” I’m assuming you’re not really wanting to play at tempo from a score, but simply be able to read through a tune until you learn it.

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

Word usage often strays from purely technical definitions. As I understand it, “score” can be used to mean any printed music notation, even that with only one part shown. It tends to imply multiple parts because the term derives from the vertical score lines used to connect the staves when showing more than one part (e.g., the melody and bass parts for a piano piece or instruments in an ensemble). But that doesn’t stop (even formally educated) musicians and composers from referring to a score no matter how few parts are shown.

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

Gobby I mostly agree with you about the term “score”. For sure there are the exceptions of piano, organ, and guitar music, but the definition of terms isn’t a hill I’d die on. If we look closely at a score we see that it includes a lot of other information about how the piece should sound. Italian words like “fortissimo”, arrows like < and >, string and finger choices ( for guitar), and a lot of other “road signs” that make a piece more playable all a far cry from the pedestrian string of dots we see in most tune-books. That’s why I find the term “dots” rather dismissive and don’t use it. As an aside I think that’s one of the reasons tune-books and playing from them gets such a bad rap, but that’s a whole other discussion. The closest thing we get in books is maybe a hornpipe or strathspey written with actual dotted 8ths. Don’t get me wrong I appreciate and use this “sheet music” all the time. Still I recognize it as the simplest form, open to a lot of interpretation. I suspect there are some technical issues that make preparing a score for publication problematic. Since I don’t have a better word for this middle ground (would lead sheet work?) I choose “score” only to mean something more crafted than what the tune books offer.

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

>>Never thought i would see the group who vehemently discount sight reading embrace it with such passion and good spirit.

Maybe it’s just because we’re all sick of the ear vs. dots (sorry, Ross!) debates! As has been pointed out many times in the past, the thing people rail against the most regarding sheet music in Irish trad is that a certain number of players who already play other kinds of music try to initially approach Irish trad from the sheet music exclusively, and think that they’re playing it right because they’re accurately representing exactly as written. Most of us have experienced players that play all the notes with the wrong lilt and no ornamentation, and it can be jarring. So I think most of the vitriol surrounding the dots here comes from that.

But in regards to the OP, I will just say that players that I know that are both experienced ear learners and sight readers have told me that they initially retain the music better when they learn it by ear. And I think that is likely because playing from the dots is a learned skill that emphasizes playing what you see, instead of driving the tune in your head and playing along with it.

I certainly won’t tell anyone not to learn to read music. It is a very handy skill to have! But I guess how handy it is for expanding your repertoire depends on how well you retain the information, instead of just regurgitating it with your instrument, which is what most sheet music training teaches you to do…

I thought I’d offer my own thoughts about ornamentation and variation. That topic seems to have gotten a good airing on this thread. Would well have merited its own topic. Interesting points raised above.

If I can’t play a base melody well and with good phrasing, rhythm and tempo - then ornamentation and variation is the proverbial lipstick on a pig. If you don’t have a feeling for the tune absent ornaments and variations, it’s hard to appreciate the enhancement value of the extra layer. Step 1 to learning the tune is learning the tune cleanly.

The ornament should be considered an OPPORTUNITY not an IMPERATIVE. Yes, if you’re playing for a number of years you know that you’re likely to tap between those two EE notes, roll that GFG or do a cran on that DDD. And perhaps if you’re really good then ornamenting at the same time as learning the tune isn’t a bad thing. If you HABITUALLY play GFG as a G-roll, then reverting occasionally to GFG will sound better - almost like an ornament or variation.

I went through the 500-blinking-lights-on-the-Christmas-tree phase two or three years ago. Mindlessly applying cuts/ taps/ rolls and other wonky things in virtuoso abundance, just because I could and had learned how to do them. At some point overdoing it diminishes the tune not enhances it. Thankfully I’m over that and trying at least to do the basics right.

Doing the same ornamentation at the same points in a tune every time may be competent playing but to a >>discerning listener<< it is boring. Even to an untrained ear, when you hear the greats play a tune you often admire (a) simplicity but with outstanding colour/tone or (b) subtle yet delightful twists that you can hear but can’t explain. A discerning listener may consciously appreciate the techniques. A keen but less well trained ear can nonetheless recognise unconsciously that a particular playing is especially pleasing even if not knowing fully why. And there are others who just don’t get it, and don’t want to either.

It takes a very sensitive ear to pick out the banjo flicks or flute licks across a noisy pub session. I’d venture that most pub sessions would be best played plainly but well (with everybody “on the same page”). There’s no harm in each individual striving to play their best version of a tune, regardless of whether everybody else is focused on them and listening.

Ornamentation and variation are especially valuable for SOLO or small ensemble (performance or recording) playing. They alert us to the creative possibilities and emotion within a tune. They allow a phrase or section to surprise or delight. There are plenty of pure-drop unfanciful players who are top-tier with little or no ornamentation or variation. But the skill is definitely admired, intrinsic to the character and idiom of ITM. Command of the technique is worthwhile, regardless of how much or how little it is applied.

Ultimately it’s about control. The ability to do it at the right spots, the ability to recognise consciously that it’s been done, and the ability to NOT do it at a later point. Less is probably more; a lot is probably too much.

Re: Want to learn to sight-read music (for flute) - how do I go about it?

I learned to read music sometime around 1971. My recollection is that I started by identifying one note on the staff - in my case C in the middle of the treble staff (as I was playing a classical C flute) as this was the simplest fingering on the instrument. From there the method book talked about rhythm a bit, but then gradually expanded the range of notes to include ones around C (probably below C first so that I didn’t have to worry about 2nd octave notes). It was a slow process, but after about a month of weekly lessons and daily practice, I was feeling more confident. It probably took six months before I was reliably identifying (and fingering and sounding) the lower 2 octaves, and another six months to develop the embouchure enough to worry about the 3rd octave.

I have been playing a wooden flute a lot more than a metal one these days, and my comfort sight-reading notes below the staff and above high C has declined for lack of use. Moral of the story: use it or lose it.

It is wonderful to be able to play along with a tune immediately from the dots (if they are available), or to use the dots to figure out a passage I am having trouble picking out by ear. But, as has been said by many previous posters, only the bones of Irish tunes are found in the dots and familiarity with the genre is critical to being able to play a tune with an appropriate style and lift. The way Irish tunes are played almost always differs from a strict reading of the dots, in part because ornamentation is not usually notated, and in part because the rhythm subtly differs from what is shown - as notating it precisely would be a royal pain and make reading the tune more difficult (too much ink).

Comfort with sight reading will improve with practice - so keep at it diligently for at least several months. And speed will come with time and comfort. Some tunes are easy to sight read at full speed the first time - others not so much. It all depends on how familiar the melody patterns and rhythms already are to the player. So hang in there - you will appreciate having gained the skill!

“8) Still don’t know how to go about reading the rhythm (ignoring the melody). Indeed the bigger challenge is interpreting and applying the correct duration/rhythm to the notes. Perhaps focus on melody for now and then work out a way to read and apply weight. But at least I now recognise this as a distinct (and essential) second part (and suspect that it will be the harder of the two).”

Good point. My suggestion with Irish tunes is know which tune type you’re playing from the start. I believe I mentioned Mary Bergin’s workshop where she covered jigs and the duration of notes as they are played in Irish trad. If you know where I’m coming from (if not I can send you a clip of Mary Bergin covering jig note durations) you can manage to sight-read a jig written with even notes [because, with time you’ll be able to sight-read, at speed, any tune type where the ‘melody’ is written correctly & the rhythm is all the same on the page {but you know how it actually is played}].

There is, of course, the problem I have debated in the past about reels being written in 4/4, when they are played in 2/2. Most reels come out rhythmically wrong if you play with four pulses per measure rather than two. When I was starting out, this drove me crazy.
