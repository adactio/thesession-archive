Re: Anglo Concertina fingering tips

Not sure if it’s a tip or just an observation - fingering on Anglo C/G is not intuitive. You’ve several notes that are sometimes played on one button and sometimes on another and you have to get used to jumping around a bit.

I’ve sort of come to the conclusion that the best approach is just to play tunes and as you find the best way to play certain phrases and runs of notes, so you adopt those movements when the same runs crop up in other tunes. So the more tunes you learn in different keys, the easier it gets to know where to go without thinking about it.

Playing in key of G is much easier (less row hopping) in my experience that D and there is an argument that you should learn strategies to play well in D to start off with. It’s easier to adapt that then to G than the other way around.

But if you want success and have satisfaction in playing a few tunes well quickly, try a few in G, mostly along middle row - except for F# and notes f# g a b which are played on inside row.

Re: Anglo Concertina fingering tips

If you get many replies they will be contradictory..! Here is my opinion.

Edel’s relatively closed bellows is a sign of a concertina with very efficient reeds. It is roundabout, but follow me through this. Stability for the fingerboard is important. The less your ends are flailing about in space the more control you have. If you look at all of the good players on YouTube you will see that their moving end is very stable. While this comes with practice, it is aided by shallower folds in the bellows, fewer folds, and not having the bellows too open. All of these things are easier with a very efficient set of reeds which doesn’t need deep folds or many folds. In the interim, the thing to watch with bellows is that your bellows reversals involve no wasted movement, no tilting of the end without compression or decompression of air. If you are having trouble with basic rhythm it is often clumsy bellows reversals that are the problem. Edel would be good to copy. Her basic movement is a fanning of the concertina with the hinge at the front lower facet. When I watch her (I have only seen her on video) I think, I bet she always had a good concertina. People who hold the bellows well open may well have started on inefficient leaky concertinas.

I would suggest starting with a minimum of buttons, the top four on the C and G rows left hand side, C# on the accidental rut, first three on the C row right hand side and first two on the G row right hand side. Try to stay on those buttons until you find a good reason to go outside them.

While changing bellows direction every 3 to 6 notes sounds like a plan it really needs to be some combination of note availability, air needs, phrasing and rhythm that marks the need to reverse the bellows. After a while when you are more used to playing only the last two are strong reasons to change.

Learn the overlap notes, that area in the upper LHS G row that overlaps with the low RHS C row. Practise the G scale using many different combinations of notes in the overlap ie. play G scale on the C row, go to the G row for F# and back to C row. Next do the same but also get the next C from the G row and then go back to the C row. This will have you pulling the BCD part of the scale, then going back to in/out style. When you have this make it a different note you swap.

The Anglo is not like many instruments in that it is not a simple scale, like a flute for example , on which the note is always in the same place. On the Anglo you have choices and it matters which option you select. This is why I recommended sticking to a basic button selection to start off with. Keep it as simple as possible.

Get a teacher, failing that get in the presence of good players as much as possible, above all enjoy it…

Re: Anglo Concertina fingering tips

This is not the best way: “…the best approach is just to play tunes and as you find the best way to play certain phrases and runs of notes…” This just means you fumble with every new tune. You really need a default approach. If it should fail you on a particular tune then you will learn that variation. After a while the default system of fingering -- and the variations -- become second nature.
You should certainly join www.concertina.net.
Edel Fox plays either an old Jeffries or a Wally Carroll. At $5,000-10,000 for an instrument of this quality it’s hard to recommend that a beginner make that investment. Her apparent efficiency is also due to the speed with which she plays.
My advice: learn to play simple tunes in the keys of G,D, and C. Tunes like Yankee Doodle, Happy Birthday, Twinkle Twinkle. Then move on to jigs and hornpipes. Do not worry about bellows movement. Concentrate on the right note and the right default fingering.
While there is a lot on this site that is helpful (http://concertutor.wordpress.com/), having a teacher or attending a workshop will accelerate your learning immensely.

Re: Anglo Concertina fingering tips

Yep, all kinds of options on fingering! just have fun! I agree it is helpful to have some pre-thought out defaults.
However, I can never seem to follow “rules” so my fingering varies depending on seems to flow best for each particular tune.
There is what I think of as the Noel Hill technique where you may start on C row but switch to left hand G row for higher C, D etc…
There is what I think of as the Father Charlie technique where the higher C, D and E are played with right hand in C row.
Frank Edgley uses some different finger choices than either of these two.
Finally there is what I think of as my technique which combines all of the above and more.
Just for fun, I may play a tune using a different fingering pattern each time through. This has helped me to be freer on the buttons, because often I find the tune doesn’t follow the rules either!

Re: Anglo Concertina fingering tips

Play tunes everywhere. I’d learn a good tune like Rolling in the Ryegrass, and go ahead and learn it in D. Play it all in the G row, except the low E and low D. Then, play it all in the C row, except the F#s and maybe the highest A. Now you’ve got one tune down with the two opposite home-row options, and you can start to get a feel for where and why you would compromise on that idea, where it’s easy to cross rows, why you would cross rows, etc. Do it for every tune you learn - try playing it as much in one system, then as much in a totally different one, as you can. Then pick and choose. You’ll find out what’s feasible, what’s appealing, and all the rest of that crap just by doing that simple finger-and-brain exercise.

--Dan

Re: Anglo Concertina fingering tips

@ Danjo, I think your system has much merit. For myself, if I find a tune difficult in a passage that is in the overlap I will swap from basing it in the G row to the C row or vice versa. Sometimes when I am finding a tune dull through repetition I will impulsively reverse bellows direction on the run and sort the issues out afresh.

One of the reasons to have as basic a system as you can is not for now, when you just learnt the tune and know how the fingers fall, but for two years time when you hear it played In a session, the light bulb goes on and you join in, only to find you are missing the ability to play some of the phrases. This for me usually means I invented unique fingering for that tune and I’ve lost it.

As time goes on I find less need to invent unique fingering; in the early days I did it because I was unable to perform some relatively straightforward sequences. Many fingerings were invented to try to play as many notes in one direction as possible so as to compensate for the slower concertina I was playing at the time.

Whatever the system there is no getting around needing some unique fingering, so unlike any other instrument in the session Anglo players need to remember not only the tune but how to play it.

Nothing helps as much as a good teacher and if you are outside Ireland these are in short supply. I have had few lessons ever, but managed to score three with Hugh Healey recently. Hugh is a genius, his recorded work gives some indication of this but in the flesh he is truly astounding. I was cheered when he would say, I don’t play that tune. Some tunes you just don’t play, they are not right for the instrument. He would then often play the tune anyway and brilliantly, but he was saying, you need to pick and choose.

In recent times I find myself In a quandary. My ring finger left hand is not rising completely to the occasion, It gets there but its contribution to the rhythm is weak, and I find myself trying to avoid using it to play the A/G on the LHS G row. No trouble on the C row. I am reluctant to accept a lifelong limitation, but a system based on avoiding it would be much easier. Does anyone else find themselves avoiding use of this finger for the A/G button?
