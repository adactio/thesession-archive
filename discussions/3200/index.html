Erin,

I’ve taken a few workshops in the past year, & I’m always astonished at the wide range of reactions from students to what constitutes a ‘good class’ or a ‘good teacher.’ Some students, all they want is tunes, tunes, tunes… some want obscure tunes, others want only tunes that will be played in sessions. Any time the class diverges into stories or banter, you’ll hear them complain afterwards, ‘Well that class was a complete waste…’

For myself, I hugely enjoy hearing the teacher simply talk, about their philosophy of music, stories, etc… & yes, of course I want a few solid tunes to walk out with, but not be bombarded with like 20 tunes & not really learn any of them. Plus if the learning curve of the class isn’t right, it’s really hard to teach tunes to a group without one extreme or the other feeling very left out. It’s a very tricky balance, I suppose the key is when the class begins, ask each student to say what they have come to learn, & gear it that way.

Though I suppose, thinking about it, a week long workshop will vary from a weekend or day long workshop….. $.02

I’ve been to a few workshops, and while it’s nice to learn a new tune, there are loads of ways to do that. The workshops I’ve appreciated most have been those where I’ve picked up some simple trick that I wasn’t previously aware of, e.g. cross bar bowing in a jig. Thing is, you can never really learn that much in a workshop - it’s far too short a space of time. All you can do is hope to pick up some thing you can focus on to learn in the months/years ahead.

The thing I’ve always wanted in workshops but never really seen is feedback, but I guess unless you’re one-on-one (something I’ve never done with fiddle music) that’s unlikely to happen.

Gimme your bowings!

As an borderline intermediate on fiddle, I am not content to simply learn the notes of a tune. I can do that on my own. What I am most interested in is how to make the tune dance, how to create the lift. In other words, I want to know (1) how the instructor bows the tune, and (2) how and where she or he executes the ornaments. Although the workshops I’ve attended have generally provided good and useful instruction on ornamentation, I’ve found that many instructors (but not all) have been reticent to provide bowings, presumably because they believe that there is no right way of doing so. Fair enough, but I like to have the instructor’s take on a tune.

I’ve done a good number of workshops here and abroad and have thoroughly enjoyed the convivial atmosphere surrounding them. But, from a purely technical standpoint, I’ve found that spending money on a good tutor book that indicates the author’s bowings has been the better investment. The Cranitch book and the Burke videos (which come with written transcriptions complete with bowings) have been many times more useful to me than any workshop I’ve yet attended. Due credit also to Will Harmon, who early on in his session.org career generously provided some excellent insight on bowing and how to make the tunes dance.

What Emily says about listening to the instuctor speak - yep, can’t underestimate that. I have MD of a 45 minute dissertation by Burke (Gaelic Roots, 2002) that I go back to time and again. Talk about insight.

I’d say that if you are going to give a class, you have to be prepared, you have to assess the class level and not be afraid to tell someone that they are over their head - I took an advanced class in Welsh harp at Irish fest and really enjoyed the experience. And I don’t play harp, it was the ambience and fellowship that occurred in the class that I really took to. And I learned something about the music, probably the most important thing. So even though the teacher told me I was not a good student for the course, in the end it was worthwhile for him and me.
A workshop will not teach you very much “stuff”, mostly it is a renewal process and it can give you insight into the music because of the intensity of the process and the relationship in the class with the teacher and other students. If these factors are missing, the workshop will be a dud no matter who many tunes you learn.

Re: Workshops--What do you think? Good? Bad?--How? & Why?

Em:

I’m often asked to give private lessons at workshops, as are many of my colleagues. In most cases, a fee is offered even if not asked. I think a private lesson is a good adjunct to a workshop experience, if it is specifically intended to elicit the teacher’s feedback on an individual’s playing.

If someone seeks a private lesson simply in order to learn more tunes, I don’t think that’s a great use of my time. Better if the person says “I’d like to play a half-dozen tunes for you and get your detailed feedback on my particular playing issues.” That provides an opportunity for teacher feedback not feasible in a class of 4-6 or more.

I don’t think it’s wise for a student to presume that outside-the-class help should be offered by the teacher without recompense. That’s extra time and extra attention beyond what the teacher has contracted for; I think the student should recognize and recompense same.

WRT comments made above about one teacher being more “prepared” than another, I think same often stem from a fundamental (and usually fundamentally erroneous), often American attitude about teaching/student relationship. Many of my fellow Americans come up in an educational system in which lessons are essentially a mercantile object--e.g., “I paid my money and I want my money’s worth!”

Many Americans are accustomed to a teaching method that is very linear, verbal, expository, easily-digested, and caters to the student. Some of the best lessons I’ve had from musicians in various traditions, in contrast, were situations where I had to sift through unclear language, disjoint presentation, booze, bad attitude, or skepticism about my own skills or commitment. Some of the best teachers I’ve had began most student-teacher relationships with an attitude toward the student of “go away, you’ll never learn anything!”

In learning traditional art forms, westernized students often have to suspend their presumptions about how much the teaching method will or should cater to their particular preferences.

Re: Workshops--What do you think? Good? Bad?--How? & Why?

My tuppence, Erin. I’ve taught private lessons, group classes, and workshops, and I *always* start by asking the students what they hope to get out of the experience. This helps them focus their ambitions for the day or week, and gives you a sense of how diverse or homogenous the class is, so you can adjust your presentation accordiningly.

On fiddle (in my experience), most people here in the States--from beginners to more advanced players--seem to want to learn three things: how to get lilt with the bow, how to do rolls and bowed triplets (and where to put them in tunes), and tunage. I usually use one or two simple reels and jigs to demonstrate bowing and left hand techniques, so people hear everything in context.

More advanced players (say, who already know how to do the ornaments) respond well to taking a tune they already know and breathing new life into it. You can gradually layer in ornaments and variations to show how to build interest throughout a tune. Or you can take a common tune and demonstrate the differences of various regional or individual styles. It’s also nice to have some little, nuanced technique or concept up your sleeve for really advanced players. Don’t shy away from giving them your personal interpretations of a tune--the more personal and idiosyncratic it is, the more likely everyone will learn something new from it.

I also try to do a mix of playing (with everyone joining in) and dialogue, which gives you a chance to impart the cultural and interpersonal knowledge that’s so important to playing this music.

Finally, I provide annotated sheet music whenever possible, but I really emphasize students recording the class, so they can listen over and over. Some people resist learning from overly analytical presentations--they just want you to show them, and they’ll copy. But I’ve found it’s essential to be able to break down the details--both on your fiddle and in words--so you can walk through everthing step by step to help the people who can’t get it any other way. (And don’t be surprised if some of the folks who say they learn best by winging it turn out to need more structured help.)

Good luck!

Re: Workshops--What do you think? Good? Bad?--How? & Why?

On discovery: most students I’ve had want specific guidance. They’re frustrated with the slow pace of their own discovery process and are hoping to jump start their progress, launch off the plateau they’ve been stuck on, etc.

For a one-off class, especially with a group or four or more, it’s hard to get much out of trial and error exploration. But for a one-on-one lesson or multi-day workshop, you can help students extend their reach. One thing I’ve done in the past is to assign a common, fairly simple tune and ask each person to come back the next day with an original arrangement. For less advanced students, or if you’ve only got a few hours, you can narrow it down to one stock phrase - I like to use the opening two bars of Gallgaher’s Frolics, or the first two bars of Drowsy Maggie.

I’ve also had lots of students comment on how much it helped to be given a ‘system’ for understanding approaches to bowing or ornamentation or basic tune structure.

I found it utterly thrilling, this way of testing or assessment, but most of my American counterparts despised it, saying it placed too much emphasis on too little data. As a rule I don’t go to workshops or seminars for my traditional music. The primary reason is that I took trumpet lessons for what seemed like forever as a child and then discovered folk music/bluegrass (sitting right under my nose in Nashville). Since then I have looked at music as both fun and fulfilling. Before this little epiphany, music was something I did because I was good at it and others expected me to continue with it.
I have had three lesson sets for traditional music in my life (I am 59) and two out of the three have been a lot of fun. The rest of my learning experience has been both by osmosis and by annoying masters with questions, in itself a lot of fun except fo the time that Norman Blake threatend to hit me (but that is another story ).
As a result, I can’t conceive of looking at a lesson as if it were another post-doctoral seminar, but I have observed exactly what Chris is talking about in other contexts. Mostly it has been with Americans, Germans, and Danes, for some reason, so I admit there is a cultural imperative lurking there somewhere.
Last January I was in Dingle at a session when two American fiddlers came in. They were obviously classically trained judging by their posture and tone and one kept mumbling about how she was “told I had a West Clare Style, but I’m told that I have an East Clare style too”. The others in the session were kind to her, but one of the pipers remarked to me later, “if you come in, just f***kin’ play, we’ll see where you are.” They were so serious! To me having fun is the key to ITM and the attitude shown by these Americans put a slight damper on the proceedings.
The same is true in workshops. After all, the idea is to learn to do better something that makes you happy. There are no grades, there is no gold medal, and you will learn what you learn while you are there and absorb only a part of it, anyway. The craic is the thing that makes is so worthwhile and (if you want to get into the psychological realm) what you can associate with strong emotion will be retained a lot better than what is just drudgery. It is not just what leaves a rash on your psyche although that is a strong emotion, too.
Don’t get me wrong, I practice about an hour a day trying to get my music right. But I love to hear the music, feel the instrument resonate in my body, and flow with the rythmn. I love to hear others interpret the standards and to learn new tunes and songs. I want to be better because being better makes my music better and I feel better. On top of that, there are thousands of others just like me who will share the music and the feelings that it engenders.
So I expect to have fun at a workshop (or else ); the rest (tunes, technique, philosophy, etc.) is just part of the package.

Mike Keyes

Re: Workshops--What do you think? Good? Bad?--How? & Why?

I did electrical engineering in my youth so I suppose I have been involved in “light engineering”.
After many a workshop in various instruments, I do enjoy them, but you have to work hard or you get nothing out of it. Beginners have to pick up what they can and even clever gits have to concentrate as they are looking for the odd tip here and there.
Practically every time I go to a workshop, it spurs me on to do more technique work, and often means un-learning a previous style.
BAD TUTORS - ones with little theory who get out of their depth in a class of classicists.
- ones who get side-tracked by irrelevant questions
- ones who have not brought enough material to cover the day
- ones who have not brought enough music
- ones who let people speed up
BAD PUPILS - ones who only play from music in a “play-by-ear” workshop.
- ones who tootle on their instrument when they should be listening
- ones who obviously weren’t listening and then play it wrong

As an example of a good tutor, I went on an accordion left-hand workshop with John Kirkpatrick. Many were beginners but he got them doing two or three note runs on the basses. By coffee, he had taught them major-scales. By lunch-time, he had them doing minor scales on the basses, all without mention of anything theory-wise.
Imagine if he had told the learners at the start that they were going to learn minor scales.
