The “normal” (aka ‘right-handed’) way to play would be with the left hand covering the top three holes, and the right hand covering the bottom.

Not that it matters hugely, but if he wanted to get a keyed flute 10 years down the road, he’d probably have an easier time finding one that’s right handed (though lefty flutes are not that difficult to come by either).

Re: A quick question

…“handedness” usually being based on a list of things like whether you find a refrigerator door easier to open when it’s hinged on the right (the usual way) or not. Here’s one such list - so you can see “how” right-handed or left-handed you are:

1. Imagine the centre of your back is itching. Which hand do you scratch it with?
2. Interlock your fingers. Which thumb is uppermost?
3. Imagine you are applauding. Start clapping your hands. Which hand is uppermost?
4. Wink at and imaginary friend straight in front of you. Which eye does the winking?
5. Put your hands behind your back, one holding the other. Which hand is doing the holding?
6. Someone in front of you is shouting but you cannot hear the words. Cup your ear to hear better. Which ear do you cup?
7. Count to three on your fingers, using the forefinger of the other hand. Which forefinger do you use?
8. Tilt your head to one shoulder. Which shoulder does it touch?
9. Fixate a small distant object with your eyes and point directly at it with your forefinger. Now close one eye. Now change eyes. Which eye was open when the fingertip remained in line with the small object? (when the other eye, the non-dominant one, is open and the dominant eye is closed, the finger will appear to move to one side of the object.)
10. Fold your arms. Which forearm is uppermost?

Yes, left hand on top. It makes no difference to a whistler / fluter, and the advantages for other instruments IMO outweigh the disadvantages. I knew a left-handed piper who played with the bag under his left arm as normal but his right hand on top, and he rued the day he started learning that way. I also know a right-hander who learned left handed because that was the was his teacher played and neither realized till it was too late.
There has been an experiment done where a piano keyboard was reversed, and the left-handed pianists who tried it all reported that within a couple of hours their brains had adapted, and everything felt natural, so it would seem that it would be possible for him to swap over if he wanted to later. But if I remember correctly, the pianists in the experiment continued playing standard pianos.

Re: A quick question

I’m strongly against any attempts to change the way children first hold and then discover the potential of musical instruments.

Sadly, I grew up at a time when left-handedness was still regarded as ‘sinister’, in more ways than one, though the key influence was teacher training in the 1940s and 1950s one of whose precepts was based upon right-handedness. According to my mother my left hand was initially dominant (and there are photos to prove it), but that was hammered out of me by six years of primary school - and the compulsory calligraphy lessons didn’t help.

Nevertheless, it wasn’t all squashed out of me. In playground games and sports I still caught predominantly with my left hand and kicked with my left foot.

When it came to music I’m damned glad that my leftyness was never eradicated since it meant that my hands have had virtual equal rights when it comes to playing an instrument.

I certainly wouldn’t call myself ambidextrous, but play to the strengths of the appropriate hand when required.

Lists such as the one supplied by Mark are always interesting, but they operate upon the basis that our bodies are 100% perfect and that’s simply not the case.

Re: A quick question

Well, being a music teacher means only that I run into this question a fair amount on all sorts of instruments. Which doesn’t mean that I necessarily have the right answers for everybody.

What I’ve noticed over the years is that left-handed people tend to be basically ambidextrous because we live in such a predominantly right-handed world. We learn to function with what the rest of the world sees as “normal” door knobs, scissors, keyboards, spiral notebooks, etc. So--especially for young kids--lefties rarely have any trouble playing a “right-handed” instrument. In some cases, it seems to *help,* having the dominant hand on the fingerboard for guitar, say, better able to deal with the spatial finesse of fretting scales and chords.

On whistle, flute, and pipe chanter, the hands are performing such similar (if not exactly the same) actions, I just can’t see that a lefty would have any trouble at all learning the “righty” way. It’s just convention that left hand on top is “right-handed” anyway. Playing this way simply makes it easier (and usually cheaper) to find good instruments all down the line. Even with keyless flutes, there are more flutes with embouchures cut for left hand on top.

That said, when a student comes to me determined to play left handed, I don’t try to change them. If they haven’t spent a minute on the instrument yet, I’ll explain what I’ve just said above, but the choice is theirs. If they’re already playing lefty, that’s what we do. I would never argue against the precedent of people like Mike McGoldrick and Jimi Hendrix. :-)

For me, it boils down to this: it takes *both* hands to play all these instruments. Don’t waste too much time thinking about hand dominance. Better to spend it playing music.
