↳ Posted by Steve Lundgren on July 30th, 2019.

Re: BMI harassment of session venue

Maybe someone else can chime in on this one, but I’ve never heard of the “It’s public domain!” defense to work with ASCAP/BMI.

For one thing, the discussion is between the license organization and the venue owner, not the musicians (we don’t worry about fees, it’s up to the venue). The venue owner won’t know what is or isn’t public domain. The other problem is that few Irish sessions are so rigid in their repertoire that they never play anything written prior to 1923, the current rolling date for PD music in the USA. If you play a single Paddy Fahey tune, or a Sean Ryan, or a Liz Carroll, you lose that argument. Not that the ASCAP/BMI agent cares, they just tend to blanket disallow any PD claims because so few genres of music actually are that, outside of Classical music or a Civil War reenactment show.

The only successful defense against a visiting BMI/ACAP shakedown that I’ve seen locally is one venue owner who stood up and basically said “Oh yeah? So sue me!” He was betting on his venue being so small that the potential fees wouldn’t be worth the court costs, and he was right. But few venue owners will go that far to avoid licensing fees. BMI/ASCAP will press the case if the venue has enough seats and looks like it’s doing a healthy business. You need to be in an out-of-the-way small town, or a hole in the wall establishment that’s barely surviving to fly under the licensing radar. This has shut down what used to be some nice places to play live music of all kinds, not just trad, in my corner of the Pacific Northwest.

If you end up losing this venue, make it a priority to find out if the new places you’re shopping are already paying live music BMI/ASCAP fees. Look for bars or restaurants with an open mic or live bands, and pick an off-night for your session. Those are the “safe” ones for sessions here in the USA, although of course you might get kicked out for other reasons. :) One of the sessions I attend never had a licensing problem, but lost a restaurant spot recently due to too many conflicts with sports nights.

And this is really shady behavior when it comes to traditional music -- I’ve never heard of a composer ever really receiving a single cent of revenue that was generated by them cracking down on sessions. Re: BMI harassment of session venue

“Ok. What’s your alternative?”

The only alternative that would reimburse composers of niche music like Liz Carroll or the Paddy Fahey estate would be something like what YouTube runs automatically as a copyright check for submitted videos, with data sent to the licensing organizations.

That system doesn’t exist, and even if it did, it still wouldn’t compensate niche composers fairly due to the way the system scales. The payments to Taylor Swift or Lil Nas X are based on millions of “plays” on Spotify and background music systems in restaurants and bars. Because money isn’t infinite, there is a ceiling on what is paid, and everything scales down from the most popular artists at any given time.

For example, if Taylor Swift gets a $50,000 check every month from BMI/ASCAP for 5 million “plays” across all media for her latest hit, that’s $0.01 per play. How many times each month does “The Diplodocus” get played in Irish sessions all over the world? Maybe 50 times? Call it 100 times including niche Irish trad radio play? Those 100 “plays” of her composition would send Liz a check for a whopping $1.00 every month.

See, there is no scaling factor for the overall popularity of trad music vs. pop music. It’s all thrown into one pot as far as BMI/ASCAP is concerned. The more popular you are, the more money you get. Trad music is at the bottom of the barrel for genre popularity compared to pop music, rap, and electronic dance music, so any potential payout is laughably small, even if it could be documented.

“Or are you happy stealing music and feeling smug about preserving your own version if the tradition?”

I have never stolen music. Whenever I’ve played a gig in a former band at a bar or restaurant, the venue owner is responsible for covering any payments under current copyright law. I’ve never done any street busking, but that’s one situation where technically a musician is responsible, but there is no direct mechanism to reimburse the composer other than buying their CD’s or online music.

There is also a mechanism for sending payments to composers when producing a recording for sale, called a mechanical license. That’s actually a much more fair way to compensate composers of trad tunes, compared to the way playback of recorded music or live music is licensed for bars and restaurants.

From a discussion 5 years ago: edited

Re: Sessions and public performance/copyright issues

First - relax - and examine who signed the Cease & Desist letter. It’s probably not a lawyer - it will be a marketing rep for BMI/ASCAP. If (and probably) so, ignore its terms. The letter assumes that the sender knows the tunes/names/copyright holders - which is baloney.

Second - in a response to the letter writer, submit a play list of out of copyright or trad tunes, perhaps, a hundred of them. It doesn’t matter if you play these tunes as long as they are in the genre, and are in the public domain. (This can be tricky, however, so be careful. ‘Happy Birthday’ is owned by WB and can be subject to copyright law, so be very judicious in the tunes that you list - but make it long and definitely in the public domain, and convincing. NEVER play Happy Birthday!!!) (UPDATED - you’re now allowed to play Happy Birthday.) Also, insist in the response letter that you are sensitive to copyrights and that your ‘learning’ session is for amateurs only - not a profit making venture. Also, you should insist that you do not invite professional musicians or play arrangements that professional musicians might play. If someone approaches a session player about the music, be sure the session players respond that the music is all traditional. (This is not likely to happen - most of these C&D letters are fishing expeditions for marketing reps.)
-on a side note - this does not preclude the pub owner from paying musicians - or feeding/quenching them - that does not make you a professional.

Finally, you should note in the response letter that some players in your group may occasionally play parodies of music they’ve heard, but it is only in jest, and not meant to be a representation of the original, only a joke.

If the pub has live music on other nights of the week, they’ll need a license which should cover a session. If the pub plays recorded music, they’ll need a different license (unless they’re selling the music they’re playing), but this is not the problem of the sessioniers.

I’ve done this. It should keep you in the clear.

Re: BMI harassment of session venue

I don’t know about BMI, but PRS has a “small gigs” scheme where performers can submit their set list and receive the royalties from small venues which otherwise wouldn’t register. It’s intended for people performing their own material in order to receive their own royalties, but there’s no reason why other writers can’t be included on the list so they also receive the appropriate royalties.

Whilst the system is weighted in favour of the millionaires, it’s not true that composers of folk music don’t benefit. I’ve received payments from PRS through both the small gigs scheme and setlist returns at festivals.

The music copyright system is rightly criticised, and there’s a strong case that it’s not fit for purpose where the informal, non-commercial use of music is concerned. But what is the alternative? Without something like this, it would be the musician’s responsibility to obtain permission and negotiate a royalty for every copyright piece they perform, which is impractical for both parties. The other alternative is a free-for-all where music copyright is unenforceable and composers lose the rights to their intellectual property. The current system (which so far as I am aware has been adopted in most countries) puts the onus for paying royalties on the venue, rather than the musician, and it works, after a fashion. Issues arise in situations like the ones we are discussing, where there is little or no commercial benefit to the venue to make it worth paying a licence fee.

Re: BMI harassment of session venue

Actually the way the copyright laws work here in the USA any public establishment needs to buy licenses from PROs (BMI, ASCAP, SESAC, etc.) if any music which they represent the rights to is being publicly performed. Here is the kicker on that end; playing music from the radio etc. is considered public performance and still requires what is called a blanket license from the PROs. So unless they never have music on on the house system the session is not the only thing the PROs can come after them for, if they want to have the radio on in a public space they still technically need a blanket license.

I looked into some of the copyright laws when my ex-band was putting out an EP that contained modern compositions (this actually has little to do with PROs I discovered). The system in this country is a huge racket and PROs (SESAC is a bit better than the others but still) essentially act like organized crime rings and only pay decent royalties to big name artists. So, what Howard Jones is talking about with PRS doesn’t exist at all in the US system, PRS is a government agency and actually keeps real records and pays composers what they are actually owed. Very different from the mafia-esque private agencies here in the US which don’t publicize their records and leave composers and everyone else in the dark about who actually earned what.

PRS is not a government agency, it is a private company limited by guarantee without share capital and was originally set up by a number of music publishers. It functions as a licensing body under terms of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988.

It is certainly very active. I don’t know how its activities compare with its US counterparts. I don’t think it is very transparent about how royalties are distributed, although in fairness it seems to be aware of the flaws and is trying to find ways to ensure that composers in minority genres don’t get overlooked. Nevertheless, for all its fine words about “supporting music”, it is really interested in published music and protecting its members’ revenue streams. From their point of view, any performance of live music is likely to include at least some copyright material, and that is sufficient to require a licence.

Re: BMI harassment of session venue

A Creative Comm0ns licence doesn’t get around the issue of a performance licence. The default position is that a venue needs a licence from a Performing Rights Organisation to perform live music in public (whether or not it is for commercial purposes). The exception is where you can demonstrate that only music which is not controlled by the PRO will be performed, for example a set of their own songs by a singer-songwriter who is not a PRO member. In the case of a session is almost impossible to be certain what will be played, or to limit it to non-PRO music. Where CC music is performed that comes into the same category as public domain music - a licence fee will still be payable for any copyright music performed, but no royalties go to the composer who has given a CC licence for their works.

The onus is put on the venues because they are easiest to target, and cannot hide. The system assumes that the venue is receiving some benefit, whether from ticket sales, room hire, or simply attracting more customers, and will accept the need for a licence as just one of the costs of doing business. For 99% of music that assumption is probably correct, but it doesn’t allow for the circumstances of many sessions where a venue is allowing itself to be used for a music performance as a service to its customers and its community. In those cases, being charged for the privilege may make them decide not to bother, especially when (as so often seems to be the case) the PRO’s agents are rude and aggressive in their demands.

Re: BMI harassment of session venue

Update- we have read over the information on the licensing agency’s guidelines, and it explicitly states: that if only public domain music is played, no license is needed. One of the session regulars is also a concert promoter with a non-profit organization. He is an original member of Folk Alliance, and pays licensing fees for his concerts, supports the organization but also stating that the session plays public domain trad music. He has written a letter to that effect, to be made available to the rep who contacted the venue and also to the owners.

One of our group knows the owners well - and after speaking with them, one has already agreed to go ahead and let us continue playing, and we expect to hear from the other next week.

So maybe this story will have a happy ending ….

We will make a concerted effort to avoid recently composed tunes, make a list of public domain tunes we currently play and maybe keep some books like O’Neill’s around to encourage folks to stay in the limits. And the up side might be learning a bunch of different tunes from older sources.
