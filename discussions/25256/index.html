Re: Online guide to guitar backing?

A few quotes from Al Brown’s piece:

Be spontaneous, but not so spontaneous that you confuse the melody instruments (you are there to support them--a steady rhythm comes first).

While this article is about accompaniment, it is important to remember that in this type of music, the melody, or the tune, is king.

A session (assuming there are enough chairs) will welcome as many melody players as can play along. But there is a limit to how many rhythm players you can add before the melody players are overwhelmed, and the “wall of rhythm” muddies the overall sound. At the same time, sessions are more accessible to rhythm players, as it is easier to learn a few basic strumming and chord patterns than it is to get many dozens of tunes under your belt and be able to play the melodies at high speeds. This paradox often produces a surplus of rhythm players at sessions. Be sensitive to this. When playing with other accompanists, work together so that you are harmonious. Take turns sitting tunes out or playing melody, play with control (quiet is good), and remember, the melody is king.

~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Get the idea Jack? It’s not that I or the other melody players are kings and the backers are servants. It’s about the music. The melody itself is king, and the accompaniment (not the accompanist) is subordinate.

So it’s not about how long he has been going, or whether he has friends there. It’s not about personalities, it’s about the music.

Here are the three simple guidelines I suggested for our local session:

For people to be able to enjoy playing music together, it’s best if everyone is in tune, manages their volume so others can at least hear themselves, and plays the actual melodies (or appropriate chords and rhythm).

All but one person agreed to this as a goal. The lone person said that he wanted to play “countermelodies” and harmonies. He no longer attends the session.

We went from (before) a lot of random noise and a dozen unhappy people to (after) decent music and one unhappy person.

A session can be (A) a social/drinking club that slaughters a few tunes, or (B) an evening of good music, with fun and drinks. Depends on where your priorities are. Either way is okay, as long as those present are in agreement. If you’re aiming for (B), one person can spoil it for the rest.

Re: Online guide to guitar backing?

The trouble with shouting the key/ mode to a backer is that it is often misheard leading to even more confusion than there was to begin with!
‘D!’ at a busy session often sounds like ‘E!’ and with the variety of accents one runs into (especially here in South London) means someone hearing ‘E’ when the key shouter is saying ‘A’ with a Geordie/ Devon/ Kiwi/ Punjabi/ Polish or wotteffah accent or vice versa.
The other thing is if e.g. ‘A’ is indicated, one might assume Amajor is implied when, actually Aminor, dorian or mix is meant!
So, helpful though key shouting is, one may often end up playing tunes in, say Emin and the six string devil botherer stars flailing away in Amajor…

By the way, never ‘shout out’ the actual chords, just give the key/ mode.

Re: Online guide to guitar backing?

Just to be clear, I don’t have a problem with John Doyle in the slightest. I saw him with Moonlighting and he was incredible, and I’d have no problem with him in any session I was at, as I’ve no doubt he would be a sensitive and understanding player - he has a high degree of musicianship.

What I do have a problem with is the horde of Doyle acolytes who don’t understand the music the way he does and bang out heavy syncopated rhythms whilst playing obscure chords at the same time. These are the guitar/bouzouki equivalent of the legion of glugging water bodhran players that plague sessions the world over.
