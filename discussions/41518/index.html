What Has Irish Music Taught You?

Irish Traditional Music is so intriguingly peculiar in so many of its ways, that I can never find the words when describing it to musicians of other styles. The structure and systems are so very different from many of the more “mainstream” styles of music. It being “melody-led” has to be one of the most drastic of those differences. I mean, the look on an uninformed guitarists‘ face when they realize that 4-chords just ain’ gonna‘ fit’, is priceless. Trying to explain to them that you can’t explain to them The Music in 2 minutes because it’s “melody-led”, is just as humorous. “What are the chords man?” “…Ummm… All of them… The chords is all of them…”. Naturally, Irish music being so different from what many of us are used to, that means it has a high potential for expanding our understanding of music. From exposure to more niched instruments, to finally getting to hear what the hell “9/8” sounds like, there is so much to learn and experience from this music.

For me, the big lesson has been “Articulation”, in the sense of playing clearly and intelligibly. When I was younger, it was all about dynamics. My teachers told me I played everything loud, and that I didn’t have a strong sense of dynamic variation. So I always paid close attention whenever the topic of dynamics came up. I’ve been fortunate enough to have been taught some remarkable lessons on dynamics over the years. And when dynamics finally clicked for me(it literally took the entire time I studied music through school), it became the only thing that mattered. And I’m proud to say that after all these years I can now utilize the softs and the hards and the lows and the highs. But it wasn’t until Irish music, that this very fundamental and essential idea of “Articulation” was brought back to my attention.

It is my strong opinion that articulation, precision, clarity, is the ultimate element of a good sound. (Feel free to challenge me on that. Strong or humble, it is still only an opinion.) I’m reminded of the days singing in school choir. The directors over the years were all very adamant about clarity. Pronouncing our words correctly, especially if they were in a different language. Being in sync with each other and listening to each other. To unify the sound so that it is loud and clear what is being sung. And that’s where it is. When we play music, we are in essence, speaking, and not speaking clearly takes so much away from getting the greater artistic message across.

The funny thing is, one of the most important lessons in learning this music is “slowing down”, but slowing down is about articulation. Slow down, to be accurate. Slow down, to be precise. Slow down, to be clear. Slowing down is such an effective method of practice because it isolates all of the factors that affect your articulation. Slowing down is “the trick”, “the secret”, “the coveted knowledge”. Which is probably why we never have to bring up the topic of articulation, because it comes with the practice of slowing down, which we talk about incessantly.

This concept has been so important to me because it’s something that I could take back to the piano. Something I could take back to my own music. Something that can, and should be used, in all of the music I play. To respect every single note. To make sure every note is heard. That every note gets its due time. That every flick, flutter, and scrape is crisp and musical. These are things that I’ve been told in the lessons, and workshops, and here in these forums, and even in the books. It’s been a truly invaluable learning experience. I’m grateful that Irish music brought that back for me.

Re: What Has Irish Music Taught You?

Playing Irish trad has taught me many things. First off, it has taught me that music doesn’t have to be about money, fame, image, or competition. Music can be about people, sharing, socializing, and friendship. (Not that some of the fame, image and competition elements don’t creep in a bit around the edges). This is very different than the rest of my life with music, and I’ve said many times that this is what drew me in and kept me here.

The second thing I learned is really more about people than music, and specifically about myself. I learned that our perceived limits that we place upon ourselves can be pushed, and that we’re capable of more than we might give ourselves credit for to begin with. Playing this music, which affords the player a sense of making it their own, is really a journey of discovery of the potential (and limits) of the human mind, and it is something that I take with me into the rest of my life.

Another thing that I find profound enough to post here is that one can truly reach a point of nirvana, and when it happens, it releases endorphins that rival (if not surpass) any other pleasurable experiences in life. That rare moment when you’re playing music and everyone is in the groove, and the music just flows out effortlessly. That’s enough to keep me chasing the feeling for the rest of my life.

And finally, I have learned one of the cruel realities of being a musician. The better you get at playing music, the harder it is to find music that excites you. Including the prior point - when you’re first getting proficient at playing and start to be a productive member of a session, every session has the potential to be awesome and release those endorphins. The better you get, the harder it becomes to reach that point. So you can turn your focus inward, and try to find the inspiration from within, instead of from other people. But it’s difficult, for me at least, to reach the state of nirvana by myself.

Re: What Has Irish Music Taught You?

I never really was a musician before picking up Irish music, so I can’t say that Irish music has taught me anything special or unique compared to other musics. Therefore, I will have to base what I have learned on my music experience as a whole.

1. Music is a social activity. It is interaction with other people, even if there isn’t a whole lot of talking.
2. Social activity is uncomfortable for me, thusly the social side of playing music can be hard. I DO NOT like to be the center of attention, even among non-judgmental folks.
3. All my discomfort leads to physical tension. I have a constant struggle to avoid playing problems. Some of the tension even causes health problems (Neck problems from clamping down on fiddle too hard).
4. I have discovered how truly curious I am, and how my mind doesn’t like to settle down and focus. Every few notes my mind starts drifting and it’s easy for the tune to go to ****.
5. Despite the problems, music in general has been immensely helpful to me as a person. It has helped with focus. It has given me an artistic outlet, even though I’m not very artistic. I have literally felt the music reprogram my mind over the last dozen years. I don’t like sitting still anymore, I want to be playing music and using my mind. I can only watch a few minutes of a show on Netflix or whatever before I start getting antsy.
6. I have learned to be more methodical in my thoughts. Problem solving is immensely improved.
7. Non musicians are completely uninterested in talking about my music or my instruments. They only care to be entertained. They don’t care about the thousands of hours of time and dedication that we have put into our musical education. I tend to think of non-musicians as selfish and entitled (yes, in general, but more specifically as it relates to music). “Oh, you play fiddle? You need to come over and play for us sometime.” Translation: we don’t care much about you as a person, but we do care what you can do for us. Yes, it’s a bit of a cynical viewpoint.
8. A lot of musicians are also entitled, selfish, and/or clueless. Or flakes. Or major arseholes. They can ruin musical experiences very easily. The session leader who plays at breakneck speed and ends up playing by himself 90% of the time because of it, then wonders why nobody comes back to the session etc.
9. Paying somebody $50 or more per hour doesn’t completely negate their negative qualities. And in some cases, doesn’t affect it at all. See number 8.

I think that’s a big enough list from me for now.

Re: What Has Irish Music Taught You?

My comment about it being harder to find music that excites you is referring specifically to Irish traditional music. There are recordings that I thought were amazing when I first started out. Now I hear them with a more experienced ear, and can hear things that I don’t like about them, often to the point where I don’t enjoy listening to them at all anymore. There were sessions that I used to think were great, and now I generally wouldn’t have any fun playing in them. If I listen to my own album, where other people hear nice music, all I hear is little timing inconsistencies in my playing that I don’t like (yes, everyone is their own worst critic, but it’s not just my playing that I hear them in, I hear them in other people’s recordings as well…)

I think it was Jusa Nutter Eejit who once pointed out that learning to play this music is a bit like a fractal process. The more you zoom in on the details of the music, the more you start to understand some of the subtle nuances that were lost on you before. The better you get, the higher your standards get, because you notice things that you didn’t have the ability to understand before. And the more you have developed your personal taste in the music, so less and less of it fits in to what you like.

And it’s not just me. I have had conversations about this with several great players, only to find out that they don’t really like to go out and play anymore, because it’s hard to find music that is up to their standards. (I also know many great players who still go out and play sessions regularly. I am firmly committed to being one of those guys who plays regularly…)

And every now and then, often when you least expect it, the unicorn session appears, and keeps you hooked. :-D
