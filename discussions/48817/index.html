Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

I am curious to now how many fluters are rolling the headjoint in towards (or the flute body away) from the mouth so that the outer line of the embouchure hole is in line with the holes of the flute body. I ask this as I am reading the great and thorough insight on Terry McGee’s web site, and I have seen many types of the headjoint alignment in watching other players. I am still trying to get my best embouchure (consistently) and I am currently keeping the embouchure hole in line with the holes on the flute body.

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

I start with the embouchure hole and tone holes aligned when assembling the flute, then I roll the flute body out until the outer edge of the embouchure hole is aligned with the inside edge of the tone holes. Alignment checked by sighting down the length of the flute from the end. That gives me the best version of a three-point-hold where my righthand thumb is pushing out on the flute body, and not trying to hold it up from underneath.

You’ve seen Terry McGee’s page on Rockstro’s advocacy of the three point hold? Basically that. I had to trim my righthand thumb nail very short to get a good purchase on the flute body, sacrificing the long nail I used for fingerstyle guitar (some sacrifices must be made in the pursuit of the musical arts!).

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

I think a consistent head-body orientation is more important than exactly how the holes line up. You practice in one location until you know what you’re doing… “some” time later.

That said, I have followed from the start the advice most of the others are suggesting to orient the blowing edge of the head to the middle of the holes.

There have been other things I needed to change about my embouchure, specifically how much of the hole to cover and how to direct the airstream. I initially covered less of the hole, making me a sharper blower (adjustable by the cork), but I learned later that my tone was much better by covering more of the hole thereby playing flatter. This became necessary when I started playing an R&R Patent head flute with pre-existing cork location(s).

Yup, I’d suggest “in line” is not common for trad/wooden flute players. Most common is blow edge aligned with centre of holes. I go a little further and turn in such that blow edge is in line with the near edge of the tone holes. I think pretty much as ConicalBore has described.

Yes that describes me.

Well, I would word it “rotate the headjoint” because “rolling” inwards or outwards means deviating from your default position. Having the outer rim of the blow-hole in line with the centre-line of the fingerholes WAS my default position, so I didn’t have to roll inwards or outwards to get there.

About glottal stops, I heard a lecture on the history of Irish fluteplaying where the lecturer played a 1920s recording of what clearly sounded to me like tonguing, but which the lecturer called “glottal stops”.

Now traditional European art-music fluteplaying has long used two different “points of articulation”

T/D
K/G

which give the crisp articulation I was hearing.

I don’t know about you, but I can’t get my glottis to work quickly enough to achieve that.

Like anything else it might merely a matter of practice. But why work harder than you need to, when two other easier-to-do points of articulation are right there, ones you use thousands of times a day in your speech?

Like many flute things it can only be ascertained by X-ray filming.

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

First a small possible correction: Wodanaz wrote: “Then I saw a brief tutorial by Steph geremia I believe who said not to do that and to stay totally inline. So I changed to online.” I’m not sure which tutorial Wodanaz saw, but in the one on youtube done for OAIM Steph Geremia says to start with the mouth hole lined up with the tone holes but then quickly adds that she personally turns it in. My own position is what is probably the majority position: with the outer edge of the embouchure lined up with the centre of the tone holes. June McCormack in a workshop showed me that she lines up the outer edge of the embouchure with the inner edge of the tone holes. There is nothing new about these ‘turned in’ positions: Quantz in the 18th century advocated them as did most flute tutors in the 19th century (I believe Rockstro has a good summary). I’m not sure if it was an American ‘invention’ to line up the flute with the C-key or with the majority of the keys (speaking of Boehm flutes here), but in any event many silver flute players line up their mouth holes in the same way Irish flute players do. Here is a quote from a Czech instruction book by František Malotín (1998): “The most acceptable position, which suits most players, is that an imaginary line halving the majority of open keys on the body of the flute touches the front edge of the embouchure hole”. The Canadian flutist and flute teacher Jen Cluff maintains a huge website (www.jennifercluff.com) and has a long discussion on rolling in the headjoint. Etc.

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

Addendum: Jen Cluff’s discussion of headjoint position (for the silver flute) is given in a video on youtube:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RXIVPwdmEfI

It takes her until nearly the end of the video to say this, but she quotes Roger Mather, the author of a massive 3-volume work on playing the flute (_The Art of Playing the Flute_, available for purchase in pdf form on Jen’s website) that 75% of flutists turn their headjoints in. Note that this discussion is entirely about the Boehm flute. She talks about the weight of the key axles for most of the video but the key point is that without turning the headjoint in, the position for the best tone requires putting the key axles tipping inwards. This means that the embouchure is rolled in although she describes it as her lips covering more of the embouchure hole.

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

I think the “turn in position” for silver flute vs. wooden flute, where you’re (we at least I’m) trying for a “hard” tone are really different.

In silver flute, which was my main instrument from age 6 to 18, playing classical music you’re mostly blowing across the edge and looking for a purity of tone with little “air” in the soiund.

On wooden flute, I’m blowing more down than across with considerably more pressure than I would on a silver flute pushing the tone to the edge of breaking, with a lot of the 2nd harmonic in the tone creating that hard sound.

I’ve found it hard to get that sound on a silver flute, the traditional players I’ve heard on silver flute have a more smooth sound than the huffy-puffy rough sound. I guess Ian Anderson can pull it off. :-)

I can hardly play silver flute any more, it’s just so different. About a year ago, I gave my nice silver flute to my sister who plays in community bands.

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

Discussion of tone is always interesting, but I think it’s a red herring in regard to how one turns the headjoint relative to the flute body.

You do have to find the rotation of the headjoint that allows the best coverage of the lower lip over the embouchure hole. Once you find that, it should be independent of how you decide to line up the embouchure hole with the tone holes in the flute body. That’s about finding the best flute hold to free up tension in your arms and wrists, and free up your fingers so they’re not “holding up” the flute. It’s not about tone, except to the degree that a steadier and more relaxed hold can improve your overall playing.

Michael, you wrote: "In silver flute, which was my main instrument from age 6 to 18, playing classical music you’re mostly blowing across the edge and looking for a purity of tone with little “air” in the soiund.
On wooden flute, I’m blowing more down than across with considerably more pressure than I would on a silver flute pushing the tone to the edge of breaking, with a lot of the 2nd harmonic in the tone creating that hard sound. Remember I’m probably twice your age, so what I write reflects an earlier age and most certainly different teachers and traditions, but when I studied the Boehm flute in the late 1960s, I was taught to blow down into the flute, not across it. My teacher, Edmund Raas, was a Swiss flutist who studied with Aurèle Nicolet, the great Swiss flutist (see the wiki article about him). The sound that was aimed at was not an Irish sound, but was much fuller and sometimes more intense than the sound I hear from modern Boehm flutists. There are lots of videos of Nicolet on youtube and one or two of my teacher, Ed Raas.

I agree that some of the trad players who play the Boehm flute (including wooden Boehm flutes), e.g. Sean Moloney, have smooth sounds, not the sounds we aim for, but there are four who are very successful at getting the right sound: Joanie Madden, Steph Geremia (whose main instruments are simple system wooden flutes but who has done a lesson series for OAIM for silver flute players in which she plays a silver flute), Lucie Périer, and last but not least, Tara Breen when she was a teenager playing a wooden Boehm flute. (Note that wooden Boehm flutes played by non-trad musicians in the classical tradition don’t sound very different from silver flutes played in the same tradition.) However, I’m with you that it is hard to do this. I scarcely ever play my silver flute, and if I do, it is not for Irish music. Quite apart from the sound, I’ve grown to love the feel of my fingers in contact with the tone holes on simple system wooden flutes. Chet

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

The first time I rotated the headjoint of a silver flute towards me, just a few degrees, the difference in improved flute response was impressive, and I’ve played it that was ever since. I always first assemble the flute with the tone hole in line with the key pads, so this rotation would be slight, or even still in line though slightly off-center with the keypads (which on a silver flute are obviously large, or 70%+ of the bore diameter, apart from a few). I already had my embouchure developed by that time, but it helped to know I was both 1) getting the lower lip close to or onto the tone hole for perfect functioning, my pivot point, and 2) being at the right distance from the far side of the tone hole, so that I was not too far or too close to it, and had flexibility in approach/expressive results with the air stream in all octaves, with maximum air efficiency going on consistently. So this adjusted/rotated angle can certainly help performance, but in my experience, the angle change off the keypad line, is so slight as to not seem to affect anything else.

The other angle to change, which varies with each flute design, is to try aiming the air stream slightly to the right of 90-degrees across the key-pad line or length of the tube. This variation is slight, maybe 5 degrees, but I have found that some flute designs respond better if the air stream is directed slightly to the right. You have to play around with it to find the ideal angle on the flute model you have, it varies. And check all three octaves, perhaps the lowest octave responds to that better than the upper half of the second, or third octave. Perhaps it doesn’t matter.

Re: Flute Embouchure/Rolling the Headjoint

On the wooden flute, I have the centre of the embouchure inline with the centre of the main finger holes on the flute. For me that is the position which, with my hold on the flute, means the least movement to where I want the hole in relation to my lips depending on what I want to achieve. And that changes a lot during my playing. It is also a very simple position to recreate precisely when assembling the flute.

Acoustically, all that matters is that we can present the flute to our lips stably and in the correct position for what we are doing at the time. Ergonomically, our grip while doing that should be comfortable and not cause us any bad injuries. How these two relate decides how the relevant twists in the flute at each joint can best be set up. Don’t obsess too much about “rules” for how this should look visually. For the rest, our ears and our pain (hopefully none!) should be our guides.
