Re: The True Spirit of ITM?

My own personal take is that the uillean pipes are at the spiritual center of ITM as it relates to instruments. The limitations of that instrument contain the seeds of the style that defines it. You have a constant flow of air that can only be manipulated by fingering. The craftiness of getting the articulation and decorations we’ve all come to admire and love is a result of this particular set of musical circumstances. I’m no expert on the history of ITM, but it seems to me that the pipes were seeking to play the melodies of the songs i.e. sean-nós, and that the songs are actually the true spiritual center of the music. The pipes attempts at reproducing the melodies of these songs (slow airs) create a unique sound that, as I said, is related to its limitations. The other instruments i.e. flutes, fiddles, whistles, etc. seems to me to be recreating that particular character and flavor. Each of those instruments then ads it’s own particular advantage or disadvantage to the mix.

My understanding as well is that the earliest tunes were slow airs that were reinvented with rhythm to match dancing. Again, the flavor and character includes this continuous flow of air with decorative crans and triplets that all seem to emulate the circumstances surrounding the pipes.

Yes, like dmarie says, from the old-time tradition I’ve often heard this: http://www.vanderbilt.edu/vupress/wolfe.htm

“They called the instrument ”the devil’s box“ because some thought it was sinful to play one. Sometimes in recent years, people would be tearing down old log cabins to get at the logs and they would find hidden in the wall an old beat-up fiddle. At first they puzzled about this, but then people explained that the man who lived there was once a fine old-time fiddler, but that in later years he had gotten religion. In his zeal, he became convinced that he must turn his back on his old life, and especially that devil’s instrument, the fiddle. After all, the fiddle was for good times and strong drink. Look at the old tunes; you gave the fiddler a ”dram,“ and you heard tunes like ”Devil’s Dream“ and ”Devil in the Woodpile,“ ”Hell Among the Yearlings“ and ”Hell Broke Loose in Georgia,“ ”Hell and Scissors“ and ”Hell Bound for Alabama.“ And didn’t one take the rattles of a serpent--the rattlesnake--and put them in the fiddle to improve the tone? The evidence was strong, and many newly saved fiddlers took their instruments and smashed them against the wall. But others, unable to part with the heirlooms they had devoted so much of their lives to, quietly dropped them behind the walls of their cabins and kept quiet, hoping perhaps that some day in the future, in a kinder and more tolerant age, someone would find them and let them be heard again.”
