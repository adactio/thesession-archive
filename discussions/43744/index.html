Re: Planning to attend my first session.Any advice welcome

I started playing in a real session less than a year ago, but went the first few times without my box to see what it was like. It’s good that you have asked about the session and whether you will be welcomed! Sounds like you are, so you should go and be brave. And that it’s a slow session is all the more reason to go. Maybe ask for a list of common tunes, and prepare some of those.

Also, expect to make mistakes. It happens to everyone, even the best. So don’t beat yourself up if you can’t remember how a tune goes in a set you may be leading. More than likely someone will recognise what you are trying to play and will help pick it up. If not, you can still have a good laugh about it.

Also, a session is not a concert. The audience is you and the other musicians. It helped me to face into the circle of other musicians, with my back to whoever else is listening in the pub.

Re: Planning to attend my first session.Any advice welcome

Go. See what happens. Any advice you get will be pretty generic unless it comes from some who already attends the session you found. Be open to what’s going on around you. Plan on listening a lot, really a lot. Pay attention to the interactions among other players. Bring a recorder (phone works as good as any) and ask for tune names, but don’t be overly intrusive about it. If it really is a “slow” session, whatever that is, you may be asked to play something. Don’t be shy about it. A truly welcoming session needs to learn about you and everybody understands that ya gotta start somewhere. Know that everybody around you has gone through the very same thing. It helps a lot to be able to laugh at yourself! Oh, and plan on going back. Given enough time the others will get used to you being there. It’s gonna take time to be fully included. What new thing isn’t? Yes they can be, make that are, intimidating. The social dynamics of session is no different than any other gathering so be patient. There is only one way to get over that and it’s to get through it. Just like every other new thing you have done or will do. Too often people drop out way too soon.

Something to think about for later. You mentioned that you are a self-taught music reader, good for you. Don’t be afraid to go there. Just be sure that when you read a tune you also hear the tune. Listen to how those notes should be played and the lift of the tune. I suggest you hear it first several times before you read it. That makes it so much easier to develop the, very useful, ability to learn by ear.

↳ Posted by ross faison on July 1st, 2019.

Re: Planning to attend my first session.Any advice welcome

That sounds great! Everybody needs a place to start, and when someone with real experience and knowledge is willing to host a slow session or learning session, then you’re in luck! The host wouldn’t do it if they didn’t like encouraging people to learn, so you don’t need to worry about making a fool of yourself. Just use common sense and courtesy.

Be prepared for the fact that playing with other people is vastly different than playing alone when you’re not used to it. I remember that coming as a bit of a shock when I first started going to sessions. The main thing is that the music is going to keep going even if you make a mistake. And one of the hardest skills to learn early on is to be able to completely let go of mistakes as soon as they happen, and stay focused on what’s coming up, and not what you just did… Relaxation is key to that.

It is also very likely that they will ask you to start a set. So be prepared for that. “No” is an acceptable answer to the question, but I think it would be best for you to have at least two of your tunes prepared to start together as a set. Since it will be your first time, the easy way to go about it is to name the tunes and ask if other players know them before you start. And you can even ask for help starting the set, or getting from one tune to the next if you need. They will understand if you’re nervous, but the best thing to do is relax and try to play the set comfortably. Don’t try to play it faster than you can. And let the leader know it’s ok if they guide the rhythm and speed a bit, since you’ve never been in a session before.

By the way, just because the fiddler is an inspiration doesn’t mean that you need to be intimidated by them. You should be grateful that they’re willing to help you! The absolute best way to get better at playing this music is to play with people that are better than you. And that can be difficult when you’re first starting, because often times the pace of the music and the tunes will be well beyond your ability to even try to join in, so that’s why it’s so great that you have found a slow session, which can help you get more comfortable with playing in that kind of environment!

And finally, what makes a person a great session participant usually has at least as much to do with how fun they are to hang out with, laugh with, and share stories with as it does with how well they play music. Just be yourself, and engage with the people that you’re there with, and all will be fine!

Congrats, and let us know how it goes!

Yeah, I have to chime in on mandolins not being pointless. Depends on the combination of mandolin quality, the player’s ability to pull tone and volume (without working too hard), and the size and composition of the session. You may only hear the “ping” of the note attack on my mandolin among the other instruments, but it’s there, and I can manage to kick off sets and lead transitions without problems.

The only place my mandolin disappears completely is in the kind of huge sessions I avoid attending in the first place, where there are so many people that one side of the group can’t hear the other side. A session like that is a mess, with or without a mandolin present.

I am starting to move more of my repertoire from mandolin to flute, but that’s for reasons other than being heard, and more to do with differences in available ornamentation and expression. I will probably always bring both mandolin and flute to a session (once I get enough flute tunes under my fingers) because there are enough tunes that don’t fit easily or at all on the flute, and are great on mandolin.

↳ Posted by ross faison on July 3rd, 2019.

Re: Planning to attend my first session.Any advice welcome

Most session etiquette is just being thoughtful and following the principles we learn in kindergarten. Listen to others, take turns, talk to your neighbors, don’t be the center of attention all the time, etc. Walk in like an anthropologist and observe the dynamics a bit first. Some sessions are friendlier than others, but you’ll regardless make some great friends and find some other musicians of a comparable skill level who you can get together with for a smaller session and work on new tunes together. At this point of my life I know I’ll never be a Fleadh champion, but I can have a grand time, make some friends and hopefully learn a new tune or two.

I agree, playing an instrument that can be played softly makes it easier to learn tunes and not worry too much about being obtrusive or obnoxious. I’ve been playing for decades and it is a rare session where I solidly know even half the tunes being played that night. It’s okay to just listen to a new tune, and for a tune a I kind of know, I’ll listen the first time through then quietly join in while avoiding any noodling around. Eventually I can solidly play the tune and maybe even lead it. Life is too short to just learn tunes from written music, CDs or YouTube.

I also agree that in case you are asked you should have a set or two of tunes you feel at least a little confident playing, your “party pieces.” When you practice them at home be sure to keep the rhythm and keep going even if you make a mistake. Otherwise, you’re practicing your mistakes. If you’re asked to play (and don’t feel slighted if you aren’t, it depends on the session) of course you’ll feel nervous at first, but once everyone joins in it will flow right along and you’ll have no chance to slow down for your mistakes. It’s like diving off the high dive - some fear and trepidation at first, then elation and joy.

The great Scottish fiddle J. Scott Skinner wrote “Talent does what it can. Genius does what it must.” Pretty heady stuff there that applies to a chosen few. For my level of playing I heed the words of my friend Vance, “Dare to suck,” meaning not to be obnoxious and play something when it’s not yet ready for public consumption, but that in order to learn and get better you’ve got to start somewhere and just do it. You can’t wait until you sound perfect to finally join in. In fact, your playing may never sound perfect but in the process of learning and playing you’re very likely to have the perfect experience over and over again.

Re: Planning to attend my first session.Any advice welcome

  All sage advice, foregoing.  If you haven’t tried this already, and aren’t planning to attend the session, like, tomorrow, how about this?  You sound modestly competent as a soloist, so find an appropriately talented complementary instrumentalist (e.g. baritone-guitarist for mandolin, standard guitarist for a flautist / fiddler, etc.) to come and have a go duo-ing at home:  you get a chance, even before meeting, to explain how advanced [or otherwise] you are;  and, after meeting, to demonstrate your level, without an audience to witness it!;  your musical partner will [or should!] be fine stopping when you cock up;  fairly quickly, you can agree between you on which particular rendering of a piece where you’re going to consciously force your way through any mistakes (a habit you’ll HAVE to learn for a proper session:  a 5-part harmony group who visit our folk club actually REHEARSE the effect of each member goofing up, so they learn to recover fast — that’s dedication!)  It prob. won’t take long to get passably good as a duo, and there’s only one other person to glare at if you make a mistake ;-)
  Unless you’re screaming to join the session (you could always attend first without your instrument, so they get to know your face, how you interact with other people, etc. and YOU’ll learn about the personal dynamics of the group, even tell them you’re planning to bring your mando to a future meet), invite a third person to sit in on your ‘at home’ duo to see how it works as a trio.  This would give you TWO people to listen to while you’re playing, so you’d have to be pretty familiar with your tunes.  At home, with no [or only minimal, familiar] audience, you could consciously focus on listening while trying to play on semi-auto-pilot.  They’ll know what you’re doing, so any bloops will be understood as part of this learning process.  If you’ve found the right level of fellow musician, THEY’ll be trying to learn this, too. You could even take turns, by pre-arrangement, at being the one who’s listening harder than they’re playing.  Three is prob. enough people to give you a flavour of playing along.  One of them might attend the session with you, when you debut, so there’s SOMEone to kick off the joining-in process (an instrument different from yours gives other session members two options to copy chord-positions / fingerings, etc., if they don’t play solely by ear), which boosts the likelihood of their joining in quickly.
  Good luck.

I will offer you the much repeated guidelines for session beginners:

#1: Don’t noodle. If somebody is playing a tune you don’t know, don’t play along.
#2: Tune your instrument.
#3: You will probably spend most of your time sitting and listening for the first few sessions. Take this as an opportunity to learn new tunes.
#4: Always listen to what the session leader tells you.
#5: Don’t play anything unless you are invited to.

This applies to Old-Time, Scottish, and Bluegrass sessions too.

Bring your instrument, but sit back and listen to the session players and get the feel of the group… I myself may be a “very experienced” player, but when invited to sessions I hadn’t attended before, I will sit out the first few sets and listen to what’s going on and how it’s going on in that group.
