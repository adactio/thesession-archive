You can download for free a nice open-source program for Windows called Audacity for recording your tunes. It does support multi-track recordings. You would want to have a decent microphone, and you may need a preamp to get the mic signal strong enough for your computer to pick it up. Hope this helps in getting a start on home recording.

Re: Home recording

I would advise against standalone multi-track recorders - they are great for recording, but the little screen and awkward interface make editing and mixing very difficult. I have a Yamaha AW16G that I use when I need to record more than four channels simultaneously (because I only have a 4 channel PC interface) but the only realistic way to use it is to make the initial recording with it, then burn the individual tracks onto CD to transfer them into the PC and do the editing there, which is a right pain it the rear. Much better to buy an audio interface with as many channels as you think you will need - they range from 2 - 16 input channels, with corresponding prices. (And if necessary you can plug more than one into the PC at the same time.)

If you only need to record 2 channels at a time there are a number of interfaces from various manufacturers - Focusrite and M-Audio are very popular. I have a feeling the electronics are pretty much the same in all, and my only advice would be to choose one with a metal case to minimize hum. The interface will come with free recording software which will get you started, but it is usually a very stripped down ‘lite’ version of either Protools or Cubase which is missing some vital functions and has a restricted number of channels or tracks. When you out-grow the Lite software that comes with the interface you will want a full blown DAW - You can either pay hundreds of pounds for the pro version of your lite program, or download Reaper, which is every bit as good, but instead of only offering a pro version for several hundred punds, they offer exactly the same program on a private/small business license for about £60 (with a very genereous trial period with no disabled faetures.)

Re: Home recording

Yes, your mention of separate rooms does bring up one advantage of standalone recorders - they don’t make all the fan noise etc. that you have to deal with if using a desktop PC. I think all except possibly the cheapest audio interfaces supply phantom power. One problem I’ve found with both the AW16G and all the interfaces I’ve tried is that non of the are really sensitive enough to use with dynamic mics, and if the gain is turned up above about 3/4 the pre-amps get very noisy. I got round that by doctoring a dead mixer to turn it into an 8 channel mic pre-amp. At the moment I only use the AW16G for full band recordings, the rest of the time I use two Roland Edirol Ua-25s, which gives me four channels.

On the software front, Audacity is fine for simple recording and editing tasks, and for many people it will be all you need. But once you get into multitracking, complex editing and using FX, then you’ll find it much easier with a Digital Audio Workstation of some sort, although the learning curve can be pretty steep. Audacity isn’t a DAW, it is just an audio editor, and although at first glance the two seem to do pretty much the same job, there are some fundamental differences: The most important difference is that Audacity is a destructive editor - when you make edits or apply FX it overwrites the original recording file - if you ‘save and close’ then re-open for another editing session, you will find that the ‘undo’ button will only ever take you back to the beginning of that session, changes you made yesterday have become permanent. Whereas a DAW is non-destructive - the original track stems are never touched, but edits and FX are applied in real time when you play or render, so you can remove or re-do previous changes at any time. Another advantage is that Audacity has to render the track to apply an FX, it isn’t done in real time, whereas a DAW applies the FX and edits in real time, so you can hear exactly what you are doing as you alter the controls. The screen layout on most DAWs is also a lot easier to work with. Audacity is very simple and intuitive when you just have a couple of simple tracks, but it becomes very confusing once you have numerous tracks, all cut up intp little sections.

Re: Home recording

@ ronald.ellison, All Moldy

Audacity is a destructive editor, it is the way it works, not an opinion.

It’s true that you can mitigate to a certain extent by saving copies of the the stems - that way you can always go back and start again from scratch if you have to, (rendering to stereo instead of saving the project doesn’t solve anything - next time you open the project you then have the choice of either starting again from scratch on the original stems, or working on the rendered stereo pair that you created at the end of the previous session).

Unless you’ve used both systems it might be hard to see the importance of this difference, but to try to illustrate it, imagine you are editing a recording, and early on in the process you add some reverb to a track. A few hours later after a lot of other changes you decide that you’ve added too much reverb, and want to reduce it. In Audacity (or any other editor program) all you can do is use the ‘undo’ button to get back to the point before you added the reverb, thus losing all the hard work you did after that (and if you don’t realize there is too much reverb until the next day, then your only option is to start the project from scratch, using your backup stems). But in a DAW, because it is non-destructive and the FX are added in real time, you can change the level of reverb (or remove it completely) with a single click at any time, without affecting anything else that you’ve done to the recording.

Yes, it all depends on what you want to achieve. For just recording yourself for personal use a little recorder like the Zoom H2n or Tascam things is ideal, and if you just want to use your PC as a tape recorder, then Audacity is the right software. But the OP specifically mentioned multi-tracking, which takes things to another level and is either very difficult or sometimes not possible with a standalone, depending on the model. (Unlike Audacity and other editors, where each software author has his own idea of how the interface should work, so they are all completely different). If cost is an issue there are a few completely free DAWs such as Krystal or Anvil Studio, but I would recommend that you download Reaper - I don’t want to say too much, but if you look at the terms of their free trial you’ll see how it could be an alternative to free programs.

Re: Home recording

Keep in mind that your carpet underlay on the wall will only absorb high frequencies - those where its thickness approaches a quarter wavelength. But if you space it out from the wall on a simple frame or even hooks you extend the range of absorption downwards. Ideally, you’d have fluff of some kind inside it too, but depends on how keen you are.

Better to have a number of patches of absorption than one big patch, as this also improves the diffusion of sound in the room. Don’t have patches facing patches - that’s a waste (unless you’re going for really dead!).

Assuming your floor is carpeted (which absorbs high frequency sounds), ideally your ceiling should feature bass absorbers. But that might be getting more fancy than you are prepared to get!

I haven’t checked, but I imagine there is plenty of acoustics advice available on the web. Avoid any that recommend egg cartons, or the expensive commercial foam product that looks like egg cartons. That is like the underlay - not thick enough to control low end boom.

There is also software that can measure the reverb time (RT60) of the room at various frequencies - this can tell you whether you need more low end, high end, wideband etc absorption. I haven’t looked into this recently, although I remember the Theatre Shack forum has free software for room analysis that includes this.

Re: Home recording

You can calculate the frequencies above which an absorbent material will absorb. You need about a quarter wavelength to absorb significantly. So, let’s look at 1/2" (13mm) thick underlay pressed up against a wall or floor.

Thickness is 13mm, so if that’s a 1/4 wavelength, the full wavelength will be 4 times longer, so 50mm, or 0.05 metres.

The frequency of such a wave is the speed of sound divided by the wavelength. In this case, that is 340 metres per second, divided by 0.05 metres. That gives 6.8KHz.

So our underlay will only start absorbing significantly from that frequency upwards. That’s in the harmonic range of bright instruments, but above the range of even the harmonics of smooth instruments like the flute. To get down to the sort of frequencies we expect as fundamentals, we need many times that depth. Or we have to go to panel or Helmholtz absorbers that target lower frequencies.

Keep in mind this is all about absorption of reverberations, and not with interference by other sounds. That is another topic.

Outdoor recording can be done, but is at great risk of unexpected noises, like aircraft, frogs, sirens and fornicators in the undergrowth. Pesky, pesky fornicators.

Unwanted noise is always best sorted at the source. For example if your computer power supply is making clearly audible fan noise, time to upgrade it to a modern silent power supply. (If your computer is that old, probably time to upgrade the rest of it too!) Foot tapping is much reduced by taking the offending shoe off.

Good to make yourself a list of things to turn off before recording (and to turn back on afterwards). Depending on your circumstances and priorities, they might include the fridge, the phone, the dog….
