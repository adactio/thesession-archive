*#$%@! Rosin

Bought a couple cakes of new rosin from the local shop - the owner is an instrument maker, very knowledgable. I’d been thinking dark or sticky would be helpful - I vaguely remembered comments about it helping the bow do more of the work. I’d been using the Millant-Deroux that came with my fiddle, and figured perhaps darker rosin would help a titch.
The result is more like night and day - in Western Oregon the air’s very humid and the lighter rosin just doesn’t work, the difference is incredible! The store owner said he’d special order it for people but it simply isin’t suited for this part of the world.
Wish I’d known this a year ago - didn’t pick it up from tutors, fiddling friends, Burke video, Burke himself, Russ video, etc. There was one post on The Session about “Rosin!” that mentioned the stickiness/humidity correlation, but I’d no idea it was so crucial. I’d been complaining the whole time about having to put a lot of pressure on the bow, too.
I’ve some Sherman’s to use now, also some Hill. Thomastik was merely dark, not sticky.
The shop owner also told me about all these weird-ass soundposts he’d found in old fiddles - a good few No. 2 pencils, various hardwoods, and a few Patent Posts from the late 19th century - one was made of glass with a couple of holes in it. Sounded glassy! Thin. Another had a mile long “foot” - you’d adjust the direction the foot pointed in to modify the tone/response. It sounded lousy, too!

Re: *#$%@! Rosin

Well, to my mind, the trick to good bowed triplets--either the scratchy “cat sneeze” kind, or the three-clear-notes kind--is getting the motion inhumanly small. Then you just lean on the hair a bit for the scratchy kind. Grippy rosin doesn’t hurt, but it’s not necessary.

The bow cleaning kits I’ve seen come with a cleanser (alcohol), a hair reconditioner (no idea what its chemical makeup is), and a small-toothed comb to separate the hairs so you can really work the cleanser in. It takes a good 45 minutes to remove all the rosin, and another half hour to build the rosin back up after you’re done cleaning. But the results were quite good, and it’s way cheaper than rehairing.

Re: *#$%@! Rosin

Bob, it’s mostly a matter of keeping your bow perpendicular to the string all along the length of the stroke, and also making sure your hair is “into” the string when you start to move on it. On the e, we have a tendency to lose hair pressure on the string when our bow arm hangs straight down (which is a comfortable place for it to be on the e string). So you have to compensate by consciously applying a little pressure through the stick and hair onto the string.

Also make sure no part of your left hand is inadvertently touching the string, interfering with its normal vibrating.

Re: *#$%@! Rosin

Bob, it’s all the more likely when moving from another string to the e string on the same bow stroke. Crossing strings on a slur like this is a trademark of some styles of Irish fiddling and well worth extra attention to get it as smooth as possible, so that it sounds no different than a slur on the same string. It will improve your bow control if nothing else.

Also bear in mind that the e string squeak can and does happen to most fiddlers now and then, even the best. Whenever my tone isn’t what I want it to be on the e string, I remind myself to be more deliberate and conscious of the feel of the hair on the string. And I still like Stephane Grappeli’s advice to think of feeling that contact as though you were strumming the strings with your fingers, so the bow becomes an extension of your fingers. Hope this helps.

Re: *#$%@! Rosin

Hey, it happens to the best of them, if you listen close enough.

My guess is that you’re not playing “in” the string when you make the shift to e. Good violinists/fiddlers learn to ease off the e string a bit as it’s loud and high pitched without any extra help. You need to finesse it if you want a really sweet tone. But carry that too far, and you get the banshee’s shriek.

What’s helped me is to think of wholly committing to the string, maybe even getting closer to the frog than you might otherwise and digging in for a little extra bite. You can always back off once the string is vibrating and draw out that sweet tone.

I think this is one of those foibles that rears up now and then in every fiddler’s career. Don’t get a mental block over it, just focus some practice on more deliberate string changes.

And sticky rosin does help. I’m liking Pirastro’s goldflex these days--nice and grippy, but still smooth sounding.

But I was still getting squeaks, whistles, and dropouts that I am to this day unable to control.

I’ve talked to one other fiddler who had a somewhat similar story, but I’ve also talked to a few who haven’t had this experience. So I don’t really know what to conclude. I’m fishing for more data points.

I still suspect the rosin. I’m wondering if there’s something different about the quality of rosin that comes from new-growth trees?
