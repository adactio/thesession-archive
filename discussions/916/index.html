Re: Why is

THANK you, Joe…I’m getting really tired of all these “hate” “best” and “worst” threads…

That said, though, I of course have my own tunes that I could live with only hearing once every year or so. And I think the reason we end up talking so much about stuff like session etiquette and “wrong tunes” and such is that everyone wants to be liked and be thought well of, so it’s good to talk about what’s expected and what’s looked down upon, speaking in wide general terms.

It’s just that everyone is also very protective of what they see as their right to make the tradition over in their own shape. Which is exactly as it should be.

Take the story I told of the very good player and newcomer who, invited to start a tune, played a common session tune in a key that left out everyone at the session but the backer. Such a choice means any number of things (ignorance of other instruments and their ranges, ignorance of certain session protocols, a willingness to grandstand, could have been anything, really), but what it did do for sure is make me tick off the “watch this person a little carefully” category next to their name in my head. I don’t see that player with the complete and whole-hearted enthusiasm that I might have -- which isn’t necessarily bad or good, and doesn’t mean I think they’re a good or bad person, just that I now might not instantly invite them over for a few tunes when a few of us get together, until I get to know that person for sure and see which of those things caused that bit of a snarl. That’s a little sad for that person if what caused it was ignorance rather than a tendency to party piece at the drop of a hat, don’t you think? Yet if I invite a couple of players of like mind over for an evening of tunes, I’d like them to enjoy themselves in the manner they would choose -- so I can’t invite that person until I find out more.

Wouldn’t you rather find out about some of the rather unpleasant facts of session life at a remove, as it were, here on The Session rather than finding out that you’ve somehow removed yourself from the A list among the local players through simply not knowing something that no one is born knowing, but no one feels comfortable telling you in person? If you look at it that way, in some ways the “ill-will” (which I don’t think is, for the most part) expressed becomes something of a gift, if one that you tend to repackage up and give away again to get out of your house. ;-)

Well, guess I’d better go do some work. Dang.

Re: Why is

I think the best/worst threads are some of the most important discussions on the site!

Why should we be shocked that person X from country Y doesn’t like tune Z (or instrument A or player B, etc.)?

What we look for in these best/worst threads is a sense of where the direction of the tradition is going. What tunes are falling out of favor. What fine points of etiquette could be be aware of? What tunes are becoming more popular and what instruments are being seen at sessions around the world. What new players we need to look for and buy an album of?

What is suprising (to me) is how closely many opinions mesh! Even though most of us have never met each other, and we play at sessions all across the world and are at different skill levels. Isn’t that something to marvel at?

Besides, a good ‘slagging’ is absolutely essential in the tradition, wouldn’t you agree? But as for outright ‘meanness’, that person should be left to play a couple of tunes by himself while the players grab a pint at the bar.

In short, I find these discussions so valuable that I somehow tune out the negative.

So, I was thinking about Harry’s piece about generosity in one’s playing (over on the “most hated tunes” thread, and how that ties into this thread. Now, I don’t mean to put anyone down by this, only perhaps to propose a cautionary tale. I’d suggest that “hating” a tune would never occur to a player with great generosity of spirit, and that would show in their playing, just as it would show in the playing of someone who did hate a tune. How could you hide it? If you approach a tune with malice and forethought, sure you’ll kill it every time. But if you come to coax it to life and offer it as a gift to anyone within earshot (or simply to the four empty corners of your room), that too will show itself in your playing.

The Blackfeet Tribe up by Glacier National Park have lived in grizzly bear country for thousands of generations. Parents caution their children never to speak badly or mockingly of the bear because it will show on their faces and in their body language when they come face to face with the bear in the woods. Now, they’re not just talking about bad karma here. The point is that how you think and talk affects how you behave. And showing respect and generosity toward the bear is a wise way to survive in the bear’s backyard.
