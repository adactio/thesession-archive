Re: are these tunes public domain?

“Ok, but i am curious as to whether i can use these tunes and make them into my own original compositions”

If you do this, please state this clearly in the composition title. There’s probably no law to make you do so, but it wouldn’t be fair to your listeners otherwise. There are too many people attributing Tri Martolod to Eluveitie or Son Ar Chistr to Scooter already, there’s no need in creating more confusion.

Meanwhile, judging from what you ask, it’s very likely that folks who know these tunes and would be able to recognize them in your compositions won’t like what you’ve done, regardless of wheter you state the source or not. The reason is that you don’t know what are these tunes and how are they actually played. Imagine baroque music fan first hearing Vanessa Mae’s arrangements of Vivaldi, that would be somewhat close.

But if that does not bother you, go ahead. You’re not the first person treating Irish traditional music as some sort of clipart or free tunes bank, and certainly not the last.

It might help if you started by finding out exactly how this music is played, i.e. the style,and the social contexts in which it is used, i.e. sessions and for dances, and learn how to play it in these contexts. You might find that the music as it exists already needs no further embellishment beyond its endless recreation at each performance.
You don’t say what instrument you play yourself. I would suggest that this is important; this music is basically melodic, not harmonic, as can be shown on this forum every time somebody asks how to accompany it on guitar, or other ubiquitous chordal instrument.
Finally, there are the classical precedents; you need to give the correct attribution; “Variations on a theme by”…..“the traditional tune Whotsit”…..or “Brian McWhiskey’s tune Gargle the Turkey”….or whatever.

Re: are these tunes public domain?

Yes you can provided as pointed out that the copyright has expired. If you use a traditional tune as part of your composition, and the copyright for that tune has expired you certainly won’t be able to copyright the composition in its entirety - and depending on how much of the composition is in fact original you may find it difficult to enforce a copyright claim in relation to substantial parts of the composition against similar or identical works - it’s impossible to say for sure until you’ve completed your composition and it could be determined to what extent parts of it if any are original. There are also secondary rights which must be considered - often referred to as moral rights.

Re: are these tunes public domain?

Bear in mind that when music is recorded and then commercially released, there are always at least two rights involved: the rights in the actual recording, and the rights in the composition. In the UK the former exists, in most cases, for 70 years after the end of the year of its first release. This is a recent amendment where we’ve ‘harmonised’ with the EU.

http://www.ipo.gov.uk/pro-types/pro-copy/c-policy/c-policy-copyterm.htm

Rights in the composition are, and have been for some time, 70 years after the death of the composer. So, to be in the ‘Public Domain’ in 2014, a composition would have to be written by somebody who died before 1945.

You can’t copyright a ‘Public Domain’ piece but you can copyright your arrangement.

Years ago and as I understand it, MCPS (Mechanical Copyright Protection Society) the body in the UK that collects royalty monies for the rights to ‘mechanically’ capture (i.e. record & release) compositions, used to meet several times a year to try and allocate which percentage of newly registered arrangements were original to the arranger, and which owed more to the existing ‘traditional’ pieces. Their decisions ranged anything from 10% to 90% but, as you might imagine, this became pretty much unworkable and I believe MCPS now simply credit 100% of any arrangement of a traditional piece.

To put this in context the above Mechanical Royalty is set at 8.5% of the PPD (Price Published to the Dealer) multiplied by how many are manufactured or sold. A small company or individual would probably have to pay the former, a large company the latter.

To bring it up to an even more contemporary context, as a ‘streaming service’ and under the guise of UGC (User Generated Content) Google/YouTube pretty much ignore the whole thing and the calculations of whatever they do pay is certainly NOT transparent.
