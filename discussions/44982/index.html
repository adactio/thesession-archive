Thumb position on right hand - fiddle

Currently reworking my bowing technique as it was questionable at best. I’m finding it most comfortable with the thumb a bit further up from the frog, using all fingers apart from the pinky. I’m aiming to have a bent thumb at all times, but I’m noticing that my thumb will naturally want to straighten out when bowing down, and bent when up bowing. Is this something that I should aim to fix, and ensure that my thumb is bent at all times?

Note I’m not super fussed on getting the most amazing bow hold imaginable, I just want to make sure I’m not going down a very obvious path that’s not worth going into.

Re: Thumb position on right hand - fiddle

I find (and I know I’m not alone in this) that the bow hold you’re generally taught as THE Violin Bow Hold is actually not great for Irish fiddle, or many other fiddle styles beside. The “standard” bow hold, with the thumb at the frog, bent, the first three fingers curved over the top and the pinky straight on the end, is very much developed by and for classical violinists.
Many Irish fiddlers hold the bow a bit further up, and with a much looser grip, pinky not really making contact except when you’re lifting the bow off the strings. I think similar to how you described, in fact. It’s fine for the thumb to flex according to your wrist angle, this is natural and keeps it loose. I was taught the Standard Classical bow hold as a kid, and I only finally unlearnt it a few years ago, in my twenties!

See how Nancy Kerr holds her bow here: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2RvwoDmt8jQ

Re: Thumb position on right hand - fiddle

@Calum : “What are the disadvantages of a standard (classical) hold for fiddlers?”

Absolutely none - it’s yet another one of these myths. Of course there are a few variations in the hold, but it’s generally a highly functional one, and very good reasons for being so.

If you think about it, you need to be able to apply varying pressure on the stick at all the contact points with the string (using 1st finger). Also, you need to be able to apply counterbalance (pinky) of the bow. The thumb sits nicely in the little gap - you can see all this in celticladda’s video between 00‘20 and 00’26. Also, you’ll need to be able to tilt the bow fore and aft to alter the tone.

So, that’s what the classical hold offers - comfort, and the ability to use the bow to its full potential.

Players use all manner of holds, as shown in celticladda’s video (very informative, btw, thanks!) and each is individual to the player, and they use what works for them. So, what hold should you use? Here’s my take on it - a standard classical hold, for the reasons given above. It’s the default hold, if you like.

Using a part way up the stick will limit your bow travel, and you need all the bow you can get, eg if playing a slow air. You’ll also gunk up the bow hair too, through time, with moisture from your hand, with the result that that part of the hair will be unusable.

I think it’s a bit like deciding what type of strings to buy. You ask the question on a forum, and you get lots of different answers, so you still don’t know which strings to get. However, if you look at what sellers use on their instruments, these days it’s usually Thomastik Dominants or d’Addario Zyex - the “default” choice until you get the chance to experiment with other brands after you buy the instrument.

I think the same type of thing applies to bow hold - classical is the default, and for good reasons. I’d start with that first, and see how you go. You can adapt it or change it later if you feel the need to.

Re: Thumb position on right hand - fiddle

@Jim Dorans – it’s not “another of those myths”. I resisted changing my bowhold for many years, out of, I suppose, a kind of loyalty to my teacher who taught me the standard grip when I was a kid. But when I did finally try it, my god, the change was immense. It’s not a one-hold-fits-all, by any means – I now find myself holding the bow in different places for different kinds of tunes.
And I disagree that the Standard Classical hold is a “highly functional one”. It’s highly functional for classical music, sure. Classical violin technique is all about making a very full, rich, sustained sound, using the full length of the bow and being very exact about volume and timings. For this, you need extreme control in your right hand, hence, holding it at the frog, bent thumb, extended pinky.
Irish (and generally, fiddle) music is dance music, it’s about the rhythm more than anything (as far as the bow-hand is concerned, at least). You want to achieve a fluid, effortless and driving rhythm from your right hand, more than achieving a rich sound etc. And for jigs and reels – the fast, rhythmic dance tunes – I find that if I hold the bow up from the frog, nearer the centre of gravity, I can get a much more driving sound, more accurate attack (meaning, when the bow lands back on the strings after being lifted off), and more sensitivity, for less effort from the right hand.

Re: Thumb position on right hand - fiddle

>“What are the disadvantages of a standard (classical) hold for fiddlers?” Absolutely none - it’s yet another one of these myths.

>And I disagree that the Standard Classical hold is a “highly functional one”.

Firstly: Thank you, Randy. Good job well done.

It seems to me that the myth and fallacy is the belief that ‘the’ classical bow hold is appropriate at all for any types of music. Fortunately, ‘classical hold’ is not the default for traditional non-classical players. It is not comfortable (if it ever is) until it becomes second nature (and thus requires much practice to acquire) and has tensions even then. I prefer first nature which is naturally relaxed, and doesn’t require forcing yourself against nature. There is some figuring out to find your natural hold, then there is no need to practice what you discover.

Tension can only cause problems with flow of use and the resulting sound (center and harmonics). The obvious remedy is relaxation, which has only physical and musical advantages.

To paraphrase: Unconsidered playing is not worth hearing.

Having tried ‘everything’ physical to play this instrument, I find that a bent thumb causes tension and a totally relaxed thumb is the answer for playing all musics.

Your judges are your feeling and hearing. Not looking. Violinplayers generally do not question the way they physically operate. They try to copy what they think they see in ‘better’ players or ‘classical’ players or try to go by some imagined supposed rules of looks. Most teachers of this instrument teach by sight. Most beginners learn by sight, with or without a teacher: “That player sounds good to me, so to sound like that I must look like that.”

Having learned to play somewhat, players take teachers‘ look and demands as gospel: “My teacher would kill me if I did other than they insist I do.” It’s the human curse, like religion or territory: the first experience is assumed to be the only valid one. A player “couldn’t possibly” visit another church for an hour, or visit and understand other peoples, or experiment with physical variations for musicmaking - many times even when these ’assumed perfect’ exclusive initial situations cause them great physical, mental, and spiritual pain.

I approach learning from extremes: start at one extreme where it doesn’t work, and progress thru the bell curve of possibilities til I get to the other extreme where it doesn’t work. Then I know what is what, and can choose from that discovered spectrum.

So try this: start with this extreme, without any instrument, sitting comfortably: Bend your right thumb hard and hold it bent. With your left fingers, feel your tight hard tendon from the thumb all the way up to the shoulder joint, and for some people also in the collar bone area which is actually the third section of your arm. With thumb still bent, straighten your arm, which will probably not extend fully.

Then let your RH relax entirely and flop forward, and again feel the thumb tendon which will be totally soft and loose all the way up. This naturally rests your thumb in the center of its motion, the center of its bell curve: the opposite extreme is to purposefully bend the thumb backward, which has no purpose except to show that it doesn’t work. With your relaxed flopped hand and thumb, straighten your arm fully, which will now feel wonderfully relaxed.

You now know that any tension, no matter how small, will get in the way of your physical and musical operation. It’s your choice how to proceed from here.

The body works bilaterally. When your RThumb is tight, your LThumb will be or will want to be tight. Players with tight RT and RIndex usually pinch the neck with LT and LI. Note that a players‘ R and L hands look and thus feel the same; the most obvious indication is that both hands are either far from or close to the tools (both are instruments: tools). This is true of ’all’ players. Relax one side helps to relax the other.

A great myth and fallacy of ‘classical’ playing is ‘control of the bow’ by 1st and 4th fingers. If these fingers are active in these ways, this proves that the RH is not relaxed, regardless of what players say. A bent thumb and active 4thF are not necessary to play classical music. As the great pedagogue Kato Havas said: “Look at the length of the bow; look at the length of your 4th finger. It is not fair to ask the little finger to hold up such a long heavy thing.”

I always play with bow hair as loose as possible for the music at hand. Single notes are best with the loosest hair, 3 or more strings are helped with tighter hair so all strings can be sounded together easier.
Hair tension is directly related to where the bowing works best; where along the hair it touches the string(s). For faster notes looser hair allows playing in the middle or lower; tighter hair only works in the middle or higher. You cannot force the bow to work where it cannot. Adjust hair tension so the bow plays where you want it to.

ITM players generally play quieter than classical players, so the bow can move slower, therefore with RH up the bow there is enough hair to play slow tunes. As is known by players whose RH is up the bow: this has advantages for overall bow balance (and thus not using the 4thF to balance), and is always appropriate for the music being played - never inappropriate - if it didn’t work, the player would do something else that does work.

So, yes, adjust hair tension and hand placement for the music you will play right now. Why have everything sound the same due to dogma? Give yourself all the musical advantages. Why hold back?

Notice in the best ITM and other non-classical players that the hair is riding on the string: the violin and bow playing ‘with’ each other, using the least possible ‘control’ by muscles [see the Yvonne Casey video above]. Actions are minimal, the arm does as much as the hand, all is relaxed as possible, and the sound is relaxed and musical. Notes are clear and perfectly timed and inflected (classical playing is not unique in these, in fact inflection is often lacking).

Notice in classical players that much playing is done by muscles: bowing ‘at’ the string(s). A bent thumb - and other tensions - is much of the cause. A tense 4thF is another main cause.
To claim that the classical bow hold is more versatile is comparing apples to oranges because ITM players do not do ‘fancy’ classical bowings. And doubly empty because bent thumb and muscling 4thF are not actually necessary to play all the ‘fancy’ classical bowings.

Classical music is not the only expressive music; ITM and other traditional playing require great attention and finesse. In fact, classical music is less expressive: 1- because players are fundamentally minimally demonstrative; 2- because of the danceable nature of traditional music, there are facets that classical players don’t (and most can’t ever) understand or play correctly. Holds of violin and bow with wrong mental and physical attitudes constrict classical player’s playing of dance music - but the repertoire is full of such - so the music doesn’t dance or even flow correctly. When have you ever heard a classical gigue, hornpipe, bouree, etc. (or horrors: swing) played like their traditional counterparts? The composed notes may be further involved than for traditional music, but why shouldn’t both pieces have the same feel? I have played many dances with traditional tunes and my own tunes of many ‘styles’. As a native player of classical and traditional musics, I play classical music with traditional sounds and requirements (such as not ruining the Time or feel).

Synopsis: A relaxed (straight) thumb allows relaxed, musical playing. Do not be swayed by religious dogmas. Trust and believe your own body, because that’s what you must live in and are ‘playing’ with. Be happy and have fun.

Happy musicmaking!
vlnplyr

@vlnplayer, you said :

“A great myth and fallacy of ‘classical’ playing is ‘control of the bow’ by 1st and 4th fingers. If these fingers are active in these ways, this proves that the RH is not relaxed, regardless of what players say.”

It’s perfectly easy to use the 1st and 4th fingers and still be relaxed. (It’s possible to be relaxed with any hold, unless you are trying to use it as weapon) :) Often the 1st is just resting on the stick, not applying pressure, but ready to do so if need be. Same with the pinky. In fact, if you have the pinky resting on the stick, there’s less effort used than if you keep it up in the air.

Sometimes just looking at someone’s bow hold can be deceptive, as in it looks as though the 1st and 4th are actually doing something, when they are not.

“A bent thumb and active 4thF are not necessary to play classical music.”

If the thumb is straight, how do you alter the fore and aft angle of the bow? This is normally done by straightening out the thumb outer knuckle. How do you do that if the thumb is straight?

“To claim that the classical bow hold is more versatile is comparing apples to oranges because ITM players do not do ‘fancy’ classical bowings.”

Well, Sean Maguire did fancy classical bowings (but that’s a whole other issue!). I get what you are trying to say, but it seems you are trying to mould the music to match the bow hold, rather that apply technique to making a good sound, regardless of genre.

“Classical violin technique is all about making a very full, rich, sustained sound, using the full length of the bow and being very exact about volume and timings.”

Sorry mate, but that is simply not true. Just look at Paganini’s Moto Perpeteuo, Flight Of The Bumble Bee, Elfentanz etc - these exclusively very short D-U-D-U movements. These are all standard repertoire too. They’re not virtuoso pieces.

Another reason to have relaxed thumbs:
Your thumbs are analogous to your jaw. Animals use their jaw to hold things.
So when your thumbs are tense, your jaw might also be tense.
But players who hold the violin tight with their chin may already have a tense jaw, and therefore not notice tension caused by the thumb(s).

>If the thumb is straight, how do you alter the fore and aft angle of the bow? This is normally done by straightening out the thumb outer knuckle. How do you do that if the thumb is straight?

No mystery. I move my thumb slightly in either direction: away from or toward me. The stick stays in place while the bottom of the frog moves. Minimal muscle use. Minimal change from one RH shape to the other.

>Well, Sean Maguire did fancy classical bowings (but that’s a whole other issue!).

Yes it is: one player. Un-usual.

>I get what you are trying to say, but it seems you are trying to mould the music to match the bow hold, rather that apply technique to making a good sound, regardless of genre.

No, I made it obvious that it’s the opposite. I believe I said: as ITM players who don’t use classical holds know, their bow hair (tightness and placement on strings) and RH (shape and placement) are always in the service of the music. They gently mold themselves and their tools for best use and sound for ITM. This is good. “That’s the gig.”
Why should they use another - not as useful or musical for them - hold for ITM on the off chance that they might have one pint too many and during a hornpipe launch into Pachelbel or Prokofiev?

Not all players play more than one genre of music - so they would not be interested to play Paganini - but, aren’t ‘fast’ single bows played for reels, even if just to show off?
And off-the-string bowing is just a bounce, available to all holds, if the player cares to.

>apply technique to making a good sound, regardless of genre.

Are ITM and other non-classical holds not “good sounds”? Is a classical hold necessary for good sound in classical music?
Do all players with a classical hold have good sound? No. And I was one of them until I examined body and music and learned how to play. Without classical holds (recall our ‘pancake hand’ discussion) I have played about every genre of music played in the US (except hip-hop, which doesn’t interest me to play, altho I respect those who are actual artists). I play solo Bach and read thru Paganini’s Caprices. I use my same ‘holds’ and use for all - there is no difference nor need there be. My sound changes appropriately for the music I’m playing, because: “That’s the gig.” How you feel is how you sound. I feel different for each genre, each tune, therefore my sound is appropriate, tho I always play me.

“You don’t know ’til you’ve tried it.” I wonder how many readers tried my “extreme thumb” experiment? Have you tried holding your bow as others do, to learn why they do it? “Do not criticize a person until you have walked a mile in their shoes.”

I walked many miles up mountains and thru deep valleys in the classical straightjacket, and have been happily free for many years.

Happy musicmaking
