Concertina Vs Fiddle dilemma.

Hi all

I’ve arrived at the decision that I want to put proper time and effort into learning an Irish session instrument - having a “main instrument” per se. I can bash out pieces, trad and other, on banjo, guitar, piano, accordion, harmonica etc but i have no main instrument, per se.

In terms of irish music, up to now my experience has been mainly ballad singing for myself, formerly with guitar but now with banjo, scarcely with others, and never at sessions. At a couple of sessions, busking and with friends I have played basic accompaniment on mandolin as well as five or six tunes (polkas, jigs, one reel) that i can play quite roughly.
However while it is fun, this isn’t entirely satisfying and i feel the need/want to learn one instrument properly.

My initial decision was to learn concertina, which i’ve had a fondness for for quite a while listening to recordings of noel hill, sean dwyer and tim collins. I own a cheap concertina which I got on ebay years ago for less than a hundred euros(it’s probably a piece of shite, though i think it sounds quite nice, though it’s a good deal bigger and bulkier than most ones you’d see in trad).

However i’m now torn between that and fiddle. When asking someone else about this, they mentioned to me that with fiddle you can play in any key whereas concertina sticks to trad. While i want to learn almost specifically for trad, i do have interest in other areas and can envision myself becoming frustrated at boundaries with a certain instrument.

I also see more room for emotion on fiddle. I haven’t been too swayed by slow airs on concertina.
and there is the fact that i have some mandolin experience and they’re tuned the same (fiddle and mando), whereas with concertina i’d have to start from scratch. Basically though with both of them I would be starting from scratch as I’ve never made any attempts to learn trad apart from by ear. IE ive never learnt scales or become familiar with keys or anything like that.

Concertina is also more portable and i’d like that a lot. While I could listen to concertina recordings for a long time, I don’t listen to fiddle as much at all. However whenever i hear a fiddle in real life i’m entranced. I find it’s quite different in real life.

What do you people think? I’m sure someone can advise me well. When I have a dilemma like this, I usually know in my gut which way i’m eventually going to go for but right now I actually have no idea.
Feel free to put down any thoughts, advice, info on keys, playing styles, difficulties and so on. I’m well aware that the final decision rests in my gut but I’d still care very much to hear what you all have to say.

Many thanks

Tim

Re: Concertina Vs Fiddle dilemma.

I play and teach both, (though I’m much better at the fiddle than I am with the concertina).

I’m not sure I agree with llig’s assessment of the relative ease of articulating phrases on the concertina. At any rate, the concertina is so much easier to learn, that you’d have to factor that into the equation.

The big question basically comes down to “how hard are you willing to work for it?”.

The concertina is considerably easier to start with. The notes are pretty much always in tune and, barring mechanical difficulties, never squeak or squawk. While the set of convenient key-signatures on the concertina is smaller, it’s pretty much all doable. Learning tunes on the concertina has much in common with learning to touch-type on a keyboard. With regular practice, I usually estimate that people can become comfortable with (if not proficient) on the concertina after a couple of years.

The fiddle, on the other hand, has a much sharper learning curve. Not only is the thing infernally difficult to even HOLD correctly, but it’s so sensitive ot getting it to not sound like fingernails on a blackboard is no easy task (contrary to popular belief, 60-70% of the difficulty of the fiddle lies in getting control over the bow--your mandolin experience won’t help as much as you think it would). On top of that, the minimum standard of acceptable playing is much higher on the fiddle, probably due to the vast numbers of decent fiddlers out there--many of whom learnt the instrument as children. I’ve more or less come to the conclusion that you need to practice roughly an hour a day, for roughly 6 days a week, for roughly 5-7 years before you start feeling comfortable on the fiddle (let alone “proficient”). Much less, and you just won’t find yourself improving noticeably.

Still, the fiddle is a much more flexible and pervasive instrument. While the concertina occasionally feels shoehorned into playing Irish tunes, the fiddle seems to take to it much more naturally once you’ve got a handle on playing the damn thing. Indeed it often feels like tunes were made for the fiddle. Most of the repertoire from Donegal and Sligo, for instance, lies much more comfortably on the fiddle than the concertina (once you’ve got the hang of the instrument itself).

In the end, though, it really comes down to how much time you’re willing to put into whichever instrument you take up, and what kind of music you want to play. The fiddle probably has a bigger eventual payoff, with more popular and conventional tunes that lie well on it, but the concertina is much more comfortable and forgiving, and MUCH easier to get up to speed on.

Re: Concertina Vs Fiddle dilemma.

A very cheap fiddle goes for around $200, with bow. It’ll sound terrible, but it might play acceptably.

A decent fiddle can be had for $500 with some careful searching.

A “good” fiddle (IMHO) usually can’t be found for less than $1000.

of course the prices for fiddles can skyrocket beyond that, but there’s increasingly limited utility to paying more than $10K for a fiddle…

A very cheap concertina (something like a Rochelle or a Stagi), can be bought for $400-600. They’re not much good for anything beyond learning the notes of tunes (they’re too slow for any real ornamentation work).

A decent concertina is typically in the $1500-3000. These are typically either nicely-made accordion-reeded instruments like those from the button box or Frank Edgely, or an old concertina-reeded instrument like a Lachenal, with 20-buttons or in need of some degree of repair.

A “nice” concertina will typically start from around $5,000 (with a waiting list), and go up to $10-12K for a nicely restored Jeffries--which is about as nice as concertinas get these days.

Re: Concertina Vs Fiddle dilemma.

As one of the few who play both can I disagree with the assessment the concertina is easier to play; it is not, it is harder and will always be harder no matter how long you play it, whereas the fiddle starts hard and becomes easier. It is just misleading that you can play in tune straight away on the concertina.

The greater capacity for variation of tone on the fiddle is much easier to manipulate than the concertina. And its notes are always in the same place. The concertina is a different instrument for every tune. While you get beyond the “although these three notes are the same as in (name tune) I can’t play them with the same buttons because the next note is (name note)” dialogue, it is always with you. Concertina players are sorting the fingering out on the run for tunes they have not played in a while no matter how well they once played them.

I will allow one caveat; if you have less than good pitch perception you will learn faster on the concertina than the fiddle.

Re: Concertina Vs Fiddle dilemma.

The problem with selecting EC rather than anglo for ITM is not much to do with the bellows and much to do with cohort and hand positions.

The theory that the anglo demands bellows reversals which somehow are ideal for rhythm is widespread but I doubt it is spread by good players of the anglo. It relies on the idea that a note is likely to be in the other direction to that just played and the slightly different sound of a bellows reversal onto a note will emphasise the rhythm. But it also relies on the idea that the notes demanding bellows reversals will be in the right order and place to be at rhythm points that bear enhancing. It just aint so. You could say, it sometimes happens. Some of the time you can make it happen by using a note in the other direction, yes this is very handy, but an EC can do that too.

Here are two better reasons for favouring anglo.

Cohort: if you take up anglo for ITM and want examples, tuition, CDs to listen to, people to ask, tutors to follow; they are all for anglo. If you are a natural loner and happy to swim against the stream; not bad attributes in my book, be an EC driver.

Hand position: the anglo hand straps, rest and hand position make it possible to apply sudden and great force in both directions. This suits highly rhythmic playing and is considerably harder to do on the EC. Not impossible, anyone who has not heard Dr Dow play ITM on the EC has missed a treat, but much harder.

To support this, if you can get to hear Henrik Muller from Sweden playing his EC which is made with handstraps and a palm rest (like a anglo) you will hear the driving sounds that sound more typically anglo.

So I think it a good idea to go light on recommending anglo for a natural bouncy rhythm that will get into your playing naturally, when you hear it, it was worked for.
