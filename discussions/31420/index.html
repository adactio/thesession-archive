Rant warning!!!

It’s a wonderful thing we do, playing tunes with our mates. What’s not to like? But a bit of appreciation is in order. Certainly a session is not limited to what takes place between an arbitrary ‘launch time’ & “last call”. If it were a session would be no different from getting a root canal w/state of the art anesthesia (your teeth look grand, but you don’t remember a thing; minus a few brain cells).

For the love of the tunes & good mates, I don’t know what to do with some of the questions which (beg your pardon) just seem **too** NEEDY. No offense to players of any particular instrument, but here is a sample of the questions which make me think not everyone who comes to our session knows the least bit of how the music sounds outside the session …

“Do you have the chords?” “How many times does it get played?” “What’s the key?”
Hopefully none of you has heard these questions on a regular basis. All the same, can you think of any good responses which might either enlighten those who are ‘tune challenged’ or just encourage them to bugger off & find a different preoccupation?

Thanks in advance.
;)

I played for about 30 years on different instruments and in different bands of various genre’s, and all of those questions seem commonplace and normal to me, even among my mates who I practiced and played with for years. That’s why we used to keep it written down. Quite often after about 3 hours and 12 pints into gig we’d write it in chalk on the floor in front of us. The most annoying thing for me was acting as an on-the-fly translator between a mate from Belfast and another from Melbourne who couldn’t tell the difference between a Belfast A and a Belfast E . I would rather somebody ask than stuff it up, and indeed I think that asking is normal. If somebody purely shouldn’t be there because they are out of their depth, then they should be told. There is a right time and a wrong time to be learning (and teaching). But overall and personally, no, I remember when I didn‘’t know the simple things, and I remember with gratitude all the guys that had the patience to yell the chords at me etc., and if I had the opportunity I would still try and retain that virtue with anybody else. I love the music more than my own ego. And isn’t the largest part of ITM passing on and sharing your experience? How do people learn?

I can’t remember hearing those questions at a session; the guitarists who show up know how to play at a session and pretty much know all the tunes.

I have heard, many times, during the last time through a tune, somebody shout out “G” or what have you, letting everybody know both that they will be leading the transition into the next tune and what key it’s in.

There was here, 20 years ago, a good box player who always carried around a binder which had chord charts for all the sets he played. So he would be out blazing away somewhere and if a guitarist seemed clueless he would toss them the binder.

I’ve done that myself, but my binder is full of all the airs, Carolan stuff, hymns, etc that I play as background music on uilleann pipes at wedding gigs. Many’s the time that a legit/classical guitarist, harpist, cellist, violinist, flutist, or pianist has joined me for a tune or for an hour. At one wedding a violinist and cellist both joined me for an hour, the violinist improvising lovely harmony parts and the cellist improvising wonderful bass parts. Boy those guys were great musicians.

My binder has “lead sheets”, that is, the melody line with chords written above. Many legit/classical musicians are proficient at using lead sheets to accompany others.

Yes! As Gobby put it “all of those questions seem commonplace and normal”… But I also sympathize with those moments when the patience is stretched or gone and there’s a temptation to choose one of the several uncomfortable options, thinking of my own ethical comfort, that includes doing nothing, silence, or passing the buck with a slight cruel tease in the motive ~ “so-and-so can help you with that”… I would feel particularly awful to p-ss on anyone’s flame of passion for this music. To use an old cliche, there are no silly questions, just silly answers, or ways of answering, or not. It doesn’t take a lot of time to direct that passion positively, but sometimes the repeats can wear us down, this I understand and sympathize with. There’s no doubting that some of the work needs to be self-directed, rather than to expect that all the answers can be had in one sitting in a session. The session is in the main about playing the music, not teaching it, though that can be a ‘part’ of it, but not usually a large part of it. Sometimes recommending a teacher, a book/recording combination, a particular good collection with decent chords, but also that a beginner shouldn’t be expecting themselves to keep up with it all, to accept they’ve work to do, things to learn, and that those can be pursued elsewhere while enjoying a good earful for the night of a session.

Like learning to drive, you need to think ahead so that you don’t panic in an emergency situation, such as sliding on ice. You learn to be calm and to do what’s necessary to avoid an accident. You may need an attitude adjustment with regards to these questions, as it may be you’ve built up strong negative feelings about them, reason for your ‘Rant Warning!’ I know you through your interactions on site and have respect for you, so I also know you can work that same consideration and wisdom to good here. What you need it to find some good short and constructive responses. Don’t ignore them, as that is not just rude it is cruel, especially where someone new is coming into this music and is likely clueless. Ask yourself these questions and then come up with answers that you feel comfortable with. Play that scenario over in your head. It shouldn’t be difficult for you, I’m sure of that, to find concise ways of dealing with this that won’t blow out the flame and that will keep it alit and offer promise of increased understanding and appreciation of this music, and of what a session is about. Finding constructive alternatives to letting it wind you up will also benefit you, and might win the tradition a continued future.

How we deal with such things also carries over as ‘tradition’. I’ve screwed up royal in certain past situations I regret, where my patience wasn’t there. But, rather than airing my laundry, I’ll air someone else’s. :-D A friend lost his cool when a group arrived at a long established session with both sheet music and music stands. They took their row’s worth, and they’d even start tunes. Eventually he, the friend, had enough and approached them with something along the lines of “F‘k off!” We never saw them again. Worse, I’d been working with this lot in another situation, working to get them away from their dot dependence and listening to each other, while also getting some time in playing for dancers. I’d even gotten a few of them up on to the dance floor. Their leader, sadly, died from cancer. He’d never educated them about sessions and session etiquette, and I’d never thought, as we were focusing on other things, and originally that was with their leader present. I kept it to why they’d come to us - to get some experience playing for dancers, for dance. After this incident we never saw the again either. They are still around, but it was a great loss, at least as I see it. It could have been dealt with better, but I also understand “F’k off!” and what lead to that loss of patience. Yes, it could have been dealt with sooner and better. Sometimes we let these things build up and the result can be huge and even catastrophic. It is better to release steam a little at a time rather than risk an explosion where everyone gets burned…

An ex-English teacher eh? :-D Where’s me hoody! (It’s green and my wife refers to it as ‘your Kermit’)

These last years I haven’t had the time for doing this! But in the past I liked to turn up at to me unknown sessions with my C#/D or what ever box I travelled with. Generally, I try to help everyone who asks for it. Some people, however, I will take aside and give some advice on how they can help themselves. That only happens when it becomes apparent that they are not working to pull their own weight, simply relying on others to carry them. The accompaniment guide I put in my profile came together as a way to get people to do their “homework” and not slow the session down by doing all their learning in public, and sometimes at the expense of others and the quality of the session.
I myself, despite many years of playing, have rather modest abilities, so I am frequently benefitting from the advice of others. And I need to get a notebook, because far too frequently I am asking people to name tunes at the ends of sets, then forgetting and asking the same question next week. So I should certainly be willing to put up with some questions from others!
The only time I see real problems building is when a player’s opinion of themselves outstrips their actual abilities. Excessive modesty tends to work to the advantage of a session, but a lack of modesty always makes things difficult, as you can’t teach someone when they think they have nothing to learn.

It depends on where you are.
If you want to invite a guitarist or other musician to play along with you, it’s sometimes common courtesy to state which keys you’ll be playing in before you start and, maybe, even tell them what you are playing. They may not be as used to playing in sessions as yourself, i.e. they might usually play guitar to back songs etc.

However, it shouldn’t be expected and if a guitarist wants to feel at home in a “full blown” session situation, it is surely better if he/she have got to the stage where they can work things out for themselves.

Like Skreech(earlier in the thread), I don’t always shout out the key correctly especially when I get caught on the hop. However, when accompanying tunes rather than playing them, I can usually work this out myself without too much trouble. It’s as if I’m using a diffetrent part of my brain for the melody as opposed to the accompaniment.
