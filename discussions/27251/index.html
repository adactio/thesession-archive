Re: Making sets(Cause one tune just ain’t enough)

There have been some extended discussions on the subject - I’m sure someone of a more archival bent than I will be along to point you to those.

In the meantime, there aren’t any really great rules. I usually like to make sure the tunes flow neatly into each other - the ends ought to match up in a tidy fashion, I think. Other than that, you can get away with a lot.

For now, I’d suggest you learn sets off of records - if you like one of the tunes, learn the others as well. The logic is simple: it gives you sets that you can be sure are acceptable (argument by authority) and if someone else has that record, it’s reasonable to suppose they’ll know that set as well.

Once you start getting more tunes under your belt, you’ll start to hear sets come together.

The main thing: don’t, don’t, don’t give in to the temptation to put together reels and jigs and polkas and hornpipes willy-nilly. I don’t know why, but it’s a common failing of new players, and it never works. Play reels with reels and jigs with jigs and so forth, and everyone will be happy as larry.

Re: Making sets(Cause one tune just ain’t enough)

When I was very young (and foolish) I had the misconception that sets sounded nice if the tunes were all in the same key. But actually one of the really nice things about sets is the effect of the key changes. It is good to have tunes that fit together in some way -- perhaps some parallel structure of a key phrase or something. But there are all kinds of things that work. Some very popular sets are formed by themes in the names (e.g., jigs having to do with the sea, or reels having to do with maids). Some really nice sets even mix jigs and reels. Recordings are a great place to get ideas for sets. You can hardly go wrong following the choices made by Coleman, McGann, Bothy Band, etc.

By the way, when starting out in leading sets, it is really important that you know the start of the second tune very well, because it might not be so easy to get the start in your head while you are still playing the first tune.

Re: Making sets(Cause one tune just ain’t enough)

You might try to build a set that includes more and more people as it goes on - usually a good idea, as a lot of people dropping out will usually make for an anticlimax. To make this work you want to reduce the tonal complexity of the tunes as you go on. In a Scottish session it helps to progress towards the two-sharp pipe scale; at any rate, if you want to include whistle or flute players, don’t cut them out by switching to fiddle-only tunes after they’ve joined in.

Cape Breton fiddle sets often stay on the same tonal centre while switching mode. Can be effective.

Re: Making sets(Cause one tune just ain’t enough)

There’s some good advice above but one piece stands out. Do NOT mix reels, jigs, HPs, etc, with anything else except their own kind. The only time the ‘other’ works is on stage - as a piece of choreography - and only if you’re good enough.

Stick to the basics. First you have to get buckets and buckets of tunes. And I can’t emphasize this enough. You need a LOT of tunes. So learn, practise, learn, practise, etc, etc, etc - till your blue in the face.

Most of the tunes you’ll learn will be in the usual keys of D Maj or Min, G Maj/E Min, A Maj - whatever. Most can glide seamlessly into the other. Some, however, can sound discordant. That can be a deliberate effect or it can be a ‘hand-brake-turn’. Your own ear will tell you.

But there really aren’t any rules. D to G to A min to E min or any permutation - most can sound good. People from all parts of the country (or world, for that matter!) will add what they think sounds good to their ear. It depends, to an extent, who’s driving the session or the actual set.

Bottom line: Buckets (and more buckets) of tunes plus imagination. But keep reels with reels, jigs with jigs, etc.
However, if you want a stage act to muck about indiscriminately , the world’s your lobster!

Re: Making sets(Cause one tune just ain’t enough)

You can stick to the same key, jump by fifths in either direction around the circle of fifths (G to D to A), jump up one step (G to A), go from major to relative minor or vice versa (G to Eminor), stay in the same key signature, but go from modal to major or vice versa (like E dorian to D major). Sometimes the phrasing in one tune will lead well into another. Sometimes you can play with the end of one tune or the beginning of the other so they do flow together. Lots of options, and lots of fun experimenting!
One other piece of advice, it is lots of fun to go from less familiar tunes to more familiar tunes, so you pick up more players as the set goes on, and end with everyone joining in on an old favorite.

Re: Making sets(Cause one tune just ain’t enough)

There is no steadfast rule that you need to play sets, either. “Sets” are a relatively recent development in ITM. Not so long ago, musicians would often play a single tune a few times and end it, as you’ll hear in some of some of the older recordings. Kitty Hayes remarked that when she got back into playing after many years off, she had to learn the relatively new-fangled trend of playing “sets.” She was more comfortable just playing a tune and leaving it at that. So if you’ve got a tune you love and know really well, just play it alone, and don’t worry about following it with another tune or two. That will come.

Re: Making sets(Cause one tune just ain’t enough)

Coupla thoughts here.

First, learning sets off of recordings is okay--a good starting place for newbies. But some folks frown on copying the latest fad sets off the latest fad cds. Especially if the set is gimmicky or includes some unproven original (of the cd’s musicians) composition.

Second, a set of tunes doesn’t have to be played one way. You’ll learn a lot, get more mileage out of the set, and be more popular among experienced session mates if you can mix the tunes up once in a while. So if you come up with a set of jigs that you like, A - B - C, take the time to play around with the sequence and suss out if the tunes fit well together in another order, say, C - A - B, or C - B - A, or B - A - C, etc.

Even with a limited repertoire, this approach gives you experience transitioning between tunes in more than one way, keeps the set from going stale, reveals a lot about how tunes fit together (and what goes wrong when they don’t), stretches your memory without overloading it, and keeps your musical friends on their toes.

I can’t stress enough that with the right beginning and ending of sets you can really push in some great tunes into a session. They get popular very fast.

SOME sessions actually have set lists; if you’re looking to join one you might ask if you could have a list. Some also just have tunes that get commonly played as sets even if they aren’t in list form-- just keep in mind that the repertoire of a session is essentially the overlap of the repertoires of the individuals in the session, so standards will change from place to place.

My fiddle teacher told me to learn tunes in sets because that’s how they’re typically played. If you have However, you’ll probably also want to learn the internal structures of tunes, how the phrases call and respond to each other, which is something he also taught me-- very useful starting off to keep that bit in mind, helps build your ear for tunes!

Just to put another word in for Seamus Creagh’s ‘Tune for Practice’ CDs, almost all the tunes he plays on there are in sets.
