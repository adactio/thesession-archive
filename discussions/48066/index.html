Re: Instrument Choice…Regrets?

I’ve played fiddle since my youth (some 50 years now), and also played a host of other instruments along the way. Fiddle has been both the most challenging and the most rewarding, the most like singing. Of the instruments I’ve played, fiddle allows the greatest range of expressive technique…the possibilities seem endless, beyond my limited talents. But that keeps you always exploring, learning. Flute comes a close second. To the point of the OP and that wonderful interview between Enda and Martin, by comparison plectrum instruments have felt somewhat limiting. There’s only so much you can do with a pick on an acoustic instrument. The real magic of a fiddle is in that hank of horse hair.

As for “effort in versus output,” fiddle does beg for a lot of effort in. But the potential output is vast. I honestly can’t imagine ever running out of sounds to explore, things to learn.

Also, though I’ve focused all along on Irish trad, fiddle’s versatility has opened other very enjoyable musical doors.

My only regret is that I never emptied my bank account to pop for a truly fine fiddle and bow. Other fiscal priorities always intervened. But the fiddle I do have has been a good companion for many years.

Re: Instrument Choice…Regrets?

My history is guitar for around 30 years or so, then mandolin with octave mandolin on the side, then D flute with a low Bb flute on the side. I could also throw in piano as a child and Rock kit drums as a teenager, but that’s been my path in adult life with guitar taking up most of it until I fell into ITM about 15 years ago.

I don’t regret leaving guitar behind, or the opportunity cost of not starting earlier in ITM with a different instrument because I was in a different community of musician friends during that time. Playing acoustic and electric Blues with friends I had at the time was fun. I wouldn’t trade that for anything.

I picked up mandolin after moving to a different part of the country, and when my Significant Other decided she would try playing violin again after years away from playing it as a child. We both fell into the local OldTime scene, then quickly discovered the local Irish trad scene and here we are.

After enough years playing ITM on mandolin, I started to feel a little frustrated with the narrow scope of articulation on mandolin compared to what you can do in a sustaining instrument like the “core” instruments of the tradition. Hammer-on and pull-off fingerings can simulate cuts and rolls, but none of that can be heard in a boisterous session so we usually fall back on the banjo “treble” ornament that can be heard clearly.

In the quest for a sustaining instrument the fiddle might have been an option, but with one fiddle player already in the house, I thought it would be best to choose something different. A good button box is expensive, a keyless flute was more affordable, so that’s where I went. In a different timeline I’m out there playing the box instead.

Anyway, no regrets. Not even the time spent on mandolin while playing ITM before getting into flute, because I feel the mandolin still has a place in the music. There are tunes I enjoy playing with essential notes on the bottom G string where the flute can’t go, and “folding” to the octave note just doesn’t sound right to me. The mandolin also has expressive capabilities with partial chording within the melody line.

I know there are opportunity costs in trying to retain or build skills on two very different instruments. I might be better on flute if I avoided mandolin completely, but I’ve always been something of a multi-instrumentalist. That’s been hard to get away from, and maybe it helps minimize the regret factor.

Re: Instrument Choice…Regrets?

I don’t have any instrument regrets, even despite my current experience of myself becoming more and more inclined to the concertina, and less inclined to the fiddle.

The string family has always been my favorite instrument family tonally, while keyboards have been my favorite instruments to play. I don’t believe there is a grander musical ensemble than the string quartet. I don’t believe there’s a better partner for the piano than the cello. I learned recently that in Cape Breton traditional music, the fiddle and piano are inseparable. And even though the guitar and the piano don’t often come together, when they do come together, they sound absolutely exceptional. And yes, I adore the sound of all of these instruments as individuals and soloists. So, it is a sustaining surprise to me that I have become profoundly more inclined to commit to the concertina than to the fiddle(or any other stringed instrument for that matter).

However, I don’t regret any of it. The time spent with the fiddle and learning Irish Traditional music through it needed to happen for me. And there are just too many fortunes that the experiences have led me to, for me to regret anything about it. Too many to talk about. Even now, one of my younger cousins has decided to take up the violin; By her own initiative and interest. The kid has always been naturally skilled musically, but she had never talked about learning music, so I never brought it up to her. And then, seemingly out of nowhere she’s like… “Aye dude, I wanna learn the violin…”. So even virtue of simply being there, the fiddle deserves no regrets from me, because it’s influence in my life has been prolific.

Now, I do acknowledge the piano as my main instrument. I’ve been playing piano for over 20 years, which is twice as long as I’ve even been playing traditional music. I mentioned the fiddle because it was my main instrument for Irish Traditional Music, and now the concertina in my main instrument for it. As for the piano, I’ve mentioned here in the past that we recently “renewed our vows”. And even though that was supposed to be figurative, it’s come to be very literal and meaningful. I’m actively working on two piano projects at the moment, and I’m revisiting a personal roots musical genre that I’m taking a deep dive into.

All in all, the music is in abundance, and I am exceedingly grateful for all of the people that have helped make that happen over all of these years.

Re: Instrument Choice…Regrets?

Even though I’ve been playing Uilleann pipes since 1999, and feel like I have some proficiency on them as a session player and some studio work, I have always mixed feelings about it because I’m really not interested in being an amateur ethnomusicologist along with being a musician. This seems to be something unique to the Uilleann pipes in the tradition.

I treat it like an instrument, played in a style that is consistent with the tradition, informed by, but not determined by its history and the styles of those in the past.

I love listening to the historical pipers, but I’m not one of those people who could tell you “oh, Seamus Ennis played XYZ this way, while Patsy Touhey did this…” I just play tunes, and maybe I include some aspect of what I enjoyed in a historical recording, but I can barely remember the tune names, much less the specific historical source of what I’m doing. I don’t treat playing the instrument as some kind of historical re-creation exercise, which is sometimes the energy around the instrument.

I’m glad there are those who are focused on the historical aspects and playing styles, I’m just not one of those people or ever will be. It’s an instrument on which I put music.

I’ll have to settle for skilled poser (or heretic). I’m fine with that.

Nienna, fiddle is an endlessly aspirational instrument, isn’t it?

The more closely you listen, the more there is to hear, the more nuances that open into your playing and into the tunes. So there’s always more to aspire to, except perhaps in those fleeting moments when everything clicks and you play something you’ve never done before or even had any idea you could do. And the magical thing about those moments is that they can happen at any stage in your progress.

The whole kit makes me wish I could play 16 hours a day. Do that when you’re younger because old bones don’t do so well with marathons. :-) The saving grace is that with age, the years of experience make learning now surprisingly efficient. Tunes, technique, concepts—there’s such a wealth of context, most anything new quickly latches onto something old and familiar, like a piece fitting into its slot in a jigsaw puzzle. And it sticks. I can learn a new tune, play it three times, and it’s all there still tomorrow when I pick up the fiddle. I wish it’d been so easy 40 years ago! I wouldn’t still have so many tunes to learn now, hah!

Re: Instrument Choice…Regrets?

Nienna, James Kelly is something of a prodigy, so his estimates are about half the time it takes the rest of us mere mortals to achieve ease on fiddle. ;-)

Seriously, those first 20 years really can feel like an “endless slog.” It may help to reflect now and then on how far you’ve progressed, and to take great joy in those moments when ornaments DO come out crisp, intonation is spot on, and hands mysteriously, suddenly ARE nimble, and you play comfortably with precision and good tone. Whenever any of those qualities appear in your playing, you want to pay deep, complete, and enthusiastic, even joyous, attention to how it feels so you can replicate it again, sooner than later. (The emotional aspect is important—our brains build stronger synapse chains when the stimulus moves us, is weighted with emotion.)

On fiddle, it’s easy to focus on everything that isn’t working because there’s so much that can go wrong. At a pivotal point in my fiddling apprenticeship, I was thoroughly mired in all the things I couldn’t do—at all or well or consistently. Fortunately, a mentor encouraged me to let go of the inner critic and just focus on anything that DID work. I was skeptical (of course! that was my mindset at the time!), but I took a deep breath and gave it a try. The crucial change was, instead of dreading everything I knew would go wrong, I started being grateful whenever a roll or bowed triplet came out clean, or my bowing created lift and pulse, or my fourth finger landed where it needed to be, and so on. Celebrate even the smallest win. (Yes, you still want to notice when things don’t go as planned, but just notice it—don’t attach any angst or negative feeling to it—and move on.) The improvement didn’t happen overnight, but within six months my playing was progressing better than the old me could’ve ever hoped.

In short, every time you open your fiddle case, do it with optimism, gratitude, and a sense of fun. Focus on whatever goes well. At the very least, the “slog” can be a positive one. :-)
