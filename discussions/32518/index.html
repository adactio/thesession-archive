Bias (Time heals all wounds)

I’m sick in bed perusing ‘mr interwebz’ and I came across a video of a great player, a great player who happened to be in a crappy mood when I met them.

In short, my bias towards said player started when I witnessed them be vitriolically nasty to a well meaning audience member. If I remember the person asked them if they were from Sligo (pr with a S not a Sh) and the response was, “It’s not Sligo it’s Shligo and I’m from Galway.” Then rolled their eyes and shook their head. This was while the player was on stage! in front of everyone.

Whatever, it wasn’t pointed at me, and I could appreciate that the person had been on a house concert tour of the States and difference in cultures and whatnot, but it was a terse reaction to a well meaning question.

The thing is, from that point on I subconsciously avoided buying that persons recordings or seeing them when they came over writing them off. I’m robbing myself, ’cause the person is really talented, but the bad taste lingers.

Then there’s the case of certain tunes that a well meaning, yet utterly talent-less musician used to stumble through week after week. The person finally gave up playing, but the associations of those lovely tunes was almost permanently soiled - I’ve gone back and found most all real nice tunes, but learning them was put off by almost a decade because of his bumbling settings mixed with his know it all attitude.

Every session has it’s ups and downs and as bad as I’m making him out to be - he is a smart guy, playing music on that instrument just wasn’t meant to be - maybe he’d be better off on another.

(In case you’ve noticed - I’m being vague as to protect the accused and it’s all allegorical anyway)

Another bias I had was against sawstroke fiddling (changing bow direction on every note), because when I first learned I taught myself and that’s how I bowed everything. I was in for a harsh road of relearning how to bow, everything that felt intuitive was ‘wrong’ according to my teachers. Eventually, in my mind, I associated figure-8 bowings and slurring into the downbeats in jigs as ‘correct’. I worked so hard to do relearn it all and focused on listening to fiddlers that did the same thing. When I heard northern players (mostly) sawstroke, it struck me as jagged and frantic. I’m over that now - it’s just different. Here’s an interesting article about a famous fiddler who went through something similar --->http://journalofmusic.com/focus/tradition-and-aspiration

Sessions have bias too, I’ve often heard novice sessions and musicians gripe that advanced sessions are uppity and no fun, although I rarely hear it the other way around. If something comes up about a novice session at a more advanced session, a couple of knowing nods might be exchanged, then the topic is dropped.

…Agus faigamid suid mar a ta se

Re: Bias (Time heals all wounds)

Exactly the same with me….sometimes. Occasionally, if a tune had been regularly played by a player I personally dislike (there are not many of them, thankfully) or if a tune had been regularly murdered by a hack player, that tune will thereafter remind me of that player to the extent that I will avoid playing the tune. Conversely if it’s played by a nice person/nice player, the tune can thereafter remain attractive. Music is all to do with emotions and so the aesthetics involved in what a tune will trigger is all part of that. It doesn’t have to be, and isn’t, logical. Fortunately there are thousands of tunes to like and just a few to avoid.

Yes, I think we all associate the playing of some tunes with some people, if, for whatever reason, our brains forged a connection between person and tune. Sometimes this is a good thing; you feel like you’re reaching back to good memories of a particular person when you play “their” tune. Sometimes not. Like Brad, I have been put off tunes because I have heard them murdered week after week by people who have been playing the same 25 tunes badly for the last 25 years. Then, usually quite some time later, I hear the tune played well by someone else and think, “Hey, that’s actually a good tune!” Which, of course, it always was. It was never the fault of the tune.
