Re: Fiddle question would you play the Ds in the 1st bars of gustys frolics with pinky finger?

I definitely wouldn’t - and never do - use my fourth finger for those Ds in that first bar of Gusty’s Frolics. It’s just making things hard for the sake of it, and wouldn’t even sound as good. I’d suggest that’s why Ciaran Tourish doesn’t do it, too.

Apart from not making sense from a fingering point of view, you want the sound of those open Ds, plus there’s kind of a little bit of that bottom A that lingers sometimes and that’s nice - hard to hear on that video ’cos the sound quality isn’t great - old technology? - but I’d bet that Ciaran lets that A sound linger a bit. Too tempting not to.

Re: Fiddle question would you play the Ds in the 1st bars of gustys frolics with pinky finger?

I don’t think there are that many tunes that need - or even benefit from - a fourth finger on the G string. I’ve been trying to think of one. Take Farewell to Ireland. In the second bar, if you play it like GEDB, G,B,DB, which is one of the possible ways to play it, you could use a fourth finger on that second D. Any similar shapes in other tunes would be OK too. Plus, sometimes, in some contexts, it’s nice to double a D - so, play it with both a fourth finger and an open string simultaneously. That’s a really nice effect. But you have to know where to use it.

The only time I would use a D on the G string would be in a slow air, so that I can give it a bit of a wobble. In the Shetlands, they use the third finger to slide up instead of the pinky. Re: Fiddle question would you play the Ds in the 1st bars of gustys frolics with pinky finger?

Sometimes I’ll finger a tune in what I call negative first position-e.g. playing the B with my second finger, the c with my third finger and the d with my pinky. This has the double purpose of strengthening those weaker fingers and finger combinations, and also helps to break any reliance on muscle memory. I’ll do this on the mandolin or fiddle, but not on the banjo where the stretch is still killing my left hand.
Conversely, sometimes on the banjo or mando I’ll play tunes in higher positions, but incorporate open strings for lower notes, sort of a cross between Keith style 5 string playing and McReynolds style cross picking. This I do entirely by ear, deliberately not thinking about the notes. I also sometimes do this to incorporate drone notes on open strings where the melody normally falls on a fingered note on that string. I can’t do this on the fiddle, though, because my bow hand isn’t up to I. Also I would never do this in a session, where I’m usually hanging on for dear life to just play the tune.

Re: Fiddle question would you play the Ds in the 1st bars of gustys frolics with pinky finger?

I believe the steel E string came into use in the early years of the 20th century. Before then, gut strings were the norm - plain gut for the E, A and D, and wire-covered gut for the G. I use this set-up on one of my violins, and it is noticeable that the open gut E has similar tonal qualities to the gut A, so there is a smooth tonal transition for example from the D on the gut A string onto the open gut E. From the Baroque period to the end of the 19th century players would have used the open E as a matter of course for that reason. My feeling is that the general use of the open steel E today in Irish fiddle playing is a continuation of the older practice, and so we get a distinctive open E sound coming through which might not have been so obvious with a gut E. A possible consequence of this could be changes here and there in the accentuation of a tune - an open steel E being used on a weakly accented note, for example.
