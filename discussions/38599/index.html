Abc Notation: A background on traditional music notation, it’s application using ascii code, MIDI, tune databases & apps.

Recently I was surprised to find there is a growing number of people using some form of abc notation with little to no knowledge of how this system developed. Beyond that I realised how easy it is becoming to utilize abc’s apps w/virtually none of the front-end software. Things are moving faster than ever.

Except for me. I’m taking a step back in the hope of appreciating where this began, why it seemed to emerge out of nowhere in the late 1980’s - early 1990’s, & where it might be headed in the late 20-teens ~ early 2020’s.

I’ll begin with myself & invite anyone interested to fill in the blanks or simply post how you discovered abc notation & how you are now using it or ask any questions relevant to the subject.

My initial introduction to using letters for shorthand music notation was during Willie Clancy Week. I took a tinwhistle class where we learnt tunes hearing them played by our instructor. After class she would assist us by providing a “copy” of the tune however we wanted it. I did have a hand recorder (2002) & recorded each session. But I also kept a small notepad with all the tunes jotted down in a very simple form of abc. It wasn’t much, but I’d heard the tunes & it served it’s purpose as a reminder.

It was later, when was I putting together a tunebook that I learnt abc code for computers. I began w/ABC2Win by Jim Vint < http://www.abc2win.com/ > I eventually began using AbcEdit by Joop Coolegem. Joop offered great support back in the day. Ultimately though I found the simplest way for me was to transcribe the abcs & convert to pdf scores using command files. Though if I were to use software, EasyAbc would be a good choice & it is still being supported.

There have been some great abc developers. Two who come to mind are Phil Taylor ~ BarFly & Wil Macaulay ~ 5-Line Skink.

I’ll shut up now. Thanks in advance for anyone who contributes their bit about this very old, yet modern form of music notation.

Ben

“I reject the idea that ABC’s came into prominence in the ’80s — Tom Billy was giving them to his students in the ’20s and O’Keeffe used abcs as well as his special notation in the ’40s”

For discussion purposes, we should probably distinguish between the informal letters-for-notes systems that people use for writing tunes on napkins and the modern well-defined ABC syntax which we’re all using on this site. Anyway ABC has a load of great properties which are not necessarily shared by pieces of paper, PDF files or JPEGs:

* It’s readable and writable by human beings. It can be written on napkins.
* It’s text-based so could be sent using email systems of the 1980s
* It’s searchable by title, and notes, easy to store and index in databases
* It can easily be transposed and generally transmogrified by computer tools
* It can be typeset to sheet music in any style you like or converted to (shudder) MIDI
* It can be imported into and exported from many pieces of music software
* It makes sense in small fragments - e.g. that bit goes “EB~B2”
* It has a well-defined syntax with a real specification which has helped to stop incompatible variants springing up (although these always do)
* It’s non-proprietary. Anyone can use it.
* It’s concise - many times smaller than PDF or JPEG. You can fit thousands of tunes on your phone.
* There’s loads of software and online apps for dealing with it.

All these things and more explain why it’s become the most popular way to share tunes. It’s not perfect, but it’s handy and useful.

So you have an instrument like a whistle, have been told the ‘names of the notes’ and want to jot down something to remember a tune that you have worked out how to play.

From there writing down the note names hardly counts as an invention (though it wouldn’t surpirse me if some chancer tried to patent it). The need for a convention to indicate the octave appears quite quickly. I suppose not knowing the note names one would end up with hole numbers or, more likely, some form of tab.

I suggest that it didn’t come from ‘nowhere’ in the 1980’s but came from the musically minded early adopters of personal computers. Suddenly there was access to a way of turning letters into sound and, later, musical scores. I can’t remember how it worked on the Sinclair Spectrum (mine had 16k of RAM !!!) but somewhere in the shed I may have a cassette tape with recordings (analogue, like the sounds a modem makes) of a program that played The Harvest Home transcribed from O’Niell’s.

Adobe published the PostScript language in the mid 1980’s and it was adopted so widely that it soon looked as if it was going to be around for the long term and was free from restrictive intellectiual property rights issues (Adobe had a commercial interest in getting it adopted). Back then anyone interested in computer graphics was still familiar with doing it at a level similar to plotting lines on graph paper and for those folks the most basic implementation of Postscript gave the wherewithall to generate the dots and squiggles that make up a musical score - the file generated by ABC2MPS starts off with a little library of them.

So my suggestion is that in the 1980’s the technology and the people who knew how to use it (and were prepared to do so into the wee small hours) were both there.

I use EasyABC but still make use of the command line programs for when I know how to get them to do something but can’t find it in EasyABC.

Re: Abc Notation: A background on traditional music notation, it’s application using ascii code, MIDI, tune databases & apps.

In the 1960s and 1970s there was a lot of work being done on typesetting programs (such as RUNOFF at MIT and troff at Bell Labs). A lot of development of fonts and standardized printer languages were being done in the 1970s (e.g., Postscript and TeX). Open source ghostscript was developed in 1986. Note that pdf was not created until 1991. These digital typesetting technologies were essential enablers for a text to sheet music system -- such as MuTeX, which was developed in 1987. A significant motivation for the early abc language was to generate input for MusiXTeX (the improved version of MuTex).

Thanks for keeping up the discussion. The lot of you have done good work responding. I think my motivation was from the revelation that something such as music notation, abcs, any concept of music is bound to have it’s own ebb & flow. Musicians play, think about what they’re doing, one thing leads to another & before you know it a variety of systems develop & grow for remembering what you played last night; or much farther back.

Now the old systems are a foundation for a much faster paced, multi-purpose way of *notating* music ~ postscript, PDF, TunePal, abcjs, Thesession TuneSets. But none of this happened in a vacuum. Didn’t say it did, Thady. Thanks for pointing that out though.

Whenever I want to consider where something on the mustard came from I go digging in the archives. One person who never disappoints is ceolachan, great name, he has plenty to say on the subject. Here’s a gem; ~

“while I actually studied the ink to paper way of notation, with dots and all the other paraphernalia, ABCs is easier, less fiddly, and the fit between ABC notation and traditional music is as beautiful as that between the dots and trad music, though one is more convenient than the other, and less fiddly, but I shouldn’t have to say which one. I have always been in love with communication, including that of music, and including the written forms. The graphic representation of music, and that history, is wonderful, and I have no problem with accepting ABC notation and Solfeggio as part of that history, that tradition. The earliest examples I have and have seen of the ABCs we now use as an ascii code for computers are from the 1800s…”

I came to abc through Chris’s abcmtex in the 90s, and started using it to retranscribe a bunch of Cape Breton tunes I’d got from Sandy MacIntyre. IIRC, I was the first to ask Chris to support a notation for the Scotch Snap which became the A>B B<A we’ve come to know and love.

do not want to (or cannot) use the package ABC2MTeX but who wish

the document index.tex, a guide to using ABC2MTeX for archiving
and indexing tunes, and to the example files English.abc,
Strspys.abc and Reels.abc. It also refers to playabc, a separate
package for playing abc tunes through the speaker of various
machines. It is best read in conjunction with an introduction to
abc notation available by anonymous ftp from

celtic.stanford.edu/pub/tunes/abc2mtex/INTRO.txt

or, if you have WWW access, [previous link, currently deceased]

http://celtic.stanford.edu/pub/tunes/abc2mtex/INTRO.html

transcribing tunes, you can ignore most of description of the
information fields as all you really need are the T (title), M
(meter), K (key), and possibly L (default note length) fields. I
have included a full description however, for those who wish to
understand tunes transcribed by users of the package.

Finally, the notation can easily be expanded to include other
musical symbols. Please mail me with any suggestions."
