Re: Assorted flute questions

F is good. So, too, is G# especially if you want to play much fiddle music. I miss these a lot on my keyless flutes and use them a lot on my keyed ones.

Long F is more universally useable than the short one, but when the short F works then it is easier to use. (Think D->F…)

If you ever need Eb then there is no realistic alternative to the key. But, in ITM you rarely need it. Whether you need Bb really depends on what you are wanting to play. Sometimes it is useful, but again less often in ITM. Some flutes cross finger Bflat acceptably (for a given value of “acceptable…”).

C Natural keys allow an acoustically distinct C Natural. The others are all still available, but the key allows a loud, clear and in tune C Natural. It can also be used to help with the tuning of some other notes especially C# on my flute. A key and a thumb hole for c natural are acoustically identical, the only disadvantage of a thumb hole is that is so good and easy to use that you have problems when you pick up someone else’s flute and want to play a tune on it…

Re: Assorted flute questions

Keys - More is always better than less. How necessary is your call. I use the Cnat every time I play. It is easy to finger and more accurate and powerful than a cross finger. I cross finger on fast passages alternately with using the key depending on where I am going and the note I’m coming from. I’ve never used the short F because it is awkward. I use the long F often. D#, C# and low C all come in handy for songs and airs, as well as for accompanying singers. G# comes in handy, although not often.

Flute in F - Will not affect embouchure in the slightest, and is no harder to play. I have one and find it carries better when playing outdoors, but you will no longer be in pitch with anyone else you care to play with. I can’t recommend getting one unless you have money to burn or really, really want one (why?).

Don’t worry about the fact that others have played a given flute. A simple swab will take care of any slime (ew!), which is mostly condensation and thus clean, anyway. It’s a non-issue unless you have a psychological hang-up about it. Someone who smokes might leave a smell, but that’s all. Don’t worry about it.

Re: Assorted flute questions

I have a slightly different perspective, because when I started playing flute in the 1970s there weren’t loads of new makers all over the place, where one could get various keys and features a-la-carte.

You got an old flute and you played it. Nearly everyone I listened to or played with played 100+ year old flutes.

So you learned to play a vintage 8-key flute, and that’s really all I played in over 30 years of playing. For many years it was a Rudall & Rose (their 1827-1837 address) then for many more years a Koehler & Son, London, c1860, with the big Pratten style bore and holes, a real honker.

So you have all these keys under your fingers, why not use them? I used all of them, including Low C and Low C# on the footjoint. The Low C# was great for a sort of gracenote on Bottom D, which gave the illusion of sliding up to that Bottom D, like fiddlers do. I loved playing with fiddlers and trying to imitate the stuff they do.

The C natural side key was my least-used, I only used it in the 2nd octave.

About the Long F and Short F, the flute has both for very good reason, though for sure with practice a fluteplayer can get on fine with only one or the other. As was pointed out above the Long F is needed to go completely cleanly between F and D, or indeed any notes which require the ring finger of the lower hand to be down. From going between E and F the Short F key is easier. But not necessary!

So were I buying a new bespoke “Irish flute” I would, at minium, have Eb, Long F, G#, and Bb. That gives you a full Chromatic scale with only 4 keys. In my opinion the C side key and Short F are handy in certain situations but not necessary.

About smell, I’ve handled and played hundreds of antique flutes and never was there a smell… well, except the Hard Rubber flutes! It was quite a fad in the 1880s and so forth to make flutes out of that stuff, that smells like what it is, rubber.

About F flutes, they’re great players! No harder to play. I used to have one, and it was great fun for the guitar and mandolin to capo up 3 frets and for us to just start blasting through all the regular tunes, but have them transformed.

Re: Assorted flute questions

o’muirgheasain, with respect, you’re missing the point completely.

In an Irish session where everyone tunes to a nice solid A=440 Hz, the tunes will sometimes be played with a slightly sharp C nat, either because enough diatonic instruments are involved (pipes, flutes, whistles) where that’s an idiomatic sound, or there are fiddlers that are just used to hearing the C played that way in Irish traditional music.

There are notes between the notes. This isn’t Western Classical locked into 12TET tuning. It’s one of the fascinating things about this music, and I only learned it as a mandolin player when I discovered that…. “hey, something isn’t quite working here.” I can still play the music on mandolin, but I’m at least aware of what’s going on.

If you play flute and always lock your C nats into perfect 12TET pitch by bending the note with embouchure, you might be missing something interesting.

Re: Assorted flute questions

Anyone who has gone to the trouble of learning to bend their notes into tune will (or should) be doing it in conjunction with their ears. Unless they have absolute pitch which I can only imagine as being a disadvantage for a musician…

That means you bend the note until it sounds right together with what else is being played around you. Obviously a bit difficult when the tune is fast, but still necessary. Utterly impossible when people on either side of you are out of tune with each other and also often difficult in a session when you cannot always hear those you assume are reliably in tune. And all of that is easier, the closer the neutrally blown note is to being in tune. Exactly which in-tune is not so important as listening to what is going on around you. By “neutrally blown”, I mean the note you play when not consciously tying to adjust the tuning.

And exactly how is the blue note meant to fit with an accordeon in ET? And the tuning of many whistles, as played, is so appalling that it is almost impossible to play 2 whistles next to each other without cringing, let alone try to work out where their C Naturals are. All you can do is listen. To the others and to yourself.

Tuning is an agreement between friends - that means there is no absolute right or wrong. I do not like the electronic tuner approach, but many people I see do not seem to know how to tune by ear. I would rather have a whopping loud drone note coming from somewhere in the middle of the table (G is best for flutes, but A for Fiddles and is more usual. A good D would do too. In fact, do it for all of these…) and everyone should try to play in tune with that, maybe all the notes of major arpeggios? (e.g. DFAdfa for a D Drone.) Or even better, a simple tune which works with a drone. And it all only works as long as everyone does the same thing. Leave the drone on until everyone is in tune with it.

(I do the above with my small pipes as it is the only way I know how to get them in tune at 440Hz. The electronic tuners I have tried do not work well. A toothpick jamming keys down on an electronic keyboard works for me…)

(I, personally, use the C Natural with its own tone hole by default. In my case using a thumb hole, but acoustically it is the same as the key.)
