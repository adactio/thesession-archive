Ideally, watching people shouldn’t give you anymore help than just hearing them (try telling a blind person that their handicap is also a handicap to their ability to play music).

However, it’s a performance you’re going to and it’s good that you’re excited and you should soak it up through all your available senses. Pay close attention to his economy of movement.

Also, make sure you speak to him. He’s never not approachable and it’s important that you view him as just the ordinary bloke he is - important because it makes learning to play as well as him more easily attainable.

Re: I get to see Kevin Burke!

Jerone, definitely see if you can get a lesson, whether or not Kevin does a workshop. You can reach him through his website (http://www.kevinburke.com/html_booking.html) and ask if he’s doing a workshop in your area, and if not, can you book a private lesson.

Kevin’s as down to earth and charming as they come, and he loves to help out newcomers to Irish fiddle. You have a knack for it and he’ll recognize that right away, so you’ll have a great time.

One of the best ways to use a lesson with any decent teacher is to have them walk you through a tune you already know. Ask them about variations, watch and listen to their bowing and pulse and phrasing, listen to where they put the twiddly bits and how effortless that is. Using a tune you already know gives you a baseline to compare their take on it against yours and the differences will really jump out at you.

Re: I get to see Kevin Burke!

“Trad gods” - that gave me a chuckle. You’d be thinking of Tuatha De Dannan, then, eh? ;-)

“Accessible” is Mr. Burke’s first name (“Kevin” is actually his middle name). Just don’t let him talk you to death. If he starts in on “coming up with variations is like making a fine salad…,” cut him off after 45 minutes and insist on playing a tune together.

You can learn a lot from Kevin (or any other good player) by listening to him explain how he thinks about what he does with the bow and twiddly bits, etc. But you’ll go home with a lifetime of stuff to woodshed on if you spend 5 minutes playing with him and really listening.

Jerone, I live in Montana, which is measurably farther off the beaten path than where you are. But even here we get Irish traditional musicians touring through now and then. It’s rare when we can’t get them to do a workshop or lessons, and many join in on our session. I get food for thought and a jolt of inspiration every time. But they’re also just tickled to find avid fellow tune hounds out in the back of beyond. (Here in Montucky, they’re usually relieved to find that we have electricity and indoor plumbing, and the cattle aren’t stampeding down Main Street.) I’ve yet to meet one that thought of him or herself as a celebrity. So do yourself a favor and pay attention to tour schedules. Get in touch with anyone coming through your neck of the woods and ask about workshops and lessons. You’ll be surprised at the opportunities--and often friendships--that arise.

↳ Posted by Michael C. Gill on March 6th, 2012.

Re: I get to see Kevin Burke!

Yes, I got to talk to him after the show. I asked him what’s a good way to get ahold of the Irish sound and he said to listen to a lot of different players. Then he talked about how in language, accents in a specific area may be stronger or weaker in different parts of that area, but how you can still tell that the accent is from that specific area. And how even though there’s a lot of freedom in the music, there are a lot of things that you can do wrong. With all this he said that listening to different players, you learn the similarities of the “accent” as a whole and also the things that make them different. He also said to “soak your head” in the music. He was a bit more elaborate with all this and even though the advice was common, the way he explained it made it invaluable.
