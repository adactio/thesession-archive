Re: Newbie issues…

You seem to know at what point fatigue kicks in when you’re playing, which is a good thing for anybody to know.

The obvious suggestion is - play within your limits: don’t commit yourself to spots / tune sets longer than you can properly do. It’s better that you should play well over a short time in public than not well for a longer time, even if the latter may seem more ‘productive’. Take breaks as long as you need, and so on. If a gig seems likely to stretch you beyond what you can decently do, pass it up. No point in taking on ordeals.

Good luck with it!

Re: Newbie issues…

I don’t know for sure without seeing and hearing you play, but I would guess that it’s your brain rather than your muscles that need a rest after a long session. I don’t mean that unkindly -- there’s a heck of a lot of work going on in there when you are playing, and if you haven’t yet developed the facilities to handle it all, your brain will start to run out of options, and you lose concentration. Rather like reading a complex book, or doing fine work like embroidery or etching, your brain says, ‘enough’.
Also there is the added dimension that you are learning (very quickly it seems!) all the time, so the third time through a tune your brain might suddenly make a neural connection, and now you don’t have to think of that bit any more -- rather like driving a car, or learning to walk. The problem is that once the connection is made, you are now free to see or hear something else that you hadn’t noticed before, and new connections start to get laid down, and it goes super-mammary.
The good news is that you are unlikely to become a sloppy player if you are aware of the problem. Just keep going the way you are, and let your brain sort itself out. A good night’s sleep often helps -- that’s when your brain’s tidying up the mess you’ve just made in there.

That’s one reason to continually learn new tunes, from new players, with different styles. I used to play the banshee years back but when I learned Maire’s (which I originally thought sounded like an alternate setting of the former) It started taking the place of banshee in my set building.

As others have pointed out, everything takes time.
This applies both to your musical ability and the skills of public performance.

Some people will play for many years before embarking on a public performance, if ever, and not feel confident enough to do so until they feel that they are really proficient. Many great musicians never perform live gigs or do so very infrequently.

On the other hand, others adopt a “brass neck” approach and can be lucky enough to procure gigs very quickly. Of course, serious music lovers might very well “walk out” of a pub or restaurant when they hear a “newbie” who isn’t quite ready but others might offer encouragement. It is still good experience, nonetheless.

I certainly don’t think there’s a “one fits all” approach to this. It depends on the individual and his/her circumstances. The younger and inexperienced you are, the more likely you are to enter situations where older and wiser people won’t but that’s not necessarily a bad thing.

“Nothing ventured, nothing gained” as the saying goes.

Jerone, there’s one thing in particular I think you should be starting to do to get some variation in your playing. At the moment, you play it the same way every time.

Choose a spot in a tune where there is two notes that are the same note next to each other where you differentiate between theses two notes by changing direction with the bow. Now, instead of changing direction, do a little flick of the string with your ring finger right on the beat. You just touch the string as lightly as you can for as short a time as possible to interrupt the vibration while keeping the bow going in the same direction. Slow the tune right down, it’ll be easier.

This is gonna do two things that will be very important. Firstly, it’s the introduction to finger articulations. They get more involved than this, but this is the start of it. Secondly, you will be immediately out of your comfort zone with the bowing. you’ll be back to front from what you are used to and it will at first feel like the first time you ever picked a fiddle up. But go with it. It’s vital at this early stage to learn to play either way with the bow because the longer you leave it, the harder it will be to fix later.

Good morning, Jerone. :) First of all, you’re playing incredibly well for 1.5 yrs in! I wish I were a teacher and knew exactly the right advice to give you to help you along. I had an Irish teacher when I started and one thing he had me doing quite early on was pedal bowing---the string-crossing technique where the bow stays on both strings, like the E2 BE dEBE beginning of Drowsy Maggie---it’s a very common feature of Irish fiddling, and characteristic of the style. Will Harmon has explained it well here several times (see his post here as one example: https://thesession.org/discussions/18410#comment384235 ) and you can see Kevin Burke doing a lot of it in the last tune here: http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fjyG3rW_yLA (gratuitous Kevin Burke video there, I love that clip).

The best thing for you is exposure to good fiddlers, to see how they do it, and maybe even for them to give you advice. Keep it up, you’ve made a really good start. And I like the piano playing!

Re: Newbie issues…

Fiddlelearner, I haven’t read this whole thread…only your initial post. It’s good that you’re thinking about these things. There are plenty of people who don’t bother, and it’s a drag for the rest of us. It sounds like you have a few tunes, and you’ve been out to perform them without being entirely at ease. You might think about slowing things down a little.

Get out to as many sessions as you can, hone in on the good players, and listen, listen, listen. You may not know many tunes yet, so when you’re sitting out, listen closely to these players and how they shape the tune and express it rhythmically. This music is all about rhythm; everything else is secondary (though still important!). This is a fact that eludes a surprising number of people. Also listen for the varying shades of lift and swing; these are the things that give a tune life and make it sound like something much more interesting than a steady stream of notes. If you haven’t already, you should find a good Irish fiddle teacher who can help you work out bowings, rolls, new tunes, etc. Listen to recordings of the old players; try to find as much solo fiddle music as you can.

It sounds like you practice a good bit, which is great. Think about how you practice though. Play slowly.. There’s no use in trying to play a tune fast if you can’t execute it properly slowly. Use a metronome if you’re having difficulty keeping a steady rhythm. Work on your rolls, triplets, and other techniques you’re developing independent of the tune so as to not play a tune into the ground just to get a bit of technique sorted out!

Keep learning new tunes…preferably from the person sitting across from you! Sheet music is no good for this music. It pigeonholes the music..attempts to set something in stone which can’t be. A tune is merely the framework for a continuous variation and change. Also, you’ll need to learn thousands of tunes, and several years down the road you’ll find the tunes you learned by ear are recalled much more readily than ones you learned from music, even if you haven’t played those tunes in years.

Sounds like you’re on the right track. Stick with it. It can be slow-going, and it certainly takes time to develop a repertoire and to become comfortable with your playing. Patience is the key! Taking your time is well worth it down the road. Good luck!

Id agree with a lot of Jzm post but Id strongly disagree that you have to learn thousands of tunes. You dont at all. There are 2 types of session player, those who can sit in any session and play most of the time [followers for want of a better expression ] and those who dont . They have their tunes, their sets they play them very well[or not]! and they dont need to have that many tunes. Enough for their gig, or their session.
For example I play a good few hundred tunes on fiddle/mandolin. But on whistle say, I only have at a guess 50 tunes. So If im whisteling I have to lead the set or Im stuck. same with other instruments. I have at a guess 50-100 tunes worked out on guitar but its pointless playing tunes on a guitar in sessions so I dont. I dont need to have a large set. I play solo or with a backer.

I hasten to add that there are extremely good players who dont have large sets, they dont need them, they lead with the tunes they like ….
Secondly use the dots if you like, its really not relevant how you learn a tune if your a ‘leader’ you play your tunes, as you choose, its that simple.
If you wish to sit in with high level sessions and follow, yep you need hundreds of tunes and you need to develop the ability to pick them up by ear.

As far as thousands… well there are players who over the course of their lives have learnt thousands [they say] but how many do they play regularly?…..
