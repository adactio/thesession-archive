Re: composing new songs, in a folk/traditional style.

Michael; I think that trying to write “in the traditional style” is a tricky thing. The environment, social, musical, work wise, has changed so much that one does not have the background that created the songs you are trying to emulate.
A few thoughts;
You/one can more easily write pastiche or parody than a traditional song.
I think of an actress in a made for tv movie set in the 19thc, who sang “Shenandoah” with the vocal mannerisms of a 2000ad pop singer……..YUK !
Also a folk club night at a festival a couple of years ago where every other song was “How great it was in the old days” ( not to mention TB, Tetanus, death in child labour, hanging, famine, poverty, the pillory, deportation, debtors prison, and press gangs, to name a few ).
Some people think Mumford and sons are folk singers.
Some people think that if you’ve written it, and are singing it to a badly-played acoustic guitar, then it must be a folk song.
Better you try and write songs that are true, and not bother about them being not yet traditional.
And as for folk clubs…….some of what I remember as the folk scene from the ‘60s’ onwards has gone, some is still continuing, new things are springing up. A new club has started once a month near me; it’s mainly run by young people, who sing good and unexpected versions of traditional songs, along with other newer ones. Needless to say various much older people have taken the opportunity to invade this new opportunity to take their music out in public. There’s also been various sessions, some in public venues, some private, where people can express themselves. Keep searching, and asking. The scene is not as dead as you think, by a long way.
As to “a market”; if you want to make money, there’s better ways in music. If you want to have fun, that’s different.

↳ Posted by Guernsey Pete on January 6th, 2014.

Re: composing new songs, in a folk/traditional style.

I think most traditional singers probably have a few self-penned songs in their repertoire. Probably not about farming and frollicking in the hay, because these days neither singers nor audiences live that sort of life.

As for folk clubs, they seem to be going through a bit of a revival at the moment, at least in this neck of the woods (S. Scotland/N. England). But the economics are the big bug-bear - the ratio of what artistes need to be paid to what people are willing to pay on the door has changed - gone are the days when you could book a main act and support and still cover costs if only 30 people turned up. Headline acts need big audiences to pay their way, which is why so many are only ever seen at festivals these days. But the clubs still seem to be doing OK by relying on regulars and floor singers, and only booking guests when finances allow.

Re: composing new songs, in a folk/traditional style.

I think you killed that horse in more than one of your earlier threads, Michael. Folk music, ballads, dance tunes, is a niche interest but within it’s niche it does well. It will never be “mainstream.” So what?

Looking at ballads tells me people have always had a fascination with the grisly, the grotesque, and the macabre, hence the modern popularity of crime dramas and the presence of far more ballads like “The Cruel Brother,” “Mary Hamilton,” “Barbara Allan,” “Andrew Lammie,” “Fair Helen of Kirkconnel,” etc. than ones about happy, pastoral rural life. Well, that and falling in love outside one’s social class, another common theme of these, which of course may or may not end in murder. It often seems to.

Looking through the School of Scottish Studies song archive on Tobhair an Dualchais, one could count the songs about death, betrayal, and murder, and the pastoral songs, and compare them. A quick and rough search for “ballad” in English and Scots reveals 1100 results. If you include the Gaelic ballads, it’s more like 1208 results. A quick glance through the results show that many of the Gaelic ones are stories from the Fenian and Ossianic cycles, so not pastorally themed or warm and fuzzy, either, as they’re often about battles against natural and supernatural enemies.

THE MONTH OF MAY (AKA The Haymaking)

`Twas in the pleasant month of May,
In the springtime of the year,
And down in yonder meadow
There runs a river clear.
See how the little fishes,
How they do sport and play;
Causes many a lad and many a lass
To go there a-making hay.

Then in comes that scythesman,
That meadow to now down,
With his old leathered bottle
And the ale that runs so brown.
There’s many a stout and a labouring man
Goes there his skill to try;
He works, he mows, he sweats, he blows,
And the grass cuts very dry.

Then in comes both Tom and Dick
With their pitchforks and their rakes,
And likewise black-eyed Susan
The hay all for to make.
There’s a sweet, sweet, sweet and a jug, jug, jug;
How the harmless birds do sing
From the morning to the evening
As were a-haymaking.

It was just at one evening
As the sun was a-going down,
We saw the jolly piper
Come a-strolling through the town.
There he pulled out his tapering pipes
And he made the valleys ring;
So we all put down our rakes and forks
And we left off haymaking.

We called for a dance
And we tripped it along;
We danced all round the haycocks
Till the rising of the sun.
When the sun did shine such a glorious light,
How the harmless birds did sing;
Each lad he took his lass in hand
And went back to his haymaking.

A great song, with a beautiful melody BTW. Recorded by the Copper family of Sussex, England (amongst others).

Hey Michael, yes I’ve composed several songs in a folk/traditional style. I started out while I was doing Scottish historical reenactment with Clan Tartan. I often based some of my songs in a somewhat Scottish brogue and used historical references to keep them within the time period we were portraying in the group, the 1630’s. One song I wrote is called “Dunluce” and the premise was taken from the happenings that lead to the McQuillan’s loss of their land and Castle during the Elizabethan period. I also wrote a somewhat traditional based song that is on happenings that took place during the many nights we played at the Dubliner Pub in St. Paul, Mn. which is on one of our CD’s. I was in a traditional Celtic group called “Triall Ro-Crua” at the time. I often wrote songs that had a traditional flavor but were about event’s in my live such as love lost etc. These will probably never become “hits”, but were quite fun to write and play for people and commemorate many memories in my life. So, if you are of a mind to try to write songs in a traditional style, I say go for it. It’s never a waste expressing your creativity.
