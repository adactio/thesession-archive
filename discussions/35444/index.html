Re: Political correctness and unsatisfactory tune names

Melmo, titles — famously, The Hag with the Money — with “hag” in the name need not require a pejorative reading. It is a translation of the Irish word Cailleach which can indeed mean hag but can also mean merely an old woman or even just a woman or a nun. Cailleach an Airgid is usually translated as The Hag with the Money. But, referring as it does to a real-life returned wealthy Yank by the name of Máire Ní Chathasaigh, I would prefer the Irishism — The Auld Wan with the Money — as a translation. Hence:

‘Sí do mhaimeo í, ’sí do mhaimeo í
‘Sí do mhaimeo í, ’sí cailleach an airgid
’Sí do mhaimeo í, ó Bhaile Iorrais Mhóir í
’S chuirfeadh sí cóistí ar bhóithre Chois Fharraige

She’s your granny, she’s your granny
She’s your granny, the auld wan with the money
She’s your granny from Iorras Mhór
And she could run coaches on the roads of Cois Fharraige.

On the other hand, if you really want a dodgy title, there is the slip jig An Phis Fhliuch, which I am not going to translate; as they say, you could look it up. A polite alternative title is The Bridegroom’s Delight.

Re: Political correctness and unsatisfactory tune names

I’m going to chip in a twa penn’orth here, it being Burns night and all…

Many of the songs and poems collected by Burns in the original Kilmarnock edition of his works were considered not just impolite but unrepeatable - unfit for polite society. If you have the inclination, check out the original words of ‘John Anderson my Jo’ - filth, critical filth, and written from a female point of view. There’s a huge tradition of ribald song, bawdy pub singing, and some of that continues to the present day. These songs and poems were edited, Bawdlerised by later collectors and romanticists of the Burns tradition, particularly (but not exclusively) by the Victorians. Take away some of the dangerous material, and I think you lose the true picture of the man.

I think there are several reasons to be cautious about editing out anything viewed by modern standards as “unsavoury”. Would we do that to Shakespeare? There are some deeply, deeply disturbing things in Shakespeare, and yet the integrity of the plays is almost fanatically preserved because however appalling some of the actions of his characters may be to our “modern” sensibilities, an effort must be made to contextualise the plays, and to understand the literature.

I know the song “The Bonny Banks of Roses” (there are hundreds of these songs, sometimes involving infanticide, sometimes involving a young courting couple and a knife/penis metaphor). I’ve sung it. As a child. I didn’t find it disturbing. I didn’t find fairy tales disturbing either, and I’ve read the original Grimm tales. If that song caused a “WTF” face you should try reading the original version of Cinderella. Give it a second listen; there are often clues in these songs to suggest they might not actually be about what they appear to be on first hearing. Or they’re viewed as ‘nonsense’ songs, there to hold the tune and nothing else. Change the content of the song, and you might destroy something, you might even be making things worse, not better.

As Noel Fahey points out above, there are also huge dangers in accepting translations at face value. Much context is lost in a bad translation, a good point to be aware of when talking about older tunes. “Bury the knife and stick it again” is a good example - it’s a really, really old pipe tune. How are we to say what the original title even was, much less what was intended by it, and to accept prima face an apocryphal story of “oh, it’s about stabbing witches” seems silly. Where’s the evidence, please? At least question any story you’re told about a tune name. It might just be hearsay…

Equally, avoiding tunes because of their title is just daft. I’m sorry, but it is. “Oh, I find that offensive”. It’s “unsatisfactory”. Well, fine, but that’s a personal prerogative - a matter of taste. Is it a good tune? That’s a matter of taste, too, and a more important one. Does the title make it memorable? You bet. I accept there is a kind of historical dissonance when we compare historical tradition against modern sensibilities, but that pretty much describes the way I feel about royalty too… So where do we draw the line? How do we decide (and WHO is to decide) what is or isn’t acceptable? Then what? Do we change all the tune names? The Valued Older Person at the Churn. The Persons of Castlebar. His/Her/Its Endearing Young Charms. (Please don’t) Physically Assault the LGBT Harp. The Bird “of colour”. Ridiculous.

(On the other hand, the euphemisms employed in renaming An Phis Fhliuch are comedy genius: The Choice Wife, The Groom’s Delight, The Wet Cat… but if all of these still offend, then you can call it O’Farrell’s Welcome to Limerick. I often wonder who he was meeting there…)

Personally, I find revisionist attempts to ‘clean up’ a tradition are in equal parts ridiculous, unwelcome and unhelpful. How, in all honesty, does changing the title of the tune affect anything other than a subjective discomfort with some words? It doesn’t. I quite like the way Stephen Fry puts it:

“It’s now very common to hear people say, ‘I’m rather offended by that.’ As if that gives them certain rights. It’s actually nothing more… than a whine. ‘I find that offensive.’ It has no meaning; it has no purpose; it has no reason to be respected as a phrase. ‘I am offended by that.’ Well, so f***ing what."

What on earth are we supposed to do? Cover our ears in outrage when somebody plays a tune with an “unacceptable” title? Run away? Put them in a book and never play them again? It’s a living tradition, the tunes don’t care what you think of them, and the people that wrote them, or named them, or both are in all probability long gone. They just want to be played. The music was once primarily there as an accompaniment to dance. Now, it is more commonly played as a social engagement with a mixture of friends and semi-strangers. If a tune has a daft name, mention it. Have a laugh. Humour is the correct way to dispel discomfort; not to attempt to hold a moral high-ground against a name that may or may not once have referred to something that runs counter to current moral standards (whatever the feck they are). Discussion and understanding are more poweful tools by far than censorship.
