Danny Boy

Um, dare I raise this subject? On this website? Anyway, I shall endeavour to be strictly academic about it.
My local newspaper, the Bristol Evening Post, has just run an article about “Danny Boy” and, in particular, the person who wrote the lyrics. The words were written in 1910 by a lawyer and lyricist Fred Weatherly, and published three years later. Weatherly was an Englishman born and bred, born in fact in Portishead, a small seaside town by the Bristol Channel, about 8 miles from Bristol. He never went to Ireland and seems to have spent most of his life in the Bristol and Bath area of the West Country. He was a prolific and popular song writer, with about 1500 published songs and at least one opera to his credit.
The origin of the tune of “Danny Boy”, the Londonderry Air, is obscure. It seems it was first heard being played by a blind Irish fiddler and was published in 1855, but disappeared from the scene for the remainder of the 19th century. The Australian composer Percy Grainger, who spent a lot of time collecting folk music, came across it in 1911 and composed an orchestral version.
The story is that Fred Weatherly’s sister-in-law heard the tune being played by Irish immigrants during the Colorado Gold Rush in 1912 and sent it back to England. Fred Weatherly liked the tune and soon realised that the words of his “Danny Boy” would fit, with only minor modifications. The rest, as they say, is history.
Trevor

Re: Danny Boy

There are a number of performing musicians who are deaf - Evelyn Glennie is the prime example, and I’ve been told there’s a viola player in one of the London pro symphony orchestras who is completely deaf. Apparently this lady lost her hearing during her ’teens through illness but was able to continue with her playing to a professional level. She “hears” by feeling the vibration of the instrument, and her training since childhood tells her where her fingers go. And, being in an orchestra she uses her eyes.
I had a viola section leader in my chamber orchestra many years ago who got quite deaf as he got older, and had to use a hearing aid, but it didn’t affect his playing at all. But he regularly switched the thing off when the conductor addressed him, and would just sit there smiling blandly and nodding in supposed agreement. The conductor never found out :)
Some years ago there was a blind international concert cellist (Eleftherios Papastavro, if I remember correctly - I think he’s now no longer with us). Blind organists are not uncommon - but they probably have someone to help them with the stops on the beast.
Beethoven became completely deaf and composed his greatest music without ever physically hearing a note of it. J S Bach became blind and dictated his music note by note to a scribe - one of his children, I think.
Blindness need not be a barrier in some sports and athletic activities. Cricket and rounders (the old English game from which baseball is derived) can be played blind with a special “sounding” ball and a sighted person to do the running. You see blind runners too in competition - they’re linked to a sighted runner. In my youth when I did judo I once came up against a blind japanese 4th dan who was visiting Bristol. He was led to the centre of the mat to meet me, and as soon as he caught hold of my jacket that was it - I got bounced around the mat for the next 5 minutes, as was everyone else this guy came up against. A consequence of this was that our coach occasionally got us to practice blindfold, to improve balance and the kinaesthetic “feel” for what the opponent was doing without actually seeing him.
To get back to music :)… it may be a good idea for a fiddle player to practice occasionally with a blindfold or in complete darkness so as force him/her to listen to the pitch and quality of the sound - you never know, the lights can go out in a pub session and the punters will be enormously impressed when the musicians continue playing as if nothing has happened. Vladimir Horowitz once continued a piano recital in the Carnegie Hall in complete darkness when there was a power outage.
Trevor
