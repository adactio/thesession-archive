Added by chris stolz on December 2nd, 2025

What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

Curious what other melody players “do” with their brains when they are listening to tunes (either recordings, or when a tune is well-played at a session where you can hear it properly) that they don’t know or maybe are learning. Do you predict what’s coming? Do you look for connections to other tunes? Do you “accompany” the tune by humming/whistling/lilting? Do you just listen? And do you listen differently when it’s a tune you know better?

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

When someone plays a tune I don’t know in the context of a session, I kind of go into a trance state where I close my eyes, focus, and try to deeply listen to the tune

First to determine if it’s one that I actually do know and might stand a chance of playing.

If I determine it’s not one I know, I think I’m concentrating on the overall contour of the tune, might even move my fingers up or down in sync with the overall pitch flow.

Occasionally, in this process, I’m fairly certain I’ve got it sorted and can play it without being a distraction by the second or third time around I’ll join in, but that’s not that common.

If the tune is one I don’t play, with the intense listening I’ll have a good idea if I like it or not.

If it’s a tune I’d like to learn I’ll ask its name after the set and work it up for future sessions.

I typically let my brain do what it’s going to do. Depending on the tune (its complexity, its similarity to other tunes I know, how interesting I find it, etc) and the player and my own set and setting, I might just revel in the sound, or I might get some ideas about how the tune works (I might start to “hear how it goes”, or even start to get an idea of what it feels like to play it - these are a bit different for me, although some people might find them synonymous), or I might just not get anything from it at all. (there’s a lot of tunes, particularly a lot of modern compositions, that just bore me, and I’m not going to try to force my brain to attend to a bowl of note salad if it doesn’t care)

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

When I’m just listening to a tune, I tend to lilt along with it (much to the chagrin of my wife, when she’s riding in my car!) I will quietly lilt along with tunes that I haven’t heard before as well as ones that I know. It’s like practicing the tune with a different instrument.

If I’m hearing a tune in a session that I don’t play, I am much like Michael. My eyes are generally closed. I will figure out the key, and double check that with my instrument. I will listen to the flow of the melody the first time (not necessarily what notes they are), and then I’ll start quietly picking notes or phrases that I think I have, and that helps give me context for the phrases that I don’t have yet. There are certainly times where I realize that I don’t have enough of it to even try (often with tunes that are switching modes or keys, or are in keys I am not that proficient in, like F or Bb, etc.), and so I’ll stop trying and just enjoy the tune. With those tunes, if they’ve piqued my interest, I will try to find some sources of the tune (often by asking the person who started it to send me a recording), and learn from there…

It’s somewhat common for me to play most or all of a new tune by the third or fourth time through. My sessions don’t generally stick with a hard 3 times through a tune, and if the person that started the tune thinks I am close to having it, they’ll often play it another time or two (and vice versa). The next time that tune appears in a session, I have to repeat that process, but it’s much easier, and after a few times of doing that, it will be more firmly embedded in my mind and move into the “known” column. (Or at least the “session known” column… The tunes that I learn that way usually take a while before I would consider starting the tune on my own…)

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

In a session: first I have to decide what the tune is and whether I can play it. And if I recognise it, can I play it on the instrument I am actually holding, rather the one on which I can play it fluently but did not bring

If I think I don’t know it then next I try to work out what key is it in
If I can figure that out I’m well on the way
I’m probably on the first repeat of the first part - at least - by this point
I might quietly hit a few root notes of the chords I think I am hearing just to check I’ve got it
I’ll let the tune complete at least once before attempting to play along

I need at least a rough mental picture of the structure - and I think I am probably identifying familiar elements in the tune - the kind of comfort zone from which to then try to stretch to the less familiar bits

If it’s not too unusual a tune, with luck I might be able to have a stab at it on the second and third repeats.
But some tunes can have little baffling bits in them

One problem is volume, I need to hear myself play. But I don’t want to be hitting wrong notes over audibly

The usual ‘three times through’ will not usually be enough for me to get to grips with a completely new tune (bluegrass is better as there can be multiple repeats of just one tune)

If I like the tune (it’s probably only about 20% of new tunes that grab me), I’ll probably ask what it was afterwards with the intention of tracking it down and learning it. Usually I forget to do this :)

Not in session, for example if I am listening to music at home or in the car: if I like something enough I will keep repeating it, listening harder for a while. For example last week I was playing a Joni Mitchell song on nearly constant repeat trying understand what she was doing with the guitar

If I can whistle/hum the tune then I know enough to have a go at playing it
I should add though that at home I will probably improvise variations way beyond what would work in a session

Most of the above is the ‘analytical’ side of my brain. Alongside this there is what could be described as the emotional /affective side - this is responding to the feel of the tune, what it evokes, what memories it sparks and all that stuff

Sometimes it’s nice just to listen and not try to play along

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

What goes on in my brain, which happens automatically, instinctually, and naturally, in other words is NOT “thinking” or “overthinking” or “analysing”, is the same process that happens if I’m consciously working at picking up the tune to play it.

It’s like my doctor telling me he can’t watch medical-related television programmes. “I can’t un-see what I see, I can’t un-know what I know.”

I’m simultaneously aware of the scale (but not necessarily the key because I don’t have Perfect Pitch) and melodic features such as scalar runs, arpeggios, etc. and especially if it’s a fluteplayer or uilleann piper the timbre, tuning, tempo, and techniques on display.

All this stuff and more is simultaneously present in the performance and I can’t un-hear it. Once again it’s NOT “thinking” or “analysing” but simply experiencing the performance as a musician.

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

I find I can pick up tunes fairly quickly in a session, although whether I can remember them afterwards is another matter.

Most tunes follow a similar structure, and a lot of phrases are repeated, although possibly with slight variations. There are probably only half a dozen bars you really have to focus on, and a lot of note patterns are shared with other tunes so they should fall under the fingers easily. You can usually have a fairly good guess where a tune is going to go.

I listen to the tune and start to join in quietly on those bits which I can pick up, The bits I can’t I skip over - I either hold a harmonising note or chord, simplify what I’m playing or simply don’t play - and hope to get it the next time. I try to keep it simple, without too much in the way of decoration, and try not to get overconfident and thinking “I’ve got this”. You have to keep listening. Depending on your instrument, you may be able to make a bigger contribution to the sound by focussing more on rhythm and/or harmony than the melody - leave that to those who know the tune.

Of course you have to watch out for the places where a tune may shoot off in unexpected directions. It’s more important to remember where in the tune these come you can steer around them, rather than trying to get them right and failing. Once the tune is over I’ll have a quick noodle to try to figure those phrases out, so I’ll have a better chance the next time the tune comes up.

It’s more important to NOT play wrong notes than to play all the right ones. By that I mean whatever you play should not tread on the tune or clash with the actual melody. It’s better to miss a bit out and pick up the tune later than to make a mess of it.

Of course it also depends on the culture of the session. If it’s one of those which expects you to know the tune thoroughly and play the approved version exactly then you’re probably best sitting it out. If it’s one which welcomes explorati0n of a tune then you have more latitude. I rather enjoy the challenge of playing a new tune on the fly.

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

Mmmm… If it’s a tune I don’t know, I think I try to hear what’s happening until I have a clear mental picture of the melody. If it’s not very coherent(too fast, muddy recording), I go to my instrument and compare different patterns to what I may be hearing until something lines up. As for active listening, I recite the tune in my mind’s ear as I’m listening to it to see how much of it I understand. I have a mental exercise where I’ll slow down the music in my head and listen for every distinct pitch/note to make sure i’m comprehending the music clearly, rather than in slurs and smears.

For tunes I know, I’ll play around with the melody, working out variations and mentally reviewing articulations and options.

”What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?”

If it’s a new recording, I immediately know if I already know the tune (and might think of where I’ve heard it/learned it), or if it sounds annoyingly familiar, it’s something I once knew and now should know. Completely new tunes fall into the category ”figuring out the key/mode/contour”. Then I might check the DB.

If it’s a session (or similar) where a tune I know is played, I don’t think at all, or rather think of something else than the tune itself. Possibly where I’ve heard it before, where I’ve learned it etc. If it’s an annoyingly familiar tune, I might fumble through it until I realize that it’s better to sit out. If it’s unknown, see above in re key/mode/contour. I never (ever) record stuff, but instead ask its name.

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

I’m similar to Reverend.

If I’m interested in the tune I need to identify the key and mode.
Then I work out the structure: the phrases and where they repeat in the tune, how they resolve.
As I’m doing this, I’m comparing the tune to other tunes I know.

If I get through a first hearing and I like it I will investigate further - listen to the tune, on youtube or spotify, look for the notes here.

I’ll go back to the next session and hope I can play along with it, in some way.

This process usually works out but I have been caught out with different versions of the same tune, in the wrong session!! Or not remembering where I learnt a tune - until it starts.

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

If I like a tune - i engage with it emotionally - let myself feel it in its intensity.

I think that engaging with a tune emotionally - before figuring out technical details is better for the memory. Listening to it over and over - really enjoying each little detail - thinking about the effect it had etc… This is what good listening - for listening’s sake - is: appreciation.

Emotiona is a key cue to store something in long term memory -

I would advise against rushing to technically engage with a tune - unless you desperately need to learn it fast.

When you learn it - do it well.

Better to have 15 tunes you can nail - than 150 you can sloppily (or robotically) play.

Re: What do you actually do when you listen to a tune?

Late to the party, again.

If it’s in a session situation, as most respondents have alluded, it really all depends on how the tune “hits” me at the time. It might just wash over me, in which case I’ll sit it out or go to the bar. Sometimes, it may interest me and I’ll sit and listen. Perhaps, I’ll even have a go at playing it second or third time around.
Generally, all tunes are at different stages of familiarity. Some are ready to play and others not.

“Shallow interaction” ? Not sure if I like that expression. We used to call it “Small talk”.

The majority of people I know are NOT “close friends” BUT are still important to me. I ’ve known many of them for years and some most of my life. It’s not possible to bring everyone into your inner circle or enter everybody else’s and it’s not really advisable either.
So, it’s nice just to have lots of people you can have a chat, laugh, joke with (sometimes talk about about more serious things though) , and occasional beer etc.

As for chatting during tunes, it all depends on the circumstances. In between tunes is probably better, I’d suggest.
