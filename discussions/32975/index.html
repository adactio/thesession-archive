Bent wrist or straight wrist

For years we have discussed trad teachers who insist on a straight left wrist for right-handed fiddle players. I have said that they concentrate on this useless technique because they didn’t know what else to talk about. This is a good example of why it doesn’t matter. Johnny was capable of playing with straight or bent wrist depending on where his fingers needed to be. In the clip, in 3rd or 4th position his wrist automatically straightens out. But in first position it doesn’t matter so much.

Re: Bent wrist or straight wrist

Some fiddlers play with pancaked wrists, some hold the bow almost 1/3 way up the stick, and many still get a good sound, and (as far as we the public know), they have no problems. However, it provokes debate.

If you play with a pancaked wrist, you limit yourself in the reach of your fingers. Try playing a high C in 1st position on the E-string without moving your wrist.

If you need to change position quickly, it is far easier to do so with a straight wrist. In the video of Johnny Cunningham, to change to 3rd position, he had to straighten his wrist to prevent the heel of his hand hitting the heel of the fiddle (extra effort and movement required). When he reverted to 1st position, he could have saved energy by maintaining a straight wrist, but he pancaked it again (another bit of excess and unnecessary movement).

Good classical teaching is unsurpassed for promoting comfort, ergonomics, mechanical simplicity in all manoeuvres of left and right hand. That has yet to be proved wrong. There’s no reason why it can’t be adpopted for trad. Even just for the basics, which is what this whole discussion is about.

Inverted snobbery was mentioned earlier, and maybe there’s an element of that in this discussion. There has been on the past, on the subject of fiddle players who were held in high esteem, having excellent timing and rhythm, despite having atrocious tone and intonation.

I have taught two (previously self-taught) fiddle players, and both of them made a fairly good sound. One was having trouble getting into 3rd position, simply because of the pancaked wrist. It was so difficult for her to get out of the habit, that I gave up on that aspect, and concentrated on improving on other issues.

Re: Bent wrist or straight wrist

“Unnatural” indeed, Jerone.

There is a lot of talk about the advantages of the straight wrist, but no-one has mentioned the advantages of the bent wrist. I was just sitting here wondering what they might be -- I play with the ‘classical’ position, so I am not aware of any -- and I found one straight away. Try this: hold your left hand in the air at eye level in the place it would be if you were holding a fiddle, and with a classical straight or slightly arched wrist, try to line up your finger-ends with your eye. It is very difficult. Now switch to the trad-style bent wrist, and your finger-tips practically fall into a straight line, perfect for playing the first four notes on a fiddle string. Given extra stability of position from the heel of the hand, it seems to me that the bent wrist is a perfectly sensible approach, and all the knocking is a symptom of ‘my-teacher-said-it-so-it-is-true’ syndrome.

↳ Posted by Dr. Tøm on December 16th, 2013.

Re: Bent wrist or straight wrist

This is silly.

People have been thinking about violin technique for centuries, and most of it’s figured out. One of the things they’ve figured out is this: People who play for years with a flat wrist are more prone to tendon inflammation problems than people who play with a straighter wrist.

Another thing they’ve figured out is this: People with a straighter wrist have more freedom of movement around the neck, ability to switch positions, and have access to a vastly greater repertoire than people who rest the neck of the fiddle on their palms.

This is elementary to anyone who spends five seconds thinking about the mechanics of it.

And when you look at the players who have made careers of playing extremely demanding repertoire, taking hours and hours of practice, to the concert hall standard, and remaining relatively injury free into their seventies and 80s, the straight wrist wins, hands down.

To insist on poor technique that will vastly increase your chances of injury when you have the chance to know better is silly. And the fact that there are great players who play with a flat wrist has nothing whatsoever to do with it. We don’t know what the future holds for Martin Hayes and Liz Carroll, other than if they continue in the vein they do, with the repertoire that has been serving them so well so far, they won’t be spending much time in 4th position or higher, as performers.

A good friend of mine who is a world-class Irish player, who plays with a bent wrist (toured the world for years doing it) now doesn’t pick up the fiddle between gigs because of tendonitis. I used to have to substitute regularly for local gigs because of tendonitis, and this was someone in their 30s. This is anecdotal evidence, of course, but if you’re citing a few top fiddlers, you’re ignoring the effects of selection bias: There are many talented flat-wristed players you may never have heard of, because tendon problems forced them to give up professional performance.

And as great as Hayes and Carroll are, and as great as Dezi is, to cite them while not dealing with the very real mechanical problems of flat-wristed playing is falling into a counterfactual problem: They may well be even better players with more conventional mechanics. It may well still come to pass that they run into longevity problems.

With Mr. Cunningham, he didn’t live long enough to see.

I’d take it a step further: What is it that makes these players so great? I think it’s something besides the fact they play with a flat wrist.

That said, there’s also a tendency to a reverse snobbery fallacy here: The notion that good, sound technical advice that might is invalid because a few great players demonstrate themselves and exception to the rule makes no sense.

Re: Bent wrist or straight wrist

Good technique isn’t dependent on the genre of music played.

Whether it is important or not probably depends on how far you want to go. If you set out with the goal of ‘I want to become a mediocre fiddler’, then learning good technique from the outset probably isn’t important.

But if your goal is ‘I want to become a great fiddler/violinist’, then good technique is vital from the outset. Teachers teach things like wrist position at the outset because those things have been identified as causing problems further along the line - at some point it will stop or impede your progress if you don’t have the right technique. Of course, it may be that point is beyond the limit of what you want to achieve, even beyond the limit of some professional fiddlers. But why limit yourself? Get it right from the outset and the sky is the limit.

Far better to spend a few hours sorting out how you hold things before you worry about phrasing and rhythm than to find yourself ten years down the line unable to play trebles because your bow grip is shit.

I have come late to this thread, so people may not check in to read this, but because some will benefit I have decided to chime in anyway.

I have played fiddle and violin for 50 yrs and for the past 20+ yrs have seriously paid attention to the ‘issues’ discussed in this thread. Here are my considered opinions, which I don’t expect people w/o my experiences or physique to understand or agree with. However, those who spend considerable (considered) time experiencing and understanding these approaches may come to similar conclusions. At least, their efforts will put them further along their path to their best musicmaking, as my efforts have for me.

I am a native plyr of violin and fiddle, meaning I began plyg both at the same time; I did not start with one and then learn the other. I have plyd classical music in orchestras, chamber music, and solo, and many other types of musics in bands (as leader or sideman) for dancing and listening. It’s all music. My ‘violin’ plyg opinions and suggestions apply to all musics, without exception.

IMO the basic problem with violin/fiddle playing is that people teach/learn and play by sight, not by feel. This error causes the problems, leading to inflexible plyg and inflexible opinions. The error is trying to mimic what others look like, not how they feel. Of course we don’t know how others feel, so feeling is rarely a consideration, and therefore looks is the default learning option (‘fault’ is the operative word here). Mostly I find that plyrs try to mimic classical violinists, because ‘they must be doing it right’ or ‘someone told me this is the only way’ or somesuch nonreason.

For me, plyg an instr is best when I figure it out (experience, reason, science), rather than doing because someone said so (dogma, belief, religion).

I began with a traditional classical vln hold, taught by sight (L wrist straight etc, R arm this and that). These ‘positions’ caused tensions everywhere in my body, wch of course were heard in my musicmaking. I now play as released as possible, wch is an evolving condition, and is very different than what was demanded of me when I was taught by sight. 20 or more yrs ago I removed the chinrest, and have not touched my chin to the vln since. I let the instrument rest on my wrist, wch frees up the L arm and hand. According to my body, this is not injurious or inefficient, and is not uncomfortable feeling as I play, and it sounds good, so who cares how it looks. The sound and the feeling of the music is the important consideration. Looks while plyg is the same as personal looks or clothing: The musicians looked real good on the recording :-)

I find that my wrist touching the violin has many advantages. This slight touch entirely stabilizes the instr without my chin. (I use a Resonans shldr rest, wch also helps stabilize the instr.) Without any pinching of my thumb and index, the hand is released. The wrist is already ‘in 3rd position’, so there is no problem plyg all notes in most music, especially fiddling. When I learned to smoothly slide my wrist/hand along the vln body, I found that accurately plyg to the top of the strings is effortlessly possible. When you intend to hear (play) a sound with a certain finger, the body naturally makes it happen, with no thought of or technical practice of wrist or thumb.
(The only disadvantage I have found to touching the wrist to the vln is that this touch creates 2 points of contact with the instr, instead of just the 1 point of ‘hand under, fingers on top’. 1 point of contact is most flexible; a 2nd point makes y slightly less flexible. But this is of no consequence unless y play up the strings alot, and can be managed if y do. That is another topic.)

IMO the wrist shape is not the problem with tendonitis. Problems come from misuse, not overuse. A poster wrote above that bending the wrist is misuse; but this is not necessarily true. Misuse involves inappropriate tensions. I play gigs for hours with no adverse effects because my hands and arms (etc) are released. I use the least muscle effort possible, so I do not touch the strings to the fingerboard. But even when I get excited and do press strings to the fingerboard, or do so to sustain bass notes in chords or play pizz, I do not have tensions, pains, or troubles. Playing is healthy and fun.

IMO the wrist (bent or straight) is not the problem with playing notes (such as c‘) higher than 1st position. A wrist touching the instr is already ’in a higher position’, so notes up to the octave (halfway up each str) are available without moving the wrist (shifting). A straight wrist needs only move slightly to play the same sounds, and the instr body is there as a helpful gauge.

The problem with plyg ‘high’ c and similar notes is the L thumb. The L thumb is often seen opposite the index, pinching the neck to one or another degree. Play by feel, not by looks. So don’t mimic this thumb-index pinch. When the thumb is not pinching the neck, it is most useful across from the 2nd or 3rd finger, which allows the hand to be released enough so the 4th finger easily plays released. No reaching or stretching ever. This works especially well for small hands. I am a medium-size person with medium-size hands.

In the videos above in this thread, note that Liz C has her thumb across from her 2nd or 3rd fingers as I describe, and that all 3 guitar plyrs also do.
The plyrs whose thumbs are near their index appear to be reaching their 4th finger to play its notes (or don’t play 4th at all). One plyr changes from bent to straight wrist just to play his 4th F.

Trim The Velvet bars 17&18: ~g3a bgaf|g2ab c‘baf| Move the 1st finger from (bar 17) f# to (bar 18) g and bar 18 is no problem. The 1st finger easily opens back for the f# and the hand is ’in 1st position’ again without shifting.

IMO where the RH is on the stick does not necessarily cause or cure bow bouncing. Regardless where the hand is along the stick, bouncing is caused by too long (too energetic) a stroke for the energy of the music. Use an appropriately smaller amount of bow and bouncing will never occur.

I hope this helps.

Re: Bent wrist or straight wrist

Good point about tension in many players. Some fight it constantly, others don’t have it at all. A lot is down to technique and good posture - there’s no doubt about that.

As for clamping the violin between chin and shoulder (collarbone, surely?), it’s no surpise that people have problems. If you use a properly fitting shoulder rest, there should be no problem. If it precisely fills the gap between collarbone and chin, your posture with / without fiddle will be almost identical, plus the fact that your left hand is free from having to support the weight of the instrument.

Some have the ‘Bob Hoskins’ physique, so there’s no need (or room) from a shoulder rest. Others are able to support the instrument with the left hand only, and only ‘clamp down’ a litle bit when shifting position.

There’s really nothing difficult in getting good form and posture. Much of the good teaching in this respect is based on keeping everything near the centre of range of motion. So - head fairly upright (same as it would be without the instrument) - wrist upright too - the natural position for it, right in the centre of range of motion (as opposed to pancaked, where it’s nearer one end of the natural range of motion).

There’s a fair amount bad teaching and misinformation going around - there’s no doubt about that.

Let’s have some more myth-busting too :)
