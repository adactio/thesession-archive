My personal manifesto: it’s not how many, and it’s not how fast.

At one time I thought I had a reasonably sized repertoire of maybe pushing four-hundred tunes. Ninety-five of them were reels, which also seemed reasonable, being significantly more than the number of jigs, which was the next most popular type. The majority of this repertoire, though far from all, I had learnt during my years in Ireland.

Then I went to another country and was surprised and, yes, disappointed, to find that not only were more reels played than any other tune type, which is probably quite typical, but more reels (by far) were played than everything else PUT TOGETHER! At any one session you might find 80% reels and 10% jigs, while the airs, barn dances, highlands, hornpipes, marches, mazurkas, planxtys, slides, waltzes and even polkas had to try for a look-in in the remaining 10% of the time. So whereas I had expected to be able to join in with at least a reasonable proportion of the music (One quarter? One third? Even just one fifth?) it could quite easily turn out that I only had two or three of the tunes in the common repertoire over the whole evening. To make matters worse, most tunes were played hell-for-leather, at a pace that my medium-at-best talents could not match.

I drew two good lessons from this.

The first lesson is the futility of lists of “basic tunes that everyone should know”. Attempts to produce such lists have shown remarkably little overlap. I view that as a good thing: prescriptive standardisation is a very cold, wet blanket. A list of, let’s say, “50 tunes that are commonly played at THIS ONE PARTICULAR established session” is a great aid to a newcomer. Someone who is kind enough to take the trouble to write such a list deserves our thanks. But on the other side of the railway track, or on the other side of the ocean, the 50 tunes most commonly played at THAT session may be almost entirely different.

The second lesson is something I was told by someone much more experienced when I was a relative beginner, but it took a long time to sink in: it’s not how many tunes you know – it’s how you play them. One of my biggest problems was that in spite of having what I still reckon was a reasonably sized repertoire, I simply couldn’t play them well enough, with the strength, reliability and musicality needed to lead off. If my repertoire had extended to only 20 tunes, but if I had those tunes really under my command, then a couple of times in an evening I could have said, “OK, I’m going to play this one, that one and the other one – are you all ready?” In all probability people would have joined in and enjoyed it. A week later I could have gone back and said “OK, I’m going to play pip-pip, pop-pop and ping-pong – are you all ready?”, and before too long somebody might have said, “Hey, why don’t you play that one and the other one that you played a week ago?”

Well, that’s not how it was. I was neither that good a player, nor that wise a person. I chased the idea that if only I knew enough tunes, they would “come round” often enough that I could join in.

Over the last couple of years I have reassessed. I’ve scratched a lot of the tunes that I sort-of knew, in the sense that I could sort-of join in if somebody else was leading the set. I’ve added a few that 1) I like and that 2) I know that at least a few other people like, although the first criterion has been the most important one. I only now count my active repertoire, and by that I mean tunes that I know well enough so that if someone says, “What about The Tingle-Tangle Jig?” I can respond with, “Yes, it goes like this…” This list has only recently passed a hundred, and I consider that PLENTY to be getting on with. Of course it can grow, and probably will, but merely bumping up the number is not a priority. Of that hundred, only six (in letters, S-I-X) are reels. They sound good at 110 bpm, perhaps even 100 bpm or less, and I flatly refuse to attempt anything above 120. This allows them, I think, to display their character. They can be danced to, and they don’t all sound the same.

Comments not really needed - it’s all a question of taste, and your mileage may vary, of course. I just wanted to get it out there.

There is nothing whatsoever unexpected in the proportions of tunes you described. And your trimming of the list to perfect a much smaller repertoire is also a sign of your maturing as a musician and likely not a bad idea at all.

I think most of us have been in the need-to-know-hundreds of tunes zone. And learning/half-learning tunes that you can play along with across a broad repertoire, even if they are not played especially well, is still helping you learn the phrasing, tempo, progression, sequences, modes etc. You couldn’t learn to play 100 tunes really well without first learning 500 badly perhaps.

I could tick off and agree with most of the points you make. And I think it is probably and awfully common path from beginning to wherever we now are on our journey. Enjoy.

Re: My personal manifesto: it’s not how many, and it’s not how fast.

I don’t mean to say that this was *your* mindset, OP, but I think a common and problematic mindset in trad learners is that “learning” a tune means getting the right notes in the right order. The truth is, that’s only the first step. The notes are a skeleton; you have to put flesh on it in order to make it music. That flesh is phrasing, ornaments, variations, the overall rhythmic feel, etc. Playing G3-G-A-B-A3-A-B-D all in a row does not mean you’ve learned the Kesh any more than saying the words “to be or not to be” makes you a Shakespearean actor.

It’s often very easy to pick out the people with this mindset, because everything is a string of notes, one after the other. It’s fine if you’re playing with others who are carrying the session, but the minute you try starting a tune or striking out on your own everything falls apart.

All that being said, dashing through tunes while learning and playing them a few times through before moving on *can* be very helpful in getting your fingers working on autopilot. Playing the same tunes over and over again will polish *those* tunes, but they won’t get your muscle memory working for anything but those tunes. A good flute player isn’t thinking “A, that’s two fingers down, OK next is an E, so that five fingers down” at every turn, they’ve got everything automatic. The only way you’re going to get that level of familiarity is by playing a lot of unfamiliar music, to the point where you immediately put two fingers down when you hear the corresponding note in your head.

The key, of course, is not to think of those tunes as tunes you’ve “learned,” just ones you’ve played or woodshedded a bit. Then, go back to the ones you actually want to polish up and learn them properly.

Re: My personal manifesto: it’s not how many, and it’s not how fast.

First, reels are almost always dominate in sessions. Take a hint from Irish albums. Jigs follow. The rest depends on the session, but, except for one I used to frequent that featured an abundance of hornpipes, reels and jigs are the name of the game.

Secondly, if you want to play more, start more sets. Create them and write them down. The most common practice is to record the sets that are played and learn them. When I moved to Idaho, I played a fair amount of the existing repertoire and started 2-3 sets per session. I now play everything I like and sit out the ones I don’t.

About speed, I can’t help you. If the rule is to play fast, it’s not the session for you. Or me.

I’ve encountered people like that, who produce precisely timed, well executed, in-tune, and invariable strings of notes. Thing is, some of them know all the tunes the session plays, and have fun playing all night.

People with an unsuited repertoire (smaller and/or different) sit there for hours with their instrument cold in their lap, no matter how finely crafted that repertoire is.

My first mentor/teacher would teach me a tune, then show me how to re-work the tune over and over with interesting new twists. He wasn’t just teaching a tune; he was teaching the structure of the music, and the art of variation.

The dichotomy of these quite different goals reminds me of two quite different RSCDS teachers we had here in the early 1980s.

One was all about technique and he would have us practice one dance for weeks until all the footwork was exactly so. Thing is, his students would show up at the monthly dances and only be able to dance a couple of the dances on the evening’s programme.

The other teacher wanted his students to be able to show up at a dance and dance all night. To him the priority was to be in the right spot at the right time for your partner. About the intricacies of footwork he cared not at all.

So of course I attended both of their classes!

Hi bex! You didn’t ask for additional comments, but for comparison and contrast I have a slightly different world view.

I have a few hundred tunes that are the tip of my repertoire iceberg, ones I have under my fingers and that I have no problem starting in a session. This iceberg is gradually but constantly melting, reshaping, and rolling over. Underneath is a larger body of tunes that I’m less well acquainted with, either because I never really played the hell out of it so that it got wired into my central nervous system, or because I just haven’t played it in a while (because it got pushed out by other tunes). Many of these underwater tunes are ones I can play along with if someone else leads, but many are ones that I might just have to sit out and listen, thinking “ah that is a lovely tune I need to dust it off at the earliest opportunity.”

I’m always listening to music and players, and constantly being inspired by new tunes. Learning tunes and getting them up to session standard is pretty hard for me. I’ve never been one of those people who can just pick up a tune from one listen. Sure, I can approximate the contours (fake it) with cliche runs, and in a big session maybe no one notices. But I like the effort, the process of learning new tunes, it stretches me, keeps me fresh, and makes me think about the music in new ways that I can apply to other tunes. I’m constantly learning because I have so much to learn.

I don’t think many will argue with the idea that a lower number of tunes played well is better than many tunes played less well. I wish I was one of those people with the talent and the time to amass a huge repertoire and able to execute it at a very high level. Well, we have to work with what we have.

I couldn’t put numbers on them, but there are tunes I know well enough to lead and to play unsupported even if no one joins in, and which I am happy to play to an audience in a concert situation. There are others I know well enough to start, but it’s a relief when others join in, as it’s then not all on me. There’s an even larger number of tunes I can play well enough once someone starts them, but wouldn’t be confident to lead. Finally there are tunes I barely know, or maybe have never heard, where I’m generally bluffing my way through. These aren’t fixed, and tunes will float between them depending on what new (or newly-remembered) ones have inspired me and what I’ve got bored with.

Speed is another matter. I think most of us fall into the trap when we are learning of thinking that fast and flash is the same as good, whereas for all but a few exceptional players it almost always results in a loss of musicality. It takes maturity and the wisdom of experience top realise that most tunes sound better if they’re not played at breakneck pace, and that it’s actually harder to play more slowly and bring out the musicality than to just play a blizzard of notes. Unfortunately many players don’t make that breakthrough.

Re: My personal manifesto: it’s not how many, and it’s not how fast.

One thing I’ll add that’s usually missing from these discussions is that the amount of effort needed to fully learn a tune goes down considerably with practice. A lot of people think that fully learning a polishing a tune takes time for each tune. Thus, learning 20 tunes well takes time to individually polish each tune, and then each additional tune after that takes time, etc. This gives us the false dichotomy usually presented of “play 100 tunes badly or 20 tunes well.”

The fact is, you can put brand new tune in front of an experienced player and they’ll be able to play it well in about half an hour, if not sooner. That’s because things like ornamentation, phrasing, and all the rest are baked into their playing, as is their instrumental technique. For them, learning a tune really *is* just about hitting the right notes in the right spots, because after that everything falls into place. And a lot of that comes from the practice of playing a lot of tunes, learning and re-learning.

I’d go so far as to say that it is not really possible to “play 20 tunes well,” at least not in the way it’s usually presented. If you have only ever played 20 tunes, you are not going to be able to play those 20 tunes well. No matter how much you work to polish them, you’re only ever going to be running through muscle memory of that tune. To go back to my acting analogy, it’s like an actor only ever practicing Hamlet’s soliloquy. They might do an OK job with it, but it won’t be nearly the same as someone who has years of acting experience in tons of roles under their belt who then takes it on, even if that second actor spends a lot less time “polishing” it.

With all that being said, being the absolute best musician you can possibly be is only one reason to play. I wouldn’t play if I wasn’t having fun, so there’s no need to run through tunes 6 hours a day just to “get good” if that’s not fun for you. Sometimes it best to just enjoy yourself, RSCDS organizers be damned!
