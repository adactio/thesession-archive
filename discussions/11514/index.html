Practice Styles - How and Why ?

In a recent thread (https://thesession.org/discussions/11493) the question of “looking for ways to ensure that time spent practising is optimized” was asked. The responses were, to say the least, diverse and fragmentary as if none of the respondents has an overall plan for practice nor do they have specific and general goals. Nothing was mentioned about development of technique and style or the sequence in which such should occur. References were made to such basic tools as visualization, listening to music, practice scheduling, and concentration but surprisingly none to basic technical skills, hiring a teacher or development of style. No reference was made to level of skill or talent

I have my own ideas on this subject, but was wondering how the others on this thread develop their style, how they practice technique, how often, on what schedules and especially why they do it this way. (Also, if you practice with blinking lights in your glasses or while listening to high pitched frequencies or tin foil hats, why <G>?)

I am specifically interested in how you analyze your practice sessions and how you work on those issues that your analysis points to.

Does anyone adhere to an overall plan or have specific goal in their practice schemata? And does anyone practice differently now than they did when they first started? Does anyone use the practice skills they learned in another type of music such as classical (I know you are out there?)

System? Moi?

I don’t have a system, really. I don’t do etudes; I learn on the tunes themselves. I was one of those who had classical music crammed down their throats as a kid, and when I took up trad, I decided that I just didn’t have to do that anymore.

I have more time these days to listen to music than I do to actually pull out the fiddle. I mostly listen driving to depositions (I’m a court reporter). I sing back the tunes I tape at sessions when I’m trying to learn them. It works great because I’m pleasantly surprised at how much I know when I finally get a chance to pick up the fiddle.

I play as often as I can, which sometimes is as little as three times a week. No routine, really. I just play one tune very slowly, listening to each note to make sure I’m in tune. I maintain a list of tunes I’ve learned recently, and I generally play a few of those to further program the cerebellum. The tunes I’m learning, I play those several times each, maybe five or as many as ten times if I have time. I may play with the tape or CD for awhile, but the idea is to wean myself away from it.

Hope this helps.

I have a general belief that what you practice is what you do, so I try to practice doing what I want to do. With the fiddle specifically I want to play mostly Irish fiddle in a traditional way, so I try to play tunes the way I think they should sound, based on what I’ve heard.

Quite often I record myself and listen to it and think about it, and recording it also seems to give it a bit more point, so that if one day I did actually play something nicely there would be a record of it. I’ll let you know if it ever happens.

I am mostly self-taught, I play a range of instruments and styles, and this is more or less the way I have always practiced.

I never practice scales or arpeggios, I can only play things in the keys I play them in.

Another general rule I have is not to time things unless I need to for some reason, so I don’t know how long I practice for. I play most evenings, a couple of hours some evenings, sometimes half an hour.

I am not going to achieve a “professional” standard this way, but I suspect I might never reach a professional standard no matter how I practice.

I think a more talented person than me could get to be a very good Irish fiddler just by practicing the way I do, and playing by ear.

I don’t think anybody could get to be a good classical player that way, could they?

Re: Practice Styles - How and Why ?

For some, developing good (any?) practice habits can be a particular challenge if (A) you play more than one instrument (B) you play both rhythm and melody instruments and (C) you sing.

Most of my practice happens during the weekend, i.e., Friday night through Sunday night, plus whatever time I can manage during the week. In general, I try to be somewhat systematic and disciplined about what and how I practice. For instance, I might think, “Hm, I’ve got a good solid hour or so here. I will focus on my bouzouki-melody work,” and go over tunes that I know or have been working on. Or, if I have somewhat longer stretch, I might dig out recordings of my fiddling friend/bandmate and focus on rhythm guitar.
But I also like/want to spend a lot of time on my singing and guitar accompaniment -- and I even make a point of focusing on practicing my thumb-picking, as opposed to strumming.

The odd one out in this arrangement, however, tends to be the bodhran. But to be honest, I find just practicing beats, rolls, etc., for very long on my own is of limited benefit to me. I really need (an)other instrument(s) around, and recordings just seem to throw me off; gotta have the live sound.

Sometimes, however, I eschew this compartmentalization for a “sampler approach”: I figure, OK, even playing short bursts of things is better than not playing anything at all, so maybe I’ll play about 15-20 minutes or so of tunes, another 15-20 of rhythm, and then do three songs.

This ‘system’ seems to work, since, over the years, I have played enough music, for the sheer love of it, to see gradual improvement in my playing. My long term ambition is to be a better player than I am now.

One aspect of practice that always intrigues me is the how the practice regimen changes from being a novice to becoming an expert on the instrument. I suspect that in the States, at least, that most of us have come from other types of music and were not novices at our chosen instrument. We did not have to learn basic technique (where the notes are, how to make the notes, right and left hand basic technique if appropriate, etc) but did have to adapt our existing styles to the new music. If the latter is true, practice consists of listening a lot , developing and keeping the stylistic signatures of the music and learning to interpret the music according to our understanding and background.

In practice we would also play a lot in sessions or in groups for fun which spurs us on to continue to learn.

At least that is what the group so far has said.

How many of you have an ITM teacher? How many had a teacher for basic technique many years ago and did most of that technique survive to be used in ITM?

How many practice to become and elite player and how many practice enough to maintain so you can continue to enjoy the music?

Some of this interest is spurred by my local session in which there are participants who obviously practice learning new tunes and developing an individual style and others who never pick up the instrument until they travel to the session. I suspect here is one poor soul there who is so lacking in talent that no amount of practice will help, but that person is still having fun.

The strivers are all trained in classical music or have been very successful in other genera of roots style music. The others seem to have no reason for playing other than the aforementioned pleasure of sessioning and are not motivated to improve. I suspect that since most of them are novices and older novices to boot, grinding out those technical exercises is just plain boring work so they decide to skip that part. As a result they have no base upon which to improve and, unlike some children, can’t just model themselves after some elite player because their brain doesn’t work that way.

Re: Practice Styles - How and Why ?

I’ve never had a problem with practicing, but since I started the fiddle about a year ago, within the first 6 months, I stopped thinking of it as practice and more as playing. The way I approach my playing is to play a scale, then just play tunes. When I want to improve my technique, I do two things. One, I start on a tune, and play slow, then increase my speed til I get it as fast as I can play it without it sounding out of control ( like my bow is running away, or I am going so fast I am not getting all the notes!), and too sloppy. But the most significant thing to improve technique is using the metronome. I will start very slow, and slowly work up to speed, playing the tune each time about 1-5 beats per minute faster. After playing a tune about twenty or so times, I notice an improvement in how it sounds. I have used this technique will the rolls, which I have had trouble getting nice and even, and it seems to work excellent. I have had comments that I play very well for 1 year, but I think this is due to playing alot, up to 3 or 4 hours a day, and persisting even when I get sick of playing or am tiring of trying to get better or learning new tunes.
As to schedule, I play in my spare time, whenever I can, unless people will be disturbed too early in the morning! I like playing so much I never feel a need for a schedule. Right now I am focused on just playing because I like to . I don’t want to decide whether to be a professional at this point or not, so I am just enjoying playing. Regarding a teacher, I take irish fiddle lessons 2 times a month, and do well. However, when I took a month of fiddle lessons from a classical teacher, I did not improve nearly as much. I seem to do better will a more relaxed less scheduled approach. I suspect that people will differ in the different ways they practice best in.

I love Sara’s sentiments above.

I have real trouble with the implication that practice “has to” have a schemata, a structure, etc… because that whole way of thinking seems to come from a very different world than the world of the “music session” which we all know and love.

Surely you “practice” by doing it. Don’t you? You pick up ideas, someone picks them up from you, you all get together and explore the art of making music together. Otherwise, what’s the point? I’m probably coming from the opposite end of the spectrum than mikeyes (who started this thread).

My thinking is that session playing is a way of a group of people, whether they are playing or listening or both, creating new insights as a group each time they play together - and the differences each time are what makes it special. I think of it as a conversation, or perhaps a group improvised performance - and I’d have trouble “rehearsing” for a conversation, and I certainly wouldn’t be thinking about become an “elite conversationalist”. That’s not to say that as part of our contribution, we couldn’t improve our grammar or our repertoire of stories, but surely it’s about playing with other people, isn’t it? We just have to be “good enough”.

One advantage to “practicing” this way is that any and every time you play is an opportunity to learn something new, develop a latent ability, or improve some aspect of playing. Simply playing the tunes can be just as intensive and methodical as practicing scales and arpeggios. I sometimes play one tune for 20 minutes straight, paying deep attention to tone or intonation timing or phrasing or crisp triplets or cleaner rolls, etc., or one after the other.

Mark,

I don’t really have a practice regimen much different from yours although it may be a little more focused. My interest in asking how others practice comes from looking at how competitive athletes and competitive musicians (such as the serious classical pros) order their lives. There is a disciplined way that they go about practicing but then they also have goals that are not shared by most of us. Nonetheless many of us share some of the traits. Serious musicians are perfectionists, have a great knowledge of the music, and a strong work ethic to achieve the idea that they have of the music. The fact that it is also a source of relaxation and fun for most of us is very important, but I think that we all want to play the music well.

One of the benefits of being able to play the music is that it is more fun to be good than not to be good at it. Going from a novice/beginner level to being comfortable with the music and the style takes a lot of work. For a lot of us, the technical work has been done even before we discovered ITM so the more pleasurable aspects of making music are to the fore. After reaching that stage, the exploration is a lot more enjoyable and in many repects easier even if we are never going to be at the level of a Kevin Burke.

One of the reasons for this is that KB probably has a lot more talent than you or I (certainly more than I) and he has devoted his life to the music making the kind of comittment that most can’t afford to make. The same is true of Olympic athletes, etc. The risks are high and the rewards only for a chosen few.

So most of us practice out of pleasure and the drive to learn more new (and neat) tunes. Our perfectionistic traits make sure that we learn those tunes well but we don’t have the drive to excell in the elite manner. Or if we do, we don’t have the time to do so (or in some cases, the talent.)

I do have short term and long term goals for my practice. I have been embarked on a project to learn as much as I can about the Irish Tenor Banjo by listening to every CD I can get my hands on and by learning from the best when I can. In two weeks I will be in TX at the O’Flaherty Retreat where a great line-up including John Carty will present. I take this information, process it and then put it on the Mel Bay web site with my interpretation. It has made a great difference in my approach to the banjo and the mandolin and I feel as if I have learned a lot which I try to apply in practice.

Since I have a life and a profession, I can only devote a small amount of time to ITM, but I try to make the best of that time. I am lucky to have a weekly session which serves as a training ground and a source of relaxation. It is a terrific journey.

I try to do at least a little bit of practice every day….start with finger exercises to a metronome slowly getting faster each time for about 10-15 minutes. I’m playing concertina and flute and I practice specific techniques in isolation of the tunes to train my muscle memory before I apply them to the music. I can’t imagine any other way of doing it. These techniques require very subtle minute actions that function almost subconsciously for the most part. I don’t think I could have leaned these techniques by just playing the tunes. Being able to execute these techniques easily in the context of the music is the reward for concentrating and practicing on them in isolation. The goal is to make playing the tunes easier and more fun to play.

Re: Practice Styles - How and Why ?

Very interesting thread--I just wanted to put my two cents in. Being fairly new to the fiddle (a little over a year) AND the genre, I have a lot to learn, so besides listening to recordings, and playing with other folk (a lot), I do spend a lot of time working on technique. But it’s not technique in a vacuum. Whatever tune I’m currently working on suggests the technique I need to work on--crisp beginnings of notes, particular emphases, particular bowing patterns. If those things are difficult, then I’ll usually pull them out of the tune and make an exercise of them. Practice the bowing pattern up and down scales. But it’s not tedium at all, and it’s getting results. I’m also learning a lot of tunes….
