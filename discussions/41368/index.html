Air travel with instruments

A little shout out to Delta Airlines for those looking to fly with instruments.

After reading a recent forum on flying with Ryan Air ( https://thesession.org/discussions/41357 ) I thought I would add this, in it’s own discussion, in that it may help somebody.

On a recent trip to the US I bought myself a bouzouki. It lives in a slightly longer than normal guitar hard case. Having never flown with an instrument I was reluctant to check it on and determined to try and carry it on. However I figured that with the size of it they would probably object so I prepared it well in case it definitely had to go under. i.e. slacken the strings and fill every space with socks and undies.

My first flight leg home NYC to LA I asked the stewardess, as I was boarding, if I could store the instrument in a coat locker. They apparently have some toward the front of the plane. I was told to try this approach by a guy I met at a session who travels regularly between US and Europe. She gladly took my case and at the end of the flight even made an announcement as to where it would be waiting for me at the front of the plane.

On the second leg, from LA to Sydney, I again asked the steward as I boarded and he told me to just lay it across the top of other cases in one of the larger, outer, overhead compartments and that there would be plenty of room. And there was.

No effort. All smiles. Thankfully and thank you Delta.

I know there are plenty of horror stories out there but does anyone have other good advice or tips to help fellow travellers?

Re: Air travel with instruments

My experience flying with a set of uilleann pipes in a viola case:

Aer Lingus: never gave them a second glance; always allowed into the cabin and in overhead compartment without question, regardless of the size of the airplane.

Delta, United, and American: have always been able to get them on as carry-on in the cabin, though sometimes with explaining to do.

Budget carriers, USAir, JetBlue, etc.: have managed to get them as carry-on, but sometimes not without paying extra fees, and getting conflicting information/instructions from the ticket counter versus the check-in desk at the gate.

My advice is to avoid the budget/heavily discounted airlines if at all possible.

Re: Air travel with instruments

A common misconception is that holds are depressurised. They have the same climate as the rest of the plane.

I have only a couple of pieces of advice. One, once you’re through the gate you have no (final) say on where it goes. You can’t even guarantee you can walk off the flight with it. You can argue, but if they decide it goes in the hold, it will be going in the hold. So dress it appropriately. And buying it a seat means nothing. If they are oversold, seats are for humans, not instruments, and it goes in the hold.

Two, your chances of getting it in the cabin are much improved by being at the front of the queue to get on and not making a fuss about it. Wearing it over your shoulders like a backpack often means it goes unnoticed.

Re: Air travel with instruments

I’ve traveled a lot with a banjo, and I’ve found the main key is confidence. Most of the people at the desk/gate/etc. don’t really know the exact company policy regarding instruments, which is almost always that they can be taken on even if they’re slightly larger than the allowed carry-on size (obviously cellos, harps, and other massive instruments are a different beast). They are usually trained to err on the side of “no,” so if you ask them about taking it on, or whether it’s too big, they will say you’ll have to check it or pay for an extra seat.

So read up on the policy beforehand, and then just confidently walk through with your instrument. If anyone asks, don’t be arrogant or unpleasant, but firmly state that you’re bringing it on. If you get agitated, they’ll dig in. If you’re pleasant, explain why you need to be able to bring it on, they’ll usually put it in the coat locker or find some extra space somewhere.

Note that this does not apply to those super-low cost airlines, like RyanAir, Spirit, Wow Air, etc. Their whole business model is in extracting fees for everything they can, so they can and will charge you an arm and a leg. But most other airlines, especially the trans-oceanic ones, have large enough planes and lax enough policies that you should never have to check anything guitar-sized or smaller.

Re: Air travel with instruments

I used to fly from and to Boston on Aer Lingus a few times a year with a regular size fiddle case, which was over the size limit. I learned a few things:

If I could get through the baggage check-in desk without having to give it up, I always made it onto the plane. Often the check-in person would say something like, “Well, OK, you can take it to the gate and let them decide” - and the gate would let me on.

Being early to the gate and quick in line is good advice, since the more hassled the airline folks are the more likely they’ll be in a bad mood.

You can’t guarantee success. One time I was stopped at check-in and told I’d absolutely have to check it into storage. I said “But this violin is worth $5000 and your insurance won’t cover it.” To which the check-in person said “Then don’t bring it.” Not much I could say to that. I let it go - it was actually worth maybe $400 - and it survived the trip with no problems.

Re: Air travel with instruments

I travel with a banjo fairly often. And nowadays, I always travel with it in a gig bag. Partially because you can wear it like a backpack - and I am generally never asked about it, I just carry it on. And the airlines know that they’ll break it if they make me check it. It helps if you are traveling with someone that has a soft carry-on, like a duffle bag, which can fit alongside the neck of the banjo (which helps keep people from trying to ram an oversized carry-on bag in without seeing that there’s an instrument there). A couple of times, I have been flying alone and if boarded early, watching for people that have bags that will fit along side, and getting them to use the space.

It’s always a nerve-wracking experience, though. I often pay for early boarding, and if you’re flying an airline that allows seat selection, you can try to strategically choose your seat where there might be fewer conflicts with other passengers (even if you have to pay for it). It’s much worse on the regional budget airlines, but usually the international flights have larger overhead compartments anyway. And a few times, I’ve had flight attendants offer to place it in the crew closet for me…

Re: Air travel with instruments

I have flown back and forth with my fiddle from NYC to Ireland 3 times, on Delta. My technique is to book a seat near the back of the plane, because then as you walk down the aisle (if you’re worried there might not be room by your seat) if you see a bin with nothing in it yet, pop the fiddle right in and continue to your seat. I make sure everything else I have with me fits under the seat in front, so as not to be more of an overhead hog than necessary. Also the back is sometimes the first group to board. If I’m in the last group to board, I go up to one of the gate agents who is managing the boarding process (after all the early groups have gotten on) and I say “I’m so worried about getting my fiddle on board with me” and their response without fail has been to just let me skip the line at that point. I also wear my fiddle on my back with backpack straps, never mentioning it to the check-in agent in the departures hall as I check my suitcase. They have never brought it up.

My trip to Australia with a guitar on Hawaiian Airlines was not so fun. They made me check it on 3 out of 4 legs. Once it came out ON THE BELT, I found it going round and round after making it through customs. Other times I found it sitting alone just waiting for me to find it. The time it came out on the belt I couldn’t make myself open the case for 3 days, I was sure I would find matchwood inside. Very nerve-wracking. I had packed it well and it was in a flight case, which may have helped it survive. I do wonder if I had just brought it in a gig bag whether they would have been so insistent that I check it.
