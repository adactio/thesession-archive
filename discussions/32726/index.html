Re: Busy Folks, how long do you practice?

I get in a half-hour or 45 min every morning before I leave for work. I focus on one task and work on it until I get it right. Then I get to play a tune--new or old. Then I go to work. I listen to tunes on the iPod while I ride to work--that’s another twenty minutes twice a day. I loop the tunes I’m trying to learn. Tedious, but effective.

You have to plan to schedule time for yourself, or everyone will have dibs on the hours when your brain cells are still functioning. Playing tunes is for fun, but practice is for you. Just like exercise and everything else you *should* do, it’s best if you can build it into your day.

Soldier on, Jerone. It’s worth it. You won’t get any less busy. It just gets crazier.

Re: Busy Folks, how long do you practice?

It comes down to priorities -- yours, not someone else’s.
The chances of your existing at all are literally incalculable, and the time between life and death on a cosmic scale is zero. Do what _you_ want. By this I don’t mean shirk your responsibilities, or go on a crime spree: sometimes you actually want to wash up or put the rubbish out, because it makes life more pleasant. It is possible to actually want to do unpleasant things, so don’t moan about doing them, just try to get the timing right.
There are two things that philosophers have said to me (one of the advantages of being a publican :) ) over the years that have stuck in my mind:
1. You can only do what you can do -- this from a seasoned barmaid because I was panicking during the Friday-night rush when customers were four-deep at the bar. The weight immediately fell from my shoulders onto those of my employer.
2. Will it matter in a hundred years? -- the catch-phrase of one of my regulars whenever he encountered a problem -- his or anyone else’s.

As for Mz Whitebread’s priorities, I subscribe to the Quentin Crisp school of housekeeping: After Two Years It Doesn’t Get Any Worse. When it comes to chores or music, I’ll take the music thank you. I have images of my mother frantically cleaning every day _in case_ we got visitors.
The best bit of advice here is to get rid of your television. The next best is to keep an instrument close to hand and pick it up whenever the fancy takes you. Better a minute in your hand than a week in its case under the bed.
Also, pens, spring clips, chair arms etc. all offer continuing opportunities to exercise and develop various parts of the body throughout the day. Just don’t do it in your pocket in mixed company.

Learning something is not an easily defined transaction. It’s not a case of I have done x hours of practice and therefore I am able to play x number of pages of O’Neills. If you are having to set yourself goals to spend so much time doing something that is ultimately meant to be an enjoyable form of self expression then the question is really “have I genuinely an aptitude and interest in this?” Or is this just an acquired behaviour that is an attempt to satisfy other needs indirectly?

When it comes to finding time for practice in a busy schedule though I admit it can be a nightmare. I am a highland piper and border piper they are loud instruments and I recently spent the best part of 2 years living in hotels because of work. I ended up playing in the car park on evenings and days off just to keep my hand in. Keeping your instrument close to hand at all times means you can just pick it up and fiddle away whenever the muse takes and is something I know that one extremely successful drummer does. He has practice pads and sticks in just about every corner of every room and he will just tap away wherever.

In our household the time preparing an evening meal has become a favoured practice period, with everything bubbling away on the stove time is free for a little play while keeping a weather eye on things. The kitchen also has the added advantage of being the best room in our gaff for the acoustics.

Re: Busy Folks, how long do you practice?

Don’t let the music become more work. Don’t feel like you HAVE to do it (unless your ultimate goal is to be a professional musician, I suppose). They call it *playing* music, not working it. Use music as a release of the stresses of the rest of your life. Then the 15 minutes here or there will feel less like a tease, and more like a vacation.

This is why you would pretty much never find me practicing scales and arpeggios (not to ignite an old mustard board argument…) It’s OK to use your limited time to work on something specific in your playing, like intonation, tone, bow pressure, attack, lift, etc. But if you’re concentrating on that one element while you’re actually playing music, you can still get lost in the tune, letting it wash away life’s worries. I would much rather play music for 15 minutes than not play because I’m worried that it will feel like a tease if i don’t play for longer…

Re: Busy Folks, how long do you practice?

I play 3 to 4 hours a day. I don’t call it practice because to me it is playing. I mean really “playing”. I play for my own pleasure and will probably never play in a session. I play in half hour blocks and record each in a book because, at the end of the day, it is really easy to delude yourself into thinking you have put in more time than you actually have. Discipline is largely habit and so while the recording may seem somewhat obsessive, it gets you back to the playing even when you think you are too stressed/tired/busy. In between the half hours I stay fit in my home gym and read. I’ve chosen to live where I have no neighbors to impinge on playing. I limit my work (skin cancer surgeon) to 6 hours a day so it doesn’t interfere with fiddle time. So, I admit, this is all too weird.

Having been happily married several time, I am now completely free to play my fiddle as much as I want. I know I could never get away with that with a wife or girlfriend. So I am lucky and I pray to be free of such incumberances on my fiddle time. When it is windy, I windsurf and get to the mountains to snowboard. I do have a life of sorts outside the fiddle ….. but it is not for most…. I just love the solitary time playing and have set my entire life up to do so - at least for the last 4 years - never a regret and never looking back. You more normal folks with families and different life situations may love the time with your instrument but have to ration now. But life can be long and you can reshape your life -live “life as the canvas” - choosing based on what you love priorities - for most of you normal people out there - relationships, family and careers trump playing - but you can have an eye toward the future.

In the end, music will never fail you.

Re: Busy Folks, how long do you practice?

It depends on what you mean by “practice”. Is it time spent on technique, such getting clean cross-string bowing, good bowed triplets, neat rolls, intonation … ? That list is truly endless. Or is it getting a tune into memory, or getting a tune you know up to performance standard? Or all of the above?

I take a broader view that “practice” is any playing that is not actually performance, provided - and this is the killer - that some thought is put into it. On this basis it is fair to say that I’m practicing at least 12 hours a week - and enjoy it, otherwise I wouldn’t do it. And performing at least twice a month. Folk music sessions nowadays average no more than about an hour a week, but I can’t do everything.

Re: Busy Folks, how long do you practice?

If you’re going to be practicing for a couple or more hours at a time (there are quite a few who do), use the 50-minute hour system; in other words take a full 10 minutes complete break every hour. Get up and walk around, get yourself a coffee or tea, talk to the dog. If you’re practicing with the right sort of attention, as you should be for it to be of any use, doing without those few minutes breaks is counter-productive. My orchestral rehearsals, for example, are usually a little over 2 hours, with a 10 minute break at halfway.

Also, before you start, do a few minutes relaxation exercises and stretching. Shoulder shrugs are useful for the violinist. This warm up is vital.

One of the world’s greatest violinists said that more than 3 hours practice a day (4 hours top max) is a waste of time. Worth bearing in mind.

A professional or good amateur musician, when starting on a new piece, will prepare by studying the music and its background in depth before he goes to the instrument. This preparatory work significantly helps in understanding the music and hence its playing.
