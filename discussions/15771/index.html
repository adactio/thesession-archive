How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

I’m now to the point where I know enough tunes that I’m always guarding against losing some of my old friends. I can’t stand that feeling at a session when someone launches into a tune that “I used to know.” I think some people feel okay about sort of knowing a number of tunes but I’ve always approached the learning of tunes by taking them one at at time. In my mind I either know the tune, am in the process of learning it or don’t know it.

One thing I’ve done is keep a playlist on my iPod for “Tunes I know.” I play this playlist (currently at 124 tracks) regularly to keep the music in my head. Since I don’t have hours and hours a day to practice I like to use any method I can for both learning new music and retaining the old.

I also have a “coming soon” playlist. These are songs that I want to learn. I listen to these tunes reguarly so that, when I do sit down to learn the tune, my brain is already familar with the turns and feel ot it.

Can anyone share a way you organize and build your tunes given the time limitations that most of us face?

Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

I keep the tunes I know on an iPod playlist too. I spend the first chunk of practice time working on sight-reading (it’s the vegetables before dessert model), and the second chunk of time learning the tune I’ve gotten that week from my teacher (which goes much more quickly if I’ve listened to the tune enough so that I can hum it-I do that mostly while driving so it doesn’t take up practice time)

The last chunk of practice time (definitely the dessert) is with the playlist of known (or formerly known…) tunes on shuffle. Sometimes I only make it to the first tune, and have to spend that chunk of time relearning it, other times I get through several, but the shuffle gives it a randomness (I don’t just get to pick my favorites) and means there’s a good chance that, over time, most the tunes will stay somewhat current. I’m nowhere near 124 yet, but some day… :)

Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

My experience may be wrongheaded and stupid, so take it for what it’s worth.

For the first 15 years or so, I did “memorize” tunes--encoded series of specific notes that formed the melody. But over the last 15 years it feels less and less like memorization. I’m not sure how to explain what takes its place--some agility with the instrument and the idioms of this music, I suppose, so that it’s now so much easier for me to suss out a tune and string the bits together on the fly. If I can do that with a tune I haven’t heard before, then it’s all that much easier with a tune I *have* heard before, and easier still on a tune I’ve actually played before.

This approach to “retrieving” tunes comes in large part from never playing the tune the same way twice. Instead of storing the tune as a predetermined and inviolate sequence of notes, I store it as a melodic outline, to be filled in, fleshed out, as I play. Contraray to SuzThoughts’ post above, “wrong notes” or notes left out are rare because I’m using a relatively limited range of possible notes (within the key and mode of the tune) and possible articulations of them (the cuts, rolls, triplets, doublestops, etc., common within the tradition). It’s also common to “chunk” bits of the tune, so I’m not making things up one note at a time (although this too sometimes happens).

So (analogy time), instead of memorizing every word in a story and retelling it exactly, word for word, I’m memorizing the structure of the story, paragraph by paragraph. And I use a somewhat narrow vocabulary and grammar to flesh in the paragraphs.

Gzeg, I’d suggest that the way to get better at this is to “improvise” more with the tunes you already know. Come to understand how a phrase such as |G2 BG dGBG| occurs in lots of reels and can be “replaced” by many other phrases while remaining true to the tune:

|GABG dBGB|
|G~B3 d~B3|
|G~B3 dBAB|
G~B3 GBdB|
|G/G/G BG dBAG|
|GABd eBdB|
|GABc dBAB|
|GABc d~B3|
and so on….

And learn to recognize all the variants that work for a certain chunk. Then, when you run into |G~B3 d~G3| in another tune, you now have a menu of choices, derived from a different tune, right?

For instance, you could take the opening phrase of Duke of Leinster: |G2 BG dGBc|dBeB dBAB|

and use it in the B part of Last Night’s Fun:

Instead of |d~f3 a~f3|d~f3 egfe|d~f3 a~f3|egfe dBAB|
you play: |d2 fd adfg|afbf afef|d~f3 a~f3|egfe dBAB|

Sabe?

Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

Mehere, the trick to getting tunes to stick from the dots (besides the suggestions in my post above) is to ***hear*** the tune from the dots--don’t just “read” the dots.

“sight reading” is redundant and neglects the sense that really must be engaged--hearing--to transfer the tune from dots on a page to a melody in your head.

Once you can “sight hear” from the page, the tunes will stick just as well as tunes you learn by ear--because hearing the inky dots is just another way of learning by ear.

Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

Oh, and in response to the initial post at the top of this thread--the approach I’ve suggested here is very efficient. You can learn and retrieve many tunes with much less investment of time than storing and retrieving them by rote. Plus, it’s the only way that works for me once you get over, say, 500 tunes, in your repertoire. Not surprisingly, the more tunes you know, the more interchangeable chunks you have available to you, and the easier it is to learn new tunes. After a while, most players stop counting how many tunes they know, and stop keeping lists--that all becomes irrelevant.

The first 500 tunes are slow to pile up, but the second 500 rush over the cliff into your mind’s ear like lemmings….

Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

Ottery (and sbhikes, too), I think the stress of accumulating tunes tends to peak for most people at about 150 to 200 tunes. So a few years into it. Then it begins to ease because patterns and structure and interchangeable chunks become clearer and more familiar.

And I’m *not* advocating learning tunes just for the sake of ammassing tunes. All the tunes I know are ones I enjoy playing. Nearly all of them are played by at least one other musician at the sessions I participate in.

Yes, sometimes two tunes really aren’t distinct enough to count twice--if you play Dr. Gilbert’s you can immediately play Dispute at the Crossroads. But tunes like Eileen Callaghan’s and Milliner’s Daughter diverge enough (to my ear) to make them two distinct tunes.

And I’m also not just talking about jigs and reels here. We regularly play hornpipes, mazurkas, barndances, germans, airs, waltzes, etc. (even the occasional polka-gasp!) at our sessions. So there’s a wealth of tunes to choose from.

As a rock guitarist 20 years ago, my band mates (and audience) expected me to know the chord progressions and signature lead riffs of hundreds and hundreds of songs, from the Everly Bros and Buddy Holly to every Led Zepplin and Aerosmith anthem ever recorded. Plus some blues, pop, reggae, and folk. Compared to all that, this Irish trad stuff is relatively manageable.

Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

For what it’s worth, I keep a list of tunes that I have “officially learned” in Barfly (the Mac ABC program). There are currently 218 tunes in there. I’d say that probably 200 of those tunes I would play in sessions if they were started, the rest probably wouldn’t come out at speed. But I am also in that nebulous area, where I learn tunes faster - sometimes “on the fly” in sessions, although, I have to play them several times for them to stick, and I keep a list of those on my phone. There are about 30 tunes on my list of “ones I play but haven’t officially learned”.

To go along with CPT’s assertion that you learn “chunks”, I also think of it as learning “shapes”. I learn tunes by shape much more than by note now. So I remember the shape of a tune, and from that, I can figure out what notes are generally used. This “shape learning” style also lends itself more to variation or interpretation, and I’ve let go of the idea of there being certain notes that follow other notes real specifically in a lot of tunes.

The result of this is that in some ways, I don’t think I learn the tunes as well as ones that I learned early on, where I learned every single note (of at least one setting), and practiced it every day. On the other hand, I think of it as knowing the tunes better, because I am more able to express the tune in different ways…

Even only playing about 250 tunes or so, I constantly come across tunes that I know, and haven’t played in a long time, or have “forgotten”. To me, it’s very fun to be reminded of a tune, and even if you can’t play it real well to begin with, it’s a constant journey of discovery that I enjoy. It’s like running into an old friend, and getting reacquainted again. And the longer I play, the better I get at recalling old tunes, even if it has been a longer time since I played the tune!

So I am with Ottery and llig here, don’t stress about letting tunes go. You’re not losing anything, and you can always get them back. :-)

Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

>>> the brain is just like an attic. (It has limited space).

I think you’re underestimating how much the the human brain can remember. If you grew up with rock music, for instance, you can probably recognize 8000 songs or more, and can probably sing along with a good third of them, going back to your early years.

The thing is, you can’t *recall* many of them until you hear them, and you can probably only start singing your favorite ones when you’re in the shower…

Add that to all the stuff you remember from your life growing up, your schooling, your friends, etc. The human brain has an incredible amount of information that it can store. It’s not like a hard drive on your computer, it works differently. It uses neural pathways that cross paths, and share neurons to remember things more like patterns and shapes than like binary bits.

And in some ways it’s the *opposite* of an attic or a hard drive. The more tunes you learn, the easier it is to remember more of them!

And without starting the merits/drawbacks of the sheet music debate for the third time in a week, I would point out that you can come home, look up a tune, and *still* learn it aurally. There are numerous different ABC players out there, for instance. (They don’t generally sound anything like the music should sound, but you can still use them to learn the basics of a tune by ear if you don’t have another aural source!) And I would tend to agree that things learned aurally probably have better “staying power” than things you learn from a printed source. Re: How do you hold on to all those tunes!?

I think Pete is spot on there. I believe we actually remember everything we have learned in our life, but some of us actually bother to hone our system of recall a lot better than others. The stuff Will says about how to learn (how to set your brain up to retrieve stuff?) is a sensible and efficient way to do it. But I think what is useful to get across is the idea that you can learn shedloads of tunes (All the stuff about how many tunes there are is a different discussion really), but they don’t have to be in the forefront of your mind all the time. To use a computer analogy, your mind has an almost limitless amount of hard disk space, and if you file your tunes away in a clever way you can retrieve them more efficiently. But what a lot of people seem to want to do is to keep ALL the data/tunes they have in the RAM section of their brain, which doesn’t have unlimited capacity. That’s why some people, once they start to get to the point where they have learned about as many tunes as can be held in the forefront of their minds for instant recall, start to panic and start making lists and things.
