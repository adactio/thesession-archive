What to do when a session player consistently noodles in the wrong key and plays wrong notes…loudly.

We consistently have one person (guitarist) who does not know Irish tunes and continues to “noodle” loudly each and every song as well as accompanying our singers. Anyone else had this experience? What was your solution? (I actually think I know the solution however, open to all creative and kind ideas as to what to do). One rejected person can rumor a session right out of existence.

Re: What to do when a session player consistently noodles in the wrong key and plays wrong notes…loudly.

Don’t know where you are in the world, Ency, but I will assume that you are not living in the heartland of traditional Irish music.

A common problem in our neck of the woods is that people are interested in ‘celtic’ music, but only have experience with bluegrass or generic old time music. Our solution was to organize a slow session that meets a couple of times a month so that we could encourage their interest, teach some tunes, and give everyone a chance to play tunes at less than warp speed. During the course of teaching the tunes, I get to drone on about things like session etiquette. And, big bonus!, you get two more sessions per month. Yay us.

Mr. Sims herds the guitar people off into a corner and works on strummy stuff while I teach tunes. Then we get back together and run through our ever-expanding tune list. Sometimes we listen to sound clips if we need to clarify a point about style (like why you don’t need to whack away at your instrument while someone is singing).

My theory is that it’s about including people who are motivated enough to get out and play. Stuff happens, though. You just have to communicate, and one would hope you can do it in a friendly way, but I keep my Session B*tch Tiara handy just in case. (Usually reserved for drunken instrument grabbers.) Yes, you do have to be careful not to alienate everyone, but you do have to draw a line somewhere if chaos is to be kept in check.

I also took a hint from my friends who run an Appalachian old-time session in town: they send a reminder for the upcoming session, which includes a very nicely-worded summary of acceptable behavior. We do that now, and it seems to draw in more people. Go figure.

Re: What to do when a session player consistently noodles in the wrong key and plays wrong notes…loudly.

In some situations you can’t be both friendly and sincere.

In this scenario you have to be sincere - you’re duty is to the the session first and the other genuine musicians who are there to make the most of it.

If the guy is as bad as you seem to indicate then for the good of the session you have to tell him to stop - ignoring the problem won’t make it go away.

If he has anything in his repetoire that is half good you can suggest every hour or so that he play a song or back a tune, but if he’s playing something that isn’t right (all of the time) and doesn’t know it - then the only solution is to tell him to go listen to some good trad guitarists and come back with tunes he knows and stay out of ones he doesn’t. Tell him to sit and listen for the remainder of the session and leave the guitar out of his hands until a tune comes up he actually knows.

Sure he’ll probably be upset/defensive - but you can’t have a free for all and you can’t be overly nice either. In my experience a “nice” session is one where if you’re learning, you’re welcomed to sit among the musicians or nearby and listen (even play lightly where there are lots of loud instruments) and invited to start (or suggest) a set occasionally that you can play with more experienced players depending on how the evening is going.

As an aside I’ve seen plenty decent players turn random guitarists away at sessions before they even play a chord (most often when there is already a guitarist present) and that’s just down to ensuring the rhythm section doesn’t get muddled and cross over - that’s one of the main draws of picking a melody instrument - you don’t have to worry about that, so even an open session can have a limit on bodhrans/guitars which is necessary to keep things nice for all the musicians there.

Re: What to do when a session player consistently noodles in the wrong key and plays wrong notes…loudly.

The answer to this is so simple, but first a piece of general advice when approaching someone who attempts to join your session: Ask the person’s name before you do anything else, then tell them yours and shake their hand. Second, ask them if they’ve ever played at an Irish session. The first question breaks the ice and the second puts them on the defensive because the answer is likely no. Follow that advice and I think you’ll find it forgives whatever else follows. What follows will either be a brief explanation of what it takes to join the session or, if there seems to be no alternative, a request for the person to please leave. I find it hard to believe that the situation would ever demand more than that. It should never become necessary to, on the one hand, put up with nonsense or, on the other, create a scene.

If you’re talking about a situation that has gone on for a number of sessions, it’s time for whomever leads the session or, in the absence of a leader, commands the most acknowledged respect, to have a private word with the offending party.

Re: What to do when a session player consistently noodles in the wrong key and plays wrong notes…loudly.

No one is born knowing how to ‘sit in’. Leadership is actually necessary to help create what ends up sounding ‘effortless’. If you’re in a position of ‘leadership’, it’s very helpful to lay out your expectations specifically. If your session has no score, the least you can do is verbally describe a structure. You expect it, why not ‘speak it’?

Back in the day I over-noodled (I fiddle) at my first band session. I got ‘the Ray’ immediately and knew what I was doing was wrong. While the majority scoffed, one kind person took me aside and explained timing and the kind of harmonies expected. I’d been playing over the guitarist’s solo. Who knew? I thot that was ‘helpful harmonizing’. Prior to that time, I’d been the concertmaster in middle and high school orchestras, and thot I knew how to ‘get along’ with a band, easy peasy - pffft! But it’s very different from following sheet music where parts and timing are built-in. I was wrong, inexperienced, and very grateful for the advice I received, which was a revelation. For someone who has no group experience, it can’t be expected that they have any idea about cues, breaks, solos, anything. So be helpful.

But there’s a break even point. If said musician has no clue about keys, it is not your responsibility to teach them music theory. Some people have the talent of knowing without having ever read a note. Some will play for years but never get it. Observe, and cut your losses, but do tell them why. They need to go back to the ‘drawing board’ and practice until they’re ready, not use your session for practice.

Re: What to do when a session player consistently noodles in the wrong key and plays wrong notes…loudly.

I have led sessions for the last seven years. The way I prevent noodling and rude behavior is through a process a prescreening. First of all, new members contact me by email so I determine their level of capability to start off with. If they have no experience, then I’m going to suggest that they take private lessons until their teacher thinks they’re ready to start participating in a beginner session. Since I have not even given the directions on how to get to the session yet, I have control over whether they attend or not. I do stratify our sessions into beginning, intermediate, and advanced. Since I set myself up as authority figure in the sessions and have established that position over time, any newcomers attending seem to accept my leadership with very little question. I generally address any difficult issues in the beginner session as they come up and openly in front of everyone. I use it as a teaching opportunity rather then a method of intimidation or embarrassment. This seems to work well for everyone. I find that by the time our session players become intermediate or advanced they have accepted the set of rules and will actually remind one another if they have broken a rule. I agree with those session leaders who are saying that structure makes sessions work. There will always be those times someone needs to be pulled aside and talked to about their behavior. But that’s why you’re the leader, because someone needs to do it.
Jim Wells - Dallas Slow Sessions
