Re: Frank advice for classical musicians playing traditional music

In some ways the article seems a little unbalanced - professing to be a fully knowledgeable and integrated traditional player at this stage, and yet still throwing in certain snide remarks at both parties, such as the imagined situation of the fiddler auditioning for orchestra (‘Good luck at the audition, honey, I’m sure they’ll be grateful you showed up.’) …

Anyhow, these sorts of discussions always interest me immensely because I find them personally relevant. [Having just turned 23,] I took up the fiddle aged 16 with classical lessons, having never played an instrument before, nor hardly even pursued recreational music listening. It was about two years later that my teacher gave me a book of fiddle tunes (interestingly, not being something she’s into at all) which I enjoyed playing - poorly of course, by technical and stylistic standards!

Over the following handful of years I enjoyed playing fiddle tunes but still wasn’t too concerned with getting the style, and had hardly listened to actual fiddle music, what with still learning so much about classical music. It’s only been over the last two years or so I became increasingly frustrated with my limitations in classical playing and was feeling more fulfilled by the freedoms and sheer joy of playing fiddle music; it’s only during and since this period that I have been trying properly to get to grips with style, and I now know that this is what I’m going to stick with hereafter.

So of course these kinds of discussions interest me - well, it’s scary if anyone suggests that you CANNOT became a decent trad player because you didn’t grow up with the music, or because you began playing your instrument classically. Needless to say I don’t believe that (why would I bother carrying on if I did?), but I do think the approach has to be right, and I endeavour towards that. The writer says that one has to be humble enough, and I can assure all that I would never walk into a room of fiddlers and presume to tell anyone what they’re doing ‘wrong’ (nor would a lot of current or ex-classical players, I feel, and so I think the article does misrepresent a fair portion of the classical community.) In fact, I still feel like such a newcomer that I haven’t yet been to a proper session through sheer timidity, but know that such experience will prove essential for the future integrity of my playing.

Where I find this discussion can become really murky is where we deal with what ‘trad’ even means (as ever!…) When I first started getting into fiddling properly, it was initially and for a long time through the legacy of Jean Carignan - which is telling, considering the large amount of classical influence that was present in his style and outlook (I guess I saw him as my ‘way-in’ from where I was at). Considering that, does he count as trad or not? He knew thousands of tunes, learnt 99% of them by ear (teaching himself to read the dots when he was middle-aged, I believe - not to mention teaching himself to read outright), was mentored by such Quebecois masters as Joseph Allard, grew up playing for dances, house gatherings etc… Nothing un-trad in any of that, surely, yet I know many disagree with his playing style and find it doesn’t satisfy their feel for a real fiddle style, but rather an over-virtuosic amalgamation of traditional and classical technique.

Yet none cannot admire his skill and versatility - he clearly had those unusual prodigious capacities that could have made him an extraordinaire of whatever style he chose, but it seems that it was his APPROACH that made his playing style the perhaps uneasy amalgamation that it was; he didn’t have to incorporate aspects of virtuoso classical technique into his playing, but he chose to anyway. So I suppose my point is this: I don’t feel that there’s any reason why past experience in other musical styles should inhibit the development of true skill, style and taste in another style, with enough effort, dedication, immersion etc. etc. Rather, the attitude surely needs to be correct. If I continue my musical life without weaning myself off sheet music reliance, without trying to really integrate with traditional players, without immersing myself or striving to match the sounds of trad players I admire, without searching for the meaning of this music and experiencing its socio-cultural function, then I’m unlikely to develop into the bonafide trad player that I hope someday to be. I don’t see any of those problems as being to do with my former classical technical training at all - they would be problems with my attitude towards traditional music, and are problems I don’t intend on encountering.

My previous technical experience of the instrument is classical, but I don’t see it so much as a need to ‘unlearn’ that - rather, to move laterally across to fiddle technique(s). I feel on the rhetorical brink of a point I’ve rather lost (‘murky’ indeed!) but I hope some of what I’ve said seems sensible. I could continue with a whole spiel about what I may consider to be even more important - unabashed faithfulness to the totality and plurality of one’s musical influences - but that’s another discussion, I think!

I thought it was a good article--remember, its audience is NOT the trad players who frequent our discussions, it is folks from outside the tradition. Re: Frank advice for classical musicians playing traditional music

Classically trained players were once trained to produce what is on the page…every articulation, dynamic, bowing mark…everything. They were, and sometimes still are human replicating devices. At one point the defining standard was that of the romantic era. But in the past years, say 40 or less, the classical folks had to discover that there are very different playing styles needed for the very different kinds of “classical” music. Listening to a major orchestra destroy show tunes in the 50s and 60s was terrible. Now, they are much better at it, and that example can be multiplied and applied to various styles. In short, current classical players are often much more flexible than they used to be.

The “bad classical players” take their classical style to everything they play. They do that either because they have no experience with other kinds of music, because they don’t listen carefully or because they have a distain for any other style. Avoid them at all costs.

The good ones listen to the styles they are attempting to play and adapt their playing. Those folks wouldn’t think of playing a style without listening, and constantly study and listen to get better at those styles they are interested in. The most flexible of those folks are free lance players. They might get asked to play something classical, something show music like, something jazz related, and something in one or another traditional style all within a week! They have to learn. And if they encounter something they don’t understand they’ll go and listen or take a lesson or two with a good person to figure it out.

In sum, I think what the blog writer was writing about is what I have termed the “bad classical players,” those folks who think there is only one way. As such the article makes good sense to me. And, if it slaps a bit hard from time to time…well perhaps those folks need to be awakened. Remember the mule and the baseball bat.

Hi Na éisc,

What I meant was that I’d always been under the impression that sessions were not a type of performance, but rather a musical gathering - regulars play together, know the tunes, and no one’s particular contribution is actually applauded but simply appreciated as a matter of course (as I say, I’ve not been to one so I can’t speak from experience!).
This one I went to worked more as a musical show-and-tell (minus the tell), with different musicians often performing solo (this partly due to much of it being songs) and the others would applaud after; I came very much as a pair with my guitarist friend, as we knew tunes/chords mutually from busking together.
So I guess I was trying to define the kind of trad gathering I went to, as I felt many might not consider it a ‘session’ - either way, we all had a great time! Certainly gave me a taste of what essential elements sessions could bring to my music-making as a trad fiddler too.

I’ve had a few thoughts since last night, and am wondering if it’s knowing the feel of the mechanics of the instrument that is most important, not what you started out playing. In that Gilles Apap video above, he arguably does a good job overall of making a genuine fiddle sound - considering he’s clearly a virtuoso classical player too, I’d say he succeeds not because of these classical credentials as such, but because as a part of that he clearly knows the workings of the instrument inside-out, from his huge experience and no doubt a LOT of technical drilling - the physics of the bow in particular. I don’t see it as a problem that I started and continued with classical for the majority of my time so far (5 years classical, 2 years trad), because it’s been during those fundamental years where you learn so much about the nature of the instrument, regardless of style - the same technical-to-instinctive learning that everyone goes through when they start playing. Of course it’ll be more immediate to players who have always played imitating actual fiddlers, and/or have grown up surrounded by the music, but with enough conscience about the mechanics of the instrument and the nature of the musical culture you are moving into, I feel that you can certainly move across to a trad style if you do it wholeheartedly and without any reservations - technically, musically, socially or culturally. And if you genuinely love the music, that shouldn’t be difficult to do!

Re: Frank advice for classical musicians playing traditional music

this discussion has been about “classical” violinists taking up Irish fiddling, and while I know next to nothing about that, I have a lot of experience of trying to teach “classical” flutists and recorder players how to play Irish flute and whistle, and trying to teach Highland pipers how to play Irish uilleann pipes.

It’s the same issue, that is, people have a certain package of technique and style deeply ingrained, and have to be perceptive enough to recognize how the way they’re accustomed to doing things varies from they way they’re done in Irish music, and be flexible enough to set aside their ingrained ways and learn new ways.

The unperceptive, the stubborn, the inflexible, will fail.

It’s not easy to “sound Irish” because there’s such specificity, so many little things that have to be precisely “right”. People have likened it to learning a different language, or as I think of it, learning to mimic an accent different from the one you were raised with .

It’s the same in other genres too of course. There’s a very specific Appalachian vocal sound, Appalachian fiddle sound, that outsiders have to really work at to get “right”. I know a load of jazz musicians and they say it’s nearly impossible for orchestral musicians to “pass” as jazz players; every jazz guy can spot them as soon as a couple notes come out of their horn.

A little story about “classical” violinists trying to play Irish music: A fiddler friend of mine went to a violin shop to pick up some rosin, and the woman behind the counter asked him who he played with (assuming he was a classical player). When he said that he played Irish music, the woman beamed a smile and exclaimed “Oh, I play Irish music too!” and picked up her violin and proceeded to play Irish Washerwoman, each note staccato, the bow bouncing up and down the whole time.

Yes, a book called ‘The Ceilidh Collection’ from a slightly unusual series of traditional music books, of which I have a few; as I’ve learnt more about the music, the series has gone down in my estimation somewhat, making no real effort to educate about ornaments and phrasing etc., and therefore would probably most suit players who have no real interest in playing the music in a representative manner and who want to play from the dots (in fact, it comes with written piano/violin accompaniments etc.!! I shudder to think). Nonetheless, a fairly standard collection of good-enough tunes (English, Irish and Scottish), almost all in the unattributed category.

Yes I still play many of the tunes, and keep meaning to properly memorise the rest (I’m very bad at getting round to learning more tunes at the moment, not helped by the fact I’ve been earning my summer keep by busking, recycling a load of tunes I’ve known for ages). Needless to say I don’t play from the sheet music, and yes I have since heard many of them played in recordings of traditional players, and am always eager and interested to hear these real-life versions. As for my own playing, well, needless to say I started out very much glued to the dots - no real attempt to ornament or phrase idiomatically, which wasn’t surprising as I wasn’t a trad listener then either, so I didn’t have a good aural picture in my mind of what this music could and should sound like. Nowadays I know a lot more (yet still much to learn!), and so play the tunes with ornamentation and enjoy cooking up ideas and variants to give the tunes vigour, variety and, perhaps, my own personal stamp! So I like to think that nowadays I have the right idea, even if various technical aspects are still going to take some time to develop - pulling off some ornaments properly (eg. treble), getting that really infectious, foot-tapping sense of rhythm and pulse, and getting better at learning tunes by ear, which I now see as by far the best way of doing it.
