I admit I’ve not played flute for several years now, hand cramping forced me to switch to whistle, but I did play Irish flute for over 30 years. I’m out of the loop as far as current makers go, but during the 80s and 90s I tried hundreds of flutes, covering every prominent maker there was during that period, and what I found, over and over, is that most makers tended to make mediocre flutes but every now and then would make superb one, all except for Patrick Olwell. I’ve played dozens of Olwell flutes and all were excellent, better-playing than the best flutes of any other modern maker, with one exception: I did try one of Patrick’s very early flutes, and it was very good, as good as the best flutes from other makers, but not better than them.

So my experience is that Olwell is more consistent than any other maker I’ve tried a number of flutes from.

I don’t know anything about the new makers who have recently taken up Irish flutemaking.

To be clear, the best Irish flute I’ve ever played is a c1860 Pratten that was perfect in every way. The closest to that have been the Olwells.

“ Is it worth it for a professional at a top level? Ask Mike Marshall, Chris Thile, David Grisman, or Adam Steffy about their mandolins.”

Funny you should mention that. As I was reading this thread, I was thinking about the mandolin market, and I have to say, I think even at the top level, sentiment enters into it. Frankly, I think Chris Thile got a better sound out of his Dudenbostel mandolin than he does out of his Loar(s). I think he even said something to that effect in some interview or other, but if you’re a mandolin player, it is hard to escape the romantic notion of playing the same model as Bill Monroe. And of course, it was his choice to play a Dudenbostel which created the incredible demand for that maker’s instruments. In terms of what goes into the construction, probably not, but the scarcity of players makes for a scarcity of builders, which makes for high prices for both new and vintage instruments.

I can’t speak to the flute market, but it seems like there are plenty of makers whose prices haven’t hit the stratosphere. So I have to echo An Draighean…count yourself lucky that you’re not a piper.

↳ Posted by DrSilverSpear on December 7th, 2015.

Re: Is it legit to sell an Olwell flute 10 000 $ ?

I think non-refundable deposits are a necessary evil. Without them managing the waiting list becomes a nightmare - people will put themselves on four makers lists, and decide later which instrument they are actually going to buy. You can land up losing custom because your wait is too long, when in reality half the orders are duds, and your three year list will only take a year to complete.

Selling places in the queue does seem unethical, but the other people in the queue don’t really lose out - that place ahead of you was already taken, and your instrument will arrive at the same time regardless of whether that instrument ahead of you goes to the person who placed the order or someone else.

Pricing is another big problem for makers who are lucky enough to be that popular - if you are making a healthy living selling instruments for $3,000 but you see them getting sold on for $10,000 what do you do? Do you put your prices up to $10,000, reducing the opportunity for the sharks but knowing that some deserving musicians now won’t be able to afford you? Do you keep selling at $3,000, knowing that others are exploiting you and making profits at your expense? Or you do take what is probably the least ethical but most practical solution, and set the price of each instrument according to who the customer is? Ever wondered why so many top makers are so secretive about their prices?

If a maker produced 60 flutes a year and had been doing so for upwards of 30 years, then there would be approximately 2000 of his instruments around. If players kept his flutes for an average of 10 years (with some never selling and others moving on much more quickly - perhaps to keyed models from the same maker), and assuming that half of sales were private, then you would expect to see 100 flutes per year changing hands. About 2 per week.

At the weekend I met a good player who was playing a keyless Olwell in preference to the fully keyed Lehart she has had for many years (and out of which she produced a mighty sound).
