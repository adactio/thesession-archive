I think people worry too much about regional styles. Most of the big name players have quite distinctive personal sounds.
Play with good musicians and listen to good recordings and you’ll be in a good place in terms of developing a nice sound. Re: Style

In my experience style and technique are often confused. Technique is knowing how to do a roll on every note, style is choosing whether or not to do so.

More often than not you see people on this board attempting to emulate what they perceive to be a particular style without understanding what technical elements contribute to it, and so end up stuck sounding like an odd parody of what they intend to present.

I play military band music for a living, and I play a lot of 19th and early 20th century marches at work. The style of that music is intensely important, and if not played in the marcato march style sounds dreadful. I can’t imagine anyone wanting to hear a Sousa march played in the style of, say, a western classical orchestra composition.

In the same vein, I challenge the assertion that it’s all good, so long as you enjoy the music and are doing it “your way.” At a certain point it ceases to be Irish traditional music and becomes something else. There’s nothing wrong with that per se, but it’s not my cup of tea personally and I don’t care to listen to it in the context of Irish traditional music. For reference, I enjoy Ralph Vaughn Williams arrangements of English traditional music for western classical ensembles, but I wouldn’t want to walk into a pub and hear a violinist playing “the lark ascending” in that style.

An old teacher of mine had an adage about Irish music that makes more and more sense as time goes on. “There are only 8 or 10 ways to do it right, but there are hundreds of ways to do it wrong.”

Re: Style

"there are many approaches and a fiddler from pennygheal or new Jersey or from jazz or from classical traditions all simply play the musuc as they feel.
IMO there is no right or wrong in this regard."

Yes, to an extent. But there is a tipping point when that ceases to be true. A point at which you can’t say someone is playing Irish traditional music. For example, when an Irish tune gets absorbed into a different musical tradition and becomes so established that it becomes a standard in that other tradition. ‘Smash the Windows’ has become something of a standard in English Morris dance accompaniment. It’s recognisably the same tune as the Irish original, but it’s completely differently and it would be absurd to say that it is “irish traditional music” when an English melodeon-player is accompanying a Morris side with it.

There are plenty of instances of Scottish and English tunes becoming Irish-ified and absorbed into the Irish repertoire too of course.

How much of the above is due to design, chance, osmosis, coincidence or synchronicity it’s impossible to say.

You might be able to have irish trad without ‘ornaments’ (though I can’t think of any example where there were none at all) but you can’t have it without articulation. To me, there are a number of varied and overlapping approaches to articulation that are distinctively traditional Irish and a whole other series of approaches that aren’t. A significant part of Irish articulation is cordoned off in a box marked ‘ornaments’ while other parts aren’t.

If you use a bit of jazz or classical articulation and a lot of trad, for example, you add something different to the music. People have been doing that forever- sometimes it really works and sometimes it really doesn’t. Sometimes it works so well and gets emulated so much it becomes part of the tradition. If you come up with your own approach the same is true. (Although you’d have to work pretty hard to find something that didn’t exist somewhere out there.)

If you use a lot of stuff from outside the tradition and very little from inside, then you’re not playing traditional music anymore. That’s what tradition is. It’s handed down, (or emulated down if you prefer.)
None of this means we have to be prescriptive about other people’s playing. I have no problem with musicians from any genre borrowing Irish tunes. I’ll quite happily appropriate tunes from another tradition or genre and play them at a session. It’s probably not an approach that would particularly interest Breton musicians or Sicilian musicians or whatever, but I’m not really claiming to play Breton or Sicilian music.

Rolls are not uncommon in Donegal fiddle music, though it must be said that Donegal fiddlers in general use the bowed triplet more regularly.
