The Life-blood of Irish Traditional Music

“New Compositions are the life-blood of Irish traditional music. They are part of the natural variety of a living tradition, evidence of its vigour and power of its renewal, and they are essential for the healthy continuance of the music into the future”

- Nicholas Carolan, Direcotor, Irish Traditional Music Archive, in his introduction to the tune book Father Kelly’s Favourites.

I found this quotation in the new tune book by Sandie Purcell

www.originalwriting.ie/sandiepurcell

So what do people think of Carolan’s comment. Are you interested in new compositions or are you content to stick to the old standards for the rest of your life. Do you agree with Carolan when he says the tradition needs new compositions “for the healthy continuance of the music into the future”?

Re: The Life-blood of Irish Traditional Music

Good tunes are “sticky.” What makes a tune sticky?

- The parts fit together well--there’s a sense of each part completing the other.
- The melody drapes well on a rhythmic pattern that is standard within the tradition--strong notes tend to fall on strong beats.
- The melody includes a charismatic “hook”--one phrase or turn that is memorable because it juxtaposes different intervals in a way that pleasantly jangles our synapses.

Criteria… Hmmm… I think different tunes survive for different reasons, but the criteria that probably have the most influence are

* how ‘catchy’ a tune is
* how playable a tune is

Catchy is a bit subjective, but for me things are catchy if they are easy to remember, and a good combination of predictable and surprising. Melodically interesting would be another way of saying that, I guess.

Playability is also subjective. I think a lot of “modern” tunes might be classified as more complex. Certain tunes are noticeably “fiddle tunes” or “pipe tunes” or whatever, and something that is easily playable on one may not be on another. But if they’re easy enough to play while you’re slightly drunk or tired, they have a better chance of surviving…

Re: The Life-blood of Irish Traditional Music

Danny, I suppose I’m thinking of musical symmetry in several different ways. The most obvious perhaps is the 8 bars of each half of a reel, say. (Though I love set dances and would never discriminate against them because one part has a few extra bars. The asymmetry is part of their charm.)

But music can be symmetrical in other ways--the reach of the intervals both above and below the home note, repetitions or inversions of a phrase, even the “call-and-answer” form. Their is a certain balance or symmetry acheived when one half of a tune stays fairly low in pitch, and the second half jumps up an octave. So much so that us more epxerienced players come to expect it even of tunes we are hearing for the first time.

Re: The Life-blood of Irish Traditional Music

grego, I hope you don’t feel like I’m demonizing you here… I’m arguing for the sake of the discussion. And I’m really enjoying the discussion. :-)

I agree that automobiles aren’t the “perfect” analogy, if there is such a thing. But cars don’t wear out the same way sandwiches become stale. Cars wear out with use - sandwiches go stale with time alone. A tune that gets overplayed is often dropped from the repertoire for a while (or at least has people rolling their eyes when someone starts it…) Even in the relatively short time that I have been playing, I have seen some of the cyclical nature of the popularity of tunes.

But you seem to be treating the tradition as a static object. If you take a percentage of the tunes away, then it still exists, so therefore it is still healthy. I am thinking of the tradition as a living, breathing entity, of which we are all part, and I am concerned about the future as well as the present and the past.

The concept of “life blood” fits well with this image. If you take a percentage of a living being’s blood away, they can continue to exist (generally, by manufacturing more blood in the system). If you take away their ability to manufacture new blood, they’re going to wither over time.

Nobody here would stop participating in a session simply because no new tunes were being composed. But that’s the short term view. People might have less to be enthused about over time, which in turn might have the effect of fewer new people becoming interested in the tradition, which IMO would eventually spiral, and make the tradition wither…

The results wouldn’t be visible immediately, but over the course of, say, 300 years or so, the tradition would become more of a museum piece than a living, breathing tradition.

Interesting discussion, thanks, frisbee!

Pete
