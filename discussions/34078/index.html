So it is not too bad.

It is too bad. It’s horrible. You should not think that, because “…the Orchestra was supplied with replacements,” it isn’t a huge thing for a musician. It can be devastating to lose a favorite bow. Good bows are equal in value to good fiddles. A good bow is as essential to the music as is a good fiddle, and bows vary as much. They sound very different, and feel very different, from one bow to the next. The quest for a good bow is as intense and exasperating as is the hunt for a good violin.

Many musicians aren’t even aware of the bit of ivory on the tip of the bow. The ivory itself is not essential to the bow and musicians don’t ever think about it. It weighs less than a gram, if you can imagine that: 1/28th of an ounce. It’s only purpose is to protect the tip of the wood from damage. My favorite bow is about 75 years old. I wouldn’t know how to go about getting the tip certified as old ivory. It isn’t a stupid rule but it is stupidly applied, as in the case of the Budapest.

Most contemporary bow-makers use either old ivory or a replacement. To say that somebody who gets screwed by a man in a uniform deserves what he gets is very harsh.

“Perhaps you could take the lead in laying out iron-clad documentation that would get an legal ivory-tipped or forged bow through customs?”

I cannot supply you with a receipt for a bow purchased before Feb 25 2014. That seems to be what you require as a US citizen. If a bow was manufactured after Feb 1976, and contains elephant ivory, you have a problem. However, you would have had problems before recent legislation.
The Endangered Species Act, 1973 (introduced under the Nixon administration) required government “permission” to import raw or worked African elephant ivory, and those who obtained permission would have to have kept meticulous records of all items handled.
African Elephant Conservation Act, 1989, (began under the Reagan administration, and enacted during the Bush snr administration) placed more stringent controls.
Things are somewhat different over this side of the pond, along with slightly different “legalese” involved. I’m quite confident that I could provide all that is necessary to travel within Europe.
A document from John Norwood would obviously be a sensible step, and it is also clear that you would only meet obstacles should you travel abroad (or perhaps interstate under some circumstances). Maybe you could seek out an acceptable bow that obviously has no ivory or questionable substitute ivory for such travel.
My own favourite bow was made in Pernambucco (the state) by one of a bunch of Brazilian makers who got together to produce bows where trees were felled. The guy who sold it to me travelled round selling these bows to dealers and makers. He was a cool dude with a conscience and I’m confident that I would have no problems bringing it to the US again (it’s been several times during the first few years of the 21st century). Then again, it contains no ivory and the synthetic tip is clearly synthetic. That cuts out a lot of documentation.
