Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

I’ve been at the fiddle now going on three years, and while I’ve upgraded to a sweet old fiddle I’m still using the same bow I started with, the cheapest carbon fiber bow in the shop. I’m pretty sure I’ve worn the hair out and rather than rehairing it for about what I paid for it I’ve decided to upgrade.

The problem is that at my stage of development, while I feel I’ll benefit from a better bow, I don’t feel very confident about my ability to find the right one. To make matters worse I don’t feel at all good about the prospect of trying out bows at the local violin shop. They’re nice enough, but the thought of actually playing in there just freaks me out. Instead I’m going to be doing an in home trial via an online retailer.

Rather that restrict myself to a certain price point I have decided to go with a range of Codabows. They seem to be very well regarded and I’m sure I’ll end up with a good bow I can grow into. I’ve ordered the NX, the SX, the Luma, and the Joule. Basically that’s two levels of bow, as well as two specialty bows, from what I understand a light agile bow and a heavy powerful bow. I get a week with them to make a decision.

So, if anyone has any advice regarding evaluating bows in general, or any experience with any of those specific models please share. Also, any discussion on the pros and cons of lighter vs. heavier bows as applied to the fine art of diddley would be most welcome.

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

That’s a bit like asking someone else how to choose a woman. The only a sensible advice is to try them all, and play each one for several hours to give yourself time to adapt to it. If you just give them a quick try you’ll land up choosing the one that feels most like your old bow, because that is what you are used to.

Don’t get hung up about the weight of the bow - dealers often make a big thing about how many grams a bow weights because it’s about the only thing they can actually measure to write on the label. But actually the weight distribution is far more important that the overall weight - A light bow that has more weight at the tip will feel heavier in your hand than a heavier bow with a light tip, and one of the most important aspects is the moment of inertia, which is almost impossible to measure - a bow with a lot of its weight near the balance point is much more controllable that one with the same overall weight and balance point, but a lot of weight near the tip and frog (if you’re unfamiliar with moments of inertia, ask a Porche driver to explain about ‘the dumbbell effect’).

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

Bow hair doesn’t wear out. Contrary to popular belief there are no prickles on the hair. It is the “stick and release” of the rosin that does the trick. You can clean it easily enough with denatured alcohol. I can text you the method or you can google it easily enough. Most people use way too much rosin on the bow. Before you buy a bow you should google “Violin Bow Study” and look at what various experts have said about bow stick, hair, and rosin. I rosin my bow about once every four-six hours. Frankie says over-tightening your bow is a bad habit. It’s one reason I never lend my bow to anybody. Try to tighten it so that the hair is only about a pencil width away from the stick. If it works at that tension then you probably have a good, stiff bow.
I have three bows-- a Coda Classic, a Coda Aspire, and a very pricy German silver-mounted Pernambuco bow. I like them all. The Aspire is a bit lighter than the Classic. I generally use the Classic in sessions because it seems to me that a heavier bow (we are only talking a gram or two or three difference) yields a stronger sound to my ears, and so does more of the heavy lifting. It could be about the balance. The lighter Aspire requires a bit more control but it is a joy to use. The price difference is around $500 though you’d not know it to play them. I’d hate to have to choose between the two.
The pernambuco bow combines the best of the the two carbon fiber bows and is worth more than the two of them together. It’s resting now because the eyelet got stripped and I am waiting for the new parts to arrive. That’s the advantage of having a spare bow or two. If I were you I’d sort through a few Aspires. They aren’t as finished as the pricier Coda bows, but they are more than adequate, and are fine for someone who has been at it for three years. Be on the lookout for a good one- carbon bows vary, as do wooden bows, and price is no guarantee of quality.
Bows are tricky enough. Spiccato technique is where you need a bow that is light, stiff, and responsive. Do read some of the bow studies. The only thing more fascinating than a violin bow is a violin. And vice-versa….

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

Bow hair does wear out. How long it lasts depends on how much you play, your playing style, and how much tonal degradation you are prepared to put up with. Professional classical players tend to get their bows haired every 4-12 months, for fiddlers who play less that might stretch to 2-3 years. Sometimes you get fiddlers who proudly boast that they haven’t had a re-hair in ten years, then when you fianall do re-hair their bow it sounds like they’ve got a new fiddle and they suddenly realise what they have been putting up with for all those years.

That said, too much rosin or grease from your fingers can have exactly the same effect, so if your bow has lost bite it’s worth cleaning it and seeing if that helps before you commit to a re-hair.

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

In response to Cheeky, a lighter bow is easier to bounce. It is easier to do a bowed triplet with a lighter bow, or so it seems to me. The need for more control can result in greater flexibility as well. You only want the bow to bounce when you want it to. So that means more control is necessary, to keep it from bouncing.
Maybe “stronger” isn’t the best word. “Louder” might be better. Quiet doesn’t always mean weak. In a noisy session it doesn’t matter so much if my bowed triplets are fast and precise as that my fiddle speaks out and i can hear myself.
The Aspire weights 58gr. The Classic weighs 62gr. But that’s only part of the equation. Part of the different feel is due to the balance (where the weight is - towards the tip or towards the frog), or the quality of the hair, or the inherent strength (stiffness) of the stick.
When you try the bows, try not to know which bow you are playing. Don’t think that the pricey bows are necessarily better than the cheaper ones. You shouldn’t have to tighten the hair more than about 1/4-3/8" to get it sufficiently springy. If the hair is too tight the bow will bounce when you don’t want it to, and if too loose it will feel dead.

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

There are a couple of things you can check just by looking -- hold the bow hair-side up with the frog close to your eye and look along it. The two ends, frog and tip, should be parallel, with no twist to the hair.
Turn the bow round and over, so that the tip is next to your eye and the frog is away from you, and look at the hair past the stick. There should be an even strip just visible at each side, with no bend in the stick. When you tighten the bow, it should remain straight from this point of view, and not curve left or right.
Any bumps or hollows, random changes in thickness, will also be visible from this angle. A good bow will be smooth and nicely tapered along its whole length.
Good hunting, and as David Levine says, don’t use price as a guide -- use your eyes and ears and fingers.

My understanding of ‘tracking’ is simply how ‘straight’ the travel is - in other words true tracking would imply that the bow travels from end to end, keeping the same distance from the bridge all the time. As you probably know, there are several ‘bow trackers’ around to help the player in the early stages to keep a straight bow.

As for gliding from string to string, I’m afraid that’s totally up to the player :) … although with a lighter bow, there’s obviously less effort required. Sometimes there’s an unintentional and very short continuation of the note on one string, just before crossing to an adjacent string if your glide / crossing is not clean enough.

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

The “gliding from string to string” thing - it isn’t the overall weight of the bow that counts, it is the weight distribution. A bow that has more weight at the tip and frog needs more effort to start it rocking, and more effort to stop it once it’s started, because it has more angular momentum. A bow with its weight centred near the balance point takes less effort to rock from string to string.

With wooden bows this is what accounts for the fact that you can find two bows of seemingly similar quality, weight and balance point, but one costs £300 and the other £5,000. To make a bow with a light tip but still with enough spring you need very stiff wood, which is very rare. (The weight at the frog end is of less importance, because the bow maker can adjust the weight of the grip to match the weight of the tip and get the balance right, but everything depends on how thin he can plane the top 1/3 of the stick.)

Of course, with carbon bows that shouldn’t be an issue, the stiffness/weight ratio is much higher than even the best pernambuco, and the manufacturer has complete control over where they put the weight.

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

Weaving is a very simple bowing technique. Take the first few bars of Lord McDonald’s: https://thesession.org/tunes/507
I have changed the setting a bit, to: DGBG dGBG | cGBG AGEG | - I bow it so that I slur starting every other note. Slur two/slur two/slur two….
I slur in groups of two notes: the DG, then BG, then dG, BG, etc. The cG BG is tricky but you’ll get it.
That’s basically all there is to it. Keep it slow, steady, smooth. Slur when you cross strings. Try not to bow each string with a fresh bow. Pretty soon it will smooth out and won’t sound so clunky. There will be times when you bow the first note and slur the next two, and vary the pattern. Or not slur at all, or slur a triplet sometimes rather than bow each note.
For the Lark in the AM I would start with an up bow on the A and slur two with the up bow, then one down on the A, bow two then one, but then I’d often play a fat, juicy roll on the B and avoid the BGB. But not every time. The trick is to play it very slowly and smoothly. Let me know how it goes.

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

LM’s is the Irish equivalent of Leather Britches, the great American old time tune. With LB I played using the pump action rather than the weaving pattern of bowing. Allen Block used to teach bowing as the Oodlechunka, where in a reel you’d slur two note and then bow two notes, as oodle (slurred), chunk a (bowed). You should experiment with that pattern for a while though of course be ready to do something else depending on the phrasing. I find that singing the tune helps me find the best phrasing for it, and tells me when to slur and when not.
You will learn a lot about bowing by slowing down and trying to find a pattern rather than just winging it. It’s a part of focused, or deliberate, practise. I used to think the point was to collect tunes in my head. Now I try to perfect each tune, not for the session, but for my own pleasure. Jimmy Noonan, the great Boston fluter, once told me that all you need to know are three tunes -- but you need to know them really, really, really well. And Seamus Creagh told me that there is no such thing as practising too slowly.
A good bow makes everything much easier and much more obvious. The other thing to keep in mind is to use as little rosin as possible. I rosin once in the morning and that will do me for hours. Sometimes I’ll play the fiddle for days without using any rosin at all.

Re: Fiddlers - Bow help, please!

My advice would be not to take a notion that you’re going to buy a bow. Take your time. The problem with buying a bow is that if you try a load of bows, your mind will get clouded and you won’t be sure what you like and what you don’t, but buying one online and trying them is the other extreme, you won’t really get much choice there.
You could just use a bow some day and say “this is nice, this works for me”. Also, you may find that certain bows will bring out the sound better in your fiddle than others.
It’d be worth calling to Noel Burke or Gary Leahy. They know their stuff. They’d give good advice; they’ll notice the characteristics of the bows you seem to like, which will make ur search easier; for example, Gary Leahy spotted that I seem to like a light, but stiff bow, so instead of looking at hundreds of bows, you can come back on a different day with a fresh head and ask to see all of the light but stiff bows (or whatever your preference is) and pick your favourite, which maybe one of 5 or so, which makes your search far easier than trying to pick your favourite bow out of a load of them!
