One sharp, tune ends on G, so it’s in G, right? Er, no.

What about this one: https://thesession.org/tunes/812

Two sharps, tune ends on E. Um… E dorian? Not.

There are quite a few conundrums like this tune - Tomas O Cannain called them “tunes with complex tonality” and devised a method for assigning a “tonal centre” to them taking other factors than the last note into account.

Re: Looking at the first note to tell the Key.

For tunes that resolve on their tonal centre (most, but not all do) you need to consider the last note AND the key signature in order to determine the key and the mode of the tune.

For example, a tune with a tonal centre of “D” with two sharps in the key signature would be D-Major, whereas a tune with a tonal centre of “E” with two sharps in the key signature would be E-Dorian .

That’s not the whole story though, as some tunes use hexatonic or pentatonic scales, or they might consist of a mixture of keys and modes.

“What I want to know is why some people say that a tune is in the key of the first note when it’s not.”

Well, SOME tunes do begin on their keynote - but certainly not all. It’s much more likely that the last note of the tune will be the keynote - but even that is not invariably true, as some tunes do not resolve on their tonal centre at the end of the tune.

Whoever it was who told you that all tunes begin on their keynote is just plain wrong!

A great many tunes end on the key note, and some start on the key note, so either of those methods will sometimes work but not always. The sure-fire way to identify the keynote is to hum or play a drone through the tune - the note you land up humming will be the key note.

Even if the tune is actually in E dorian, if you ask the key you will normally be told E minor, because the root chord is Em, the mode isn’t that important.

Re: Looking at the first note to tell the Key.

“What I want to know is why some people say that a tune is in the key of the first note when it’s not.”

Because they don’t understand the whole concept of ‘key’ - even though they may be a very fine musician, with a very keen musical intelligence. The thing is, music ‘theory’*, as taught in the Western Art Music canon, is just a construct, designed to explain rationally something that is, by its very nature, not rational; there are, no doubt, other such constructs in other traditions. But, by whatever method you are taught music, you need to have an intuitive (irrational, if you like) understanding of it in order to play or appreciate it. So, a musician who has learned without the aid (or hindrance) of music theory, when playing with one that has an understanding of theory, and uses it as their ‘language’, will naturally try to speak the same language, but does not necessarily ‘get’ what the terms really mean.

*The OED’s first definition of ‘theory’ is as follows:
“A supposition or a system of ideas intended to explain something, especially one based on general principles independent of the thing to be explained”

i.e. not a statement of fact

The second definition could be said to be more pertinent to the case of music theory:
“A set of principles on which the practice of an activity is based”

The practice of music, however, is clearly not necessarily *based* on music theory, since there are plenty musicians that manage very well without any knowledge of theory.

Re: Looking at the first note to tell the Key.

“Ross didn’t tell me it’s in Eminor because the tune starts on E but because the whistler plays it on a E whistle.”

I’m dieing laughing. Not at you Anna, but at the situation. This is a story for the books!

Music Theory can be pretty complicated because music is such a big idea. But let me try to help simplify key signatures for you, from a pianist perspective.

Like anything musical, key signatures can be subjective, but for the most part, especially in western music, they tend to be pretty straightforward and definite.

What you wanna try, is to find where the music is centered. That’s where the idea of looking at the first or last note comes from. But you can also do this by looking at what notes are in the tune’s scale. That’s when you look at how many sharps or flats it has. This combination will usually tell you what key a tune is in. But there are more ways to find where a tune is centered.

You can also listen to the first phrase, or the first bar. For example, The Foxhunter’s Reel. In A, it starts on E, comes down to C#, and then down to A. That phrase is a broken A chord. In the next phrase, you hear an F#. So now you know there’s a C and an F#. You don’t have to wait until you hear a G#(the last sharp in the key of A) because the tune is already centered in A with it’s broken A chord. Even though you’ve only heard two sharps(like in D), it doesn’t start or finish on D, and none of it’s phrases end on D, so you know it’s not in D.

Another way is to “test the tonic”. You do this play playing a drone of the note you think the tune is in while playing the tune. If it sounds “in-tune”, or stable, then you’ve found the tonic(because every scale degree can resolve back to the tonic). But if it sounds off and “out of tune/key” at any point, it’s not in that key.

Try it with a tune you know, first with the key that it’s in, then with a key that it’s not even close to. It’s an intriguing exercise.

Re: Looking at the first note to tell the Key.

I encounter this type of situation on a regular basis playing with a fiddler and a cittern player. The cittern player, from an English trad background uses an open tuning (DADAD) and relies heavily on his capo for changing key; so, to him, *key = capo position*. (He also playes Anglo concertina, on which he mainly plays up and down the rows, so *key = row*). The fiddler, on the other hand, comes from a classical violin background and to him (quite correctly, some would say), the key is determined by the key signature - either major or relative minor. Both are valid perspectives but the trouble is, neither is able to understand the other’s perspective. So Mr. Fiddle calls out “G!” and Mr. Cittern puts his capo at the 5th fret, but senses that something isn’t right and looks puzzled. Then I call out “D!” (it’s in D Mixolydian) and Mr. C. takes off his capo; but Mr. F. thinks it’s a key change and moves up a string; and I don’t know what to play.

So, the best tactic is for me to get in there before either Mr. F. or Mr. C. shouts a key to one another, tell Mr. C. where to put his capo and/or tell Mr. F. what note to start on.

Mr. C. and Mr. F. both play good music.
