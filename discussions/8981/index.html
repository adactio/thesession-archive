Re: An old fiddle

Nothing wrong with old fiddles. I’ve got a stack of them at this point, although, perverse creature that I am, the reason I have them is make them look newer.

Thing is these fiddles look old because. . . wait for it, wait for it:

They are *old.*

They’ve been played, knocked around and generally abused for 50 to a couple hundred years and aquired their “aging” by undesired accident, oxidation, UV damage, etc.. Their varnish needs cleaning, polishing and touching up. Their cracks need mending. Some small number of them even require that their original finishes actually be stripped and replaced with something that is far better than they ever had when new in the first place (heresy, I know, but true nonetheless. The truth is often heresy).

The idea of spending many hours building a finely crafted instrument and then spending two or three times more hours cracking it, giving it dings and making it a painting of an old fiddle (and raising its price by the “value” of those hours) is anethema to me.

I do not understand the current mania for “antiqued” fiddles, but it’s reached the point where it’s hard to aquire a new fiddle that isn’t antiqued other than by special order, or buying in the white and doing it yourself.

What makes an old fiddle desirable is not that it *looks* old, but that it *is* old, and, ironically, the most desirable, and thus most expensive, old fiddles are the ones that *don’t* look old.

If you want a new fiddle that looks like it’s old and might be worth ten thousand, buy something beat up, but if you want a new fiddle that looks old and like it might be worth ten million, well, you’ll have to buy a new fiddle that looks new, just like an action figure that hasn’t been played with is more desirable and valuable than one that has.

There is one perfctly good reason to make a new fiddle that looks old. To duplicate an existing old fiddle for its owner. Lete is reputed to have so exactly duplicated a fiddle that it was even able to fool its owner.

But this isn’t done for fashion. It’s done for the same reason paste jewelry is produced, so the real thing can sit in a safe deposit box while it is the copy that is put at risk and many’s the violinist who has had the tone of his Strad or Guarneri praised to high heaven when what he was actually playing was something he took out of the shrink wrap last Tuesday.

If Rachel wants an old fiddle that’s ok. I wouldn’t spend so much time caring for them if I didn’t like old fiddles myself and if I were in the Highlands area I’d be happy to supply her with one at a quite reasonable price.

But if she came to me asking for an old *looking* fiddle I’d be obliged to supply her with an actual old fiddle, because all of my new fiddles look new and will remain looking new until they become “antiqued” in the proper fashion. By time and accident. I’d advise maintaining to look reasonably new though. A new looking fiddle is a happy fiddle.

Oh, i guess I’ll point out that in the case of the “paste” fiddle it doesn’t actually cost the owner anything for the dupe, even though it’s expensive.

The quarter mil and up fiddles are invariably insured. A fiddle used for performing is at high risk of loss and so the premiums can be pretty hefty.

So. . .you put it in the insurance contract that you’ll only play the real thing so many times and only under certain conditions and otherwise use the dupe, which reduces the risk of loss, which reduces the premium.

The reduction in premium pays for the dupe and everyone’s happy with the deal.

Re: An old fiddle

“does it drive you nuts when old fiddles are kept in bank vaults and on walls and stuff?”

Well, it drives me nuts when jewelry is kept in a bank vault, I mean, what’s the point of owning it and wearing paste? The whole point of jewelry is to look at it and say, “Ooooooooo, shiney!” So yeah, a fiddle rankles a bit.

Museums don’t bother me that much, especially if they’re the more perspicaious sort that have them played now and again, which is becoming more common. I may love old fiddles, but I don’t belong to The Cult of The Old Fiddle. A lot of the old stuff really is more valuable as a historical object than as an instrument. In fact, a lot of the old fiddles, stuff that goes for hundreds of thousands, aren’t as good instruments as something you can pick up off the shelf for a couple grand. It’s the dirty little secret of the commercial fiddle trade.

But as an example of a fiddle it may well be “priceless.”

So go ahead, put it in the museum. It belongs there.

Stuff on the wall? Well, depends, there really are a lot of old factory student instruments that would simply be a diservice to put into the hands of a student. Better to have them on the wall and give the poor kid something that won’t scar him for life.

So it makes no sense to have something decent, or that could be made decent, hanging on the wall with nobody playing it. Pass it on to someone who can use it as it’s supposed to be used and put a good looking piece of junk on the wall. Shouldn’t cost you more than twenty bucks.

I do actually “waste” a lot of time on some of these. They’re the ones I learn and experiment on. If I screw up it’s no real loss. And sometimes I get surprised. What’s a crap violin may turn out to be a decent octave fiddle or something, if you can deal with things like a nonstandard scale length.

The case that broke my heart was a local antique shop. Big place in an old dept. store building. Maybe 4000 square feet. The owner “collected” old fiddles. No junk. Small workshop up to master built. Mostly American made because those went cheap at the estate auctions.

But he didn’t do anything with them. Didn’t play, wouldn’t fix them, refused to sell a single one.

And he didn’t take care of them. They were all over his store. Hundreds of them stuffed wherever he could find the space. He actually used them to prop up dressers missing legs and stuff.

Broke my heart to go in there.

One day the whole sheebang just up and disappeared. Big empty storefront. Have no idea whatever happened to all of those fiddles, but you could have opened a museum on American fiddle making over the past 200 years with what he had.

“Making a violin duplicate in looks is not that hard…”

Making one to fool a concert audience isn’t that hard. Making one that can fool a casual observer when the two are lying side by side on a table is rather harder.

Making one that can fool an owner who plays is a feat so extraordinary it challanges believability. Try duplicating just a fingerboard that precisely.

“. . . you can’t duplicate the sound of an old instrument..even one that is only 75-100 yrs old…it takes playing in to do that…”

Ah, time and technology have moved on. They can do that by machine now. It can take many weeks to accomplish, but the machines don’t get tired. They keep at it 24/7 until the job is done.

“One of the best things anybody ever said to me was to buy a violin that you can afford to enjoy….”

Good, solid advice indeed, and maybe back it up with one you can afford to throw away. I don’t know how people stand playing with the underwriters looking over their shoulders and cringing all the time.
