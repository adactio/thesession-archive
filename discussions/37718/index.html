↳ Posted by irishfiddleMA on July 30th, 2015.

Re: Oak tree for inspection version tres.

I wouldn’t try learning how to do rolls at this stage, that will only make your timing worse. But one thing that can help you get a feel for the timing of a tune is to break the long notes down into quavers - so instead of the dotted crochet you play three quavers (all on the same note). Once you’ve played it that way a few times and got a feel of how the tune fits the beat, then you can run the quavers together and play the tune as it should be.

Re: Oak tree for inspection version tres.

Arthur, if it’s 3 equal notes the count is always 3. There are several ways to play through the duration, but regardless of whether it’s a jig (6/8) or reel (4/4) 3 quavers, a dotted crotchet, or anything equivalent they are each the same. 3 = 3.

However, if it’s easier for you to count 3 quavers in a jig that’s grand. It means you recognize the count of three & don’t get an uncomfortable feeling that it leaves you hanging. And you’re right that’s easier, for learning, to do in jigs than reels.

The count is the same. So learn to count. But the beat & emphasis change.

A metronome can help, but your playing will only get better if you develop your sense of awareness of differences between counting in 2s, 3s &4s, both with & without a metronome

This is not rocket science, but it does require some concentration, overcoming personal embarrassment barriers, work, and musicality. Start from the beginning, go very slowly, consider the musical phrase, recognize the timing and dynamics. Think of the tune as a poem, with no words, that expresses an idea. Don’t skip over any portion of the tune - each part is equally important…. and learn to count. If you do this, life will be easier, music will flow, and friends will be at hand.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIbRfTUWMg4

Jigs:

Sean Ryan’s
Morrison’s

Reels:
Father Kelly’s
Lady Anne Montgomery
Star of Munster
Buckley’s

…among a multitude of tune options. The Oak Tree is simply too complicated to navigate when counting is a problem. Within The Oak Tree is the argument about when to play a C nat/vs C#, full rolls, triplets, key change, descending and ascending third intervals, and the constant question of where (and how often) to use the G#. I suggest putting it away for a year and coming back to it after other tunes are mastered. Discovering it will be a joy.

All musicians do things like this, play individual quavers, add triplets, change notes as long it still adds up to a whole measure - nothing more, nothing less.
ABce a3e
ABce a2ae
ABce aaae
ABce abae
ABce agae
ABce (3aaa ae
ABce ~a3e
ABce (3aba fe
ABce (3aba ge
…and so on. Not all possibilities sound great, and if you choose to play this tune with other people there’s a chance that other musicians will think “What the h*ll was that?!” - but the rhythm is intact and the notes add up.

Re: Oak tree for inspection version tres.

@Arthur, I’ve just been listening again to your various versions of this tune, and there’s one thing that’s common in all of them. I think you need to take a step back here …

It’s your bowing - it doesn’t matter what tune, what bowing pattern you use, or even what genre you play in, it’s something that needs looking at, at a very basic level. I’ve posted on this subject before, but here it is again.

As to sounding mechanical : all your notes sound the same in terms of tone, volume, accent. There’s no *bite* at the beginning of each note, and there should be. That degree of bite is what gives each note its accent and presence.

Experienced players just ‘do’ this - they’re probably not even aware that they are doing it, but they are. It’s something that just happens over time, through playing and listening to others, and soaking up whatever music it is they are playing. It’s this one single thing that all your favoured players do as a *bare minimum* in getting the sound that they get. It’s the repetition of this in varying phrases that gives a tune ‘lift’, ‘lilt’, or wtf-ever.

For the moment, just forget about tunes, styles and everything else. Have a look at this thread here :

https://thesession.org/discussions/34227

If you only just bow down and up with the same pressure, it is analogous to sawing through a piece of wood. You need to keep constant pressure on the saw, otherwise it will stop and snag in the wood. You don’t have any choice there.

As you play notes in a tune, you have a choice. You need to put pressure on the bow at the beginning of the note, and release the pressure immediately for the duration of the note. That’s the ‘accent’ at the beginning, and the quieter part for the rest of the bow travel. Optionally, you can do this on the return bow.

You could try a few phrases of any tune, just to see if you can get the ‘bite’.
