How do you like your triplets?

There was a lively fiddle thread a few pages back about reel bowing, and the subject of triplets came up - where on the bow they were played.

This is not about that, but the triplets themselves. Do you play / like them played so you hear the 3 distinct notes, or do you prefer just to hear the rhythm? From player to player the sound varies a lot - sometimes the notes are crystal clear, other times the bow travel is so small, and the bow pressure so great that the string is “scrudded” or “choked” - in other words, the notes are killed before they can sound, but you can still hear the distinct punctuation in the phrase.

I like to sound all the notes in the triplet (assuming the same note repeated 3 times) and of course if the triplet notes were, eg BDB on the 2nd string, then they would need to be sounded.

Does anyone have a liking for playing triplets in a particular way?

Re: How do you like your triplets?

I used to just do crunchy “cat-sneeze” triplets, but listening more to players who played every note in the triplet cleanly got me to playing them that way. Now I have a range of different triplets available and use whichever fits the tune and the circumstances best. Though generally now I tend to most often play three clean, distinct notes, while still keeping the tight, percussive effect.

Same with rolls: I first learned to do rolls from Kevin Burke, so I tended toward that poppy and percussive sound. Later, Brian Conway got me doing more evenly timed, open, notey rolls, and I really like doing them that way to match when another player (most often a whistler, fluter, or piper) does them that way.

What makes sense, to me, is to have a broad range of options so I can match another player’s feel, or match the feel to suit the tune (each one with it’s own personality).

Re: How do you like your triplets?

Variety is the spice of it. I don’t understand why someone would have a preference?

(Also, it’s worth noting that what is commonly referred to in this music as a triplet is actually much closer - if not almost exactly - a couple of semi quavers and a quaver. It’s worth akin the distinction so as to realise the difference between what is actually a triplet in a hornpipe. )

It’s way way more interesting that the either/or of clean or crunch. As with all these little minutia of articulations commonly - but wrongly - called “decorations”, it’s all in the timing.

I’ve just been listening to the way Matt molly plays The Groves on his first album. It’s a really great examination of the specifics of the timing and how it can be pushed, pulled and subdivided.

It’s a hornpipe, but the way he plays it in that lovely languid way where there’s almost no swing at all subtly accentuates what swing there is.

A heavily swung hornpipe has notes unsubtly paired as long and short, and the long note moves towards being twice the length of the second note. So the standard skipperty hornpipe bits divide the first note in two and the second note falls in the same place it did without the skipperty bit. But the way Matt plays it straighter makes his skipperty bits (listen to the fourth bar of the first part) approximate real triplets. The standard western definition of a triplet being an even division of four into three. It’s a very beautiful cascade of subtle time shift of, in this case, three over two.

But there’s more. Listen to the second and third parts and how the long rolls languidly delay the beat and how the short rolls on the down beat are based around the two semi-quavers and a quaver thing. And then in the last two parts how the normal hornpipe skipperty bits are just that little bit longer than two semi-quavers and a quaver thing, but still no where near an even triplet.

Then back into the second time through. He puts in some extra notes in the cascade bit which means he has to speed up the decent as he gets to the end and bangs back in bang on time for the next bit. Pure magic.

And then the last time through the last part, instead of the two semi-quavers and a quaver thing, he put is a really short roll at the front end and divides the rest up more evenly into threes. Very clever. Very artistic. Very fluid, Masterful. And most importantly, in complete control.

Re: How do you like your triplets?

I think that technique becomes less important with regards specifically to the kinds of music in the world that require less technique.

And of all the different kinds of music in the world, Irish diddley music is pretty near the bottom with regards to the amount of technique that is required.

It’s not at the bottom - the dreaded blooze springs to mind - but the gulf between playing Christmas Eve masterfully and Paganini’s Caprice No. 5 masterfully, for example, in terms of technique is enormous.

And what interests me is that because all musics equally “require” as much of a keen an ability to listen, knowledge, artistry, personality and invention as one can possibly muster, the ratio of these prerequisites against mere technique when the amount of measurable technique required is relatively low, is much much higher.

What’s an awful musician? Not necessarily poor technique, but certainly a lack of any one of the necessary: knowledge, artistry, personality, invention and keen ability to listen.

And where the technique required is relatively low, the chances of excellent knowledge, artistry, personality, invention and keen ability to listen outweighing a slight deficiency in required technique is higher. You see this all the time with diddley players, especially the older ones. Their techniques may well be under the general standards of the youngsters about nowadays, but that is irrelevant to how good that can be.

I’m not dissing technique per say, I’m saying that one of the characteristics of being a really really good musician is the knowledge of where your acquired technique becomes, at best, redundant, and at worst, a distraction.

Re: How do you like your triplets?

I’m certain that the reason when someone like Yehudi Meuhin comes along and are amazed by the speed of rolls, bowed triplets etc. it’s because they try and do it and can’t do it straight away. Through their years of study they have gained such technical mastery of their instrument that they expect to be able to do anything the world of music can throw at them instantly. But I’ll bet you that if they stuck at it for even only a couple of hours they’d get it no bother. In the scheme of hard things to do on a violin, rolls and bowed skipperty things are a piece of plss.

What they’d find much harder though would be fluidity of the phrasing. This can only come with many many hours of listening hard. The whole art of Irish diddley music is making personal artistic invention with little skill. So yes, Diddley music is easy. All it takes is the application of listening long and hard. And anyone who thinks that it’s not easy has really only just not listened enough.

So yeah, people new to the music might think it’s hard, but you have to remember that it’s easy to get bamboozled into thinking that just because you don’t understand something, it must be hard. A lot of these new people stick at it and very soon get the revelation that’s it’s actually pretty easy and they then can become good players.

But there are those who cling to the idea that it’s hard. They often develop reasonable technique through dedicated practice, but will never become half decent players … mainly through fear, but occasionally through the opposite of fear, where they congratulate themselves too much for achieving something that they are under the impression is difficult.

I’ve never met a decent player who didn’t find the music easy. (though some keep up a pretence of it not being easy out of a daft false modesty thing)
