Is it me or the tune?

Ok. We’ve all agreed (or disagreed) that much of this traditional music is quite easy. :-) Of course, some of it is extremely difficult but we don’t have to (and some argue that we shouldn’t) play it, if that’s the case. However, I find that there are so many tunes which are very easy to learn apart from, maybe, two or three notes-usually in the second half. It just seems natural to play something else at that point and I have to go over and over that part until it sinks in. Even then, I often have to consciously think about what I’m doing whenever I’m playing that particular tune.

Does anyone else suffer from this problem? Perhaps the tune is badly composed or I’m trying to learn an inaccurate version. Maybe, whoever wrote the tune is just trying to separate the sheep from the goats (Scott Skinner was particularly twisted this way) or, maybe, I’m not understanding this music as well as I think.

If this happens to you, what do you do in a session? If you’re like me, you’ll just play something that’ll fit in, so should we worry about it too much? :-)
Any thoughts on the matter?

John

Re: Is it me or the tune?

You’re not alone JohnJ.

I have often found there is a bar in the B part that takes more work to get playing it right than the whole of the rest of the tune. I spend a few minutes working on that bar on my own at home and soon enough I can either play it as written or find a suitable workround.

The answer to what to do in a session could be: “Look at the rule which says Don’t play a tune if you don’t know it.” But if that seems too harsh an approach then play something that will fit in. Me and the boy often change a note here or there to make something work better. It would be a strange “traditional” musician who would pick you up for doing that.

Another thing that sometimes happens when you are attempting a new tune - especially if you are working it out laboriously from the dots - is that because you start at the beginning every time you play (of course), you inevitably reach a point where you can play the A part well enough, but not the B part, simply because you haven’t played the B part as many times as the A.

A few more times through and Bob’s your uncle.

Re: Is it me or the tune?

I think that in some cases the problem with learning the B part is to do with the way in which the tune is being learned.

What can happen is that a lot of concentration is put in to learning the A part, and when that’s done, the B part is looked at separately, almost as if it’s a different tune. The mental link between the two parts of the tune isn’t being established properly.

One way of tackling this problem is to learn the A part two bars at a time in the “question-and-answer” manner, and then when you’ve done the final question-and-answer of the A part start on the B part, again learning 2 bars at a time in the same question-and-answer manner. BUT - and this is the important bit - you go through the A part each time before you go into the B part. This way you’re building up the entire tune two bars at a time and reinforcing the connection between the A and B parts each time you learn some more of the B part. You’re also perfecting (you hope!) the A part as well.

Don’t forget that although repetition is the best way of learning tunes it is also a marvellous way of permanently embedding mistakes if you’re not thinking and listening all the time to what you’re doing!

Re: Is it me or the tune?

I’ve always been a firm believer in exercising your left hand fingers
in order to be able to play tunes properly. By exercising I mean
violin exercises that work the fingers in different ways. (They don’t
have to be heavy-duty - in fact, some of them are quite simple). Why waste time on a tune, learning to make your fingers work properly instead of developing and remebering the tune itself?

In I.T. most of the tunes follow pretty well-worn patterns, and where you stumble is playing a slightly alien pattern if you haven’t heard it before. A simple example : I bet you’ve all being playing tunes with fingering patterns like 3-1-0, 3-2-1, 0-1-3, 1-2-1 etc etc until you’re blue in the face. You don’t stop to think about it because you are listening to the music you are making. Now - what happens if you’re required to play 3-1-2-4, 2-4-1-3 or 3-1-4-2 up to speed? Bet you’ll notice a difference.

As funny Michael Gill mentioned, there are a few phrases like the above one of these in the ‘Salamanca’ (just one example). That’s why so many people take the ‘easy’ option of a more common phrase. No harm done so long as it fits the rest of the tune (and the version everyone else is playing). Big muck-up if it isn’t.

That’s only one aspect of it, of course. There can be various other
tricky things in a tune that veers away from the common patterns.

By the way, I say ‘funny’ Michael because I still laugh at one of his posts, where he asked if there was an “Amazing Speeder-Upper” for Martin Hayes’s playing!

Re: Is it me or the tune?

I think a lot of the changed settings are down to playing the same tune on different instruments.

For many years I played ITM on the piano - possible due to long fingers and a high pain threshold. Some of the jumps can be very dangerous. Relearning these tunes on the box suddenly makes sense - many of the large jumps disappear due to the non-sequential order of the keys.

Could it be that tunes which are hard to play were written for (or written down from) another instrument?

Having struggled with one or two difficult phrases in certain tunes I managed to score a lesson from a local expert - and discovered that she played those phrases differently. Usually the changes were slight, but made the fingering much simpler. The overall tune is undeniably the same, but the playing is a lot easier. I suspect the main source of this is the ease of moving from one string to the next allows for jumps of a fixed number of notes and back again to be made very rapidly, wheras a keyboard instrument generally doesn’t allow this.
