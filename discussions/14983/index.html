Calling Second/Third Generation Migrants

I’d be interested to hear how people have dealt with that part of ‘Irishness’ that has been handed down to them, while growing up or living permanently in another country.
I’m a third generation migrant to the UK, 75% Irish. I’ve always drawn strength from identifying with my Irish-born parents and grandparents, even if this meant remaining something of an outsider here. At the same time, I have always tried not to idealise that Irishness. I attribute both my best and worst traits to my heritage (I might expand on that if this develops into a civilised discussion :) ).
Would I be playing the music now if it weren’t for that allegiance- difficult to say, but I know a certain sense of belonging drives me on.
Perhaps it easier now owning and being proud of one’s Irish roots? Maybe there is even a certain street cred in claiming Irish descent- certainly not the case in the UK half a century ago.
So how about you? Has your receding Irishness been a help or a hindrance, a source of pride or an albatros? Would you rather have been something sensible (Scandinavian, for example) :) Have you only recently come out of the closet? As I say, I’d be interested to hear how other second or third generation people have lived/come to terms with their Irishness, and how this has evolved over the years.

I am Irish by marriage to a Red-head with family coming from Donegal and Maough. But Herself’s family arrived in the US about 1865.

It has been an interesting experience as the “OutLaw” in the family and watching Herself become increasingly Irish centric with age. Her parents (second generation born in the US) were very “American” when we started dating. Now there Grampa Mac sports the McFadden coat of arms, reminisces about his once red hair and even sways from his traditional Miller Light to Guinness, Smithwick or Harp.

Strangely, our Kids all think they are Irish and the baby is even seriously dating someone second generation.

Being from the same neighborhood, when we were growing up the same Chicago neighborhood, I was aware that there was alot of pressure on the Irish to play down their heritage. But that seems to be changing. The change seemed to coincide when in ’74 we moved into a neighborhood where the Irish Catholics had run up against very self-righteous and anti-Irish Baptist Protestants in the early 20th century. So the new neighborhood’s Irish were much more galvanized in their Irish Identity than in our old neighborhood.

And they even appreciate Polish accordion players like me-at least twice a year (Christmas and St. Pats) so they must be very ecumenical.
