Re: sheet music for wooden flute

Steve,
If you are in America, start with http://www.ossianusa.com/.
I would recommend “O‘Neill’s Music of Ireland,” the big yellow book that is probably the biggest single source of Irish tunes. She might enjoy the O’Carolan tunes, as they have a very classical flavor.
I also like “100 Essential Irish Session Tunes” by David Mallinson, which is first of a series of books of popular session jigs and reels. For slow aires, I like “Traditional Slow Airs of Ireland,” compiled by Tomas O Canainn. This should be bought with the companion CD or tapes, as it is difficult to notate aires on paper well.
These are just my favorites, but there are lots and lots out there, and I am sure you will get more recommendations as well (and maybe a few opinions about how my choices aren’t the best!).
Your gift is very thoughtful, and as a classically trained musician, I am sure she will appreciate some written music to get started with (although as many will tell you, it is no substitute for listening).
Best wishes for your upcoming wedding!

Re: sheet music for wooden flute

Sounds like a few folks don’t understand the structure of a fake book. The term “fake” is a bit of a misnomer. What it really means is that there are few or no dynamic markings or any other kind of indication of how the tune should be played. It is the bare-bones structure of a tune. The Fiddlers Fakebook is useful to anyone playing a trad. The musical notation is not slanted towards flute. In fact, in the foreword, it is noted that the notation for the tunes is taken from the recordings noted, many of which do not feature fiddle. The notation one finds in the Fiddlers Fakebook is essentially the same as what you will find for the same tune in other books, although I find that Fiddlers usually does a better job.

While I’m at it, let me also recommend a book I just got at the FolkLife Festival in Seattle, Washington. The book has 625 tunes that are played at the sessions there. I knew it would be valuable when I saw that it had almost every tune I know, which leads me to believe that the tunes I don’t know that are contained in the book are worth learning if I want a better session repertoire. I am not at home as I write this, so I can’t tell you the name of the book. It is essentially a home-grown effort, although well printed. If anyone is interested, I will post the details in this thread on another day.

Re: sheet music for wooden flute

Over the years here, plenty of people have echoed what Jode says about learning tunes from the dots--that it’s harder to make them your own, and to make them stick in your memory than when learning tunes by ear.

I have not found that to be true myself, and I wonder at the difference. I can sight read single melody lines pretty fast, but I also tend to pick up tunes by ear pretty fast. In either case, my focus is on getting the tune into my head so I can play it from aural memory. If I later find it hard to recall a tune in detail, I find myself mentalling returning to the source--either playing back in mymind’s ear how the person I learned it from played it, or conjuring up a picture in my mind’s eye of the sheet music. Both strategies work well for me, so I enjoy relying on both aural and visual cues to recall tunes.

That said, some tunes are easier to remember than others--but for me at least, it has little or nothing to do with the type of source I learned them from.

Years ago, someone gave me a copy of the Fiddler’s Fakebook as a present. I gradually played through nearly every tune in it. But I didn’t learn any Irish tunes from it--in fact, the only tune I play even close to the setting that’s in the fakebook is West Fork Gals.

And I’ve often gone back to my own transcriptions to learn a tune, which comes with a strong aural memory of the source I transcribed it from. Re: sheet music for wooden flute

Ailin, don’t take it so personally. I’m allowed to dislike FF and crow about it if I want. I don’t care whether you agree with me.

Of course, I have my reasons: Fiddler’s Fakebook and O‘Neill’s are both heavily slanted toward fiddle. The ornamentation in the Krassen edition (the most widely available version) of O’Neill’s is fiddle specific (Miles Krassen is a fiddler), and the title of Fiddler’s Fakebook speaks for itself. (It also features fiddle-specific ornamentation, which *are*--contrary to Ailin’s protestations--slanted to one instrument--the fiddle. Just look at the discographies--most of the Irish tune transcriptions are allegedly based on the playing of fiddlers.)

Consider that:
1. Both include tunes in keys or octaves out of the range of a keyless D flute.
2. Both include articulations (ornaments) inappropriate to wooden flute (rolls on c nats, rolls--not cranns--on D’s, etc.).
3. Both contain indications of bow slurs that are less than helpful, even for fiddlers, nevermind how a fluter new to Irish music might read them. If you play a tune from the Fakebook as marked, it will sound anything but “Irish.” The setting of Drowsy Maggie is at once bland and too cute.
4. Half or more of the tunes in the Fiddler’s Fakebook aren’t even in the Irish repertoire--why spend money on a book full of tunes you don’t want to know?

So why recommend these for a fluter new to Irish music? Especially when there are much better sources (not even counting the notion of cds and other aural sources). I just think that FF and O’Neill’s are poor choices in this situation. I’ve heard fiddlers whose playing reflects how steeped they are in the Fakebook--it’s not a pleasant experience, unless you like Americana folk muzak.

I can think of many better written sources, including the two I mentioned above, Timber and Grey Larsen’s book, plus the multi-volume Breathnach collection (which includes tunes transcribed from the playing of Irish fluters), 121 Session Tunes by L.E. McCullough (with cds of each tune played on whistle), and the flute-centric database of tunes at the wooden flute site. Of course all of these have their own foibles, and none are as helpful as a tall stack of cds, or better yet, time playing face to face with a good Irish fluter. But they’re far more central to the tradition and repertoire than the Fakebook. O’Neill’s, on the other hand, is worth owning, but it helps to learn how to ignore much of the inky mess orbiting the melodies.
