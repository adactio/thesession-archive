Changing your instrument

Has anyone here ever switched instruments? Not as in learned a new instrument, but stopped playing one entirely to learn another. If so, what made you do it, and how successful was it?

Re: Changing your instrument

I have switched instruments, both in the way you mentioned, fully stopping one to play another, and in benching one for years to focus on another. The full stop I did was from the 5-string banjo to the bouzouki, and the reason was that my instructor at the time had wanted me to start on an accompaniment instrument to work in with a student group. At the time the whistle was my main Irish instrument, and the bouzouki interested me just as much if not more than the banjo. It was a successful transition, and I had hardly started learning the banjo.

In a slightly different way, I’ve also benched an instrument for years to learn another. There have been a number of reasons, but a lot of it boiled down to time constraints and having to make the hard decision of where to focus my practice time. For example, I focused primarily on the mandolin for a couple years at one point to open up other styles of music, since the whistle can be rather limited in terms of musical styles. These days I more or less play everything I’ve benched through the years but some definitely get more attention.

Re: Changing your instrument

I switched from highland pipes to uilleann pipes. I stopped playing and sold my highland pipes to focus completely on uilleann pipes. Started highland pipes because wanted to play “celtic” music and thought to myself that these two kinds of pipes couldn’t be too different and I would learn uilleann pipes as soon as a set found me.

Flash forward a few years, start learning pipes and whistle (along with Irish music) concurrently. I stopped playing highland pipes because I found Irish music holds worlds more interest and passion for me than Scottish music. If your practice time is a zero sum game, it only made sense in my case to stop one. It was funny coming from highland to uilleann piping because there is a lot that is the same but probably more that isn’t (though I say that having only played these two instruments, along with whistle.)

I felt as if this background was a handicap on my playing, unlearning technique instead of just learning new, until a piper of considerable fame told me he finds that any exposure to playing any music can only increase your ability in the bigger picture, even if it seems frustrating/dis-synergizing sometimes.

In the future (1-20 years), I’d like to try tenor sax, fiddle, or even the Gaita (me gusta la fiesta) but for now I have my hands full with the drones and the chanter, not to mention the R-word.

Re: Changing your instrument

“no matter how much I wanted to play my whistle, I would still reach for one of the more familiar instruments (recorder) whenever I knew it would work for a particular tune”

That’s been around the Irish music scene forever, around here anyway: you’re watching an Irish trad band play, and there’s somebody playing whistle, yet… you see there at their feet, along with several whistles, a recorder, and you think to yourself “this person is a convert who still hasn’t fully committed to being a trad player” and sure enough for a song or two in their set they will pick up that recorder, and the Pure Drop onlookers will raise an eyebrow.

I know a guy who’s a pro jazz musician and he went through that same thing. He’s originally a sax guy, made his living playing sax his whole life, who at some point bought himself a clarinet. Though he’s an amazing musician and was sounding great on the licorice stick he would always take that darn sax out on stage. I think it was sort of a crutch, or a comfort thing, and it was big for him to feel confident enough to finally one set walk out on stage with JUST the clarinet! I gave him props of course saying how great he was sounding on the stick and nowadays most sets will be stick-only. (As a devotee of old-school New Orleans clarinet guys the stick, to me, is the “real deal”… alas Sidney Bechet disagrees.)

With ITM you always have the issue of “the tradition” viewing only certain instruments as being suitable vehicles for the tradition, and the perception that somebody isn’t committed to the tradition if they persist in playing non-trad instruments.

A different issue is spreading yourself too thin, like I did. Some have the talent to get great on as many instruments as they choose to pick up. These people don’t have reason to set any instrument aside. I’m not at that level, and to play at a decent level I have to put limits on what I try to do.

↳ Posted by Kilcash on April 15th, 2017.

Sorry to chime in so late in the thread….

“…concertina or accordion are much more mechanical instruments, fixed pitch more or less. So whilst they have their charms, you can’t quite play the music the same way as you might on fiddle, flute or pipes etc.”
I agree, roughly. I play flute, fiddle, and Anglo concertina. I don’t find that playing the Anglo concertina is mechanical, though it isn’t easy to be as sensitive as on the fiddle or flute. Accomplishment in one isn’t necessarily much easier than in another.
Flute fingering is very accessible and so effortless that it is easy to develop phrasing and ornamentation. Breath control makes the flute very responsive rhythmically. A keyed flute -- and focused practice -- makes playing in flat keys possible if seldom joyous. But generating a tone is much more difficult than on the fiddle or the concertina. The embouchure requires daily effort to maintain a minimally satisfying, focused tone.
The fiddle has easy fingering as well: one finger (or open string), for any note in first position. But intonation is challenging and tone production further complicates matters. The left hand does something very different from the right. But it is the most expressive of instruments, with the widest range of tonal color and expression. It can be played in any key with less effort than the simple system flute or the concertina. Its possibilities make it the most challenging.
The concertina generates its own tone and is always in tune. But the fingering is enormously challenging - much more so than either flute or fiddle. It is like playing a Rubik’s Cube. Nearly every tune requires a separate map. It can be awkward to play in Gm or in some of the flat keys. The bisonic nature is challenging but the Anglo concertina is insistently, compellingly rhythmic. And it keeps fluters and fiddlers honest.
My favorite instrument is what is in my hands at the moment, usually depending on the nature of the session or the group I am playing with. Occasionally I bring more than one instrument to the session. The flute – and especially the fiddle -- is more common in the US than in Ireland, where the concertina is ubiquitous. I am living in the US these days and mostly playing concertina.
Ultimately it is about the music and not about the equipment used to play it. I am happy to have lived long enough to have enjoyed playing different instruments. Doing so has helped me to understand each tune a little differently and, often, in more depth.

Re: Changing your instrument

“I haven’t been to sessions in the US but the flute and fiddle are pretty much the most common instruments at sessions here in Ireland”

I think it varies here between areas and between sessions.

For sure we can have distinctive instrumentation in our sessions and bands due to people here playing our own traditional music taking up ITM and bringing the instruments they already play. Especially back in the 1970s when loads of American players were getting interested in ITM I saw plenty of bands and sessions made up entirely of ATM instruments (fiddle, mandolin, guitar, both types of Appalachian dulcimers, even autoharp and standup bass). Noticeably lacking were instruments common in Ireland but not used in ATM (wooden flute, box, concertina, tenor banjo, uilleann pipes) and ATM instruments not thought appropriate to ITM (5-string banjo and lap steel). Of course there were numerous exceptions to this, I’m speaking about the major trends.

Dulcimers were so common in Irish bands and sessions here in the 1970s that Americans suddenly began to regard them as “Irish” or “Celtic” instruments; I was dismayed to see both lap and hammer dulcimers being made here with Celtic knotwork decoration, shamrocks, etc despite their coming to Appalachia from German Pennsylvania. (The dulcimer flow chart is more or less Europe > German Pennsylvania > Appalachia > American Folk Revival > American Irish/Celtic bands.)
