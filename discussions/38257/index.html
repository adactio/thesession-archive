Re: banjo tutorial book for 5 string banjo and for Irish Music wanted - urgent

Tom Hanway, the author of the Complete book, has several other books (with CDs) on playing Irish trad on the five string banjo with a new one coming out soon. Check out his discussions on the banjohangout.org about playing the music.

One of the issues with the five string banjo is that you have to be at a fairly high level of competence to play the music well on this instrument. The style that seems to suit Irish trad is a cross between chromatic and single string methods, both of which are more advanced techniques. Basic Scruggs style is too impressionistic to follow note for note, but if you use the methods Tom teaches you will eventually get to the point that you sound good.

Re: banjo tutorial book for 5 string banjo and for Irish Music wanted - urgent

You’d have a job playing Irish tunes with a plectrum on a 5-string banjo. The 1st and 3rd strings are a 5th apart, for a start. A 5-string really is a fingerpicking instrument.

If you’re a competent Keith-style 5-string player, then the instrument would suit Irish tunes perfectly.

I think the book that Nate linked to is very good. It’s not for a complete beginner, though - some experience of Keith-style is assumed, and it’s not the easiest of styles to learn.

That said, all the tunes are well notated in tab format, with left and right hand fingering on all of the tunes.

All the right-hand fingering is ITM :)

Max, I agree that the Hanway book would be a good one, though it will require a learning curve to get picking patterns down before you start. Starting with slow airs and O’Carolan compositions would be a fine way to go, seeing as he’s coming at it from the perspective of liking fantasy film music.

An alternative is to go the clawhammer route. Ken Perlman has some good books for melodic clawhammer approaches that include a mix of source cultures (Irish, old time, Canadian, New England). I have his “Clawhammer Style Banjo” book which is quite thorough. On the banjohangout website, the user John D has recorded a huge number of Irish tunes in a clawhammer style. http://www.banjohangout.org/myhangout/music.asp?ID=4931 is his profile. Clawhammer is mellower in sound than tenor or finger-picked styles, and pairs beautifully with whistle.

“If you’re a competent Keith-style 5-string player, then the instrument would suit Irish tunes perfectly.”

Could not disagree more. For the record, I’ve been playing clawhammer and/or Keith-style 5-string since 1970. And I’m a serious ITM fiddler.

One of the main difficulties, as has been stated in many threads, is tuning. Whether melodic clawhammer or Keith style, the notes can be played. What cannot be easily done is dealing with multiple tunings, and fast key changes, in the context of a session or a set of tunes.

Can someone put ITM on the 5-string? Sure. Does it work well? Many differ on this point. Is it “perfectly suited” to ITM? In no manner.

Ornamentation - he plays triplets in quite a few different ways. Sometimes just a hammer-on/pull-off on the same string, sometimes 3 notes on a single string, and even 3 notes across 3 strings.

He is able to change key from G to D quite quickly, using 2 Scruggs pegs and the 5th string capo (essential for any serious player, imo). He can get to A quickly too, by sliding the capo to the 2nd fret.

I know I am something of an enigma to a lot of folks, since I try not to hang out in online forums in general. First, I have never confined myself to playing Irish trad. (I was a folky and Delta-style bluesman before I got into bluegrass and the banjo.) I enjoy non-Irish Celtic music as much as I enjoy tunes from anywhere in Ireland. I play tunes from across the whole Celtic spectrum, from ex-Celtic lands and the Celtic Diaspora. Tunes from Wales, Scotland, Cape Breton, and Shetland have great appeal to me, and they translate well onto the 5-string, especially the hornpipes. I love Welsh harp music (e.g. Robin Huw Bowen), and so did Carolan, and he borrowed some of it, that’s right, which I talk about in great detail in my most recent collection, just out this year. 20 O’Carolan Irish Classics for 5-string Banjo and All Instruments (link above).

I’ve been playing traditional tunes since the mid-’80s and have mapped out a decent body of material in “Celtic fingerstyle” over four collections (220 transcriptions with Mel Bay Publications). These collections give basic outline to the tunes, for example, in Best-Loved Jigs and Reels (Beginning-Intermediate: tab, chords, recording, 68 tunes; 2012); and Best-Loved Airs and Session Tunes (Beginning-Intermediate: tab and standard, chords, recording; 32 tunes; 2013). In other collections I use either bare bones or highly ornamented settings; Complete Book of Irish & Celtic 5-String Banjo (Intermediate: tab and recording; 100 tunes; 1998); and 20 O’Carolan Irish Classics (Intermediate: tab and standard, chords, fretting- and picking-hand fingerings, recording; 20 tunes; 2015).

I learned tunes by hanging out at sessions, staying up late, camping on hurling pitches in Miltown Malbay (Clare), sleeping on kitchen floors, recording tunes literally in the field, seeking out the best sessions, also the best company and the best pint. I moved to Ireland in 2003 after losing my first wife, Kathleen, to cancer. I remarried, moved to Longford, and I’m still collecting tunes. (I’m not the ‘Longford Collector’; I collect tunes, not taxes!)

Mine is an all-embracing approach, and, sure, it’s not for everybody. One has to really want to play trad tunes to get anywhere with it on the 5-string, and the instrument has to be considered on its own terms, not copying the tenor banjo, which it simply cannot do, though it can get repertoire and techniques from it, e.g., stuttered triplets. (Enda Scahill is the man for Irish tenor banjo instruction, and I have his book: It’s great. I know Enda, he’s a buddy, and funny enough, he subbed for me when I was caught up stateside, playing my banjo parts (and then some) for The Carnival Brothers, a pop-burlesque troupe out of Dublin. (I listen closely to tenor banjo players like Enda and Gerry O’Connor, but I also listen to everybody else who doesn’t play a banjo.)

Okay, it’s not easy to play Irish or Celtic tunes, and one has to get inside the playing culture, or cultures. Honestly, I reckon it’s a lot easier than learning the uilleann pipes or the píob mhór (Great Irish Warpipes which are today’s Great Highland Bagpipe). It’s definitely no harder than learning Gaeilge (Irish) or any Celtic language.

I was a regular at Tony DeMarco’s session at Paddy Reilly’s on Second Ave, then the longest-running trad session in the New York City. Everybody passed through Paddy Reilly’s back in the day, Liz Carroll, Johnny Cunningham, Larry Nugent, Larry McCullough, Moya (Máire) Brennan, members of Clannad, and on and on….

I was lucky to pick up tunes and medleys from Fleadh Ceoil champs (Joanie Madden, Eileen Iver, Séamus Egan), a humble NY piper (Jerry O’Sullivan) and Sligo-style fiddlers (Tony DeMarco, Brian Conway, Andy McGann, Paddy Reynolds), also Johnny Cunningham (Scottish fiddler), Joe “Banjo” Burke, Linda Hickman, Patty Conway Furlong, and sean-nós singers whose names I cannot even remember. (Gabriel Donohue engineered the sessions for my first tune collection, also backing me on piano and guitar, for example, Carolan’s Draught. (This was for Complete Book of Irish & Celtic 5-String Banjo, so it’s a guide track, played just once.)

The most recent collection, 20 O’Carolan Irish Classics, combines traditional Gaelic Harp and Baroque decorations, e.g., graces, triplets, trills, arpeggios, turns, and appoggiatura-mordent combinations. I attempted to breathe life into the Gaelic classical melodies of Ireland, and a goal for students would be to move beyond the tune templates and guide tracks, to improvise decorations for themselves, alone, at sessions or in small ensembles.

Once one has a built up a repertoire of Celtic tunes, such as jigs, reels, hornpipes, slides, polkas, marches, slip jigs, O’Carolan tunes, and the like, attention now can turn to how to improvise and ornament the tunes without straying from the melody. Improvising in a Celtic context is different from improvising in, say, old-time, bluegrass, jazz, country, or rock. Playing context varies considerably from music culture to music culture, subculture to subculture, even within regional styles and between instruments in the same tradition.

Of course, one is always free to play across genres or develop a fusion-style, but that is not the goal of any of my Celtic collections. Call me “odd”, but I play tunes from all over the lot, and I don’t apologize for any of it. The more the merrier.

The aim of my teaching methods is to get students to play within living tradition, to play whole tunes from beginning to end – not to trample on them or make them conform to exoteric, foreign, or even classical conventions. Each rendition of a tune is a whole, partially improvised but also the result of previous renditions, each influencing further renditions which might inform a playing culture’s general concept of that tune. The best players don’t simply copy others’ versions, but add personal variations to them, making them their own.

From the Complete Book, “The mastery of Irish trad or any Celtic music is a lifelong endeavor. The pan-Celtic tradition is so vast that the words of an Irish folk singer come to mind: ‘Everyone has a bit of it and no one has it all’.”*

I am also largely self-taught, and I did and still do a lot of home study, putting tunes together in sets, digging deeper, refining tunes and learning regional variations. This cannot be done by logging onto online forums or social media. One has to travel to lots of different places, sometimes a one-pub-town, just a town away from the main event. One has to “break the bread” with the locals in order to find out what local style is all about.

I’ve sought out the best trad players on both sides of the Atlantic, also down under, and learned from many of them. Keeping “beginner’s mind” is one of the things I got from banjoist Bill Keith, my friend and mentor, now resting in peace. (Celtic fingerstyle banjo is not the same as melodic or Keith-style.)

I try to maintain high musical standards, but I am no trad purist, far from it. I wouldn’t rule out any tune, and I would never let myself be limited by others in my repertoire or approach to playing traditional tunes.

*Source: Robin Morton, Come Day, Go Day, God Send Sunday (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1973), 10. Quoted in James Cowdery, The Melodic Tradition in Ireland (Kent, Ohio: Kent State University Press, 1990), 79; also Tom Hanway, Complete Book of Irish & Celtic 5-String Banjo (Pacific, MO: Mel Bay Publications, Inc. 1998, 2002), 21.

Beir bua agus beannacht and Happy Christmas to all ~

Tom
