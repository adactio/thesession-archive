jig strumming question

Re: jig strumming question

If what you’re describing is what I think it is, you actually do sort of dampen the strings.
It’s done by striking the strings whit the pick while placing the heel of your hand on the strings to mute them. It can be a bit tricky to get the timing just right, but it is an interesting effect.
I describe it as more of a “chuck” sound.

Re: jig strumming question

Most trad guitarists use a down-up-down down-up-down strumming pattern most of the time on jigs to get a punchier rhythm, as opposed to the more natural down-up-down up-down-up. It’s a difficult change to get used to, since you’re not being the most efficient ( a wasted stroke between the two downs on 3 and 4) but it’s easier in the long run to keep a consistent 6/8 jig that does’nt turn into an awful shuffle beat! Easier to watch and show than to explain…..

Re: jig strumming question

Time to call out the Kings New Clothes.

1) DUDDUD breaks a *fundamental* hand percussion convention (least effort) on which the entire percussive “building block” reuse stack is founded. That may be of little concern to the humble guitarist, but the moment you move to (say) compound rhythms, you’re screwed. (Balkan rhythms are replete with oddball 6/8 rhythms. Were you a dabuka / darabukka player, the grouped DDs in your DUDDUD pattern would compromise your entire leaning path).
2) DUDDUD also conflicts with the generally alternating D/U flow of melodic play plectrum movements.
3) DUDDUD glues the downbeat to the beginning of the first and fourth beat in the bar, which -in particular for dancers- sounds (and feels) extraordinarily leaden. This is an aspect of play often completely overlooked by guitarists.
2) A DUDDUD rhythm is much, *much* more difficult to sustain cleanly over time, requiring correspondingly more practice at a crucial (early motivation) time in the learning curve.
4) The central DD move, if utilised, horribly cramps up the guitarist at the wrist -the fulcrum of relaxed play- with huge impact on planned or spontaneous variations.
5) Because the guitarist is so focussed on maintaining the pattern, the accompaniment often lacks variation - and hence ends up monotonous.

Extraordinarily few guitarists appear truly comfortable with the DUDDUD pattern (dammit, I had to search long and hard before finding *any*). By contrast, the earlier DUDUDU pattern allows the *wrist* to drive the rhythm relaxedly and naturally, and leaves the guitarist complete freedom to incorporate dropouts, ballistic triplets and accenting.

The only drawback of the DUDUDU pattern that I am aware of is that IF every beat is hit, the additional up stroke generates a stronger harmonic response (potentially sounding rather “lush”). If your accompaniment is (as it should be), economic, precise, percussive and varied, this simply should not occur.

I’d argue that that is precisely what is good about it. Both Jigs & Reels have 2 “big beats” per measure; the ones where you tap your foot (and yes, some folks subdivide the foot tapping, but still). in a reel there are 4 notes per big beat; in jigs there are 3. so a 3 note pattern makes sense.

As a melody player, I too think in terms of strong and weak beats (with a nuanced range of emphasis between those two extremes). But they’re not fixed in place—that is, the strong beat doesn’t always fall on the first beat of the measure. And for the sake of phrasing a tune, I might not play any strong beat in a given measure.

Here’s how it was explained to me (by a highly regarded fiddler) eons ago.
For 6/8 jigs, I have several options for any one measure:

DYE-di-doh dye-di-doh
DYE-di-doh DYE-di-doh
dye-di-doh DYE-di-doh
dye-di-doh dye-di-doh
and even
dye-di-DOH dye-di-DOH

That last one gives a back beat feel, and you can hear it used to great effect by Evelyn Healy, mixed in with all the other strong/weak beat options, here:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UYlrreiBsYY

I have a hunch, however, that most backers of any experience with this music—even the esteemed contributors to this thread—move the strong and weak beats around, echoing the melody players. Even if they say otherwise. :-)

It’s tempting to take time signatures too literally and read more into them than is really there.

As I said, practice came before theory. The time signature is merely an attempt to describe what is going on rhythmically within the chosen notation and to aid in the execution of a given piece. To me, 4/4 indicates bars worth four crotchets (or quarters), 2/2 indicates bars worth two minims (or halves). It’s arbitrary which you use as they are equivalent. It’s tempting but ultimately a waste of time to try to read too much into the time signature.

I think it was Beethoven who decided to notate a conmissioned piece in 3/8 (rather than 6/8 or 12/8) because he was being paid by the bar.

DUD DUD is common because anything else makes jigs lunky. DUD DUD makes them “float.” Also to be clear: a good player will play around with DUD DUD but it is the unvarying foundation. You can do D-D D-D or D-- D-- occasionally (the - is silence or a held note).

↳ Posted by chris stolz on April 8th, 2026.

Re: jig strumming question

This is something that I use in picking jigs on banjo as well. The way that I like to describe it is that the 1 is long, the 2 is really short, and the 3 is somewhere in between the lengths of the 1 and the 2, up to, and including as long as the 1, if you’re accenting it to add some lift.

The 1 and the 3 are the important beats, meaning that they are where accents might go. (Basically never on the 2). So the DUD DUD pattern works well for this for a couple of reasons. The first is gravity. A down stroke is gravity assisted, so having it be where you might accent notes makes sense. Secondly, our musculature is more suited to using power in the down motion than in the up (think of swinging a hammer vs pulling a nail). This is not to say that you can’t accent with an up stroke, just that it’s more natural, physically, to accent a down stroke than an up stroke.

And then the lengths of the notes also fit well into that pattern. With the 3 often being a bit shorter than the 1, simply because you need a little time to get your hand moved back up for the following down stroke on the 1.

I started in this music on bouzouki, and would usually strum jigs in DUD DUD, and that translated nicely into picking melody that way on banjo as well…

On GDAD bouzouki, my tendency is towards D-U DUD.

DUD DUD feels a bit heavy to me.

The other issue is that DUD DUD, if played equally can clash with the lift/swing often done in jigs (listen to Mary Bergin play jigs) where (counting 6 eight notes in a measure) the 1 and 4 are lengthened, stealing time from the 2 and 5, and the 3 and 6 are more or less full value.

If you’re playing evenly timed DUD DUD (or DUD UDU) backing over a melody playing a jig using that sort of lift/swing then, you’re going to clash. D-U helps avoid that, but you still have to adapt the DUD to the lift/swing the melody players.

It’s all about supporting the melody and getting the effect you want, no matter what pattern you use.
