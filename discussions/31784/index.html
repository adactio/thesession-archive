↳ Posted by hoopoe on April 29th, 2013.

Re: Why is the ability to read music frowned upon in ITM?

It’s a bit of a lack of understanding on your part. The ability to read music isn’t frowned upon. The inability to learn by ear IS. Too many people come from a different tradition, style, or paradigm, and think that because they can read music, therefore they can play Irish traditional music, but really they can’t.

So the advice to avoid sheet music is to help people who can’t learn by ear to develop that skill. Because at the end of the day, that’s how you learn this music.

Re: Why is the ability to read music frowned upon in ITM?

I don’t know about out in the real world, but on this site there have been any number of threads where the use of printed music has been highly, highly disparaged. I have been pilloried time and again for promoting its use to the point of despair. The general consensus is that you have to hear the music in order to play it properly. What gets missed with this argument is that ALL music must be heard to be played properly. ITM is not a special case in this regard. But when learning a new tune, it is much faster to learn from a written source if you have the ability. Which is to say, the method of learning ITM differs not at all from how one learns any genre of music. It is exactly the same. Any musician who does not make use of both his or her eyes and ears may end up being a good musician, but will always operate under a handicap, IMO.

BTW, I don’t expect to see sheet music at a session. I don’t think using sheet music at a session is what the OP is talking about. Written music is a tool for learning, nothing more, nothing less. The only time any musician needs to rely on sheet music is when he or she is playing a part in an ensemble that either does not lend itself to being memorized (like a player in a symphony orchestra) or when one needs to be able to play a part at sight for a recording or one-time performance.

Re: Why is the ability to read music frowned upon in ITM?

A significant part of this debate is separating the difference between learning a *tune* with sheet music, versus trying to learn the *music* using sheet music. A lot of people come to this music from another form of music that relies on sheet music (most notably classical music, of course). But if you rely on your abilities to read music to try to teach you what Irish music should sound like, you will end up in the wrong place. Here’s a great example of this:

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nv0a7TPsWLM

But if you learn to play the music reasonably well, and have started to grasp the intangibles, the rhythms, the lift, the nyah, etc., then there’s absolutely nothing wrong with using sheet music as a tool.

Keep in mind, though, that a lot of people learn this music by hearing it in sessions. They don’t go home and look up the dots so that they can “really learn it”. They have developed their ear to the point where they can truly learn a tune by hearing it played repeatedly. So if you’re still in a place where you need to rely on sheet music to learn a tune, you might think about putting it down for a while, and concentrating on developing your ear more. Because learning by ear in a natural environment is a very pleasing skill to acquire!

This isn’t a problem for someone who learns by ear. Duplicating what you hear overcomes the troubles of fitting Irish music into notes where each note duration is broken into halves or quarters only. Irish music has a different set of timings.

Please be open minded that people may learn music in a different way than you learned. Also, be open to allow Irish music to have note durations that are outside of eighth notes or dotted eighth notes. Accept that the written music you have is an approximation, and is certainly off in its depiction of timing. The people who made this music were not constrained by note durations and used timing expressively. This is a difficult concept for those trained exclusively with reading notation. It is an easy concept for those trained by ear. This is probably the source of the tention here.

Amazingly, you don’t have to choose only one way to learn music. You can learn to read music and to learn by ear too. They even support each other. They are both useful tools for musicians. For Irish music, learning by ear is essential. Calling ear trained musicians illiterate misses the point and is really just shows disrespect to the ITM genre.

The only real disdain I can see is for those who discount learning by ear entirely and think that the heart and soul of Irish music is captured in the notation of the books they own and not in the recordings of the musicians we admire. The disdain comes from saying the simplified notation version is the real thing. To cut out the expression of the piece and call it the real thing is to strip it of its magic and captivating beauty. Re: Why is the ability to read music frowned upon in ITM?

I wouldn’t have thought the ability to read music was widely “frowned upon”, but there are interesting issues relating to the written note in traditional music. Firstly there’s the issue mentioned above by many, relating to the best way to learn the music, which is undoubtedly by ear, but it would be difficult for anyone to argue that knowledge of written music would hinder or thwart that process at the same time. The point is crucial nonetheless. It’s about accent, style and musical influence as much as the actual notes. For any musician with a reasonably sharp ear, there is no comparison between learning by ear and from the page. Personally, even though I can read and write music, I don’t think I have ever learned a tune from a printed source. On a handful of occasions, I’ve written down tunes directly from older musicians when I knew I would forget the tune, and in the absence of a recorder of some sort.

Secondly, the utilisation of notation betrays or at least suggests classical or formal instruction, which some construe as betraying contact with an alien culture, and I think for these people, the apparent detection of classical influence arouses suspicions at a subconscious level that one may have cavorted with the enemy at some stage. Our Irish mentality is riddled with all sorts of post-colonial psychology regarding class, creed and race, and some people desperately need to be seen and heard to emphasise the boundaries between that which seems foreign and their own usually-highly-delusional faux-authentic state.

It’s not always that simple though, it has to be said. Take the influence of O’Neill’s books and other printed collections. That repertoire had and still has a huge influence on what’s played at sessions. So many traditional musicians of a certain generation habitually learned tunes from the printed page. Before long, we had to listen to discussions as to whether such and such a version was “a good version” or not. More often than not, the unusual regional or sub-regional version would come off worst in such a comparison, because basically it seemed to stray too far from whatever was in the book. The adoption of tunes from those collections supplanted much of the local traditions around the country.

It’s changing though; a lot of younger musicians are jettisoning some of the pseudo-archaic notions harboured by their forebears, although I’m sure many of the latter would say that the baby is being thrown out with the bath water.

Re: Why is the ability to read music frowned upon in ITM?

Written music is exactly like written words. If you write down the words, you have them, but not the voice of the person saying them. Thus, an Englishman reading Ulysses doesn’t sound like Joyce, he sounds like an Englishman reading Ulysses. In order to sound like Joyce, he’d have to study the Dublin accent, listen to recordings of Joyce speaking and reading Ulysses, and then use those accents and inflections in his own speech. Even then, he’d only sound like a guy trying to imitate Joyce reading Ulysses, no matter how closely he could match the original.

That’s the way tunes work. If you’re a classical, jazz, blues, bluegrass, or Hawaiian musician and you try to learn tunes from reading the sheet music, you’ll sound like a classical/jazz/blues/bluegrass/Hawaiian musician reading tunes off of sheet music. You have to listen to others play, study the music and the tradition, and try to imitate and absorb what you see and hear. And in the end, no one gets anything exactly like the people they learned it from. That’s how the music changes and varies, from all the little personal quirks that come out when you start playing it yourself.

Re: Why is the ability to read music frowned upon in ITM?

Back in this thread someone mentioned cadenzas in violin concertos, and concert violinists playing the whole lot from memory. The fact is that if you want to get a concerto up to the standard where people will pay to hear you perform, and return for your next performance (it is your living, after all), then in the course of the several hundred hours of preparation needed to get to such a level you cannot help but memorize the music in every possible detail. If you get to that level and then attempt to perform from the printed music then a conflict between sight and memory will likely happen, there will be little mistakes, and the performance will lose its feeling of spontaneity. That is one good reason why soloists perform from memory and are expected to do so.

There are one or two exceptions - a concerto for two or more solo instruments is generally more complex and is rightly viewed as a collaborative performance, and playing from sheet music in this case is traditional. Again, a professional pianist will perform concertos and give recitals from memory, but an organist giving a recital almost always plays from the sheet music (presumably because it is such a complex instrument).

The musicians for whom I have the greatest admiration are the opera singers - not only do they have to master very complex and lengthy roles, but must be able to project without mics over an orchestra to the furthest reaches of a large theater, and simultaneously act convincingly on stage while wearing heavy and hot costumes (I have played in the orchestra pit for operas).

It’s not about reading fast, it’s about having the tune in front of you so you can first play it and then memorize it. I think you have to hear a tune many times over before it stays in your head, and even then, your memory will not be trustworthy. When you read through a tune on paper, your fingers memorize their movements as much as your ears memorize the melody. You simply have more senses going for you along with having a printed record you can easily refer to. If you forget a run of notes in the B part, you can go straight to it to get back on track. It’s a tool and a permanent record, which is all it is intended to be. No one wants to rely on printed music, especially ITM. It’s a leg up to learning tunes, that’s all. When I bought Vincent Broderick’s Turoe Stone Collection, I did not have access to the tape that used to be sold with it. The foreword mentioned two tunes - The Rainbow’s End and The Crock of Gold, so I read through the tunes, liked them and learned them. Now that I have the tape, I actually don’t care for the way VB plays his own tunes. If I had heard them first, I never would have learned them, but The Rainbow’s End is one of my favorite reels and I play it almost every day as a warm-up. I’m not saying I’m a better flute player, but VB is from Belfast and I don’t play in that style. I play it more the way Matt Molloy might (although not nearly so well!). The point is, without the book, I wouldn’t be playing VB’s tunes at all. Once and for all we have to dispense with the notion that reading music is some sort of substitute or short-cut to being steeped in how the music is played. It is a tool for setting down a basic melodic line from which a musician can take flight and make the tune his or her own within the Irish music tradition.
