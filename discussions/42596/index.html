No, you shouldn’t. You did not define, what “super expensive” is in your case.

Your best bet is to find a reputable maker and order a flute from him/her. Or buy a used flute produced by a reputable contemporary maker. Or buy a flute of English/American origin made in mid-19 c. or later.

You do not want a 19 c. German flute. You do not want a flute-like object made in Pakistan, esp. if you’re looking for a keyed flute.

Re: Would you buy this flute?

David knows a bit about flutes. First, this flute appears to have French style keys, e.g. the diagonal g# key. If it is a French built anonymous flute it will have a seriously flat F#. I’ ve never had good luck with the C and C# keys on these one piece right hand sections. I have restored several similar flutes and almost always they have had a crack in the tuning slide. The seller does not announce any repairs, but extremely close examination may reveal the repair. (Not a deal breaker at the price). The wood appears to be cocus (a plus) but could cause an allergic response. Of more concern to me would be the embouchure cut. . .these range from outstanding to meh. Much above $300 and I would be apathetic.

Re: Would you buy this flute?

From its general appearance and keywork, I would say that this is a mid to late 19th. Century anonymous German manufactured flute. Many of these cheap mass produced instruments were made for export, and were one factor in the demise of American bespoke operations. They are variable in quality, from really poor players ( a high percentage ) to acceptable, but I’ve yet to hear of a really good example from this genre. For a decent playing German flute from this period, I think those made by Meyer (not “Nach Meyer”) are among the best. Personally, I would not buy this flute. If it’s a keyed antique instrument that takes your fancy, I would recommend; an American made one ( good intonation/pitched at 440 hZ ) a French made one ( again good intonation with exception of flat F#. and sweet tone for Classical Music - often pitched between A = 435 to 440 Hz. ) or one of English manufacture ( intonation sometimes not as good as American, but often a more focused sound - later ones can be high pitched ).

Re: Would you buy this flute?

Flat F# would be because the RH2 hole is too small. As you can see on the pictures. Between the RH1 and RH2 holes is a semitone and between RH2 and RH3 is a whole tone. That doesn’t work with evenly sized, evenly spaced holes… The temptation for the maker is to make them easier to reach because if they were in the correct position it would be a long stretch between RH2 and RH3. So, most makers compensate by changing the sizes of the holes allowing a bit of playing with their location. This flute does not show signs of that. I would be wary. *“RH2” -> Right hand middle finger…)

Baroque flutes (unsuitable for trad) have evenly sized, but not evenly spaced, holes, but they get all the notes in tune by cross fingering most of them, even those on the main scale.

Re: Would you buy this flute?

Congrats on your quick progress with the keyless polymer. It’s probably not holding your learning and playing back - so take your time to pick the right wooden flute for you. That (likely) French 19C antique flute probably isn’t the “next step” you are looking for.

It looks like a nice flute in ok condition and the price might be good value. I have one very like it which came from the Pyrenees - https://flic.kr/s/aHskBaY1f4

Flutes of that vintage won’t play quite like a modern flute. You are almost certain to have to adopt non-standard blowing and fingering techniques to play it effectively. The tuning is likely to be slightly off (especially the F# @ XXXXOO - probably needs F-nat key open to play close to in-tune). So you may enjoy having a 100-150 year old antique simple system flute but it is unlikely to become your “replacement” for normal playing of ITM in a group. It is likely to have a nice tone but will be lower volume by comparison with a modern R&R or Pratten style flute.

Given your background you are likely to be well capable of playing such a flute well and revelling in the idiosyncrasies of a 100-150 year old antique simple system flute. But it probably won’t eliminate your desire to procure a well tuned and well behaved modern style keyed wooden to advance your playing.

The other negative factor is location - it will be costly (relative to price) to ship USA to Spain and there is a no-returns policy so if it turns out you don’t like you are stuck.

Good flutes are worth their price - and I would agree that the Windwards are very good. I am now on a keyed one, having had almost exactly the same flute as a keyless as my main flute for about ten years…

At the about €400 mark you have quite a few reasonable keyless options open, but keyed ones really do not start to come in until quite a lot more (well above the €1000).

Another thought about the ebay flute - if you are wanting to buy it from the US and ship to Spain then CITES could be an issue - you need to be very certain what wood it is and if it is covered by CITES or not. What matters is what the customs people determine, not what the seller claims. Not an issue when buying and shipping within Europe.
