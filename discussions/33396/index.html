Slow Practice vs. Quiet Practice

I’ve been trying to figure out how to get more dynamic range out of my fiddle. The only thing I could think of is some quiet practice, but I’m not sure if it’s beneficial. We all know the advantages of slow-practice. Better articulation, bow control, intonation, rhythm, overall thoroughness, etc… But could quiet practice also improve these skills in a different way? Or do you have experience with it and know it to improve different but equally important skills?

Re: Slow Practice vs. Quiet Practice

Try this, Jerone -- play a tune three times, the first time normally, the second time as quietly as you can (imagine a conductor glowering at you if it helps) with as much feeling and nuance as you can muster; then the third time as loud as you want. Make the transitions as obvious as you can, and while you’re at it, see if you can find any benefits or drawbacks in each version. The last, loud time through serves as a future reward that you can look forward to while playing quietly, which can be a bit of a challenge.
I remember someone saying that playing quietly can really pull at the heart-strings, so don’t think of it as ‘toned down’ or in any way lacking -- it is another tool in the box, and has its own validity.

Re: Slow Practice vs. Quiet Practice

@Jerone, maybe you know all this already, but anyway …

The elements that contribute to a wide dynamic range are bow pressure on the string, sounding points (bowing nearer the fingerboard for mellow and quieter, bowing nearer the bridge for crisper and louder), and speed of bow travel. Each of these, separately or together, can mark your lower and upper dynamic range.

As an example, play a tune as you normally would, keeping a steady tempo. Now play the next round, keeping the same tempo and everything else the same, except make slightly longer bows for each note. Your volume will increase because the bow is travelling faster across the string.

Now keep everything the same, but now play closer to the bridge - that will increase your volume even more.

Finally, without changing anything else, add more bow pressure, and you’ll get quite loud.

If you now repeat all this ‘in reverse’ you should hear a noticeable difference as your volume goes down each time. At the very last step that should be your volume for quiet practise.

Nothing wrong with quiet practise, utilising the above, but make sure you are drawing out enough tone to be heard above the bow noise. I’ve heard people practise *too* quietly (ie tickling the instument). It sounds OK under the player’s ear, but very scratchy 5-10 feet away because not enough tone is being drawn out to overcome the bow noise.

Re: Slow Practice vs. Quiet Practice

Further to Jim’s excellent post -- if you watch how a string actually sticks and slips on the bow:
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FO5bq6x_Tws
and then watch how that momentum generates a wave up and down the length of the string
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6JeyiM0YNo4
you’ll see that it’s not simply a case of pressing harder or moving the bow faster -- the stick/slip has to be in time with the oscillation of the string, otherwise they tend to cancel each other out. Getting the two in synch is what produces a good tone, and playing quietly is one way to ‘feel’ the string, especially the G string, in order to maximise the vibration and avoid damping it.

Re: Slow Practice vs. Quiet Practice

Since you mention Martin Hayes, and since Martin uses more dynamic range in his playing than most ITM fiddlers, you might be interested to know that he does not use changes in pressure so much as changes in bow speed to create his range. He sometimes demonstrates his technique using no pressure at all -- barely holding the bow with two fingertips right at the end of the screw. There is a lot to be said for keeping a loose grip on the bow, and finding that sweet spot where you have as much flexibility as possible while just barely maintaining the grip.

The bow speed versus bow pressure trade-off is not a new one. I expect you will find that many top players use just enough pressure to keep the bow on the string, and use bow speed to generate most of the dynamics. The strings support the weight of the bow, and not much more.

↳ Posted by Fiddler3 on February 24th, 2014.

Re: Slow Practice vs. Quiet Practice

What fiddlers tell you they do and what they actually do are sometimes two different things.

If you only use speed, it severely limits the bowing patterns that are available to you:

If you want to play a 2/4 march DUDUDUDU using just bow speed, every down bow is accented, so a faster, longer stroke. Every up stroke is unaccented so slower and shorter. Pretty soon you’re going to fall off the end of the bow.

[*If you want to play a 2/4 march DUDUDUDU using just bow speed, every down bow is accented, so a faster, longer stroke. Every up stroke is unaccented so slower and shorter. Pretty soon you’re going to fall off the end of the bow.*]

That is possible, of course - but my point earlier was that pressure, speed and travel are separate units. You don’t need to make a longer stroke to get an accent (up or down). Just a bit more bow pressure on the string (+ optionally closer to the bridge).

A good player will not run out of bow - if he overdoes the travel on the down-bow, he’ll compensate on the next up-bow.
