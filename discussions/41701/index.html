Re: Tune familiarity.

I don’t think modes have any more importance in ITM (or STM) than they do in lots of other types of music (Country, Pop, Jazz, etc). The perceived prominence is more to do with written convention.
So, for example, in “classical” music if a piece is in D then the key signature will either be D or F, indicating the keys of D major and D minor, respectively. Any variations from these are indicated by the use of accidentals in the score (as mentioned by Trish above). In traditional music the convention is to indicate variations from major and natural minor either by the use of accidentals in the key signature (Amix is often written with a key sig of two sharps and a natural) or by changing the key signature (so Dmix has a key sig of G, Ddor has a key sig of C).
I don’t think it’s necessary to know the theory behind the music unless you want to transcribe it yourself. The beginning player will, one hopes, eventually realise through listening and/or fingering, that there are different degrees of “major” and different degrees of “minor”.
For me personally, a good knowledge of music theory has proved beneficial, both musically and financially, but I think one can still gain enjoyment from playing and listening to music with minimal knowledge of theory.

Re: Tune familiarity.

tdrury, the key signature which has two sharps is called “D” and nothing else (not “D major”, which is not the name of a key signature). The key signature which has one flat is called “F” and nothing else.

So the key of D major, which has two sharps in the associated scale, has the key signature “D”. The key of D minor, which has one flat in the associated scale has the key signature “F”.

The key signature is not the same as the key (or root). It’s a subtle difference. This has been gone over in previous threads here. But, to reiterate: the keys of F major and D minor and the modes of C mixolydian and G dorian all share the same key signature that is called “F” and the keys of D major and B minor and the modes of A mixolydian and E dorian all share the same key signature that is called “D”.

In other words the name of a key signature is derived from the root of the associated major scale.

Re: Tune familiarity.

Keys and modes are different things. DonaldK’s mention of Aonghas Grant’s talk about a tune being “on A” is good: An Amix tune is in D but on A.

If you build a set of triads on the scale of D you get D, Em, F#m, G, Bm7, C#m7b5. those are the chords you can use to accompany a tune in D. But if a tune is in Amix the notes of the scale are the same, so the set of triads you build are the same, you are still in the key of D. So if you are working with an accompanist who isn’t familiar with modes it is better to tell him it’s in D and hope he can hear where the cadence resolves to than to mention A, which will inevitably lead to him throwing in E major chords and causing a disaster.

Re: Tune familiarity.

Jim, this is where the Irish idea that a tune is ‘in’ a particular mode falls down, and it’s why classical musicians will only ever describe a tune as being in a key, even though they talk a lot about modes when analyzing the tune.

Modes change within a tune, wherever you have accidentals there is always a mode change, so in the case of Tom Billy’s Jig with the G# rolls, those phrases are A Ionian, the rest is A mixolidian, and the key is D major.

Mode changes also occur without accidentals - if a tune is in D major and you play a phrase centred on D, then repeat the phrase one note up the tonal centre has moved to E and you’ve switched to E dorian, if the next phrase centres on D you’re back in D ionian, but the key remains D major throughout.

Re: Tune familiarity.

Thanks for the comments. . .
My being primarily an accompanist, whether in an ITM session or at the harpsichord, I think in modes, and I hold that all backup artists should know them.

To continue the example, the key of D-major (D-Ionian, two sharps) has seven affiliated modes, with the Ionian labeled as the first mode. The other six modes in the D-Ionian scale all use the same notes as D-Ionian but start and finish their scales on other notes within that D-Ionian scale. The second note of the D-Ionian scale is E, and E-Dorian uses the same notes as the D-Ionian but that scale starts and ends on E, and has a “minor” tone to it. (Then Phrygian and Lydian, third and fourth modes), then we come to the fifth note of the D-Ionian scale, which is A. The A-Mixolydian scale still uses the D-Ionian notes and the A-“Mix” scale has that characteristic flatted seventh note. The sixth note of the D-Ionian scale is B, and a scale that starts and ends on B (but uses the two sharps of D-Ionian) is the Aeolian mode. What I’ve tend to call the “Aeolian natural” uses the same two sharps, but the “Aeolian harmonic” adds a sharpened seventh, in this case A#, to the scale.

What I’m really getting at (however verbose) is that the mode is what makes a given tune “sound like that,” and, modes often further help define a musical culture, e.g., Iranian and Middle Eastern music is heavy on the third, or Phrygian, mode. For example, try playing the E-Dorian tune “Ships are Sailing” in its usual Dorian mode, then play it in the Aeolian mode (replace the C# with C natural), and something has definitely gone very wrong!

The mode of a tune also defines the chords and progressions I will put in on backup.

But, as I’ve said before, being an effective accompanist goes beyond the rhythm and modes - you have to know the tunes in the first place, or otherwise have a damn good idea of where a tune is going!
