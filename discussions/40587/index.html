Re: Constructive Criticism Please.

I find it interesting that people are suggesting that you play with a metronome to help with your rhythm, and I personally think that’s a bad idea. A metronome is rigid by design. Too rigid. They CAN be useful for some things, like steadiness, and keeping the tune flowing after making a mistake. So you can use a metronome as one of your tools when working on something specific like that.

But the real rhythm of the music is what’s happening in between the beats and a metronome isn’t going to help with that. As an old friend used to say, “If you want to sound like a machine play with a metronome. If you want to sound like a musician, play with musicians”. To work on the aspects that you mentioned, like sounding rushed, playing with another musician (or if you must, a recording of a musician) can help with that. They can help provide the fundamentals of the music - the tempo and rhythm, most notably. And that can free you up to work on matching their flow and expression. That’s when you can start to internalize the *feel* of what it should sound like, instead of just the sound.

I would also encourage you to not beat yourself up over this. You sound just fine, especially for as long as you have been playing this music. Don’t be impatient. There is no destination, and no deadline. It’s not a race, it’s a journey, and you might as well enjoy the scenery along the way! Keep building your repertoire, and do as much playing with people who are better than you, and everything will fall into place.

There are some who are blessed with a flawless internal metronome for whom a metronome isn’t necessary… and then, there’s the 99%, the rest of us.

Yes playing live with other musicians is the best way, but practicing to a metronome at home means you’ll show up at the session with your baseline being correct rhythm, onto which you can then add the subtleties of phrasing. Let’s not forget that it’s dance music, and dancers need a steady rhythm.

A metronome does two things 1) train your internal metronome 2) train you to play matching a rhythm outside of yourself (neither of which playing to the tapping of your own foot does). Your potential session-mates will be happier if you show up playing in good rhythm and also with the ability to listen and match the rhythm of the session.

About your playing, I agree with everyone else that it’s lacking in both a clear steady beat, and also lacking in phrasing, or ‘groove’. I’ve heard it before! Violinists to take up fiddling often sound like that for a while, as do orchestral fluteplayers who take up Irish flute. Much playing and listening cures it in time. You’re doing all the right things, seems like, lessons and exposure and all, and I don’t doubt that in time everything will click.

Re: Constructive Criticism Please.

On the metronome - I use one myself, but I’m inclined to go with Reverend on this. As a classical player, you’ve surely used a metronome to death already. You might be conditioned to behave a certain way - breathing, tension, etc. - when you start one up. For that reason I’d say don’t make the met a big feature of your improvement at this point.

Phrasing - that’s what I would work on, more than anything. You’ve got to learn to identify and understand ITM phrasing, and that’s where the listening comes in. But for many tunes, particularly jigs, the basic phrasing is pretty simple: first two measures are the question; next two are the answer. Then those four measures will likely form a larger “question,” and will be answered by the next four measures. That’s all I’ll say - better you listen and figure out the phrasing for yourself.

But here’s the trick. Take a phrase, any phrase, and try playing just that phrase slowly and evenly, and with some different bowings - single bows, some slurs, mix them up, etc. What you’re going for, in addition to being curious and having some fun, is to be able to play that phrase in a relaxed way and withy a variety of bowings. Don’t try to skip ahead - that’s not the point. The point is to convince yourself that it is possible to play - even a small bit - in a relaxed way. Then get used to that good feeling.

Here’s a clip that might help you with understanding phrasing and also learning how to play in a relaxed way while still concentrating on the music.

PaleAle,

I believe relaxing is key here, and trying to navigate over an entire tune creates tension since it requires a lot of thinking and, for many,worrying about correcting little things. Take away that thinking and make the playing as simple and hopefully enjoyable as possible, and you do that by playing through that one phrase. And you can play that phrase simply and easily, once you settle into it.

The key is to show yourself - prove to yourself - that you can play without tension. Once you believe that, then you can go on to larger chunks.

It’s the thinking that creates tension, so even trying to follow a met can introduce tension - as in “oops, got there late, so pick it up a little,” that kind of thing. The simpler the goal, the less thinking that’s required.

That’s what I do when I feel tension seeping into a tune. I slow it down, pull it apart, and find the offending phrase(s). But the thing is not just to execute the phrase, but to play with it a bit, understand it, experiment with it, get it so you like it and it’s comfortable - and NOT rush to finish it so you can get on to the next thing.

Re: Constructive Criticism Please.

When you think about phrasing a tune, you can use a language analogy. There is pacing, timbre, and emphasis that are uneven in a spoken sentence. And depending on what you’re saying, changing those things can change the underlying meaning of the sentence.

If you think about musical phrases, they can convey an underlying theme or meaning too. You can play a tune sweet, or you can play it happy, or you can play it angry, all without changing what notes you’re playing. But to do that, you change the phrasing, which might include changing the pacing, timbre, emphasis, swing, dynamics, etc.

A lot of times, the melody itself will suggest the phrasing, and it’s up to the player to interpret it in a way that sounds musical. If all you’re doing is spitting out a string of notes, then has the same effect as an old style movie robot speaking in monotone, with no inflections (or a midi player regurgitating notes very evenly with no inflection).

So when I talk about phrasing a tune, there are a lot of little bits and pieces that go into it. Timing of the notes, connecting phrases (pickup notes, etc), dynamics (volume, attack, etc), articulation (ornaments, etc), the underlying rhythm (the inequality of the notes, or “swing”. This is the rhythm in between the beats that we were discussing earlier). All of these things can be combined to make the music, well… musical. The really good players can phrase the music beautifully, with a lovely flow and overall feel. The example video above with Mary MacNamara and Martin Hayes is a nice example.

And, as Ergo suggests, one of the ways that you can put more “feel” into your music is by slowing it down. If you can stay relaxed and focused on the melody, you can start to put some inflection into your phrasing. And once you’ve built up your ability to phrase tunes nicely, it’s not too hard to take it up in speed and still keep the relaxation and musicality in your playing.

Re: Constructive Criticism Please.

Just for the record, I never said that practicing with a metronome would hurt ones playing or rhythm. I was just pointing out that the swing and lift aren’t set out by the metronome. Generally it’s just providing the down beat. So I can play a reel and a hornpipe to the same metronome beat and the rhythm will sound completely different even through the metronome didn’t change. So my point was just that practicing with a metronome won’t give you “good rhythm”. It can give you a steady beat, and that’s about it.

I used to have a clip on tuner that had a metronome built into it. It could be set up to play dotted rhythms, and different time signatures, so it was pretty flexible in its ability to try to help with rhythm. However, even if you set it up to play the up beats as well as the down beats, with whatever swing you wanted, it was still way too rigid. The melodies provide you the ability to phrase the music, and the timing of the notes in between the beats are going to change if you’re sounding “musical”. If you’re playing every bar with the exact same same swing and emphasis, you’re going to sound like a machine.

Another example of the rigidity of the metronome was something I found while recording my tenor guitar album. About 1/3 of our tracks were done with a click track (which is just a metronome in your headphones). What I found with using a click track is that when I listened to what was recorded with the click turned off, I could hear myself making little adjustments in timing to match to the metronome, and it didn’t sound good (It was subtle, so I was probably the only one that could hear it, but it really grated on my nerves). So yes, that means that I don’t play with as steady a beat as I would like to think. But it’s never an issue when playing with humans, because of the give and take, and consensus of the beat. The way that Charlie and I were recording, we didn’t have full sound isolation between us, so we would record a “scratch track”, where we would play through the track together. And then we would each come back and record our parts individually, playing to the recording. So when we were using a click track, we would turn the click off after the scratch track, and the music would flow better, since it felt like we were playing with players instead of the click track. But even with that, the most fluid and natural sounding tracks on the album were done completely without the click track, and that’s how I preferred it.
