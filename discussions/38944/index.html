Welcome, Chris! To play low register, you do more of an exhale than a blow. It’s warmer and slower air, much like exhaling to steam glasses you are trying to clean. The upper register is faster, colder air, much like blowing out a candle. The sound of the second octave may sound louder because that register is more penetrating, but you don’t need to blow harder to get that range once you’ve got the hang of it. If you were even just fair on flute, it should come back to you quickly. Whistle is much easier than flute in this respect.

Cheers.

Re: Introduction and seeking advice on the whistle

Hi Chris, As a flute player you already know about CTS, a very bad thing. On flute, the trick is to keep both wrists as straight as possible, at all times and in all ways. My background is flute and I am a newcomer to whistle, but I apply the same straight-wrist technique to the whistle. On flute, the lower hand balances on the tip of the thumb, with the tip of the thumb tucked underneath the palm of the hand and centered about halfway between the first and second fingers. And now what you need to do on whistle is to learn how to similarly balance the upper hand on the tip of your thumb. This is known as a piper’s grip, with fingers more or less straight. Your (gentle) lip strength then keeps the whistle from flipping sideways and then balanced on the tips of your thumbs both of your wrists can be held comfortably straight.

Re: Introduction and seeking advice on the whistle

Chris, to answer your question (how do you play in the upper register of a whistle without the huge difference in volume?): you can’t. The upper register is always going to be louder, and the increase in volume gets exponential at high G and above. On the whistle (in contrast to the flute, where you can achieve a lovely small round sound on those high notes) there isn’t a great deal you can do about it. It’s a feature :-)

Of course you should avoid overblowing the very high notes (although a common mistake beginners make is not to blow them assertively enough, precisely because they are afraid of all the noise that those notes have to make). The make of instrument may play a part. And when you’re playing into a mike you can pull back in the shrieky bits.

Re: Introduction and seeking advice on the whistle

A whistle is not a flute and a high whistle (Clarke) is not going to be held the same way as a flute
or any low whistle. As far as controlling the volume (and tone & intonation) it helps to keep the angle
of the whistle where you’re blowing as straight through the windway as you possible. I don’t want to use the *p* word so I’ll suggest whistlers not slouch. And if the angle on your tin whistle is such that it gives you the most direct airstream (through the fipple) it will also assist w/placing your wrists and digits comfortably.

About the lips; they don’t hold the whistle. The fipple can easily rest on the lower lip. Along those same lines if you don’t push the fipple too far into your mouth there will be less of a tendency to *grip* it and you should have better control (further out than in) directing your airstream where you need it.

Re: Introduction and seeking advice on the whistle

The point of “gripping” the whistle with your lips is not to mechanically support the instrument. The whistle can be held stably enough to cover and uncover any hole with virtually no strain anywhere using a four-point hold: mouth, two thumbs, and any finger except maybe the upper-hand pinkie. The reason to apply lip pressure is to adjust the tone and pitch of the uppermost notes, and then mainly when they’re held for a long time. Try playing a high A or B and alternately compressing and relaxing your lips. You should be able to discern a range of pitch variation--sharper when lips are compressed and flatter when relaxed. The high notes on a cylindrical whistle tend naturally to be flat. To bring them into pitch you have two choices: blow harder and create a loud harsh sound that may still be out of tune or compress your lips on the windway and adjust to pitch while keeping air flow more or less constant. I’m not talking about a death grip, but just enough adjustment to push the pitch up a bit. I find the whistle much less demanding physically than the flute but at least as challenging musically, if not more so. The flute offers more control on tone, pitch, and volume. With the whistle, the control knobs are more finicky and easier to overshoot. Like the harmonica, it defines the distinction between simple, which it is, and easy, which in my experience it is not. Just my $0.02.

Re: Introduction and seeking advice on the whistle

OK, this is about wrists, and more. The piper’s grip works just fine on pipes and has been doing so for hundreds of years, but it really is not your better choice when it comes to gripping a whistle and it certainly is wrong to use on a flute. And now you need some real evidence of that, more than just my word for it. OK, here is a quick and easy way to see for yourself. Go to a convenient edge of a tabletop, a bookshelf or countertop, etc., and push your thumb on that edge in two different ways. Hold your hand out in front of you with the palm facing downwards and the fingers extended frontwards. Then with your thumb held out sideways, push on the edge of the tabletop, etc., and notice that your fingers will then immediately turn toward your thumb, meaning that your wrist allows them to turn. Then tuck your thumb under the palm of your hand so that the tip of the thumb is approximately between the 1st and 2nd fingers and push again, and notice that your fingers do not tend to rotate but that they keep pushing straight forwards with no turning of the wrist. See the difference? Not having the wrist turn means no muscles are involved in keeping it straight, meaning the hand is more relaxed and so the fingers can move more freely. Flatten the palm of the hand to better allow the fingers to spread sideways, and adjust the height of the tip of the thumb to control the rotation of a flute or whistle.

Let me add that on a proper flute grip the upper hand thumb does not get used at all.

Re: Introduction and seeking advice on the whistle

Not piper’s grip, but for high whistles, to find you natural ‘curve’, for the hands and fingers, shake them out and just let them go limp, and take note that they won’t be straight. Relax! RELAX!!! ;-) Starting with whatever you choose for your top hand, don’t take the hand to the whistle but bring the whistle, first, to that limp hand, and gently adjust the whistle until the top joint of the first three fingers find their respective upper three holes on the whistle, then bring the thumb up to the underside of the whistle, relaxed, not arched. Do not arch the thumb, make that clasp a gentle one. Start working with just those three fingers to start with. To get a decent seal, don’t grasp or pinch the whistle, the whistle also just sits on the thumb, again ~ gently. Move the fingers, or the whistle, gently around until those three fingers make a good but gentle seal. It is rarely dead centre, but it tends to be around the softest part of the finger, opposite the bottom half of the fingernail and cuticle. That soft ‘underbelly’ is kind of like a small nipple, where the whorl of the fingerprint usually centers. If you’ve one finger longer, usually the middle finger, that finger will be more forward, and the area covering the hole will be a little back of the other two fingers, sometimes a ‘little’ more arched.

For awhile always start with this shake-and-relax, taking the whistle to the three fingers first, and then the thumb rising up and under ~ for the whistle to sit on, usually between and opposite the index and middle fingers. As already said, the wrists should be relatively straight, the hand and fingers gently curving from there. If at any point, and please be aware of this, you feel tension, or you start GRASPING the whistle, pressing to cover the holes, put it down and go away for a break. DO NOT PRACTICE TENSION! A proper seal does not require any heavy pressing. That kind of tension, including arching the thumb, will only, eventually, bring pain and frustration. Go slow, and get that relaxed grip right first, first with the top three fingers and holes, and then, later, the next three… Never use your fingertips. It’s the pad of the finger that ‘gently’ covers its respective hole, the pad ~ opposite the bottom half of your fingernail and the cuticle…

Best of luck. I too have problems with CTS, but mine is from very small wrists. I did a lot of wood chopping and regularly dislocated my wrists doing that, and then I’d have to hang from somewhere to set them right again. I’d guess that working with other tools, power ones too, like chainsaws, wasn’t helpful? Typing can and has aggravated that problem too, but never playing an instrument, not yet anyway. I do know string and free reed players that have had to completely relearn their instrument(s) to avoid bad habits they’d developed that had lead to pain. The thumb is one that free reed players can suffer for the same reason mentioned above, arching them and the tension that causes, and inevitable pain.

To review again ~ shake out your hands and fingers, and let go, relax, let them drop into their natural arch, then take the whistle up to them, starting with the top hand and those top three fingers and holes, and gently find a good and relaxed seal.

Will, it’s one thing, learning to play w/out ornamentation, but that’s not the same as suggesting learners should avoid using fingered articulations for as long as possible *for the sake of exploration*. There are so many tunes which beginners on whistle tend to learn with options to play cuts, or taps, short rolls… it’s inevitable for anyone, who is learning (and making progress on) whistle, cuts will come into playing on some tunes. Then taps. Etc.

I agree, playing a tune without ornamentation can be very instructive. One can explore deeper into the tune.
But why make this a requirement for all practice or all tunes when learning tin whistle?
No cuts ~ seriously? G rolls are not fundamental?

Re: Introduction and seeking advice on the whistle

I’ve never taken whistle lessons, but something I use on sax and flute seems to help on whistle high notes. Try experimenting with the inside of your mouth and the the muscles behind your nose. Think an ee or ih vowel, sort of an inside smile like when you’re going to sneeze, or like trying to get your ears to pop. See what changing the shape back there does to the air stream. It can help focus things higher. It also helps for making a note which is flat come out sharper.
