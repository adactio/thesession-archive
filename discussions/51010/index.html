Re: Top twenty pick and mix

If you’re backing tunes on bouzouki, it’s tempting to think, “Ah, I’ve learned three each of reels, jigs, hornpipes, polkas, slides, slip jigs, plus a fling, a march, and a mazurka. That’s it sorted.”

At a very basic level, yes, that will give you a starting place for backing other tunes of those same forms. But if your ears are keen, you’ll soon bump into the reality that each tune has its own groove—there’s no such thing as one universal jig or one universal reel pattern or pulse. As well, each melody player gives a tune his or her own pulse and phrasing, which can change from one night to the next. A good backer will do the same, adapting to and supporting the pulse and phrasing of the melody player(s).

Re: Top twenty pick and mix

I would steer clear of trying to learn “the chords” for a given tune. Mostly because there ain’t no such animal, unless you’re in a stage band with set arrangements that you play the same way each time. (Which hopefully doesn’t describe anybody’s actual session.)

Instead, I would focus on learning to hear where the tunes are going to go - if you hear the start of a tune, you’d like to be able to “hear ahead” and have a clear picture of where it’s going. You can do this in various ways, some people prefer to learn how to play the tunes (either on bouzouki or on a mandolin or a whistle or something, there are advantages to either approach) while other people have a good auditory memory and can remember a tune in detail without actually having to learn to execute the tune itself. Whatever way you do it, the goal is to be able to picture some “canonical” version of the tune well enough that you don’t have to think about that part and you can pay attention to the interesting stuff that the players are doing around that.

That, of course, is if you really want to play accompaniment. Which, I have to say, is a lot less fun than playing the tunes. Wouldn’t you rather just play the tunes? It’s a lot more fun being a musician than an accompanist!

Re: Top twenty pick and mix

Well, there’s no “substitution” because there are no chords in the music to substitute. Tunes don’t have chords, chords are imposed on the music by well-meaning people with chordal instruments in their hands. You can’t substitute what isn’t there.

> Every tune is different and has its own unique chord pattern

The first part is true, but the latter is pretty questionable. The chords that will work for a particular tune depend heavily on the way it’s played on that occasion. For just one example, think of Dennis Cahill playing with Martin Hayes, and compare his accompaniment to literally anyone else accompanying anyone else playing the same tune.

If you’re in a stage band playing fixed arrangements, then yes, you’re going to have a standard accompaniment for a tune that will work every time.

If I were feeling cynical, I’d suggest that the reason people get away with playing rote chords for a given tune is because literally nobody is listening to what they’re playing or cares, including the person playing the chords. But that would be cynical, and I’m only cynical from Sunday to Friday.

I would agree with Jon in that learning chords by rote for tunes will only get you so far.

The ultimate aim, in my opinion, is to be able to hear the (possible chord) changes in your head, just as you can hear the tune. That way, you don’t have to remember the changes (I find it much easier to remember a tune in my head than remember a chord sequence).

Of course, that is an awful lot easier said than done. As has been said above, it’s generally easier playing melody than accompaniment - it is for me anyway, and I consider myself primarily an accompanist.

Where I disagree with Jon is with his six day a week cynical idea that melody players don’t listen to or care what an accompanist is playing. I know plenty of players who do care and are not shy in telling you when it ain’t working.

Oh, and accompanists are also musicians.

Re: Top twenty pick and mix

A bit of a digression, but I wouldn’t send anyone to either the Fiddler’s Fakebook or the Portland Collection for Irish music - both contain a few Irish tunes, but both of them are primarily focused on American music, the former on bluegrass with a bit of old-time, and the latter almost exclusively on contra. This is quite aside from your opinions on whether it’s wise to memorize “authoritative” chord settings for particular tunes - if you want that, surely you can find some source that’s actually focused on the music you’re interested in! I’ve never looked at the Fioinn Seisum (I hope I’ve spelled that right) volumes, but if they have chord settings, I imagine they might be more useful to people interested in playing Irish music. Since they’ve become a sort of standard intro collection, you’ll also have the advantage of working with a set of tunes that many people you’ll be playing with will have heard and probably played.

But really, if you honestly feel this music isn’t any good without something to distract from the tunes, then the best way to learn to add that is going to be to learn a few tunes, figure out what you think might possibly work along with them, and then sit down with a friendly and patient musician and try out your ideas. And then ask that musician for suggestions and try those, and iterate until they seem reasonably comfortable with what you’re doing.

Some useful things that every accompanist should know, while we’re here:
- you don’t need to play full chords. Sparse is often preferable. Leave room for the music.
- you don’t need to keep the beat. The musicians have that covered - and if they don’t, if they need someone to keep time, then the “backers” need to back off and let them figure it out. There’s nothing you can do to help if the session’s lost its beat.
- Learn to recognize intentional tension, and don’t try to resolve it. A lot of the best tunes will let a seventh note hang in a way that is critical to the tune. When you hear that, you want to support it, not defeat it.
- Learn to recognize when a tune isn’t in the obvious key. For example, the Bank of Ireland fools a lot of people into thinking it’s got something to do with the key of A minor, because it starts out by obsessing on an A and a C natural. It’s a great head fake, if you’re going to bother the tunes then you really ought not to fall for it. Similar examples include Old John’s Jig and Hardiman the Fiddler. Other tunes to watch out for would include the Earl’s Chair, the Virginia Reel, Sailing Into Walpole’s Marsh - the list goes on, but if you learn to pick those tunes, you’ll get a good idea of why it’s a mistake to assume these tunes will follow bluegrass rules.

The latter two points are obviously good examples of why someone who would like to play chords needs to have a lot of tunes in their head. There are plenty of others. And frankly, if you like this music enough to spend hours listening to people play it, then it shouldn’t be a huge sacrifice to spend a lot of time learning how to play it yourself. This is the absolute best way to either become an accompanist that will make even a grumpy bastard like me complain a bit less about your playing than you might expect, or (ideally) to realize that actually playing the music is a lot more fun than playing something other than the music, in the same room where people are playing the music.

Re: Top twenty pick and mix

The only things I have to add to or reiterate what other people have said are:

• This music is built backwards from a lot of the music we’re familiar with, like Rock, Jazz, etc. In those styles, the music is built on top of a rhythm section, where there is an underlying chord progression, and the melody is built on top of that structure. In Irish trad, the melody players are providing the structure, both of the melody and the rhythm. So this music can be considered complete without any accompaniment at all. In the case where there is accompaniment, it should be there to support what the melody players are doing, and not drive it in any way. It’s important that you defer to the melody players for providing the rhythm, and that your rhythm doesn’t impede what they’re doing at all. To that end, sparse accompaniment can be useful, and even just droning the root note of the key can be preferable to stepping on the twists and turns of the melody because it’s moving through chords in unexpected ways. (That’s where knowing the tunes shows its importance!)

• Bouzoukis are nicely suited for playing sparse accompaniment that can consist of picking small patterns, combined with things like hammer ons and pull offs, use of double stops, and contrapuntal harmony lines. So it’s good to work on that stuff, and not simply play chords the whole time. That helps with supporting the melody instead of stomping on it with and endless stream of fully strummed chords.

• Formal teaching, even in small amounts, can help put you on the right path. There are a lot of online resources, as well, if you don’t find someone local who teaches. But often times, just going to sessions and getting to know people can help you find people who are willing to help you develop your playing in the right direction!

• Have fun with it! Don’t worry too much about the people that are bashing accompanists (although, it’s useful to know how a lot of people feel about it). Everyone takes their own path into this music, and you’ll learn what you need to know by getting out and doing it. Just be aware that this can become a life long obsession! Consider yourself warned! ;-)
