Re: selection process Willie Clancy

Everyone turns up to the school (or nearby location for some classes). You pick a classroom depending on what level you think you are (beginner/intermediate/advancesd) and then play a tune for the tutors in there so that they can assess which class you are best suited for.

Don’t fret about it though. It is really not that scary. More like bedlam. If the class you find yourself in doesn’t end up suiting then you can also move elsewhere.

Re: selection process Willie Clancy

I’ve done classes down there on both flute and concertina. As NCAF says above, there are three approx. ‘gradings’ - ‘beginner’, intermediate and advanced. In fiddle there would several groups at each level - i.e. different tutors. Note beginner grade is not supposed to be beginner, the person is expected to be able to play a few tunes. Likewise advanced could include some very talented individuals. It’s up to yourself, but if it’s your first time, I’d be inclined to pitch towards the intermediate classes. You might well find them challenging and if they’re too easy, well you can move up after first day. If you try and start at advanced level in WCSS, don’t be surprised if you find it tough going.

Re: selection process Willie Clancy

In fairness, the practicalities of dealing with a few hundred people who need to be allocated a suitable class are a problem. Especially the fiddle classes usually have to get through a large number of people. Noel Hill used to grade the concertina classes in person, every student had to play a tune and would be pointed towards a teacher, last time I witnessed that the whole process took nearly three hours. The process was changed at that point.

Technique is only used as a rough indicator in trying to grade the lower and mid range groups. There will always be a percentage of people enrolling who rate themselves very highly, they will struggle in class and their teachers will advice them to move to a more suitable group. I have seen some crushed ego over the years, great learning experience.

It can help if you know what you want and indicate the teacher (ofcourse I also remember the woman who had been going to Martin Hayes for six years in a row, she may well have benefited more starting at the lower intermediate level and take it from there but she made her choice and insisted on getting it her way).

What a lot of nonsense kook. It is not about rating someone. It is about trying to group people together for the purpose of a class so that you are with other people of, hopefully, similar abilities. Re: selection process Willie Clancy

“similar abilities”

I think that’s one of the main problems with it … because there are so many disparate skills and mind sets that make up this elusive thing called “ability”.

For a start there’s technical skill, which I always dismiss as an irritating irrelevance, not least because this music requires a relatively low amount of it.

Then there’s all those disparate “abilities” to communicate. Not just to be able to communicate a simple instrumental melody to like minded souls, but also all that stuff about presenting yourself as a nice open minded balanced human being that people will warm to and consequently listen too, etc etc. It’s important that stuff. Are you false or true? I think it’s a load of nonsense, but most people don’t (just look at the back lash against Sharon Shannon’s smile, for example). And then there’s just the simple ability to get on with the other people in your class … that’s surely important?

Then there’s knowledge of the core of the music, it’s nuances etc. And it’s related one to do with repetoir.

And then there’s that really illusive one that’s just about pure musicality? How the feck do you “rate” that one?

Someone here (I wish I could remember who) related a story the other day of a school headmaster who retired and took up the fiddle. He’s been listening and dancing all his life, then retired and thought he’d have a go. I want him as my tutor.

Re: selection process Willie Clancy

[This whole process sounds like a right tin of worms. ]

it isn’t. i can’t say i need to do it again, but it is not a tin of worms or torture or anything like that. they just want to hear roughly where you are so they can put you with a class where you’ll get something out of it but it’s not beyond what you can handle ear-learning-wise or technique-wise. …..:with adults who are not from ireland, they occasionally ask if there was a particular master player you wanted to go in with probly out of consideration since you’ve put in so much expense….

of course, i did the box classes for three years, and there is less hothouse atmosphere (because box draws fewer Perfect Children and fewer Stage Mommies) than fiddle or concertina. there is something about the violin that brings out a certain type of wrought-up parent and child….

but it DID take three hours, during which in box at least, everybody hangs out and has a good old time doing whatever the hell they want.

you have to remember something. i’m not saying it’s good, i’m not saying it’s bad. i’m saying, this is the way it is. you are welcome there, but it’s more like, you are "welcome.’ it really isn’t FOR you. it is for the kids, specifically, the irish kids, most of which have teachers in their own localities, and most of whom are already being taught the way classical kids are taught. they learn technique, they learn the scales of their instrument, they ear-learn very impressively, and they are all also learning using written music, though you might not see direct evidence of that in the festival classes. how they are learning is like classical pedagogy, but focused on the traditional style. and it gets results, big-time. they go to the festival classes with all the moving parts in place, and it is very impressive…..clancy week is really for them to go and get some polishing, learn some new tunes, etc. and that is part of what the sorting is about----it is for THEM, to optimize the experience for them.

i was placed into advanced box classes from the start, at a period when from my current perspective i could barely play. the advanced box class there was really just a pure-ear-learning class, and people who couldn’t keep up were very nicely sent to a class that used written notes. i was just barely able to keep up with the ear learning and it was very hard for me, but in retrospect, very, very good for me. sitting in classes with advanced players who are more accomplished than you are (in this case, irish kids and teenagers), is really good for you provided you can play.

i have seen that some festivals have a class that they seem to steer, viz., dump, non-irish adults into, and i would NOT go into one of those unless i was a beginner. horrible, horrible, horrible for your playing. ok, one exception, being that jackie mccarthy apparently teaches one here and there, and i’d do it once just to spend a few days around her lovely style……:)
