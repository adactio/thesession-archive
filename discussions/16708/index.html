Re: fingers or pick?

Using a pick…. it gives the volume needed to be heard at a session. It gives the ability to stress a note more strongly. I can’t think of anyone in ITM backing anyway who does not use a thumb or flatpick… unless…. could Dennis Cahill possibly just be using nails on the nylon strung? He does of course use a flatpick on the mandolin!

Fingerpicking tunes though, many use nails, including thumbnails to act as a thumbpick. McManus, Renbourn, others, they wear fake nails on the right hand and are always manicuring them to play just right.

Personally I am a thumbpick person, I like the straight out angle of it, though have and can play with a thumbnail, it broke too often so I got used to a thumbpick. I back fingertsyle as well as playing tunes, and can be a bit heavy on the thumb when backing and getting carried away, and switched to less bassey guitars, OMs000s rather than dreads (which also hurt my shoulder these days!), and like the more balanced tone.

In backing I use only the thumb and index finger, for tunes I play two finger as much as possible, and add the middle finger when needed. The fingers have nails and I play with them, though when they break I go on anyway… it just feels funny. maybe I should get those press ons too and join the club! I can’t function with fingerpicks, period.

Contrary to many beliefs, and maybe justified due to how people often do back fingerstyle very softly, you CAN get great volume and attack with your fingers and thumb when you want it. Think of Martin Carthy…Great attack… great ability too, to stress individual notes ….though theoretically you can do that with a flatpick too, but unless hybrid picking, you will usually only get one note at a time.

I love fingerpicking because you can, while backing, pick up on bits of the melody, which you can with a flatpick too, but with fingerstyle your thumb goes on droning at the same time, giving more depth.

Fingerpicking is very complex, a lot more so than strumming, having done it both ways. You may get told you’re not playing “Irish Style” by some, the popular way these days is the hard driving strumming, some jazz chords, and not to knock it, it sounds great when done well, but I prefer the fingerpicking because in a sense you have the combination or both backing and melody or counter melody going on at the same time your thumb keeps backing, and you can shift back and forth between them, or do both at the same time. So I am never much discouraged the few times I am told “It’s not Irish Style”, nor should you be either. I was playing this way with some pretty great players before this new Irish Style came along… maybe I am lost in the past a bit, but I find it light years more satisfying and intricate and intriguing to continue on this path…. and learn yet another little something literally every time I play the same old tune again. There are six strings with seemingly endless possibilities onces you add in every position for every note and combination then with others. Once you know the tunes you are backing…..and you should learn them, not only because you “should” but also because it ultimately makes your own playing more easy and sensible, you find there are endless ways to compliment the music.

I also like the subtlety of fingerstyle (though I mean SUBTLE and not soft with no attack) and how it rides under the music, but to answer the original question, the thumbpick certainly makes it easier for me to stress a note more fully when say, the fiddle happens to stress a note.

anyway, my four cents…. went on too long as always! And that was while restraining myself. apologies!

Hmmm, I must’ve missed the memo on flatpicking being taboo. :-)

I like to do both, though advancing age and decrepitude are making flatpicking seem too much like work. A couple of my friends can play their flatpick tunes with either flat pick or thumb and index finger (fingerpicks). I don’t like to use fingerpicks, so I didn’t go that way.

I use fingers and nails and I put considerable effort into maintaining the nails. I keep them strong and stiff with super glue and silk. I mostly use a classical type hand position, to get a good attack with the thumb nail, but sometimes I drop the palm down and play with the flesh of the thumb to get a softer bass line and emphasize the melody line.

Of course, if you use a thumb pick, you can mute the bass strings with your hand and get that nice thumpy Merle Travis bass line.

Re: fingers or pick?

I’m not sure I entirely agree with Iris‘ statement that “fingerpicking is more complex, much more so than strumming…” it sort of seems like a bit of a sweeping statement. To be sure, having heard a few of Iris’ sound clips, what she does is more complex than what the average plectrum user does…but a good plectrum user does exactly the same thing, follows the tune and accents certain notes. There’s a hundred ways to create chord parts, from simply strumming I-IV-V over a tune (which I’m not a big fan of) through to tracking the melody in the top line or bass, and picking your notes carefully whilst thinking very thoroughly about the intervals being created, much in the manner that a composer would arrange the orchestra around the melody. I think every way of doing things has it’s relevance, and sometimes the oddest harmonies have their place. I’m also a big fan of the percussive elements available to the pick user, but I’m very well aware some people don’t like that.

Anyway, what I like about playing tunes with a plectrum is that, much in the way that a fiddler has an up-bow and a down-bow sound, I like having the difference between the up and down stroke with a pick. Also the action of turning your wrist motion around slightly alters the patterns of the notes relative to the beats. If you’re of the opinion that the pick is the realm of the lazy dolt, do check out Anna Massie, Ian Carr, Chris Newman & Kris Drever to name a few. All are skilled accompanists, but they all can also play a good tune.

I’m not sure strength should have anything to do with it, if you’re imparting much force against the neck you might well be over-using muscle groups you shouldn’t, which could lead to problems later on.

Re: fingers or pick?

Andy:

I like the way flatpicking gives me more freedom to concentrate on the melody and put in more inflections and embellishments. In fingerstyle, you’re severely limited as to where you can do an ornament.

Regarding strength, you’re right, there shouldn’t be a lot of _brute_ strength involved, but plenty of _coordinated_ strength. Fingerstyle playing can force the left-hand fingers to stretch in multiple directions, all the while fingering solid notes and maybe playing some diddly with one or two fingers. For most people, this requires some significant muscle development. When you’re holding a three-finger chord and the fourth finger needs to reach out and execute an ornament, that requires extra strength and coordination beyond what you’d need for strumming a chord or playing an unaccompanied melody line.

Over the years, my left hand has grown bigger and stronger than my right.

Hmmm, Iris brings up an interesting point. How far can we trace the guitar back in accompanying trad? The earliest bloke I can think of is Willie Johnson back in the late thirties who was a big fan of the jazz guitarist Eddie Lang, but I’m sure there must be examples prior to him. To my knowledge many of the open tunings came over from the bluesmen in America with the revivalists, although I’d be interested if anyone has any examples of them being used before then, although drop-D, I believe, comes from the classical world.

It all seems to boil down to simply doing what you enjoy, music should be fun. One of the things I’ve come to realise lurks as a danger to trad is people policing ideas and techniques simply on the grounds that they’re not traditional, and trying to channel people’s creativity down certain paths. No matter how you play there’s always gonna be some people who like it, and some that don’t. So you might as well play for yourself because true happiness comes from within. Answering the original question, if the music played by those that fingerpicks excites you and makes you want to play it, then get learning. If not, don’t look to others for approval, do what pleases you. And if anyone tells you you’re wrong, ignore them.

‘’One of the most veracious of Irish chroniclers, Tighernach, who died in 1088, has preserved for us a poem, dating from 620, wherein the Irish harp is extolled. Walker says that “the cionnar cruit, or Kinnor, had ten strings, and was played on with a bow or plectrum.” He describes it as “similar to the canora cythara of the Latins of the Middle Ages, and the origin of the modern guitar.” Another form of cruit was the creamthine cruit, which the same author tells us was “the crwth of the Welsh,” and is said to be the parent of the violin, “but having only six strings.” We also have a record of the Fiddle being used in Ireland as early as the eighth century, as is quoted by O‘Curry from the poem on the Fair of Carman. In regard to the very favourite and oft-quoted instrument known as a timpan, it has been variously explained as a drum, or a sort of tambourine, whilst an Anglo-Saxon MS. makes it equivalent to a bagpipe! Dr. W. K, Sullivan cautiously tells us (relying on the authority of Dr. Charles O’Connor) that it “was a bowed instrument,” whilst the credulous Walker gravely assures us that “a Timpanist [player on the timpan] was simply a musical conductor.”

The timpan was, in reality, a small stringed instrument, having from three to eight strings, and was played with a bow or plectrum, being also called a benn crot, or peaked harp, by an ancient Irish writer. Recent research has almost conclusively proved that the Kinnor and the Trigonon, or three-stringed timpan, are identical, whilst the Nabla, or Psalterium--a favourite Celtic instrument from the seventh to the eleventh century--was generally of eight strings, and hence called the Ocht-tedach, or the eight-stringed.[2] We meet with constant allusions in the old annalists to timpans and timpanists; and a skilful performer on the timpan was held in the highest esteem. ‘’
