Keeping time vs …

Mulling over a few concepts I was thinking about how often musicians refer to rhythm when they mean keeping time, or referring to good timing as though it’s synonymous with playing at a fixed bpm; with absolutely no variance.
Keeping precise time helps when playing music with other people, especially as the number of musicians increases. But even when everyone is playing on the beat that doesn’t mean the beat remains constant throughout. So, how far do you go with keeping the bpm fixed; & when do you want the pulse of playing to be less clockwork? For me the rhythm doesn’t ignore the beat, each plays off the other, though rhythm can be subtle & complex.

Re: Keeping time vs …

I think you need to be able to play with a metronome, and I work with one constantly for several reasons - probably the most painful one is that I’m convinced that my internal timing is excellent, until I turn on a metronome or a drum pedal - where I discover exactly how sloppy certain passages can be.

A true test of tempo and timing is to slow down a metronome to say 80BPM and record yourself, then speed up the recording to about 120BPM to hear what you sound like. Then record yourself playing the same tune at 120BPM and compare the two. The results are pretty revealing. I’ve found that working with a consistent beat has improved my playing drastically.

It gives me a reality check - I think I’m a good player, but when a metronome is in the mix the weaknesses of my playing become apparent and these are the areas that I work on - which helps me become a better player.

Re: Keeping time vs …

Yes, you have to be able to play WITH a metronome, but you don’t have to play LIKE a metronome. In a band (or session) there is always one person leading - for everyone else he is the metronome. They have to be able to play with him, and practicing playing with a metronome is a good way to learn that.

But for the person leading, keeping a steady tempo isn’t necessarily essential. It certainly makes life easier for everyone else, and in a session of players of mixed ability, who don’t play together very often it can make the difference between music and chaos, but it also removes one mode of expression, and flattens the music. Amongst a tight group, tempo changes are a valuable tool, even when playing for dancers. I don’t know much about playing for Irish dance, but for Scottish dancing, if it’s a good band and they’re actually watching the dancers, you’ll notice a distinct change in tempo between the bit where they’re delicately balancing to their partners and where they stampede to the other end of the set.

Two types of rubato

Regarding the difference between what I think you mean by rhythm and beat:

There are two types of rubato (stealing time). In the first type a singer or melody player takes time from one note but adds the time back to another nearby note while the rhythm players (maybe we should call them tempo players) stay on tempo. In the second form the melody player or singer changes the tempo and the rhythm/accompanists have to follow the tempo changes.

Previous posters have pointed out situations in which one or the other of these two forms of rubato may be appropriate.

1) Rhythmic flexibility within a phrase or measure; a relaxation of strict time.
2) Flexibility of tempo in performance

Re: Keeping time vs …

time keeping in an ensamble is by concensus, really. One guy insisting that he is “right” will kill the time feel just as fast as a guy who is all over the place. We all work together to establish the time feel becuase where exactly “One” is is not absolute. We have to deal with human perception, and we are not chronometers.

that’s why you hear about people playing “behind the beat”, and why the downbeat in a 70s funk band is so fat. There are a few milliseconds on either side of dead on, and that’s what we have to work out among ourselves in an ensamble

when it’s done right, it feels like you are riding a well tuned bicycle. When it’s not, it can feel like you are dragging an anchor.

OK Ben, to continue. Many discussions here have ended in acrimony, and some have been deleted, because experienced people here were in denial of the that information we have about KB and metronomes. At least one messenger was shot.

So the beginner is left to come to their own conclusions about metronomes from the debris of failed discussions. At the moment I have it resolved down to two things.

A. A metronome can be used as an aid to technique. If one sets out to play a series of notes in time with a metronome one has to learn to hear what is and is not in time and to develop the motor skills to sound the note on the click. If someone can’t hear that their timing is off and correct the problem they are not going to be able to play tunes well. People who play the way ethical blend just described probably don’t need this sort of practice - but they may well recognise that others do and recommend it.

B. When played well dance tunes have a constant beat (as eb has just said). This may be an implicit beat, not always marked out by the attack of individual notes, but a even a non-musician listener who likes dance music will appreciate the rhythm of the tune relative to that steady beat. A musician needs a solid feel for that beat. If they are doing complex things which their timing as part of marking out that beat (‘playing across the bar lines’ etc) they may conceivably find it useful to have an explicit representation of the beat when practicing a tune - even if they can play the way eb just described. And they may recognise that others would find it helpful to practice in that way.

How does that sound for a theory ?

Metro Gnome

I played trombone for 40 years off and on, never used a metronome, and was never allowed to tap my foot. I just followed the little stick or in the case of a brass choir the first trumpet. I have been playing whistle now for 10 days and I am working on the skeleton of two tunes for my first slow session. I use a metronome (website) to keep me from playing the easy parts too fast, identify places in the tunes I need to work on, and to track my progress.

The metronome reminds me to stay on one tempo while my brain is thinking six other things like this is the part where you put down two fingers and then two more, or use the cross finger here but the half hole in the C part, or why is this note not clear do I have a leak or is there moisture build-up, or does this bit sound better tonged or slurred. I find a tempo I can play the entire tune at but I don’t try to put every note exactly on the beat. If I miss a note or squeak I just keep going on tempo. After once through I work on the spots that didn’t flow easily. When I think I can speed up the tune and stay mistake-free, then I speed up the tune about 10 beats per second and play it through again. After 30 minutes I put the whistle down and wander around the house for a few minutes. Then I come back and play the tune without the metronome or dots just for me at whatever tempo strikes my fancy.

So as a beginner I have found a metronome useful for learning to play the skeleton of a tune from beginning to end at a constant tempo and for gradually increasing the executable tempo of a tune by identifying trouble spots.

Re: Keeping time vs …

I think the whole point (and well-put by fiddlelearner) is that an external reference always helps, be it for posture, timing, intonation and anything else to do with playing an instrument, regardless of whether you play diddley or anything else … whether you’re a beginner, improver or accomplished player.

That external reference can be a metronome, a teacher, or a bunch of really good musicians playing well. The fact that Kevin Burke sometimes uses a metronome when practising, tells us a lot, I think. Maybe he even uses a tuner / tuning fork to get his A string spot-on, too.

A metronome is a tried and tested tool, or external reference, for helping to keep strict tempo. Forget phrasing, the concept of ‘the beat’, pulse, accents and anything else. At this level, these concepts only obfuscate the issue of maintaning strict tempo, which is what a metronome is meant to enforce.

A bunch of good musicians playing really well, as in eb’s heavenly session earlier, is another form of external reference (if we take eb as the “subject” for the sake of argument, and btw I’m sure his timing is very good anyway), and as such it would be a very good one - that cannot be denied.

As for advice as to whether a novice player should use a metronome to enforce strict tempo, I honestly think that is better advice than ‘why don’t you just tap your foot?’, ‘you need to internalise it’, ‘listen to others’, ‘get yourself a tin whistle’, or anything else.

I keep harping on about strict tempo. Keeping good, solid time. That’s all. Phrasing, pulse, accents, syncopation - they are all important, but metronome use has no bearing on them, and won’t help develop them either. Only human experience will. Has anyone honestly heard a human play like a metronome, or a MIDI file, as a result of using one? Many would like to think it does. It makes me wonder.

Going back to the OP, I believe it’s still possible to execute all the subtleties of rhythm, phrasing, etc while still ‘sitting on the click.’

Re: Keeping time vs …

“he takes a tune he more or less knows already.He sing the tune to himself, in his head, and then sets the metronome to that speed”

Well that’s the main use for a metronome. To set a tempo. It really has nothing to do with timing.

All the issues with bad timing come from poorly developed motor skills and/or not hearing one’s self properly.

Motor skills can be tightened up pretty easily. It takes a bit of time, either practice or playing, but it’s pretty straight forward. It’s merely about getting your fingers/lbreath etc to obey your brain.

So the real question is why do people have trouble hearing themselves? I think they more than likely have trouble hearing any properly … and this is, of course, why the metronome doesn’t help them.

I’d say that of the two kinds of people who have bad timing, those who know they have bad timing and those that don’t, the former suffer mostly from underdeveloped motor skills. The latter, however, have a harder problem to solve … not least because of their ignorance.
