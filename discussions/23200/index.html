LOL, careful Michael--your advice is a tad deconstructionist. :-D

Bad Finger, try this little piece of a tune (lifted from Humours of Tulla):

Bow DOWN on the ring finger D on the 2nd string
Bow UP on the index finger F# on the first string and do a roll with your left hand fingers
Now bow DOWN on the ring finger A on the first string
and bow UP again on the index finger F# with another roll

If that feels too awkward, just pay a ringer finger cut on those F#s instead of a full roll. And if *that* feels too awkward, then leave out any left hand twiddly bits and just do the bowing:

DOWN on d for a count of one
UP on f# and hold for a count of one two three
DOWN on a for a count of one
UP on f# for a count of one two three

That’s just one very common way to play this sort of phrase.

It makes a difference depending on what string you are playing, where on the bow you are playing and what direction the bow is moving, all these things affect the weight of the bow, and the precise amount of resistance has to be applied with the right hand to carry that weight or the bow will fall out of rhythm. It is just a matter if you learn with your brain, or you simply play enough difficult bowing until your hand muscles memorize all the possible scenarios

Re: Bowing on Rolls.

Just my $0.02, but I think of rolls as purely a left hand twiddly bit, having nothing to do with which direction my bow is going and everything to do with lending pulse (in just one way of many) to the tune. To play this music with verve and spontaneity, pulse has to happen regardless of which direction your bow is going.

It’s important to be able to create emphasis, and hence pulse, on both down bows and up bows, with a single bow and within slurred notes on a long bow, on one string or when crossing strings, with the bow never leaving the string, and both popping off the string or pouncing back onto it.

In short, no matter what the bow is doing, you want to be able to create pulse with it.

↳ Posted by Gallowglass on November 29th, 2009.

Re: Bowing on Rolls.

Bear in mind that it’s not unusual to play those up bows slower than neighboring down bows. It can be fairly common in this music to bow phrases like the one I outlined from Humours of Tulla above, where the down bow is usually faster than the up bows because you’re recovering in one eighth note stroke roughly the same distance as the equivalent of three notes up bowed on the roll.

Long and short of it (sorry) is that to get good crisp, clean rolls, you really need to relax those left-hand fingers and move with quick finesse. As long as the bow stroke is strong and clear, that won’t affect the roll one way or the other.
