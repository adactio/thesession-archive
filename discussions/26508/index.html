Re: This is what annoys me about this site.

No, there is a difference between what a ‘trained musician’ would call the key signature, and what an experienced folk musician would call the key signature.
D major has two sharps, but so does A mixolydian, because those are the two notes that need to be sharpened on the stave when writing out the tune; the difference is that the A mix tune will have a strong A major chord hinted at for its primary accompaniment, plus probably only G major and D major, and is also likely to end on an A.
Plus; different people can, perfectly reasonably, perceive a tune as being one or the other, and it might all turn on a single phrase that hints at one thing to one person, another to another.

Re: This is what annoys me about this site.

If you want to (ahem) accompany ITM on guitar, zouk, piano, etc., you need to understand the modes that are used in this music.

Granted, the modal approach to tunes is not generally taught. I had piano performance studies at the university level and don’t recall learning about the modes back then.

But there is really good information available on this subject. I recommend Chris Smith’s book, “Celtic Back-Up for All Instrumentalists,” and Grey Larsen’s “Essential Guide to Irish Flute and Tin Whistle.” Both have very clear explanations of the meaning and importance of modes.

So why does this matter? Well, if I’m leading a set and come to the end of a tune, it matters a lot to the guitarist I’m playing with if I call out “D” (major) or “A mix.” The proper call will get him off to a good start on the next tune.

Re: This is what annoys me about this site.

“A Mixolydian” isn’t a key signature.

“Two sharps” is a key signature. “One flat” is a key signature.

True that “normal” musicians will often call, for short, a key signature by it’s Major key. Thus a key signature of two sharps might be called “D”, but this doesn’t mean that the piece of music has a D Major tonal centre- it just refers to the key signature of two sharps.

The tonal centre of many Irish tunes is open for debate. But if a tune has all the Cs sharp and all the Fs sharp it can be written in a key signature of two sharps, which can be referred to as the key signature of “D”, regardless of the tune’s tonal centre. Writing a tune in the key signature of D does not neccessarily imply a tonal centre of D.

In short, key signature is different from tonal centre.

Anyhow, it’s why I don’t specify key signature when I use the search function, and take the “key” specified with a grain of salt.

What annoys ME about the search function is that it’s not “smart”. There can be a dozen ways of notating the same phrase which all sound more or less equivalent to the ears, but the search only gives a match if it’s exactly the same. (For example there are several ways of notating roll situations.)

Re: This is what annoys me about this site.

@Richard D Cook: Even in the conventions of Western art music, two sharps doesn’t necessarily mean the key of D major: It can signify D major or B minor. One has to listen to the music to discern which is correct.

Similarly, in ITM, two sharps can connote several different modes. And the mode is more than the tonal center: For example, tunes in A mix and A dorian both have the same tonal center (A), but their modes are different.

But if you get the tonal center correct when you submit a tune -- say, if you correctly identify ‘A’ as the tonal center of Diplodocus -- that’s helpful to accompanists, even if you don’t know or can’t tell whether the mode is, say, A mixolidian or A dorian or A minor (aeolian). An error on the mode is a small foul.

I concur with Ben Steen that it’s most helpful to get the tonal center correct for the beginning of the tune.

Re: This is what annoys me about this site.

I think we’re substantially in agreement on the major points of this discussion. At the risk of unnecessarily prolonging the thread, allow me to try to summarize areas of agreement for those who haven’t completely tired of it.:

1. The key signature of a tune merely shows the default sharps and flats.

2. Looking at the key signature alone, you can’t tell what key the tune is in or whether it’s major (Ionian mode), minor (Aeolian mode), or one of the other modes (in ITM, usually Mixolydian or Dorian). You have to listen to or otherwise analyze the tune to determine key/mode. Sometimes the result is ambiguous.

3. Similarly, you can’t identify the tonal center from the key signature alone. Again, the result may be ambigious.

4. Standard abc notation of a tune is supposed to indicate the mode in the headers and again if the mode changes. Valid modes in abc (see http://abcnotation.com/wiki/abc:standard:v2.0#kkey) capture both the tonal center and the mode.

5. There is nothing on this site or in any abc software, to my knowledge, that enforces correct entry of the tonal center and mode. This may be a weakness of the abc system, but one that is especially apparent on a site that displays user-generated content. It’s not a fault of the mustard board.

6. Some of us -- probably a small minority -- are bugged by tunes that are entered (here and on other sites) with incorrect modes embedded in the abc. To me, having the correct mode helps place the tune in a ‘tune family,’ and having the correct mode is useful to accompanists. Others (probably most) don’t care, as long as the dots generated from the abc come out correctly.

Finally, one minor area of disagreement: “The key signature of two sharps is called ”D“ as a shorthand” (Richard D Cook) and “many folks use the ‘major key shorthand’ of calling a piece in 2 sharps (in ‘D’) when all they mean is that the key signature has 2 sharps (cboody).” I consider that usage incorrect, and I’ve never heard it in the circles I play in, but if it’s acceptable and customary in your music communities, so be it.

Hmm. Maybe some practical remediating advice?

I’m a big fan of properly choosing the modes for a tune & to make them easier to find, but also recognize this is not a topic generally and/or meaningfully covered in typical music education. Therefore, how do we fix this?

Backend: Having some calculated field in the DB that takes the tune’s K: field (if any) and splits it into two parts, key signature (sharps/flats) and tonal center. Allow advanced searching on these calculated fields. That way those of us looking for an A mixolydian tune can filter the search on 2 sharps and turn those 10000 tunes into a more manageable 5000 tunes.

Front end: allow up/down voting on mode choices, maybe allowing citations of recordings and backing chord selections to provide supporting evidence for purported modes which can be independently verified. If 80% of your guitar/piano backers agree a piece is solidly A mixolydian, it might be worthwhile to change the listed key from C# locrian.

None of this will ease the suffering of those that try to search for tunes that waver between raised/lowered thirds (Bank of Ireland, Hunter’s Purse as performed by Beeswax Sheepskin) or tunes that are pentatonic where the tune never hit scale step 4 or 7 which could affect key signatures as applied to that note, but it will be a start, and having mode assertions be accompanied by evidence may help educate those who haven’t had exposure to the topic.

While I’m recommending mechanisms for improving the site, I think user ratings applied to tunes might be useful. How awesome is the tune? How clean/accurate is the transcription? Maybe if we could annotate our comments to indicate a block of ABC, then alternate versions of tunes could be farmed from the discussion and surfaced under the ABC/Sheetmusic tabs. This plus ratings would allow clean, useful, high-quality transcriptions to rise to the top.

Just a few thoughts.
