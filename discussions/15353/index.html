Re: you either have it or you dont!

Session savage and cuchulain54, what you say about the difference between children’s and adults‘ ability to learn to play is so interesting, because I have found the exact opposite to be true of me - which might be why it took me fifteen months as an adult to get as good on the fiddle as I got on the piano in nine years as a child. When I was a kid, I thought I knew everything, and never accepted *anything* adults told me as fact. I argued incessantly with authority, and much of the time I was right and they were wrong, so of course I wouldn’t accept what my piano teacher told me, even though she knew far more than I did. As an adult, although I am still stubborn and contrary, I gladly accept that my teacher knows more about fiddling and learning to fiddle than I do, and I trust him absolutely. But I think that it’s important that teachers gain their adult students’ trust by explaining *why* we need to practice a certain skill the way we’re told to practice it - otherwise, as session savage says, we’re liable to find shortcuts that don’t end up helping us in the long run. In my case, it took *months* of my teacher urging me to practice more slowly before I finally internalized the message. What did the trick, was him explaining to me how practicing slowly was remapping my brain, and how I didn’t have the mental capacity to be thinking consciously about every time I moved a finger of my left hand or changed bow direction or pressure at speed. The skills involved in those things, he told me, had to come from muscle memory, not conscious thought, and the only way to make that happen was to reinforce what I was playing, slowly and repeatedly.

I was chatting with a fellow adult student who started a few months after me. She’s just as enthusiastic a beginner as I am, and puts as much time into her instrument as I put into mine, but I was far ahead of her when I’d been playing as long as she’s been playing now. I attribute that to the fact that I don’t argue with my teacher or tell him how to instruct me, while she’s told me that she’s the one paying her teacher, and hence should be dictating the exact terms of her lessons.

And, oh, cuchulain54, I take tremendous joy in small successes, far moreso than I did as a child on the piano! As a child, I could only tell when I played a piece “well enough”, and I never did. As an adult on fiddle, I can tell you, to the week, when I was able to bow each string separately without hitting adjacent strings; when I was able to bow parallel to the bridge; when I nailed a functional vibrato; when my rolls started to sound like ornaments, as opposed to a series of five notes labouriously cobbled together for no particular reason; when my triplets came out crisp most of the time; when I got to the point that I could instinctively see bowing patterns as part of the tune, not something to be painfully written in as an afterthought; when my right wrist, unbidden, loosened…the list goes on, and is still growing.

↳ Posted by Tall, Dark, and Mysterious on October 1st, 2007.

Re: you either have it or you dont!

I am not one who believes in the “musically gifted” to a large extent. Yes, there are the very few who show incredible prowess, even at a very young age. But I think *very* few of the great players of this tradition probably fit that description. I think most of them got good because of the countless hours of time, determination, and dedication that they put into it. And to be told that they’re only good because they were “born with it” is an insult to the amount of work that they did to get where they are!

For a long time, I considered myself to be “not musically gifted”, even though I played guitar. It wasn’t until I started playing Irish that I found the love for the music to the point that it made me truly motivated to get good at it. It’s that motivation, and the willingness to work very hard at it that separates people that are good at it from people who don’t ever quite get it. It’s not some “god given” inherent talent that you’re born with.

Having said that, I think there is something to be said for “grasping” the music at a young age. Or, at least, being exposed to it. I think that people that grew up hearing Irish traditional music have an *easier* time learning it, even if they don’t start playing until later in life.

Those of us that were raised outside of the tradition are at somewhat of a disadvantage, but that disadvantage can be overcome by immersing yourself in it. There’s no doubt that you have to listen to a *lot* of Irish music to be able to play it well. The nuances of what makes the music sound Irish are not things that are natural to those of us who grew up listening to other forms of music, like rock, jazz, or country. And are foreign even to people who grew up with other forms of traditional music.

An outright statement that you had to grasp it as a child is ludicrous. There are numerous professional musicians that play Irish quite well that didn’t come to it until later in life.

Pete

Re: you either have it or you dont!

Hmmm. I’ve taught music to all ages, as young as 5 and as well-aged as 70. And everything in between. Starting from scratch (no previous musical training), what seems to matter most is whether the person enjoys making music, and how much time they play each day and cummulatively through the time between lessons.

People who actively listen and respond to music tend to have a better ear and knack for recreating music than people who don’t listen to music. But it’s rare that someone shows up for a first lesson with no urge to play based on intentional listening to music they enjoy. (It can happen--parents drag their kid to a lesson on an instrument they no nothing about to learn a music they’ve never heard. But that’s not the norm with trad music.)

A big part of teaching someone to play an instrument is teaching them how to listen more closely to the music--to the parts (tone, timbre, pitch, volume, rhythm, timing, etc.), and also to the whole. The better they hear what they’re listening to, the more likely it is to inform their playing. And they progress more quickly and more fully this way.

That works for kids and adults alike.

When I was a teenager, I was a professional juggler. I dabbled in it for a few years, then went to an international competition. A year later, I won several categories of that same competition, “coming out of nowhere” to end up a world class juggler in one year. People asked how I did it. I wasn’t more talented--in fact, I had a nerve disorder that affected my hands. The answer was two-fold: (1) I had a solid grasp of the fundamentals from 3 prior years of basic juggling, and (2) I put in a minimum of ****56 hours a week**** of practice ***every week*** during that one year. (This *is* the thread about obsessive-compulsive disorders, right?)

So in one year I put in around 3,000 hours of practice. That was more than many other jugglers did in 10 or 12 years.

And it was attentive, intentional practice.

The physical skills and nuanced coordination of making music aren’t much different. They require a certain number of hours of repetition to master. You can put those hours in every day and improve from week to week, or you can put those hours in a little this week, a little next week, and see the same improvement spread over months.

All of the blindingly good players have put in massive numbers of hours honing their skills.

Luckily, Irish trad is “easy” (as Michael likes to remind us) and so takes only a decade or so of intense effort to master.

(Not that I’m advocating playing music 56 hours a week--unless you really want to, your body can handle the punishment, and your housemates are all in a vegetative state already. Better to find a pace that suits you, include rest and recovery time (where a lot of the learning sinks in), and enjoy the trip.)

I’m coming into this discussion a little late, but concerning vibrato, the early 19th century violinist, composer and famous teacher, Louis Spohr, wrote a very detailed book on playing the violin, a book that even today has much to teach us. *

On page 136 of U.C. Hill’s US translation Spohr talks about “tremolo” (his term for vibrato). This is part of what he wrote. It’s still relevant:

“It [vibrato] consists in the wavering of a stopped tone, which alternately extends a little below or above the perfect intonation, and is produced by a trembling motion of the left hand, in the direction from the nut towards the bridge. This motion must, however, be slight and the deviation from the perfect intonation of the tone should hardly be perceptible to the ear. … avoid its frequent use or in improper places.” [n.b. Spohr refers to “vibrato” as “tremolo”]

Note that Spohr refers to hand movement - not arm movement – to produce the vibrato. He doesn’t explain any further how to do it (presumably leaving those details to the live teacher) but I think we can understand that if a pupil has progressed to that stage in Spohr’s violin course he/she will have a well relaxed left hand, wrist and fingers, and will be able to move freely up and down the fingerboard. Vibrato will then come quite naturally and needs only to be under the player’s full mental control. It should feel, and be, absolutely effortless, and should only be used to meet the needs of the music.

Spohr muat have been talking about the vibrato of his time, and dating back a century or more – in other words to the Baroque period, which is where Irish fiddle music has its roots. What I don’t like to see or hear, and what I think is definitely not in the Baroque or the 18th century Classical tradition, is the rapid “juddering” and unpleasant vibrato produced by what appears to be a nervous and tiring movement of the arm. Incidentally, I’ve also seen this being done far too often today by some classical players, who I think should really have been trained better.

The arm vibrato on the violin is, I suspect, a modern development arising from the prevelance of vibrato today in “classical” (in its broad sense) music, and, I think, even when done properly, doesn’t have a real place in ITM. A good occasional vibrato emanating from the hand and fingers seems far more suitable.

Incidentally, the cellist uses an arm vibrato because more amplitude has to be produced at the finger tips due to the length of string. Do this on the fiddle and if you’re not careful you’ll be doing a vibrato that’s too wide. I’ve known classical players use the same amplitude of arm-generated vibrato in high positions as they would in the first position. The musically unacceptable result is a vibrato on a high note that can extend over a semitone.

* You can download Spohr’s book for free in pdf format from the International Music Score Library Project at http://imslp.org/wiki/Main_Page

I didn’t start to learn the fiddle until I was 27 years old. For the following 20 years I didn’t have the opportunity to play much with other musicians, living as I did in a remote rural community where acoustic music had pretty much died out.

I also play guitar and mandolin, and I think the skill which has benefited my playing the most is a knowledge of chordal arpeggios and modes. Generally I can learn a tune “on the fly” in a session these days. I try to encourage fellow players to not become reliant on sheet music, but it’s like a security-blanket drug for most of them.

As for vibrato, it can become a bad habit to cover up faulty intonation on the fiddle. I can do it, but I try to avoid the technique. IMHO it has become an overused technique in modern classical playing, one reason I tend to prefer baroque recordings of violinists such as Manze and Wallfisch. Such retro violin-playing also seems to have some similarities to good Irish fiddling!
