Re: Who taught you the tunes?

@Harold:
“So Jeff would I be presumptuous in saying that maybe those few tunes you got from other people are the most memorable?”

It depends - the first tunes I learned properly on the fiddle (sixteen tunes) were in a one-on-one setting, and another ten or eleven learned on tenor banjo or just picked up by osmosis). Those have stuck pretty well, as have other tunes I share with that same person. I’ll always associate some tunes with sessions in Miltown Malbay (some of which I picked up in sessions, some which I already had on recordings - but the association will still be Willie Clancy Summer School), some with workshops (same story there - some tunes I already knew inside-out, but the musician reinforced my memory of the tunes).

Some tunes that I got from tune books and had played for years suddenly showed up on recordings many years later (the Battering Ram is one example - I got it from one of my first tin whistle books but to this day, I can’t play it without going into Kinnegad Slashers and then the Leg of the Duck - all are on “Jeannie C” by the band Oisín).

And so on. There are tunes I’ve learned from people but which I haven’t spent as much time with as sets I’ve put together myself/CDs I’ve listened to/recordings I’ve transcribed… The memory of a certain tune can relate to the time I heard it/learned it/reviewed it/played it with someone else/arranged it with a band/heard it in a concert/taught it etc.

Re: Who taught you the tunes?

being that I’m an American, and was living in a desert (yes with sand dunes and coyotes and roadrunners around, just like the cartoons) with no players around, much less sessions, and it was in the 1970s before the internet, I had to pick up tunes off albums (huge round black things). Whenever I drove up to the San Francisco Bay Area to visit my sister I’d stop into a big record shop in Berkeley which had a wonderful selection, and I bought gems like the Paddy Carty album and started learning those tunes.

Everything changed when I moved to Greater Los Angeles in 1976 and, in a folk music shop, ran into a guy who was a very good Irish-style fluteplayer who also played tenor banjo and box a bit. He took me under his wing and taught me quite a few tunes. I was playing a basic Ralph Sweet flute at the time, my first flute.

He taught me in a very special way that as the years have gone by I appreciate more and more: he would teach me a basic version, then when I had it under my fingers he would say “you know the tune, now let’s f!ck with it” by which he meant explore the tune at length through the means of a large number of variations. His axiom was “better to know 20 ways of playing one tune, than to know 20 tunes” and that’s how I was taught. I learned the mechanics of Irish music, how to construct tunes out of the building blocks, and rearrange the blocks to create endless variations.

Re: Who taught you the tunes?

Off albums, as the above correspondents, to start with, mostly…
But I was lucky to be in the right place at the right time, when I started going along to the Thursday night sessions at the Green Man in Harlesden, NW London from 1985-1991, which Mick O’Connor (banjo) provided lead, at a steady pace. Those steady paced sessions lead me into a false sense of security, as I then embarked on exploring other (faster) London sessions in Camden and Holloway Road.
I cringe now at my innocent boldness, lack of finesse, general mediocre playing, but I have somehow come out the other side and survived, and I hope, flourished - most often helped by the kindness, patience and generosity of more seasoned players. I only hope I can emulate their attitude when I encounter younger or genuine learner players.
And I don’t mean the ubiquitous boorish spoons or bodhran or 3-chord guitar hacks incapable of ever becoming musicians but who insist in drowning out the listening to of any real musical talent, in preference to their own senseless battering. These people will never be musicians. They cannot learn. But they are too stoopid to know they cannot learn.
By learners I mean people who so love the music that they are genuinely inspired to learn to play it. Some people are quick learners others are slower, it doesn’t matter, if after a period of practice they can play some nice tunes, and contribute to peoples’ listening.

(just to edit - there are several fine guitar players and even bodhran players. These are not the ubiquitous hacks to whom I refer.)

I and my mate Paidin were up late one night playing tunes in front of the turf fire, having a nip of potcheen too as you will, and it was getting very late when down the chimney comes a wee luradan hoping to have a go at the bottle. Paidin and I seized him by the foot and arm, and he cried out that he would teach us a tune if we would but let him go. So he taught me the first part and he taught the turn to Paidin. Paidin was much deeper into the drink than I was, poor lad, and the next morning could not remember the turn half so well as I could recall the tune. That’s why when we play the tune “An Luradan” the first part has a bit more draíocht than the second part.

Re: Who taught you the tunes?

I was taught very few of my tunes one-to-one. I’ve learned a handful in classes or workshops. The vast majority, I have learned from a combination of sessions, recordings and sheet music - that is to say, I rarely sit down and work out a tune note-by-note from a recording or learn it from sheet music, but I form an impression of a tune when I first hear it, then fill in the details on successive hearings, sometimes also referring to transcriptions. When I was newer to traditional music, I was more likely to form those ‘impressions’ by browsing through printed or online collections, since I didn’t know where to hear the music (…there was no YouTube).

If I think about it, most of the tunes I play probably have some kind of memory associated with them - a particular session, recording, player, class, book, place, era of life etc. But, as I alluded to above, I could not necessarily cite a *single* source for a tune. Usually, the strongest memory is the first time I heard the tune.

Re: Who taught you the tunes?

Wrong question, imo… a better question is “Who gave you the music?”

I think a fair few have alluded to this already… I believe having a tune in your repertoire that reminds you of a particular person adds a huge emtional element to the tune itself. I’ve often heard performers introduce a tune with a story, or a memory, or sometimes just a mention of somebody: “So, I’m playing that tune for X” I used to wonder if it was a subtle form of name-checking, or if it were some way of remembering particular tunes or sets - “now, what was it X used to play that with? ah yes…”

As I’ve got older though, I feel there’s more to it than that. It’s about a journey.

For me, the very first tunes I ever learnt were learnt at home, from my dad and my sister (who started playing the flute before I picked up the fiddle), and these were slow tunes, mostly airs and planxtys, some Scottish waltzes, basically stuff we could pick through together.

The next tunes, the basics of repertoire I suppose, I learnt by the Dreaded Osmosis (read: bad versions, not well played, hashed out too fast with the wrong rhythm - mostly due to my inexperience at learning by ear) at a local session. I still have strong associations with some of these tunes and the people that played them, although in many cases I’ve relearnt other versions. But when I’d learnt all the regular tunes, and I wanted something else, what then? These, I call by the name of the session.

I learnt a huge (literally vast - thousands of tunes) from an american chap called John Knowles, who I know will be known to a few on this site. A lot of these tunes I still play, others have faded, but most are still in there somewhere, and again, I have strong memories of the tunes I learnt in his front room, drinking tea and eating nasty american malteasers. (American chocolate… yuck) So, these are John Knowles tunes. Around this time, I met Bev Whelan and Michael Feeley, and learnt a similarly huge amount from them - Mike and Beverley tunes.

Then summer schools, workshops, sessions, any number of incredibly generous musicians, all of whom gave their music freely, all of whom I associate different tunes or sets or anecdotes with. And, obviously, CDs, tapes, concerts and more sessions - all the places where a little shade of something different rubs off on you without you knowing.

I don’t think it comes down, really, to who taught me this tune or that, but more about the effect that person had in the course of giving that piece of music. Something about their style might have stuck, or a particular way they ornamented a tune, or wove their bow. Sometimes, when I play a tune, the association with an individual is so strong that it changes the whole way I play it. The stronger that association, the stronger the effect on my own playing. I learnt the tunes. But they gave me the music, each and every one of them.
