Session problems: What would you have done?

Just had our usually monthly session. We had 8 or 9 playing (it varies from about 6 to 15) and I think we sounded our very worst. Specifically, people were not at all together on the beat. The pulse of the music was entirely missing. And a couple of listener friends later confirmed that we were not at all together.

Some core members discussed the problem afterward. We agreed the problem was mostly two perpetrators. One is very solid on a couple different instruments, but was learning a new one, trying to figure out how to add a bass line, and was consistently at least one quarter beat behind on “unsure” notes. The other knows tons of tunes, plays a melody instrument that’s quite loud, but either can’t perceive the tempo others are using, or can’t manage to deviate from his own default - usually, faster - tempo on a given tune. So one was consistently ahead of the beat, one usually behind, others couldn’t figure out the beat. The result was cacaphony.

At one point I asked our (solid) guitarist to change to a seat more central to the circle, hoping others would key into his sound, or key in visually to his strumming hand. I took to stomping on the floor (and I _think_ I was on the beat; I’ve gotten compliments on that). But nothing seemed to cure the problem until each of the offenders left.

They’re both nice people, and usually valued as players. I didn’t give direct advice in the moment, in part because I’ve seen people take great offense from being corrected during a session. But I did make a mild comment or two about the problem. And afterwards, my usual session mates said “There was nothing more you could have done.”

Is that true? How would others have handled the problem? And since it’s after the fact, would you perhaps take them aside before the next session? Send an email? Give a phone call?

Re: Session problems: What would you have done?

With issues like that, I usually try to broach the subject gently with the offending party privately, either after the session or over phone/text/email later. You can maybe approach the subject by asking if they were experiencing the same issues, instead of outright accusing them of being the culprit. And you can certainly start the conversation by telling them that they’re valued members of the group!

I will occasionally say something during a session if there are issues like that, but I do that as a general statement to the whole group, instead of singling people out. I would say something like “Wow, we were really struggling to keep it together there… On this next set, why don’t we all try to concentrate on keeping the rhythm a bit tighter”… That at least gets people thinking about it, if they weren’t already.

For the player that was learning the new instrument, I would (privately) tell them directly that they were being a distraction. They would likely be embarrassed to know that, especially if they’re normally a solid contributor on other instruments.

And I feel for you, it’s a difficult subject to broach in just about any situation, but can usually be dealt with in a gentle way without ruffling too many feathers.

EDIT: Note, it helps that I am one of the session “anchors” at both of my sessions (and I am responsible for both of them existing). So that makes it easier for me to do the talking about this stuff. If there isn’t an actual “session leader” or anchor, that makes it more difficult for someone to speak up about these things. You mention “core members”, so that might be the same thing, if people recognize those players as being the “core”.

Re: Session problems: What would you have done?

Rhythm and tuning/intonation, and sometimes sheer volume, are the main musical issues that can derail a session. In all three instances, you can address the problem in the moment with a sense of humor and without assigning blame.

Often, simply calling attention to the issue is enough. “Anyone else lose the beat on that one? Reminded me of the time Uncle Jimmy dropped a box of snooker balls down the stairwell.”

For tuning, you can ask the box player for “one of his A’s” and then play a Bb against it and say, “That’s wet tuning, all right.” And then suggest, “Maybe time for a group tune up.”

When you say the problem out loud like this, be lighthearted and funny about it. Chances are, people will be relieved to hear it acknowledged. If the circle is mostly friends, someone may even own up to being the epicenter of the arrhythmia. Even if people squirm a bit, that’s better than letting a big problem fester only to repeat itself next week, and so on. Ask yourself, do you want a session that plays in time, in tune, and at an agreeable volume, or do you want a train wreck? If the former, then someone has to speak up.

If the public comedy act doesn’t work, and if you’re the session leader, you can talk with the person in private. Be encouraging, and focus on the problematic behavior, not the person—“Hey, you’re really coming along on the box, but it seemed like the basses were throwing you off.” Leave room for them to reveal how they perceived it, which will influence how you continue the conversation. A contrite, “Yeah, I need to woodshed more before adding the basses at the session,” means you’ve likely already said enough. In contrast, “Bliddy heck, it wasn’t my basses, it was the goat thumper next to me,” indicates you have a thornier problem.

If you’re not the session leader (and maybe even if you are), it’s usually best to first talk amongst the core players to (1) see if you’re in general agreement about the nature of the problem, and (2) decide as a group how best to proceed. This way, if you do talk with the person, you speak with the group’s support. You don’t have to divulge that up front, but it may come in handy if things get ugly.

Finally, if these two are “nice people,” as you say, then a respectful, cheerful approach should work. If it doesn’t, then maybe you don’t know them as well as you thought you did. ;-)

Re: Session problems: What would you have done?

Trish, sorry, I was agreeing with you as well, just poorly worded.

Whether sessions are “a place to learn by playing” is a murky issue. Yes, you need at least basic chops on your instrument and a few tunes to be able to participate. (And we need to keep woodshedding at home.) But beyond that, the best way to learn how to play in a session is by playing in sessions. What do we learn this way? The basics include the social and musical etiquette (often unspoken), the nature and scope of the repertoire, how to play your own instrument while listening to others, and how to blend in with others (pace, pulse, timing, swing, intonation, etc.). Later, some people gain more advanced skills like picking up variations and unobtrusively learning tunes on the fly, talking while playing, leading unpremeditated sets, etc. And sessions offer a lifetime of learning to listen ever more attentively, more closely.

Thinking back on it, sessions were where I learned the mechanics of matching anyone else’s sense of timing and swing. Also, that the key to playing fast is to use smaller motions, less effort, and to stay relaxed. And how to accompany songs (less is more), how to create lift and pulse at different paces, including in slow tunes, and more.

Re: Session problems: What would you have done?

Tangential to the original topic, but relevant for anyone who wants to practice in sessions without disrupting the music or bothering anyone:

I used and still use sessions as a place to practice rhythm and learn tunes, without bothering anyone, by (quietly!) tapping fingers and feet, (silently!) lilting the tunes, and at the same time practicing posture - straight up, no slouching or leaning back - and getting a bit of flexibility exercise swaying the head and torso with the music, and on top of all that and most importantly, practicing relaxing.

I’d sit in my chair holding my fiddle on my knee, and tap my left hand fingers on the back of the fiddle (gently, not to make noise, just to match the timing). Then when that’s going in time with the music, tap the right fingers as well. Then I practiced tapping one foot. Then the other foot. Then both feet together. Then one foot tapping both up and down beat. Then both feet tapping, one on both beats and the other only on the down beat. Then lilting the tune in my head as it goes. Then mouthing the lilting, but not aloud. Then add in swaying the head sort of with the curvature of the notes. And sway the whole torso a bit, and the arms. And combine all these together till now I can do all of them at once in time with a fast reel. I think this has dramatically helped my ability to play in time - and I can play quite a few reels now with a steady rhythm, where I could not a couple years ago. I also have learned a lot of tunes by silently lilting them in sessions, and it’s much easier to find them on the fiddle. Plus finger dexterity - For example I’ve tapped my left little finger on the fiddle far more times doing this in sessions, and much faster, than I have while practicing alone.

The relaxing layer on top of all of it is very important. All the while, I’m cycling through all the parts of the body checking that they are relaxed, or relaxing them. Left fingers, right fingers, left wrist, right wrist, left shoulder, etc etc. As I’d add in each part of this “embodying” of the music, I’d get the new part going and then go through the relaxing cycle with every part.

I still do all of this and see no reason to stop. It’s still the case that 95% of the tunes I hear in sessions I can’t really play, let alone at speed, so this is a way to turn all that time into valuable practice time while really getting into the music, learning the tunes, and keeping the body loose and relaxed. And it’s fun.

Among friends, and when it’s noisy enough that I can do it unobtrusively, I’ll hold up the fiddle and bow, and start to find the tune, silently at first, just resting the bow on the strings without dragging it up or down, but finding the left hand fingering and which string the bow is on -- intuitively/automatically, i.e. somewhat randomly at first, not thinking about the notes. All the while doing the same relaxing practice. This brings the whole practice closer to the fiddle. And when I’m confident enough of some of the notes I can start to actually bow them quietly. I’ve finally begun to find some tunes this way and now there are quite a few tunes I play along with that I haven’t yet sat and learned.

I recommend this way of making use of sessions to anyone. Particularly if like me you can’t yet blend unobtrusively with your instrument most of the time.

meself, that’s an interesting observation. Maybe I’m rhythm hypersensitive. But I think of it as merely a quality of this music. A group playing tunes together really clicks only when everyone’s timing is in sync. When someone starts a set, it’s important to listen to whether they’re playing smack on the beat, a little behind the beat (“lope”), or a little ahead of the beat (“drive”). This can change from set to set, sometimes even from tune to tune (i.e., some sets start off lopey and shift into drive). I prefer sessioning with musicians who pay attention at this level of detail.

One or two further observations on some of the comments above…

You do get sessions which “go wrong”, some which are too big and noisy where you can’t hear, and others which often contain one or two..maybe more..less able musicians. Yes, you can exercise your right not to attend or take a seat but it may be the only session or opportunity in town for some people. Also, as has been mentioned, it might not always be like that. As regards a regular member playing an instrument on which he/she is less able, I don’t see an issue as long as they follow the proper and established etiquette for that session… i.e. take a more low key approach until confident and try not to disrupt proceedings. For the player with the second instrument, that also means a sense of “realisation” regarding one’s ability and potential contribution.

A “closed” or more private session is another thing, obviously, and there are always variable factors as to when and where you shouldn’t play or attempt to play if you are an novice on a particular instrument.

I can’t really improve on what people have said already - it’s an interesting thread. Tactful ways of telling people without hurting their feelings or their pride are vital & the suggestions above are spot on.

One thing (which has also been mentioned) - I’d look at the way people were sitting. It can be hard even for a good player to keep in time if they’re a long distance from the leader of the group. I remember at The Sage summer school that our tutor made us regroup in a circle as we were getting out of sync in a longish line.

I do look at good players nearby if they’re tapping their foot and use it to orient myself. I have a good sense of rhythm generally, but can sometimes speed the tune up a bit if I’m feeling exhilarated. Luckily we have a very level-headed banjoist in our local session!

Re: Session problems: What would you have done?

Based on the number of responses this seems to be a common problem. Speaking as an amateur musician, if my playing is causing a problem I prefer the direct approach. I find it belittling to see others whisper and talk about me in the 3d person. Asking me to correct a problem allows me to feel like I’m part of the group. Passive-aggressive behavior does the opposite. If you want your session group to be enjoyable for all then encourage learning as a process.

Re: Session problems: What would you have done?

A someone who plays online a lot, I would ask everyone to hit their re-synch button. Sometimes the peer-to-peer software negotiates an average latency that isn’t right. Online, though, the effect is that everyone begins to slow down to match the slowest player. Eventually, it can sound like a 45 record being played at 33 1/3 rpm (or whatever the metric equivalent is).

Finally, if that doesn’t work, you can start talking about latency numbers and ping times, coax versus fibre, etc. That gets everyone self-conscious about being the one slowing things down, and sometimes the offender will just drop off.

All of that is said tongue-in-cheek. I think it’s a very rare player who does not realize they’re behind the beat.

I don’t think there’s a recipe to follow. Such things usually work themselves out over the long run.
