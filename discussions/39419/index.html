left hand and wrist alignment on fiddle

I’m fairly new here, and a beginner fiddler. Could some more proficient fiddlers discuss the pros and cons of playing with a so-called “pancake hand” on the fingerboard? I mean, rather than keeping the hand in line with a straight wrist, flattening the hand and allowing the base of the palm/wrist to sometimes contact the fiddle. Many fiddle teachers and most instruction manuals say this is an absolute “no-no”, but I have noticed good players (and older players) using this position. I also find it more comfortable, as a small and small-handed person, for myself. Are there both musical and physical (long term problems?) reasons for not playing in this position? Thanks!

Re: left hand and wrist alignment on fiddle

Hi Carole,

There are very good reasons for keeping a straight wrist and avoiding the ‘pancake’ mistake.

First off, ‘pancaking’ shortens the reach of the fingers - the last thing you need, especially if you have small hands. A straight wrist is the natural position for playing. If you find the collapsed wrist more comfortable, it’s possible you’re not holding the neck properly, and/or you don’t have enough support at your chin. Are you using a shoulder rest? You shouldn’t be using your hand to support the weight of the instrument.

Secondly, pancaking will impede your ability to shift position effectively if the heel of your hand is touching the heel of the fiddle - you would need to straighten your hand to get out of 1st position. Most of the tunes are in 1st position, but you’d still need to get to at least 2nd position for some of them.

As for physical problems, I can’t say - I don’t have any stats for that, but I can say that it puts more strain on the joints simply because of the hand position.

I can confidently say that there is no advantage to using that hold. You mentioned players who use the collapsed wrist and play well - yes, some do, but that’s not a good reason for recommending you use it. It’s a limiting and restrictive hold.

This subject was brought up several times before in past discussions, and there will be those who argue that it doesn’t make any difference, and point you to people who do use it. Just wait and see - if they haven’t already done so by the time I’ve finished writing this :)

It’s all about good form and best practise, regardless of what type of music you play.

Re: left hand and wrist alignment on fiddle

Speaking as a teacher of fiddle… if “pancake” works for you, that’s fine. I’ve taught people who play that way, and didn’t seem to have a problem doing so. As you rightly say, a lot of older musicians (and those taught by older musicians) will play pancake, since they’ve learnt by example. But… as the two previous responders have already indicated, this comes at a cost. The flattening of the wrist restricts what your fingers can reach, until you’re able to adapt your playing to compensate for this. In particular it makes it much harder to keep the fingers in tune, and to accurately reach fourth fingers and even third fingers on the lower strings. I don’t know if you’re playing much in A, but those G# third fingers are a long way away if you’ve got a flat wrist. Now, there’s a reason for that restriction, and I think it’s important. Basically, by bending the wrist, you’re making the tendons inside it work around a corner, i.e. operating at greater strain than they do with a straight wrist. This is bad for two reasons. Firstly, it can cause considerable pain, and carries the risk of RSI type problems - just think, if they don’t recommend typing with bent wrists, then is sitting in a session for hours any better? Probably not. Secondly, remember that when you’re using the fingers, you aren’t engaging any muscles in your hand. The muscles that control your fingers are located in your forearm, up by your elbow. That’s why pipers have such freakish forearms. Honestly. That’s what’s moving my fingers right now as I type (with a straight wrist, of course). It’s because of this that i encourage my pupils to play with a straight wrist - because I don’t want to be teaching somebody something that’s going to cause them pain, either in the short term, or longer down the road. Posture is important. It would be irresponsible of me as a teacher not to try and protect them from physical problems, if I can avoid it.

So… although I generally try and encourage my pupils to play with a straight wrist, if you absolutely cannot play with a straight wrist, and you can make ‘pancake’ work without it causing you pain, then that’s okay. But I wouldn’t recommend it, and if you feel stiffness around your hands and wrists, it might be time to rethink.

Re: left hand and wrist alignment on fiddle

There are no advantages whatsoever to learning to play with a collapsed wrist, and the disadvantages are potentially catastrophic. They are so severe that I would regard any violin teacher who didn’t at least remark on it and recommend changing it to be guilty of malpractice.

The primary disadvantage is the heightened risk of repetitive stress injuries, which can be career killers. And depending on what you do for a living, that career killer effect may not even be limited to people who play violin for a living.

Someone remarked above that the pancake wrist forces the tendons in your wrists to work around a corner. This is true… and if you’ve ever repaired a clutch cable on a car with a manual transmission, you would intuitively see how this can become a problem.

The tendons in your wrists and fingers are not free-floating in space. They are held in place in a tube, of sorts, or series of smaller tubes and bands, called a “tendon sheath.” When a violinist plays with a collapsed wrist, the tendons are forced to work around the corner inside the tendon sheath, and must rub the sheath at the point of the angle. The more severe the angle, the more force is applied to the tendon -- or rather, all the force and friction that the tendon naturally encounters is concentrated at one point, right at the angle.

http://orthoinfo.aaos.org/topic.cfm?topic=a00015

Over time, with tens of thousands or hundreds of thousand of small contractions and releases, the cumulative damage to the tendon at that point eventually becomes felt. The small tears and frays will cause inflammation as the tissue tries to heal itself. But that causes the tendon to swell. That makes the problem worse, because the tendon sheath does not expand to accommodate the swollen tendon. The friction increases, causing more inflammation and swelling, increasing friction, and you’re in a vicious cycle that will not stop until you put the fiddle down. Hopefully the damage will not be more severe and some time will allow you to recover.

This happens sometimes even with good technique. There’s no use doing things that increase the risk - with no accompanying advantage in technical capacity.

Someone always mentions Liz Carroll. Well Liz Carroll is a fantastic player, of course. But I believe she is dealing with some painful left wrist problems herself these days.

It happened to another friend and terrific player… good enough to tour the world as an Irish fiddle player… who plays wonderfully with a collapsed wrist. I know it happened because when the act wasn’t touring I’d have to come in and cover for this player on hometown gigs for a weekend because of a (thankfully temporarily) disabling tendonitis.

I hope the same thing doesn’t happen to Martin Hayes. It hasn’t that I know of.

But I won’t be surprised if it does. And if you just take the two collapsed wrist players that everyone points to, and point out that among this limited, cherry-picked sample, dripping with selection bias, the injury rate is 50 percent after decades of playing, well, those aren’t very good odds.

There will always be some exceptions you can point to and say, “see? HE does it! And he’s a great player!” Well, Django was a transcendently good guitar player, but that doesn’t mean it’s a good idea to handicap yourself by gluing two fingers together on your left hand.

Be smart, and think ahead.

The pancake wrist is bad. Dangerously so.

Re: left hand and wrist alignment on fiddle

Just another 2p worth…

If I am teaching a player from scratch, (particularly younger folks) I don’t even mention pancake wrist. We start with correct posture and how to hold the instrument, and then we go from there.

If I am teaching a player who has already started with another teacher, they sometimes come to me with bad habits; I point out that these are likely to be harmful to playing, go over the correct posture and how to hold the instrument, and then we go from there.

If I am teaching a player who has already been playing for some time, and has well-established bad habits, I will point out AT LENGTH the likely consequences of continuing to play in this way, demonstrate correct posture and how to hold the instrument and try to move on. Some older players are unable by this stage to easily overcome their bad habits, and so, if I have literally no alternative other than throwing a hissy fit and refusing to teach them if they can’t do as they’re told (which is pointless) I will continue to drone on about their bad habits while teaching them anyway. This isn’t ideal, but sometimes you just can’t teach an old dog new tricks.

I wouldn’t ever teach someone to use pancake wrist, but if a player has no other choice, I will continue to teach them in whatever way they need, while making frequent reference to neck/shoulder/arm/wrist/finger pain. Okay?
