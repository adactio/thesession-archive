Re: D whistles dilemma

High D, Alison. I don’t even own a Low D - I play flute if I want to be down there. The brightness of the high D is only shrill until you learn to control it (unless you have one-of-those-whistles that should never have made it past Quality Control. Quality Control, what’s that, cried the whistle…)

In the meantime, if the whistle is too shrill for your ears and the household, you can invent the Whistle Calmer. Roll a pea-sized lump of poster putty into a cylinder the width of your whistle’s window, and apply it to the ramp, just behind the edge. That will really quiet the whistle, but leave it capable of the full range of notes. If that’s too quiet, roll it back from the edge until you have the balance you want. Forward for new tunes, backwards for triumphant celebration!

And, yes, the park is good, once you can get through a few tunes without crashing and burning! When I was living in a hostel and learning flute, someone yelled out “Ach, you will never be good!” and crashed their window closed. So I walked down to the lakeside nearby and played on the sand. And then I saw a wake in the water, coming my way. A tiny fish swam up to my feet until it ran out of depth and tumbled over. I stopped playing and leaned down to touch it, but it suddenly woke from its reverie and bolted for deeper water. It probably still tells its hatchlings about the day the wizard with the magic stick tried to lure it onto land….

Alison, (and anyone else who’s done that), I’m glad you got yourself a low G so fast, didn’t give up on the lower range of whistles, after experiencing problems with the low D. Dealing with an instrument that is consistently problematic with the mechanics of playing, is just a pain. I once had a Yamaha tenor recorder, which had a marvelous meditative tone, but the finger stretch of the right hand, which I eventually figured out by adjusting my elbow position, remained a restrictive experience and I sold it. Couldn’t play anything fast, but hated losing it. Whatever key it was, I wish there was a recorder made half-way between an alto and a tenor, and I’d keep one and play it regularly, because physically I could! After experiencing the lower range, and after playing concert flute so long, sticking with the alto whistle range is set with me.

I think most common whistles in high D sound like linear police whistles designed to scramble criminal minds during drug smuggling raids in port city warehouse districts. However, at one point I sprang for a Tony Dixon DX006 in high D and the full-range tone was a VAST improvement over anything like the Generation/Clarke toys I’d found. With slightly larger bore and superior mouthpiece design, it doesn’t squeal, it sings and responds so well. I expect their “Trad” cupro-nickel model is similar, since user comments trend that way. I still thought (after decades of playing concert flute), that the high D whistles have a very small windway, prone to fast accumulation of moisture clogging, which the alto range would basically solve/prevent.

Some other wider-bore high Ds I really like (most I’ve only seen played, not owned), and some with a variation on design that gives a more appealing tone. Prices vary greatly: Shearwater, Busker, Anak, Milligan, McManus, Humphrey, Goldfinch, Burke, Chris Wall.

One option that ended were the Bracker Whistles with extra holes, so that you can play chromatically using any starting “key” of whistle. Not very expensive, but the Bracker website has indicated they’re not in production again since the COVID stoppage. Lots of altos and various design options were available, and the altos also had the mouthpiece window wall added on three sides which improve tone and boost volume.

I have a sopranino recorder by Yamaha (YRN-302B II) and really like the tone. I think whistle players also considering non-high D options should be aware of it. It’s a high key (high F above high D) but the tone is very balanced and listenable, and being so high, it’s lost the nasally tone prominent in the sopranos. And it’s fully and easily chromatic. Nice bonus! Otherwise the only whistles I’ve heard higher than D with a nice tone were the Anak and a couple by Nick Metcalf (Caol Pro and Silver Star). The sopranino is very inexpensive, made extremely well and it’s durable. I get the full range of quality notes out of it. It’s my high range alternative to whistles. And on a tangent, wild birds love the tone of it. Northern Flickers, magpies, Canada geese, sparrows and crows seemed to really enjoy it.

The alto recorders also have a great tone where the nasally soprano tone is not prominent. To me, the alto F, G and A are the best of the normal whistle tones out there. I’ve also seen a few low C whistles and some low Ds by makes like Reviol and Alba that have outstanding tones. Getting that kind of tone in an alto, so the fingering is never a problem, solves it. I would love to try out a Bracker in G with the 9 holes/fully chromatic.

Re: D whistles dilemma

There are four main solutions to the shrillness inherent to a high whistle D, starting with the worst:
1. Play a high F or even high G (if your fingers fit) long enough to get through the B part of a tune at least once. Then switch back to a--comparatively--mellow and sonorous high D.
2. Play a high D with a pea-sized blob of poster putty on the ramp: adjust until both octaves are playable at a reduced volume. (A similar trick is to slide part of a wooden coffee stirrer into the mouthpiece to reduce the size of the windway.)
3.Play a lower-pitched whistle at home and save the high D for social occasions (sessions). (Whistles in the low G to Bb range bear kinship to higher whistles but with a more pleasant range.)
4. Play only a low D; in moderate session in a civilized pub, it’ll be fine. But it is, in some regards, a rather different instrument from a high D.

Note: I would recommend avoiding any sort of large-bore (sometime erroneously labeled “session”) whistle. Whatever benefits it might offer in the bottom octave are more than negated by a stiff-playing second octave that requires extra push and delivers more volume right where you don’t need or want it. The standard Generation/Feadog/whathaveyou bore size is standard for good reason!

Re: D whistles dilemma

Maybe this has already been mentioned.

Many newcomers to whistle discover that they have to “blow harder” to get the second octave. While there’s some truth to this, the reality is more nuanced than that. Often high D whistles sound shrill because they’re being overblown to hit the upper octave.

Yes, you have to give more air to get the upper octave, but the trick is to learn not to overblow it. The goal is to find the *least amount of extra air* needed to get good tone and intonation. This takes some experimentation and experience, and the break point is different for every whistle.

For the lower octave, the converse is true—you want to use the most amount of air without cracking toward the upper octave.

“high D” whistle, just a way to disambiguate. a “D whistle” is the high-D whistle, but a “D flute” is a low-D flute. high-D flute is also a piccolo or a fife (depending).

only key of G exist in 3 sizes, everything else exists in 2 sizes, whistles and flutes (excluding boehm bass flutes):

G: high-G (dog whistle), low-G and low-low-G (also boehm alto flute, low-low A+G)
F: high-F, low-F (hatao must have a low-low-F hidden away somewhere)
E, Ef, D, C: high and low (2 sizes)
B, Bf, A: middle and low (2 sizes)

Re: D whistles dilemma

Great music has been made on un-tunable whistles indeed, but as a courtesy to the ears of the other musicians, I think a tuneable D whistle is a good idea for playing in sessions. Sometimes the consensus A pitch among the players will drift (those darned pipers, or a concertina A that’s a bit off), or a whistle will drift a bit with ambient temperatures.

A slightly shrill-sounding whistle isn’t a big deal in a large enough session. But if it’s off the consensus A tuning, then it’s really noticeable!

I’ve played highland pipes for 25 years, so using a piper’s grip is 2nd nature to me, but reach is still an issue. As a result, I recently purchased a Carbony Low D whistle (https://carbony.com). They build beautiful low D whistles with “hole extenders” that allow them to place the holes closer together to allow one to actually play the low D with one’s fingertips (not using a piper’s grip). The downside - they are quite expensive relative to most other makes of whistles. But they sound beautiful and are much easier to play than most other Low Ds (IMO).
