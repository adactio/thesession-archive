Re: Why do you think musicians feel the need to always have accompaniment in their tracks today?

Because they don’t have the sound/touch to pull it off solo, and have to mask their sonic deficiencies with other instrumentation.

When you can make your instrument “sing”, other instruments aren’t a requirement. Just the same as it is with vocalists - some can do it unaccompanied and it lacks for nothing.

And when players never play unnacompanied, they never really develop those techniques of finesse and nuance solo playing demands, because they don’t practice for that, and perhaps, some aren’t even aware of it.

Solo playing and ensemble playing are two different animals.

Re: Why do you think musicians feel the need to always have accompaniment in their tracks today?

The key word in the OP is “ Why do you think musicians feel the need to ALWAYS have accompaniment in their tracks today?”

A short list of players (from Eire, UK and elsewhere) who, imo, sound GREAT playing solo:
Tommy Peoples
Johnny Cunningham
Liam O’Flynn (and many other pipers)
Seamus Egan (on flute)
Billy Pigg (Northumbrian pipes)
Tony McMahon
Dave Swarbrick
John Kirkpatrick (anglo, 3-row accordion)
Maria Kalaniemi (5-row accordion)
Martin Simpson (guitar)
Martin Carthy (guitar)
Annbjørg Lien (hardingfele, fiddle)
and
it’s a whole art in itself when one includes the far east - Japan, China, etc. where ‘space’ is an elememnt that is embraced rather than treated as a hole to be backfilled. (erhu, shakuhachi, pipa, koto, guqin…….)

Once a player has the nuances/skills to make listeners cry without any help, that skill doesn’t go unused if there’s accompaniment, especially if the other player has also similar abilities.

In the naked light of solo performance, ever element and nuance, or lack of, is right there in the open. Simply playing “fast” or “loud” no longer can compensate for lack of dynamics, articulation, subtle ornamentation, vibrato, use of intentional sympatic vibration on other strings (can be activated by where one plays on the neck), tone, and various other specifics according to instrument involved. In ensemble playing, most of those subtleties are completely overwhelmed and submerged as the volume and density of other instrumentation increases.

For those whose focus is playing in a “band” or “group” etc. the effort needed to develop those finesse skills for a moving solo performance are often pretty much irrelevant and a waste of (perhaps limited) practice time.

NH

Interesting point Niles. I agree it depends on your abililty and motive. To hold your own, solo, requires greater skills. But I think there is safety in numbers when it comes to lesser players (myself included).

Most people here seem to play primarily for their own fulfilment, in which case maybe the shortcomings of the actual sound as heard by others is less important than one’s ‘inner ear’ - but as soon as one presumes to start playing for others, there is IMO an obligation to provide something that is at least reasonably listenable.

As a fellow band-member remarked the other day, “Not many of us are Mike McGoldrick”, so in that instance it seems reasonable to me to use a degree of ‘cushioning’, if that’s what it takes, for the sake of pleasing an audience. Re: Why do you think musicians feel the need to always have accompaniment in their tracks today?

@Al, i agree about the“strongest survive”. in western music there are two charts. One for the Major key signatures, and another for it’s relative minor. It’s a diagram of what chords sound good progressing from one to another, based on which ones make the “strongest” resolutions\tensions, and which make “weaker” resolutions\tensions. All of those rules have been broken beautifully. But the charts are still used, because of psychoacoustics. How our brains perceive sound.
