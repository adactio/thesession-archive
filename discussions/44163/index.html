Why flat whistles?

Could some whistle aficionado explain why he/she plays an Eb whistle?
The common keys for Irish dance music are predominantly G and D, followed by A and then C; and, of course, all the related modes. If you search for a tune by key on this site these are the keys available in the drop-down list.
I have never come across a traditional Irish tune in three flats and only rarely in two flats. So why an Eb whistle?

↳ Posted by Muircheartaigh on November 9th, 2019.

Re: Why flat whistles?

There are some sets of uilleann pipes that are tuned in Eb, although having a nominal base note of D. Some Scottish bellows-blown pipes and Highland pipes actually sound in Bb while being nominally in A. If whistlers want to play along with such instruments they have no choice other than to switch to whistles in other keys.

Re: Why flat whistles?

I think there are a number of perhaps-unrelated reasons.

1) Generation whistles have long been made in pairs related by key, one whistle a fourth up from its counterpart:
Bb & Eb
C & F
D & G
When Generation started making these keys, and what purpose they were supposed to fulfill, I have no idea.
Generations long being the only whistles available (except for the Clarke, which only came in C) trad Irish players used what was to hand.
An oddity is that each of these sizes had a dedicated head and tubing-size except for the D and Eb. It’s been said that the Eb was the original and the D was a stretched Eb, that the Eb is the better player. For sure many players prefer the Eb over the D.

2) Keyed wooden flutes have long been made in Eb.
In the 19th century D flutes were made for orchestral use but military band flutes were in Eb.
Many (most?) of the Irish flute bands played flutes in Eb, along with Alto flutes in Bb and piccolos in Eb.
This resulted in many Irish trad fluteplayers having Eb flutes rather than, or in addition to, D flutes.
Matt Molloy’s first album is mostly on Eb flute.

3) One does encounter old uilleann chanters which are closer to Eb than D in pitch. I used to have an old Taylor chanter in Eb. Joe McKenna was making Eb chanters in modern times.

These things taken together mean that it would be quite possible, in the old days, to have sessions where most or all of the players had instruments in Eb. I have heard of, and encountered, Eb sessions in recent times.

4) For using whistles outwith ITM, say doing Church gigs or Studio work, it’s common to need to play in 3 or 4 flats (Eb Major and Ab Major) which fall quite well on an Eb whistle.

Re: Why flat whistles?

1 - solo performance
2 - you used to get sessions in Eb, particularly in the 1980s, not so common now as far as I have experienced.
3 - In Ireland, again throughout the 1980s, it was very common for a recording to be made in Eb. To learn tunes from some of these classic recordings, you’d need an Eb whistle to play along.
4 - in Scotland, learning tunes from recordings made using Highland bagpipes - Eb whistle required.
Even although the whistle is pitched in Eb, you use exactly the same fingering as you would on a “D” whistle.

From a personal standpoint, I like my Bb for practice. It’s a very sweet sounding key and not so shrill when working out new tunes.

From a practical standpoint, flat keys are useful for playing pipe music or accompanying any instrument in a flat key … not to mention the occasional session tune in a flat key. They do exist! Re: Why flat whistles?

If you tuned the instrument down a half step, so the E string is an Eb, you would have to use completely different fingering to play tunes in the right keys. If you are playing a tune in D and want it to be in Eb with the same fingering, you need to tune the strings up a half step, so that the D becomes an Eb and you can play using the same fingering. What you are suggesting would make the tune come out in C#.

Re: Why flat whistles?

@Muircheartaigh: I don’t like tuning my fiddle up from standard pitch - it takes the strings time to settle into the new tuning and then more time for them to settle back into standard. There is also the worry of putting extra tension on the fiddle (even if only a 6% increase) - a healthy fiddle should be able to cope with it but there is always the risk that, if, for example, the glue holding the neck in place is old and brittle, it could give under the sudden increase in tension. Additionally, it puts extra stress on the bridge, which could warp and/or fall over. Some fiddlers keep a second fiddle permanently tuned a semitone up for sharp pitch sessions. Since I only have one fiddle, I switch to mandolin and use a capo.

Of course, ideally we should all learn to transpose into any key. I would wager that *transposition* per se is not a problem for many of us if the target key is a familiar one - D into C, G into A etc. - but rather, the technique of playing in unfamiliar keys. I am probably a fairly typical player of traditional music in that much, if not most, of my ‘practice’ comes from playing in sessions itself and, unless you regularly play in an Eb session, keys such as Ab and Eb very seldom come up. So, without a lot more regular, focused, private practice, I would not be very good at spontaneously transposing into those keys at session speed.

Re: Why flat whistles?

On the topic of the prevalence of Eb instruments, remember that the tuning standard that A=440 dates only to the post-war period. Earlier instruments would often have been made to A=452 and at a later date would have been more comfortable being sharpened to “A”=466 (B flat at modern pitch) than flattened down to 440.

Re: Why flat whistles?

As Calum points out the standard 19th century orchestral pitch in Britain, called Old Philharmonic Pitch, was A=452.

This is midway between A and Bb in modern pitch.

Whether it’s easier to move up a quartertone from 452 to 466, or move down a quartertone from 452 to 440, would probably vary from instrument to instrument.

BTW if a modern Highland piper could go back to the 19th century he would find himself in tune with the London Philharmonic. (Highland pipes are likewise tuned a quartertone between two modern pitches.)

Re: Why flat whistles?

For sessions, I only take my D flute, several D whistles and one C whistle for Dm tunes, but when I’m practicing at home along with recordings, the Eb is necessary for many groups. A number of flute and whistle players have recorded tunes in it as well as other keys which make the various keys a necessity to play along unless I shift the pitch of the recording.

The Eb whistle has a brighter sound than the D which enlivens certain tunes and is fun to play alone. Likewise the C whistle or Bb whistle have deeper tones which suit other tunes. It’s rare in these parts to find an Eb session, but there’s one gathering I go to in the summer where there is a B session to allow flat pipes to play and keep out players who can’t tune down.

Like many whistle players, I have a full range of whistles that I enjoy alone or sometimes accompanying singers or for non-ITM music.

Yep, that’s one reason for a low F!

I was at a Crawford/Vallely duo concert a coupe of years ago, and afterwards was able to spend a few minutes chatting with Cillian Vallely. One of the topics that came up was me asking “why the low F? Was it mainly for a variety in pitch and timbre?”

Re: Why flat whistles?

Yes Nigel, exactly, the Eb old-system flute was used in military bands due to the brass instruments being in Bb and Eb, and the music generally being in flat keys.

Likewise the woodwinds, the clarinets and Saxophones, were in Bb and Eb.

A major consequence of the Boehm fingering system is making flat keys easier to play on the ordinary “C flute” (the D flute in ITM terminology).

School marching bands in the USA have the brass and woodwinds in Bb and Eb except for the flutes which are Boehm flutes in C. Students begin the flute by learning the Bb scale and they’ll predominantly be playing in flat keys. The facility of flat keys on the Boehm flute makes special band flutes in Eb, Bb, or Db unnecessary.
