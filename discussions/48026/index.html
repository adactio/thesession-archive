Re: Fiddle intonation questions for drones and double stops

Fifths in Equal Temp (EQ) are the same as any other tuning system, so your fiddle should be tuned the same if you tune the strings in perfect fifths or tune to EQ.

This is because fifths are a consonant interval, like octaves, octaves are the same in just intonation (JI) and EQ. Fourths (remember a 4th is an inverted 5th) are also consonant and should sound ‘in-tune’ against a drone. So, imo, the D and A strings should sound in-tune when tuned to perfect fifths and therefore also sound intune against a drone. I can’t think of any reason why you would tune your A string flatter to be in tune with the D string, this seems like a contradiction to me.

The intervals that you do need to be conscious of in particular are major and minor 3rds and 6ths, as the EQ major 3rds/6ths will sound slightly sharp against the drone while EQ minor 3rds/6ths will sound slightly flat.

Remember that intonation is a choice and a form of conditioning, it is not absolute, and while playing the fiddle you have an infinite range of intonation options to explore.

Saying that as a rule of thumb to play in EQ focus double stops on 4th’s, 5th’s and Octaves, and adapt a just approach to playing 3rd’s and 6th’s double stops.

Hope this helps!

Re: Fiddle intonation questions for drones and double stops

A fiddle in GDAE resonates best when tuned to perfect 5ths.

If you pay close attention to the sound—the resonance and timbre—you can learn to hear the difference between being out of tune and being wonderfully in tune, both with the open strings and fingered notes. When “in tune,” there’s a beautiful sheen, depth, and full resonance to the tone.

Some people learn this just by listening for that sheen and resonance. Others get there by listening to the “beats”—no, *not* the pulse or rhythm, but the crests and troughs of the wavelengths of two notes played together. Try this:
- Slide your ring finger up to the D on the G string (the note that matches your open D string).
- Now, play that note along with the open D string. If you’re not perfectly spot on, most people notice the discordance, the “clash” of notes.
- But listen closer and you’ll hear a slight wavering or vibrato between the notes. The further out of tune you are, the faster that wavering is, like a flutter. The closer you get to “in tune,” the slower the wavering gets as the wavelengths synchronize. When they’re almost in tune, the waver might sound like “waaaaaaAAAAAAaaaaaahhhh.” And when you finally have your finger in just the right spot, the waver or beat goes away, replaced by sheen and resonance.

Over time, with laser-like focus and attention, you can learn to hear the beats when tuning open strings to perfect 5ths. And your fiddle will thank you by singing in her fullest voice.

As others have said above, now that your fiddle is really in tune, you now have to pay attention to micro-adjustments in fingering to account for playing in different keys and when playing with Equal Temperament instruments such as banjo, mandolin, guitar, piano, box, etc.

Re: Fiddle intonation questions for drones and double stops

Just a few comments to add to the pot - I’d agree with practically everything said above, even when contradictory.

On the pure fifths thing, the difference between a pure fifth and an equal tempered fifth is 2 cents, more or less. Do you know how much you can alter the pitch of an open string with speed and pressure? Try it.

For that reason, learning to tune two strings to open fifths requires magnificent bow control, and while it’s worth working on it at any level, a good strobe-style tuner is well worth using (the little clip-on ones are good for getting an A, but they aren’t accurate enough to really get strings ringing). The other thing to look at is what is called son filé, very slow full length bows. This gives you more control over intonation in the double stop.

One thing that I think is worth doing is push your technical practice beyond what you need for the tunes you play. If you play in D, G, C, and F, then make sure you practice scales and arpeggios up to at least four sharps and three flats. Do some simple scales in third position. Play scales on a single string using only one or two fingers.

Lastly, the thing that will train your ear more than anything is double stops. The Harvey Whistler book is good (and will take you far further than you need). There are also scale books out there that will give you scales in thirds, sixths, and octaves and there are useful too. I’d also plug Simon Fisher’s Basics, which is much more than just that.

Re: Fiddle intonation questions for drones and double stops

I’ve often heard fiddlers using the “rolling 5th” to to tune up. I do too. I only tune the A string to pitch, either with a tuning fork or a tuner, then tune the other pairs by rolling 5ths.

When playing alone, I like to hear a good loud A, so when I hit the tuning fork, I anchor it on something solid, so it rings out out loud. I also have an “A” pure tone, activated by a button on a little practice amp.

I posted about the “rolling 5th” method a while back, but here it is again. Really simple - get the note you want to tune to (if alone, a tuning fork, or in a session, a melodeon), then tune to that. Assuming it’s an A, then tune the D string by detuning the D, then bringing up to pitch and playing together with the (now perfect) A string.

About playing two strings at once, I always remember a tip from the early days - imagine a string in between the two you are trying to play, and play that one. It’s worked for me !

Re: Fiddle intonation questions for drones and double stops

Good tip, Jim. I’ve heard that one, too.

Another I like for bowing two strings is to NOT grind into them. Two strings at once will naturally be a bit louder than one (bow pressure and speed kept the same) because two strings are driving the top. Apparently some fiddlers hear the loudness and think it’s from really driving the bow, so they lean into it. But that tends to grind the strings, producing gritty tone or mushing out the intonation. It may help to spend some time finding the *least* bow pressure and speed that produces good tone from both strings at once. (Which then avoids the problem Calum cited above.)

Re: Fiddle intonation questions for drones and double stops

A friend of mine once asked a rabequeiro (sort of a rustic folk fiddle player from some parts of Brazil) how he was tuning his instrument (meaning in fourths or in fifths or what) and his answer was:
“Well, when it’s sounding bad, I turn these twisty bits at the top until it sounds good again.”

A slightly simplistic answer, but in a way, it covers tuning. Turn the twisty bits until it sounds right.

My approach has usually been to get a nice accurate app or tuner and get my A string absolutely bang on 440.

Then tune the D and the E to where they resonate perfectly with the A. And the G to where it resonates perfectly with the D.

If I’m having trouble for some reason I might use the tuner to get me into the right ballpark, but the fine tuning will be done by ear.

If the tuner is good and accurate, I’ll see that my A is bang on. My D is just the tiniest hair flat. My G is even a tiny bit more flat. And my E is a tiny bit sharp.

Other times I’ll tune my D to the bottom D of a piper for example and tune all the other strings from there. Which, assuming that D is bang on, means my G will be a hair flat, my A a hair sharp, and my E two hairs sharp.

While we live in a world of absolute pitch where A=440, in practice on the fiddle it’s all relative.

You’re looking for those intervals (or that unison) to ring out. Either with yourself or with other instruments. That often means your fingers will have to move a tiny bit depending on who you’re playing with or what key you’re in, or what note you’re trying to harmonise with.

And sometimes there’s no right answer, only compromises. If you’re playing with a dry tuned box and a set of pipes, you’re going to have to choose which one to follow. Or find a spot in between the two. And you won’t get that perfect resonance on some notes, but maybe you can get a bit closer to it.

Also, I’m starting to sound like my every interval is perfect all the time, which is far far from true. But that’s the target from my perspective. Try and get every interval to resonate.

I spend a lot of time working on double stops and since I’ve been taking this seriously I have taken much more time to tune up beforehand.

I do this simply by tuning to pitches from a tuner. Then checking the fiddle is in tune with itself properly, by ear, which is usually very fine tuning, the icing on the cake, very small adjustments. I don’t have fine tuners though - just on the E string. Thinking of getting some fitted actually. But very important that your fiddle is as in tune with itself as possible. Otherwise you’re just practising placing your fingers in the wrong places!

I’ve been working on the double-stopping exercises in Sevcik’s Violin Studies Opus 1 and Opus 9. They may be designed for a classical player but they are great double-stopping bootcamp exercises for a folk fiddler. And I’ve been watching loads of YouTube videos about intonation and double-stopping.

For the purposes of intonation and tone I’ve been playing with my fingers at more of a straight, vertical right-angle (not literally) to the fingerboard. This definitely helps me get notes to ring out and sustain. Although I can’t get double stops wholly in tune with the fingers in that position, so some versatility/flexibility in finger position is necessary.

I’ve also had to remind myself not to double-stop as much as I want to. Most Irish players I listen to don’t actually double-stop all that much: selectively here and there.

Last thing I’d say - how comfortable is your shoulder rest/chinrest setup? What angle is your fiddle at? You may want to experiment raising/lowering your shoulder-rest if it is adjustable. (Or if it isn’t, buy one) Or try raising the right hand side to highest extent and left-hand side to lowest. Experiment with shoulder rest height. All of this slightly alters the position/angle of your fingers accessing strings. I’m thinking of getting an Wittner adjustable chinrest too as I’d like to be able to raise my head slightly and reduce the steepness of the angle my violin sits at.

You can buy stick-on frets: I’ve seen 2 types reviewed on YouTube. Of course, these will ensure that you can Never play in tune!

Not yet mentioned because it is obvious to many posters to this thread: To adjust tuning of fingers, fingers must be flexible, which requires that your hand and fingers are as relaxed as possible, not a rigid mass. Beware beginner’s intensity, which works against you. Start from calm nothing and add only what is necessary.

“Yes, those fiddlers are so lucky, having four fixed reference points all the time. David50 ” Actually, this is precisely the problem. Having more than 1 fixed string means you must adjust your fingers to sound in tune. Including if you only ever play 1 note at a time (ie: never 2 strings together).

I have heard Classical players say that they play in ET. But, if their strings are tuned to perfect 5ths, how can they?

ET is dull and uninteresting, especially from fiddles. I always play in tune with the moment, by ear with expressive intervals according to the key and current music, which I assume is the “Irish scale” mentioned. I love this term. And that box reeds are retuned to this flavor. Hopefully, players understand that the “Irish scale” is not a fixed entity to be learned and produced mechanically.

When I play with winds, such as jazz, I must play very sharp on the A string. If I tune to perfect 5ths, my D on the A string is in tune with my instrument (open D) but is very flat to the other instruments at ET, and my intonation must rise to be acceptable.

An option is to tune low strings D,G,C in perfect 5ths when playing with med bow weight, to make drones and chords in tune. These open strings may be flat to fingered notes on the higher strings, but fingered notes can be adjusted. And at least, these strings are not sharp as in ET, which doesn’t allow changes. With this preparation, if these open strings need to be at ET, add weight and the string comes up to that pitch. Also, this way the C string is not initially tuned to the E string, but can rise to match. Note that I and Calum who mentioned this method are actually simultaneously leaning more on the lower string than on the upper string(s) where the melody is. This is the opposite of “bowing evenly and consistently on two strings at once.”

Constant compensation. Agile adjustments.

Happy musicmaking,

Re: Fiddle intonation questions for drones and double stops

One other important consideration is your left hand frame - making sure your fingers are in the same place each time you use them- also give consideration to your pinky, if it’s high it can really throw off the rest of your hand.

Thinking about the centre of gravity of your left hand (which finger is taking the weight of the hand) can be helpful too, shifting the centre of gravity from the first finger to the second or even third can make your hand a lot more stable, that tip came from Eddie Chen via Hilary Hahn

Aligning the thumb of the left hand with your 2nd finger can make a difference too.

The position of the left elbow also impacts on intonation - notice how Kevin Burke’s elbow swings out slightly when he plays string for instance.
