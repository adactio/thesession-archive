I don’t think there’s really any quarrel here if things can be looked at the right way round. The written language is an imperfect representation of the sound, not the other way round. There are two ways of learning to play tunes which at the beginning are too fast for you. One is to play along with people who can play at the speed you can’t manage and simplify or make mistakes but get the feel and the flow and a style - and keep doing that till you improve. The other is to play alone correctly from memory and/or written music at the speed you can manage and slowly speed up as you improve, but at the slow speed the feel of the music is lost and it is very hard to hold the rhythm and this is where the metronome comes in. Most traditional musicians use both methods of learning and so sometimes the metronome is useful. I use a downloaded metronome now because I can turn the volume up to an unpleasant level. The standard practise, as far as I am aware, is to state the length of note, eg quarter, eighth, etc, alongside the metronome speed, and it is the number of these notes per minute that you are actually counting. So if you set a metronome to 100 and play a reel, you are playing four quarter notes to each beat, which equals 400 notes per minute. Re: Tempo for jigs and reels

When you write music, the idea is to make it readable. Generally speaking, 8th notes are easier to read than 16th notes. The convention is to write 4/4 time and 8th notes rather than 2/2 (duple meter) and 16th notes. That’s assuming you want players to actually play your score.

Then there is the idea of “pulse” and that is completely different. The pulse of a reel is felt on every other beat. the pulse of a jig is felt on every third beat.

You can write anything in any number of meters. Basically there is duple and triple meters and then the compound meters are just that, 2s and 3s put together.

Since you can write the meter however you want, we rely on common practice. Basically, you write it so that players can play it. So if you look around, you’ll see that it is common to notate reels in 4/4 with 8th notes, even though the pulse is every other beat.

remember: music is hard and tricky

This arguing about how things are notated is silly. Scottish and Irish traditional music have their conventions regarding how things are written, which are often not at all how an orchestral arranger would write them.

I know: I’ve played hundreds of studio gigs where a composer is trying to achieve the sound of an Irish jig or Irish reel, and nearly always they notate them in ways that seem very strange to a traditional player. They notate them the way they do because it’s the way that seems most suitable and most easily readable to a professional Hollywood studio musician.

If I may add an observation here, not with any definitive scientific or proper music-teacher-type instruction as to how fast tunes should be played, but from personal experience? It’s common for many people to play tunes too fast; some seem to believe that the faster you play, the better you are. I’ve heard many tunes played very fast, which can be great foot-thumpers, but conversely I’ve also heard the same tunes played quite slow and rhythmically, which appear to give a greater understanding of the nuances and feeling of the tune. I often start off much too fast during practices and get told off by my fellow band members; keeping a slow steady rhythm allows - in my opinion - better playing, and a greater chance for those accompanying to explore counter rhythms and harmonies. Many of the great pipers, and I think here of Liam O’Flynn, play solo pieces beautifully paced and quite deliberate, while other musicians thrash them out at top whack. I think a lot of it comes down to who you are aiming at, but personally I’ve started to play at a pace I believe a good dancer could keep up with and accompany - Richard D Cook’s post seems to hit the same note with me.
So - official time recommendations aside (and I ask pardon from the OP!), the great thing about folk is that many tunes can be played as fast as you want; maybe it’s age and forty years of playing catching up with me, but things are definitely getting slower and more sedate in my playing.
