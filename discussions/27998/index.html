All this modern talk about Ipods and even CDs isn’t that relevant as this “issue” (If you regard it to be so) has been around for several years.

Unless you start playing traditional music from a very young age or have grown up in a musical vacuum, all adult learners are going to be influenced by various sources and far beyond artists such as Lunasa and Martin Hayes. They are actually the least of your problems as they, at least, play Irish music albeit at a commercial performance level.

As others have suggested, the best approach is to try and focus on what’s happening in your local session(But make sure it’s an Irish Trad one).

Personally, that’s something I find hard to do as there’s not really that many local ones which fit the bill exactly. Many play Irish music alongside tunes etc from other traditions, there are also Scottish and various other forms of sessions.

Moreover, my musical tastes are such that I enjoy playing tunes from elsewhere too esp Shetland, Scandinivia and so on which is not something to be recommended if one wants to become a really good player of Irish music.

Of course, there’s nothing wrong with being versatile but i suppose the answer is to immerse yourself in one particular genre and get a thoroughly good grounding in that before moving on to something else. Unfortunately, many of us don’t have enough time for that especially if we’ve started to play later in life or are not that patient.

Some time ago I attended a session in a city that happens to be the hometown of a well-known Irish fiddler. There’s definitely a difference between being a good soloist and being a good session player, and neither follows from the other.

Re: ITM..Which came first, Martin Hayes or the Kerry Polka!:)

Take the OP’s idea to the extreme and you have the classical approach, where the players apparently go to great pains not to listen to a recorded piece until they’ve mastered the piece first from sheet music---the idea being that they don’t want to be influenced by someone else’s style.

Listening to other players, for me, is one of the great things about playing this music. Recordings, concerts, local sessions, ceilidhs---it’s all a source of enjoyment and inspiration. It’s how you build a notion of how you hear the music in your head. And then when you go out and play a tune, it evolves based on the other musicians around.

Personally, I tend to try to emulate certain aspects of other musicians’ style, rather than the whole style itself. Some have great lift, or rock-solid rhythm, or just a general calm approach---I look for all these things to improve my own playing. Sounding like someone else has never really been a concern for me. Do others have that problem?

Re: ITM..Which came first, Martin Hayes or the Kerry Polka!:)

There is no “the” classical approach. A few players try to do that, but it’s much commoner for a performer to try to listen to every version they can find.

After all, if you’re learning a Beethoven piano sonata, how likely is it you won’t have heard it before? Listening to a bunch of different versions from different schools of interpretation helps ensure that no one version unconsciously influences you too much.

Good thread. It’s a real issue when we (those of us who are not in Ireland, nor live close to one of the US strong holds), use too many recordings to learn.

Re: ITM..Which came first, Martin Hayes or the Kerry Polka!:)

Jerone, personal style takes a long time to develop. I suspect the buds of it begin to form as soon as you start learning, but it doesn’t really blossom until you’ve overcome most of the technical challenges of the music and your instrument. And *then* it takes years of playing. And *then* it never stops evolving.

I’ve never worried about it, beyond choosing to listen to good players whose pulse and phrasing I admired. Early on, that was Bobby Casey and Kevin Burke. Then Martin Rochford and Paddy Keenan. Lately I’ve been listening closely to P. Joe Hayes and my own mental tape of Mark Donellan.

But mainly I listen to my own playing and ponder different ways to phrase the tunes, gradually finding what floats *my* boat.

Sandy makes good points. Though there are some recordings that aren’t studio-polished performances so much. Things like Paddy in the Smoke, Maiden Voyage, Music at Matt Molloy’s, and the mythic Gort session bootleg recording.

Sessions too can be a dangerous place to learn “style.” There’s a generic session style that tends to be bland, stripped down, and samey. So I think it helps a lot to play one on one with another musician whose playing you admire. Outside of sessions, just the two of you exploring the tunes. Take every opportunity you can for that.

Sandy, I run a weekly tune learning session (on Mondays) that really has helped our local players build a common repertoire of tunes. The fact that i teach the tunes provides some stylistic consistency, but I also talk about and demonstrate other ways to approach each tune, and then it evolves over the course of getting played at the weekly regular session (on Thursdays).

Of all the things we’ve tried over the years, the tune learning session has likely been the biggest asset to our local session scene. It’s deepened the repertoire and people’s understanding of the music, and eased some newcomers into the regular session.
