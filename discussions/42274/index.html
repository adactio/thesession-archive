Re: Recorder

I don’t *hate* it, and have played in local sessions with some good recorder players. I think the following two issues are the usual objections for playing The Music on recorder:

1) The tone is too pure, not dirty enough for Irish trad. It’s like the difference between the “hard” tone on a wooden conical bore, simple system flute vs. a metal Boehm flute. Or the tone of a high or low whistle compared to recorder. Edgy and raspy, vs. sweet and pure. We like our folk music on the dirty side.

2) The fingering system is different from whistles and flutes use in Irish trad, and that may make ornamentation more difficult. Not saying it can’t be done, but I’ve played with two recorder players in my area in Irish sessions, and neither one did much or anything with ornamentation, just playing the notes of the tune.

It could just be lack of interest in getting more deep into the music, but it may also be some mechanical difficulty in playing the usual trad ornaments on a recorder. I stand to be corrected on that, because I don’t know anything about playing a recorder. Flute and whistle does it for me in this music.

Just a note on the “weird instruments” in the clip above - they look to me like sub-contrabass recorders, from the workshop of Herbert Paetzold in Southern Germany. These instruments, with the lowest range of the recorder family (bottom note “F” if I remember rightly), were based on a design of his uncle’s, and made with the idea of being much cheaper and therefore more accessible than more conventional recorders. The tubes, conical square section, were made from good quality plywood, and the mechanisms for closing the keys were mounted on bicycle spokes! I know all this because I worked in his workshop for a while in the early 1980’s. It was a fairly rarified atmosphere around his workshop with frequent visits from some of the top recorder players of the time - my tin whistle was regarded with the same mixture of pitying curiosity that seems to be elicited amongst the ITM community by Herbert’s sub-contras!
But it did set me off on my journey towards being an instrument maker, now happily fulfilled with the harp. The
tin whistle is still a constant presence.
As for recorder vs whistle, they’ve always seemed to me to be very different instruments, each appropriate for its own repertoire. Re: Recorder

There’s a definition problem with whistles and recorders.

The fingering difference - which is what people notice - is the existence of an extra hole for the left thumb and an extra hole or pair of holes for the right little finger on the recorder. These exist to extend the range and allow chromatic playing and they don’t make much (or perhaps any) difference to the sound. It would be perfectly possible to put a body with Baroque recorder fingering onto a whistle mouthpiece (though it might be harder to get all the notes).

Irish players often complain that a recorder is “a C instrument”. This is simply not true. The bottom note on Descant and Tenor recorders is C but they can be played in any key and play most easily in G or D. Children are taught to play descants in C because most of the teachers are really pianists and they think in C. They would be better to teach G first.

The difference in the sound is made by a different and more precise construction of the windway in the mouthpiece of the recorder so that you can get a purer note with no overtones. The equation is that the more precision you get the less expression you have, which is a pox. Also descant recorders are an octave higher than the range they play because the lack of overtones makes them sound lower than they are. They really are very, very high-pitched and quickly become annoying. Irish music is better played on a tenor recorder and preferably a model with a less constrained windway.

Re: Recorder

OK, I’m somewhat biased (my wife is a very good recorder player.)
It’s horses for courses, the recorder in its way is an incredible instrument, so simple, yet it can play two fully usable chromatic “real world” octaves in tune. Compare the simplicity of a recorder with all the ironmongery all over oboes and clarinets. The recorder was optimised for very chromatic “classical” or “art” music, realising the composer’s intention and all that. To do that the fingering had to get quite complex. There’s a forked fingering in the bottom octave on the “home” scale of the instrument - why? It’s needed to get the upper octave in tune. That complex fingering means a certain sort of breath control and articulation is needed. The end result is an instrument that “can” play ITM, but it would be so much easier on a whistle, and compared with the whistle, which is a deeply established instrument in Irish music, the recorder just speaks with the wrong accent!

Re: Recorder

Gallopede: “…descant recorders are an octave higher than the range they play because the lack of overtones makes them sound lower than they are. They really are very, very high-pitched…”

This doesn’t make sense. A descant recorder has basically the same range as a high C whistle. It may sound more shrill because of favouring the higher overtones - and may be better suited to playing in the 3rd octave. But a descant recorder is not, overall, higher-pitched than a standard C or D whistle.

“The difference in the sound is made by a different and more precise construction of the windway in the mouthpiece of the recorder so that you can get a purer note with no overtones.”

The difference in tone is not solely down to the windway. It is largely down to a narrower bore and thicker wall. It is not the case that a recorder produces no overtones - the narrow bore actually favours the higher overtones. The main effect of the windway (and labium) design is to reduce the amount of ‘chiff’ (breath noise), giving a cleaner tone.
