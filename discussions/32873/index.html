Re: Relearning Tunes . .

As has already been said, just play the tunes the same as you do on your D whistle and they’ll come out in Eb, Ab etc. instead of D, G etc. (Playing in D on an Eb whistle may be *possible* in theory, but highly impractical - like playing in C# on D whistle. That’s why you get whistles in different pitches.)

If you want to play together with other people, you won’t make yourself popular by bringing an Eb whistle along to a session, since everyone else would have to either retune their instrument or change their fingering to play with you. Keep the Eb for playing at home, by all means, but you’d be a fool not to hang onto your D whistle as well.

Re: Relearning Tunes . .

While I have heard of a handful of Eflat sessions in Ireland, I have never encountered one in the USA. At the sessions I go to, any time you played your whistle in that key, you would be playing alone (with possibly a guitarist with a capo, drummer, or an especially adept fiddler joining in). You would be excluding most of the folks in the circle, which is not a good way make friends. And being part of the throng at a good session is one of the most fun parts of this music, so you would definitely be missing something special.
If you have only learned 25 tunes, it is WAY, WAY too soon to start blaming the whistle for problems you might be having. Low D is an especially unforgiving instrument, and I would tend to think that the problems you are having at this point are more likely something that can be eliminated by more practice, not by throwing the baby out with the bathwater. Hang on to the D, and keep working at it, that’s my advice.

Re: Relearning Tunes . .

“Well I did flatten the C# . .”

Ergo you were playing it in G. A standard 6 -hole whistle allows you to play in two major keys without using any half-holes - the key with the bottom note as its tonic (D on a D whistle) and the key with the 4th note up as its tonic (G on a D whistle). So, on an Eb whistle. you can play either in Eb or in Ab. Since you are playing alone, it makes no difference which you choose to play in - except that one key might suit the range of a particular tune better. For ‘Somewhere Over the Rainbow’ (since you mention it), you’re probably better off starting on the 4th note (G or Ab), since you need to get down the note below the tonic (F# or G). If you were to start on the bottom note, you’d need to either jump octaves or substitute another note for the F#/G; if you were to start the tune an octave up, it would get very shrill.

Re: Relearning Tunes . .

“I transposed the music on Wikifonia and the tune now starts of Eb rather than G, does this not indicate that I’m relearning the tune rather than using the ”fingering I already know“ ?”

I’d say no. If I play Out on the Ocean in G and then in A, but staying with a D whistle (or pipes), I’ve not relearned the tune. I’ve already learned how the tune goes. ;-) If I already know a tune well, I can play it in different keys (up to a point) on pipes and whistle with minimal faff and “relearning.”

If I pick up an Eb whistle and subsequently play it in Ab and, er, something, I’ve still not relearned the tune. The point is, you don’t have to relearn *anything* to play on your Eb whistle. Just use your software to raise your recordings a semitone, learn the tunes as if you were playing a D whistle, and Bob’s your uncle.

Unless, of course, you want to play with other people. Why not get a decent low D or even a high D whistle? Most people have a collection of them anyway. I have a few D whistles, a low F, a high Eb, a C, and a D/C floating around. I like playing the C and F in the house but don’t bother taking them out to social occasions.

To be fair, it depends how familiar you are with the instrument. Granted, the whistle is a simple instrument, and it doesn’t take long to get familiar enough with the notes to transpose spontaneously into any of the common keys. But if, like Palimpsest, you’ve only been playing for a few months, there will probably be a certain amount of ‘relearning’ required.

Whether you *have to* or not, it is a good exercise to learn tunes in more than one key - transposing then quickly becomes second nature. This, however, has nothing to do with whether you are playing a D or and Eb whistle.

Re: Relearning Tunes . .

"@Jeff

“where the musicians have tuned their instruments up a semi-tone (hint: Dervish, Frankie Gavin and many more)”

Is this a common practise?"

It’s fairly common, that is, before you’ve heard the recording you can’t be sure that everything will be played in concert pitch. Dervish, Frankie Gavin (and his associated bands), Máirtín Connor and many more almost exclusively play a semi-tone up. To play along to those recordings there are at least two options:
1. use an instrument where Eb (or rather D#) is the “home scale” - fiddlers tune up each string a semi-tone, whistles/flautists use Eb, those who play guitar/bouzouki/banjo/mandolin/etc. usually have a capo on the first fret;button accordionists typically use C/C# or D/D# and so on
2. relearn the tune with proper fingerings for whatever key the tune is in, Eb, Bb etc. (less likely for most musicians and instruments)
