Are Competitions good for Traditional Music

Wanted to get some viewpoints from folks here about whether or not you think Competitions in Traditional Music are good/bad for the Tradition?

I have ran across mixed sentiment on this subject as it either creates the wrong goal to “bettering” your playing and/or creates a homogeneous style that pleases judges. On the other hand, it gets young people involved and learning from more experiences players.

Re: Are Competitions good for Traditional Music

Comhaltas has been running the Scrúdu Ceol Tíre programme of graded exams in conjunction with the RIAM since the late 90s. I’m pretty sure it’s still running though my experience is that it’s not that popular.

Comhaltas is already funded partly by the Irish government, through the Arts Council, and partly by its members. I don’t have the latest figures to hand but I recall that 2005 - 2010 it had some €18m in capital grants from the government.

Every week thousands of children and adults attend Comhaltas lessons and sessions where they can “can play together in a safe environment”.

As to the fleadh causing children to “ tend to take a competitive attitude through to adulthood”, well having competed, taught, and adjudicated under the auspices of Comhaltas for over 40 years all I can note is that it’s not my experience. The vast majority of trad recording artists came through the fleadh system and certainly with few exceptions all I know are warm, generous musicians.

Is there a competition style? Yep, once one gets out of the county. Just like anyone taking academic exams is advised to adopt a writing style that’s more acceptable to the academy, so there’s a competition style of playing. But in my experience, step away from the adjudicator and the musician (young or old) reverts to their natural style. To my mind, regional styles (if such a thing ever existed!) have been more deeply affected by the modern world (the motor car, the radio, gramophone records, reel to reel tape machines, the Ceol Rince na hÉireann series, and so on) than Comhaltas competitions.

As for after-party grudges, I’ve never heard of any. In fact my experience is that deep friendships are often forged between competitors as they move through the age groups.

Have adjudicators often favored the bland of the individual? Yep. Are there some pushy “fleadh Mammys”? Yep. Do some children get upset at the thought of competing? Sure. Are some of HQ’s dealing . . . well let’s leave it at questionable. But overall, where’s the real harm?

Re: Are Competitions good for Traditional Music

“It is better IMO to have system and an organisation like Comhaltas, but without the childrens competitions…”

I disagree completely. I think the competitions are fantastic for kids, and that’s about the only place it’s useful. I used to shy away from encouraging my students to compete, but in the past few years I’ve seen great value in the kids’ competitions. Now I encourage all my young students to compete (or, rather, to play in the competetions--which is subtley different from actually “competing”).

1) It gives them something tangible to work towards. Let’s face it. There really aren’t many good performance opportunities for kids, especially not for a trad-music-savvy audience. If I tell a kid “you need to clean up your rhythm”, that has less of an impact than: “You need to clean up your rhythm on this tune, because that’s what the judge will be listening for”. In my experience as a teacher, the kids that compete become dramatically better players than those who don’t--there are, of course, exceptions; but the difference is very hard to ignore.

2) A fleadh is a gathering point for a student to meet his/her peers, and recognize those peers that are playing particularly well (and not just the ones that win!), as well as see the next-older crowd play--you could take your 10-yr old to see Frankie Gavin, and it wouldn’t make nearly as much sense as hearing a group of good 13 year-olds.

3) (related to 1 above) Competitions necessarily emphasize technique, because it’s more objectively judged. Technique is important, and is particularly helpful to get right as early on as possible (provided, of course, that you don’t ignore musicality). Breaking bad habits is harder than forming good ones, and competitions give the incentive to get it right before you’ve learned it wrong. Besides, technique is easier for an immature player to grasp and to practice than more abstract notions of musicality; as they mature and understand music better, they’ll have the chops to help them play the way they want to sound. This is why competitions for adults are less valuable--adults should be mature enough to recognize good music qualitatively; kids, by definition, are less mature.

4) A fleadh may homogenize styles a little. Still, as she matures, the student may decide that the style expected for competitions doesn’t suit her, and she’ll stop competing, and do something else (perhaps form a band?); but she’ll do so with a solid background.

5) Competing is nerve-wracking. Once you’ve competed a few times, nearly all other performance situations are comparatively easy. :)

It’s important not to overstate the value of the competive aspect (specifically the winners and losers). At every fleadh I’ve been to, the winners get showered with praise, and so do the other kids who played well. When this doesn’t happen--if the teacher/parent overemphasizes competition results--the competition can become a very negative experience. But when that does happen, it’s the teacher’s/parents’ fault, not the competition’s. A fleadh is there to recognize good players, not knock down less-good players.

Lastly, I’ll paraphrase a statement I heard from a national poetry-slam champion on public radio a few years ago; when asked if competition and poetry weren’t antithetical to one another, he said something like: “as a poet, you’re always competing with other poets; whether for a publishing deal, or a reading gig, a professorship, or anything else. Having a poetry competition is simply a more honest and straightforward competition.”

Re: Are Competitions good for Traditional Music

Like any competition there are several factors at work.
Some children learn how to focus under pressure.
Some collapse.
Some children are supported by a network of family or musicmates.
Some are on their own.
Some win graciously.
Some lose graciously.
Some develop egos.
Some never change.
Some are genuinely supportive.
Some learn how to be pleasant and mannerly.

As for the judging, there is no “set” standard.
Is it better to play a difficult piece poorly or a simple tune brillantly?
Does this judge like tunes composed in the last 30 years?
Do they want limited ornamentation or piles of it?
Does marking time loudly lose points?
Do they listen to all the tunes or just the first one?

My one recommendation would be to have a set tune for each age group. One tune to level the playing field and then the players get to pick their other tunes.

In the end a person will get out of it what they want. Some may walk away and never come back but others may wander back. And they may pick up an instrument again.

My mother’s biggest regret is that she never learned to play an instrument. Although she does love to listen.

Re: Are Competitions good for Traditional Music

Will, I didn’t mean to suggest that “all poets are after prizes”, but rather that poetry (or any kind of art) itself is not as antithetical to competition as some would like to think it is; to seek recognition does not necessarily make you less of an artist. Of course plenty of musicians play music for themselves first, a-la Tommy Potts. In cases like that, having the skill to compete is less useful (though I think the benefits in terms of technique still stand); I think that recognizing your purpose for playing is a matter of musical maturity, again--and not something I would expect most children to grasp (though it’s lovely when they do understand it).

I’ve seen sessions get competitive too, though I don’t think it’s universally a bad thing, as long as people are trying to compete to be the better session-player rather than as a better solo-performer.

For the record, I’ve visited Cape Breton a few times, and while there are no fiddle competitions to speak of there, the fiddlers are as a whole far more competitive with one another than any I’ve met in Ireland. I gather it probably has more to do with the music in Cape Breton being almost exclusively performance-oriented rather than socially-oriented, but that’s just a hunch.

I thought Seamus Connolly won the All Ireland twice in one year (under 18, and over 18).

In the end I think competitions are great for kids, so long as the parents and teachers keep it a positive experience for the kids--and I think most of them do (though some regretfully do not). Competitions like the fleadh exist to reward the hard work of the winners, to give them recognition they deserve for their efforts, not to punish the losers. While I’ve heard plenty of people that I would have thought would win in a fleadh, I don’t recall ever hearing somebody win that I honestly believed didn’t earn it.

But its not for “the best and brightest” alone. Much like Milltown in early July, all the way up from county to provincial to the All Ireland, the fleadh is a gathering of the clan. “Competition has been part of our heritage whether in sport or the arts. We still enjoy the accounts of great competitions in the time of Fionn Mac Cumhail and Cu Chulainn in the great sagas of our ancestors. But competition is not the raison d’etre of traditional music and we hope that people will not get to ‘carried away’ with this competition but rather partake and enjoy, whether listening in, or playing or arguing over the merits of one style or another, but above all else, enjoying the wonderful gift that is Gaelic traditional music whether you are from Ireland, Scotland or any other part of this world.”.

Re: Are Competitions good for Traditional Music

I know nothing about Comhaltas competitions and my remarks were about traditional music competitions in general (be it Irish Trad, Appalachian Old Time, or Highland piping).

I’ve spent over 30 years in the Highland pipe competition scene, mostly bands but also solos many years ago.

In the Highland pipe world I think competitions have an entirely positive effect. Nearly all the judging is about basic music things: are you playing in tune? are you keeping good rythm? is there good “pulse” and “lift”? is the playing clean, or are there errors?
So the judges are awarding good playing, simple as that, and most of the time any good musician who had never heard piping before, if asked to judge, would probably post around the same results.

I found this out in this way: I played a CD of a competition of the world’s top pipe bands to a jazz drummer who knew nothing of Pipe Band Drumming. To him the difference between the band that placed 1st and the band that placed 3rd was obvious.

About competition creating snobbery backbiting etc, it’s completely the opposite in the competitive Highland pipe band field. We are a single community and we get to know the players in all the other bands and we have a beer and a chat.
That’s the best thing about Pipe Band competition: the cameraderie and friendships we develop.

This is a great idea. The largest competition in the US for Oldtime style has this very setup. Here is a bit of verbiage related to this.
"…Each fiddler has four minutes to play three songs. Each must start with a hoe-down, followed by a waltz and finally they play their tune of choice, something they feel displays the best of their abilities. They cannot repeat a song they have played during the contest. Judges are kept away from the performance stage so as not to be swayed by stage presence or audience support. It’s a blind judging as well. As soon as a competitor hits his or her first note a switch is flipped which sends the music electronically to the room where the judges listen. The judges have no idea who is playing. All they know is the number of the contestant. As soon as the last note of the third song is played, the switch is again flipped and no applause or audience reaction is heard by the judges.

The key for any player is to move the judges, to make them feel the music. You can see when that happens. Judges begin tapping their feet, keeping beat. “I did it more on some than others,” says judge Daniel Carwile from Athens, Alabama. “I guess it has to do with the groove, you know, if they find the pocket. It does, it really does matter in the judging.”

Layna Hafer agrees, “That’s what a fiddler wants to have happen, and if it’s happening out there on stage and it’s happening in the judges room then you know it’s good music and that’s what they’re judging for too.” …."
