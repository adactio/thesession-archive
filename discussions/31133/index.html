Playing by ear… w/a dash of dots

I’m starting an experiment. What I want to do is combine sight reading with listening. The listening being my *focus*. My plan is to work through the tunes in Geraldine Cotter’s Tin Whistle Book.
The sight reading involves the minimal amount of visual cue needed to get an impression in my head. As I’m playing off the dots I want to concentrate on what I’m hearing, imprint that, & forget how the dots appear.
Obviously reading sheet music is not ‘playing by ear’. I realize this. The reason I want to do this experiment is to get back into sight reading without the side effect of staring at a piece of paper.
More details will be provided if and when someone out there responds to this “experiment”.

Re: Playing by ear… w/a dash of dots

You could try what I sometimes do -- listen to a recording that you want to learn from, and at the same time put an ABC version up on your PC. The ABC serves as a visual cue for what you are hearing or about to hear, and after a while you begin to hear the tune in your mind’s ear even when the recording is not playing. It might take a little adjusting of the ABC (or write it yourself to match) but you can build up a nice visual record of the tunes you are learning, and even play along with the looped ABC to polish up timing difficulties or awkward passages.

Re: Playing by ear… w/a dash of dots

I know it’s just an experiment, but it could be argued that practising and developing your sight-reading would be more productive. The argument being that the more you practise, the better and quicker you get.

For some, it gets to the stage that they can glance at a pattern or cluster, and read / interpret it just like a word of text. Given that there are countless similar & repeating patterns in this stuff, you then scan a few groups and form a musical ‘sentence’, which the brain then registers as audio.

So, in that respect, it kind of works in a similar way to actually pre-hearing the notes. I’ve seen people do it - given an unfamiliar piece of music, and playing straight off with a high degree of accuracy.

That’s the theory from highly experienced sight readers and teachers. As they say, your mileage may vary, everyone is different, and only you will be able to decide if that is worth pursuing.

Re: Playing by ear… w/a dash of dots

i think that you have a good idea here, but i would tweak it to emphasize playing along with recordings and not using transcriptions that are necessarily accurate. the general idea would be to put on a recording, look at the sheetmusic, and play along with the recording using the sheetmusic as a guide. i suggest this because i believe that sighreading is about playing, moreso than listening. technically, reading music is not the same as sightreading! i have many friends in graduate school for classical music and jazz, and to them sightreading is showing up to a performance or rehearsal and playing through the piece without ever having looked at the music.

don’t use simple stuff like from tutorials. take a track or two off an album you like, compile the sheet music from here into sets, and sight read new tunes along with the recordings. this will help you practice learning tunes in a more realistic setting. i often learn tunes using sight reading and recordings simultaneously. i also learn tunes by ear exclusively, but this is usually for tunes that i am very familiar with by listening to albums or playing in sessions. the benefit of using sheet music means that i can play along with recordings that i have never really listened to in depth before.

the transcriptions from this site will not match the settings of the tunes you will be playing, but using them will be a great exercise nonetheless. this idea comes from a gig i had about 2,000 miles from home (the u.s. is a big place!). i had to play a 3 hour ceili with someone who did not have the same repertoire as me. instead of trying to meet halfway, i decided to compile a new repertoire of ceili friendly tunes that were not in my active repertoire. i got most of the sets from a ceili band my friend runs, although i grabbed a few that i had made for intermediate players in a club i run. i grabbed all the settings from here, threw them together and made an index.

we didn’t sight read the music, as we were spending our time practicing for other gigs and a radio appearance, which was based on an entirely different repertiore. when we got on stage, we sight read the music and played for three hours without missing a beat. we didn’t actually play the settings note for note, as you are surely aware sometimes the settings found here can be strange or very different from how most people play them. we arranged them on the fly as people were dancing, but we played for 3 hours on a repertoire that neither of us really knew without even a single read through.

the biggest reason i think you should try my approach is it will teach you to play more than what is on the page, and to not get so stuck on individual notes. i can site read just about any trad tune you put in front of me, but that doesn’t mean that i play every note as written. this idea will help you practice sight reading as a trad player actually needs to do it. i think that the goal of sight reading in traditional music is to help you get the overall shape of the tune, just as learning by ear allows slight variations in the setting to seep in. it is also a lot more fun and in the long run will help you learn by ear and by sight more quickly. the way you are

i think that playing along with recordings that do not match the sheet music you are listening to will give you a much more useful skill than learning how to sightread note for note better. it will also be more like sightreading than just playing off music, as trained classical musicians can sightread an entire symphony on the spot.

Re: Playing by ear… w/a dash of dots

here is a tip for sightreading that i learned in my former life of classical training as a teenager:

when you are sightreading or reading music in general, you should never be looking at the notes you are playing or are just about to play, you should be looking ahead to the next measure. try to be at least a beat and a half of where you are, but ideally you should be reading a measure or two ahead of what you are playing. this takes a leap of faith, but it is the only way to go for being literate in sheet music. i can still remember the day when my band director told us to do this. i had never thought about it that way, but he was very adamant about it, so i have taken that as the golden standard i hold myself to ever since.

this is essential because often times you may need an extra split second to read an accidental or unusual note sequence or arpeggio. this extra time will get you behind, but you will not make a mistake in your playing or miss a beat if you are a measure ahead and end up half a measure ahead. playing along with a recording will help emphasize that in sightreading you should not stop or hesitate!

as i left classical music on the wayside when i was about 18 or so, i had often neglected the idea of reading a measure ahead. when i was in the ceili band that used sheet music, i remembered very quickly that i should be a measure or phrase ahead so as to not have any issues with timing or hesitation.
