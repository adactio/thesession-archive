There’s a bigger question hanging out there - *why* would you want to become a professional? And for that matter, what do you mean by “pro”? I didn’t start playing this music until I was 35, and I play “professionally”, meaning that I teach and perform for money. But it is not my “living”, nor would I want it to be. Could you live a sizable portion of your life on the road? Re: learning fiddle at 27 to old to be a pro?

Becoming a professional trad musician is about as easy as becoming an astronaut. The big advantage that the youngsters have is that they can live on nearly nothing. Once you bring mortgages and kids and not living in a sleeping bag on someone else’s floor into the equation it becomes almost impossible. But if you set your heart on it and devote every waking hour to playing fiddle, you might make the grade by the time you reach your mid forties or fifties.

Making a living out of trad isn’t like playing in a rock band - in a band if you can make a sound that people like you can make money, even if that sound doesn’t take great expertise on your instrument. But for traditional musicians, even top soloists, a lot of their income comes not from performing in their own right, but from playing behind other soloists, and recording fills for adverts and TV. And for those jobs they will be looking for the best fiddler they can find. Being good isn’t enough, you have to be better than everyone else that is available.

I think anyone, regardless of age, who sets their heart on becoming a professional trad musician is a fool. Far better to get a proper job and make music for the fun of it. Then, somewhere down the line, if you do happen to start making money from your music you can think about giving up the day job.

Thank God for fools - it would be a sad world without them.

Re: learning fiddle at 27 to old to be a pro?

I see no reason why you shouldn’t be a pretty good player before your middle thirties and very good in your forties - if you practise. Sometimes it comes on faster than you expect and sometimes it plateaus, and then it’s difficult to keep practising just when you most need to. It’s a bit like a diet and exercise programme - harder when it’s working.

“Professional” just means it’s how you make a living. For every soloist like Tom Kitching or Brendan Hendry there must be any number of pretty good players in bands who put in a bit of fiddle as required. A professional, however good, needs other talents almost more than musicianship. They’re a business and need administration, organisation and accounting. They must work with other people and be patient, friendly and assertive. They have to appeal to an audience - which often depends more on presentation and personality than on musical skill. The travelling is hell for personal relationships. Added to that, in this hard world even the best often need to boost their finances with writing or teaching - and by this time many of them must be wondering why they didn’t take a job which left them more time to play!

Re: learning fiddle at 27 to old to be a pro?

That’s very different, then, Glenn. Yes - absolutely! Start learning now.

For what it’s worth, I took up the fiddle 11 years ago, aged 30 (although already played mandolin and other instruments). I make part of my living out of playing (fiddle and other instruments) - although I avoid the word ‘professional’, since (QED) it can be interpreted in different ways. But, most importantly, I get a lot of enjoyment out of playing the fiddle (and give enjoyment to others too) - and I can feel myself improving, the more I play.

You don’t say whether you have experience of playing another instrument. Previous musical experience has, of course, its advantages, but it can also hold you back in some ways - for example, if you play a different style of music, it might be hard to ‘re-program’ your ear and brain to the subtleties of traditional music. In my case, experience of playing the mandolin was an obvious advantage, since the basic fingering (notwithstanding ornamentation and precise placement of fingers) was wholly transferable to the fiddle. I also play whistle, which gave me the basics of Irish-style ornamentation. But, since I was already competent on the mandolin, I would often neglect the fiddle in favour of that in the early days - had I allowed myself to step outside my comfort zone a bit more and put the mandolin on the shelf for a couple of years, I might perhaps have made faster progress. Once I reached a point, however, where I felt ‘comfortable’ with the fiddle (~4 years ago), my emphasis shifted to that instrument - and it is paying off, I think.

Anyway, you’ll discover these things for yourself, no doubt. But whatever you do, don’t let anyone tell you it’s too late. No sir!

Re: learning fiddle at 27 to old to be a pro?

Nothing very original to contribute, but just to add a bit of encouragement.

Go for it! I started the fiddle a couple of years ago at 34. No regrets, other than wishing I’d started sooner, as I had been thinking about it for a year or so before I took the plunge.

I already gig in a modest way playing guitar and banjo, and hope one day to be able to incorporate the fiddle but it won’t be for a few years. I do feel comfortable taking it to sessions though, and it’s a great feeling when you can (usually) play up to speed and join in.

Although I’m not very good, I enjoy playing the fiddle most of all . Once you get past a certain level there are moments when the instrument really seems to “come alive” in a way I’ve never experienced with anything else. This is a real incentive to practice and try and get better. The first year is a pretty unrewarding grind though, and some lessons or a class are a good idea.

I play other instruments, and have a life outside tune-playing, so I can only practice about an hour a day max, sometimes missing the odd day. I still feel I’m making decent progress so don’t be put off by people mentioning practicing 4 hours a day.

As an adult learner of the fiddle, I think you do have to accept that you will never be as good on some technical levels as someone who learnt the instrument as a child. However, we are talking about folk music that was in the past not played by professional musicians but by people with jobs who probably worked much longer hours that most people do today. If you are committed and enthusiastic, there’s no reason why after a few years you shouldn’t be able to make music that people with want to listen to.

Re: learning fiddle at 27 to old to be a pro?

Not much to add, except that I’m almost exactly in your shoes. I’ll be 27 next month, I took up the tin whistle and flute in high school, didn’t touch them for roughly 10 years, and just recently up the fiddle. I can confirm, being a few months into my first year of playing, that it can indeed be daunting. It’s been said, but I’ll say it again: find a teacher, and you’ll avoid a good bit of frustration and bad habits from day one.

I can perhaps offer one additional piece of advice that I’ve found helpful in my very limited experience: going to sessions could be particularly helpful in getting exposure to the music and getting involved with local musicians who have, as has been said before, “been there, done that.” My experience with this has been overwhelmingly positive, even though I am essentially a rank beginner.

What I’ve been doing is re-acquainting myself with the flute and whistle whilst my fiddle fundamentals are still in their infant stages. There is a “slow session” in my area which caters entirely to beginners (unfortunately only once a month). They help with tune learning, advice, “session etiquette,” etc. I’d recommend finding a whistle, getting the tunes back under your fingers (it comes back surprisingly fast), and seeking out something similar. Even if you’re not really breaking the fiddle out too much at this stage, it sounds like you’ve got a handle on the whistle and you can still participate. I think even just sitting in and listening is beneficial. It also doesn’t hurt to bring some sort of recording device to these kinds of sessions either, and in fact at my local slow session it’s encouraged.

I’ve even been welcomed to the local weekly.. erm.. “not slow” session. I showed up to listen one night, got to talking with a few of them, and they insisted I bring my fiddle the next week, despite my actually only being able to play through about one and a half polkas at the time. I’ve been going back every week (and since I dug my flute up, I’ve not been entirely useless) and I get exposure to some great music and musicians, even if I’m not playing every tune. Of course, not every session is an open session or particularly suited to beginners, so I’d “feel it out” before you turn up with your instrument. Of course I’m speaking from the limited experience of being a more-or-less beginning trad musician from the Midwest US. Your mileage, of course, may vary.

Good luck, and above all, have fun.
