ABC

Inspired by previous thread… Why is ABC notation so popular with trad tune players? In every other musical domain (e.g. jazz, ‘classical’, pop et cetera) people use conventional dot notation. I understand the ABC system is easy for computers to read and for humans to input but, in my experience, so is Sibelius, Finale and so forth! And that is normal dots! If one is go to the bother of learning ABC why not just learn proper dots instead?! And aren’t all the ‘trad’ collections (O’Neill, Bunting, Cecil Sharpe, Carolan etc) all in dots?!

Re: ABC

You have fallen into the common trap of thinking that “computer” ABC (as used on this site) is an alternative to notation, whereas for most it is a tool to represent notation with a computer. Whilst it can be read by a human (it’s just text) I believe the majority of users don’t play from the text but use one of the many free programs on online converters to render it as proper musical notation, and to play it back as MIDI. In this respect it is no different from the programs you mention. It’s no different from using keyboard input with these programs, except with those you only see the notation and not the underlying code whereas with ABC you’re in effect writing the code which is also rendered as notation. It’s just a different approach to the same thing.

Personally, I find it considerably easier than the more complex programs for writing simple melodies, and whilst it’s capable of writing more complex music it’s probably not the right tool. It’s horses for courses - for simple folk tunes I’ll use ABC, for more complex music I’ll use something like Musescore. However for most purposes I don’t need the complexity Musescore and similar programs offer, and I don’t use it often enough to become slick with it. ABC has quite a short learning curve. Most notation programs can now import ABC, or you can use a converter to change it into xml, so you don’t have to learn ABC in order to use it. If you prefer other programs then carry on using them.

It does have other advantages: file sizes are smaller (OK, not the issue it once was, but still a consideration especially when sending tunes to other people, and not doubt for the people who run websites like this with large collections of tunes). It’s plain text, so it can be pasted into online forums which may not accept attachments. It can be written and read by a human without needing access to a computer.

Not least, because it has become the de facto standard for folk tunes there are huge online resources which use it to represent tunes. If you want to find a tune from the British Isles/North American traditions (and from quite a few others) you’ll probably find them in ABC.

I believe a slightly different version of ABC has long been used for teaching purposes, particularly in Ireland. I speculate that this is because in a class situation where there may be a mix of players with some who can read the dots and others who can’t then simply using the note names is more accessible for everyone.

Re: ABC

Yet another advantage of ABC notation is that a tune transcription can be quickly and easily edited. You simply replace the notes you want modified. In contrast with staff notation, editing ABCs is extremely efficient.

This is useful, for instance, when you want settings for different instruments. Where flute, whistle, or pipes might play a long roll: |e~A3 E~A3|, a fiddle, banjo, or mandolin would likely substitute a triplet: |eA A/A/A EA A/A/A|, or perhaps even some melodic variation: |eA c/B/A EAcA|.

Or maybe you want to transcribe variations for a phrase:
|~g3 ~f3| edB e2f|
|gfe fed|edB e2f|
|gbg faf|edB e2f|
|gag fgf|eB/c/d e2f|
|ge/f/g fed|edB e2f|

Or perhaps your sense of the tune has evolved over the years and you want to notate those changes. Or you play the tune in a different key—with a quick cut and paste, Mr. Eskin’s ABC tool (among others) enables instant transposition up or down any number of half and whole steps.

Sharing any of this across the etherweb is as easy as typing (or copying and pasting) the ABCs directly into the body of an email, or into a comment box, as on this site. No attachment needed, no extra step of generating staff notation, and then other people can use that source ABC however they wish.

All in the blink of an eye.

I learn tunes by ear, play by ear, teach by ear, but notation has its uses and some advantages. I’ll happily read staff notation, ABCs, and various tablature systems. None of them are truly difficult, and it’s entirely possible to sight read at speed (100 bpm) any of these systems once you’re familiar with them. If ABCs aren’t your cuppa, no worries. But for some of us, they’re a simple, wonderfully adaptable tool.

Re: ABC

ABC was popular in folk traditions because so simple. Usually no bar markings or note durations just the melody.

Classical / Jazz repertoire is more complex and needs much more notation to capture. ABC would be poor representation in basic form or challenging to read if fully decorated.

In early computer times it was popular for being compact (bytes) and human readable and easily edited. Small text files or emails were helpful.

Its enduring popularity is testament to its simplicity (reading or editing) and portability. It’s safe to store tunes in this format long term. And of course it’s easy with so many available tools to produce booklets or sheet music from ABC.

Tunes stored in ABC format will endure long after many other formats will have faded.

Re: ABC

Notation is just a way of conveying information about music (not all information, there is a lot it doesn’t include). ABC is just a different way of conveying the same information. It follows the same rules and syntax, which is why it can easily be rendered as notation. Think of it as notation, but written as text rather than as a graphic. You still have to understand the principles of conventional notation to write it as ABC.

The great strength of notation is its ability to convey a lot of simultaneous information. In music a lot can be happening at once, perhaps across multiple staves. That is easy to represent graphically, but much more difficult using text which is linear. However folk tunes are linear, we are usually interested only in the melody and if chords are needed they can be added as names above the staff rather than being fully notated. ABC is very suited to this.

Of course the other strength of notation is that it is universal and opens up a whole world of music beyond traditional folk. However even if you have learned to read music then ABC may still be useful as a way of writing music on a computer (or on any scrap of paper if you don’t have manuscript paper to hand). If you are already familiar with one of the more sophisticated score writers there is no need to change, but if not and if your main interest is folk music and you don’t need the complexity they offer then you may find that ABC is easier to learn.

I have little formal music education, I’m largely self-taught and I play by ear. I usually use ABC as way of transcribing music. I find it easier to write out note names than enter them graphically onto a stave, but I usually refer to the output as notation rather than text. What I rely on most is being able to play it back as MIDI, which not only helps correct my input but is more reliable than my attempts to play from dots.

I see ABC and notation as complementary, not in competition. I have found that writing ABC has greatly improved my understanding of notation and ability to read it, because the playback lets me identify and correct mistakes which paper cannot do. I doubt I could have achieved this with the more sophisticated score writers (which I also use when appropriate) as they tend to assume a greater understaning of notation to begin with.

Simply put, you can type ABC and send it to your friend using your old button Nokia phone. Try that with Sibelius.

But what if I want a really pretty printout of my score with cool engraving? Export ABC as XML and import into your software of choice for printing - done! At the end of the day, it’s just a piece of code – the only difference being, the source code of ABC notation is easily readable by humans.

Bottom line: the abilities to read and write staff notation and ABC are not mutually exclusive, it’s just a matter of convenience and preference and ABC tends to suit traditional music perfectly well. DonaldK,

For Safari, don’t use the file menu “Export as PDF” item, that does just take a screen shot and save it as PDF.

You want to select the “Print” item on the File menu, then at the bottom center there is a dropdown that says “PDF”.

Click that and you’ll see an item labeled “Save as PDF”.

That will create a vector-based PDF with one tune-per-page. That’s the only option possible if you don’t use my own PDF exporter, which can put multiple tunes on a page, break long tunes between pages, etc.

Other browsers like Chrome and Safari don’t have a dedicated “Export as PDF” option on their File menu, all native browser PDF export (i.e. not using my custom PDF exporter) is done using the Print dialog.

Using the native browser PDF exporter can be used to do some interesting things (again, if you don’t care about my tool’s PDF exporter custom features) like combine multiple pages on a single page mosaic and all that sort of more printer-oriented options.

Re: ABC

To be absolutely clear, %%gracespace (and many other annotations) are specific to abcm2ps and other command line ABC tools and are not part of either the ABC 2.1 or 2.2 spec.

They are tool-specific proprietary annotations.

I have my own proprietary annotations for doing things like having a lot more control over the duration and volume stress of elements in a %%MIDI gchord control string, requesting a table of contents/index in an exported PDF with specific format, and probably 30 or 40 others, some very specific to the rendering of traditional Irish music when playing tunes.

The difference is that I don’t make them look like they are part of the spec by sticking %% behind the annotation as some other tool and ABC library builders have done.

Mine look like comments with a single % followed by a unique keyword to my tool, or use the recommended %%abctt:command syntax specified in the ABC 2.1 spec.

This guarantees that if you were to open an ABC tune file that has any of my proprietary ABC extensions, in another ABC tool, they will appear as comments and not break the tool.

You may not get the same result as with my tool but other tools should ignore my annotations. “Do no harm” is my mantra.

For example, there is nothing in the ABC spec on how in the ABC to create a table of contents/index, specify hyperlinks on tunes, all the stuff I had to make up my own annotations for my tool.

ABC, in my opinion, initially was focused on production of printed music notation with a mashup of music and page layout commands, and really not so much with a future vision of distributed interactive use cases for ABC, which is my focus.

These days there is no true ABC standard, there are just many tools that for the most part can exchange some sort of baseline ABC 2.1-ish file and usually produce some kind of reasonable output.

Additionally there is nothing like an actual standards committee to ensure compliance or any kind of path forward to unification of the various flavors of ABC and tools.

In the past I have been on actual international standards committees, and ABC is not remotely a true standard like JPEG or MPEG.

Standards like JPEG and MPEG exist because there are massive financial interests in ensuring compatibility between consumer and broadcast products.

There is nothing like that driving ABC nor can I imagine that changing ever in the future.

Going forward, there will always be those that prefer the compiled command line tools, but in my opinion the real growth will be in web-based solutions like mine based on abcjs and possibly others based on abc2svg.

abc2svg is a fantastic platform but in my experience not as commonly used on websites as abcjs. The tools I have tried built with it so far are not very friendly to the average user. That could change in the future. That’s not a strike against abc2svg, it’s just an observation.

I chose abcjs three years ago because I was able to find some excellent examples and just ran with it. I then started customizing the heck out of it for my needs. I wasn’t even aware of abc2svg until quite recently.

Both abcjs and abc2svg have their strengths and weaknesses.

For the most part I have found that I’ve been able to work around the occasional limitations of abcjs, and when not appropriate for users with very specific needs, I am more than happy to recommend other tools.

I agree that the command line tools do offer baseline stability and predictability for the static printed output use case.

My experience with the native compiled GUI tools like EasyABC is that the underlying OS developer (Apple or Microsoft) changes something in the OS and now the tool either can’t be installed without jumping through hoops or disabling security checks, or some basic functionality gets broken like PDF export and the developers have to scramble to patch the issue. Not very friendly to the less technical person who doesn’t want to have to open a command line or modify configuration files.

Browsers are also increasingly more powerful and in my mind are the new platform. I’m blown away by what can be done using just JavaScript and increasingly, software companies are moving away from desktop installed tools to web-based equivalents.

Before I retired, I worked for 12 years for SmartDraw, and as part of a small team, over the course of about 2 years completely reimplemented their long-selling Windows tool, which was written in C, as a client-side web tool written in HTML/CSS/Javascript. Over the next few years, they transitioned the business successfully to only offering the web tool and switched from selling and shipping CDs to a 100% web tool subscription model.

Browsers are also standards driven (HTML, CSS, ES5/6 JavaScript), and for at least the big three browsers that have the vast majority of market share: Chrome (Edge is also Chrome-based), Safari, and Firefox, I’ve seen very few browser-specific incompatibility issues with my tool over it’s three year development. There is always the possibility that one of them will issue an update and completely break my tool. It hasn’t happened yet, but staying vigilant for this possibility is just the reality of modern web development.
