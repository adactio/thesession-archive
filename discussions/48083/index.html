Re: Learning accompaniments

You can start by using your voice to sing along to the notes that “move” where the tune is resting on, humming a sort of bass line. That should come naturally enough, especially if you’re familiar already with the melodies.

After that start practicing the same thing with your instrument, just playing on the most important notes, the ones that seem to imply new chords. Once you’ve internalised your singing into the instrument you can start playing the full chord from the root of the note you’re on and seeing which ones work and which ones don’t then asking why. After a while of this it will become second nature what to do.

Re: Learning accompaniments

There are no short cuts here.

Either find some chords someone else has already made for the tunes in the tunes section of this site or learn to do proper backup using Chris Smith’s book and exercises or any of the other resources recommended.

If you decide, “oh, I don’t have time for that” and start just making up stuff, in my experience with other players in your situation, your chances for success in any real way are very small. It’s not that hard to learn to do correctly, and by correctly I mean at least be able to identify the key and mode for parts of a tune tune and come up with an appropriate chord sequence which follows the melody. Since you said you already know tunes, that will greatly help inform your choices once you identify the key and mode. You’re already in a much better place than, for example, a guitar player who doesn’t know the tunes or how to back, but wants to play backup in a session.

This handout might be useful to you, from a class on basic backup for traditional Irish music I taught at a festival many years ago:

http://michaeleskin.com/handouts/Irish_Session_Backup_11Mar2021.pdf

Re: Learning accompaniments

I started in this music accompanying my fiddler Significant Other on guitar, coming from a background in Blues guitar and some beginner level Jazz. My first instructional materials were the Chris Smith book mentioned above, and a John Doyle instructional video. The latter led me to invest in Drop-D tuning, which for me was a better intro than going full DADGAD as I could leverage what I already knew about the fretboard. That big fat bottom D is useful in this music and you only have to learn a few new chord shapes. Later on, I moved into playing melody which is all I do now on mandolin and flute.

The best *general* advice I can give on guitar accompaniment when you’re moving into Irish traditional music from styles like Blues and Rock, is that you’re no longer the bedrock foundation for the tunes. In other styles, chordal accompaniment is a fixed foundation for the melody players to move around on top. You establish the rhythm and chord structure, they follow.

In ITM it’s the reverse (at least that’s how I see it, others here may differ). You follow the rhythm pulse and tempo established by the melody players. Listen first to where they’re going, and figure out how to support it. Follow the pulse in the melody, which can have many variations in straight-ahead vs. backbeat vs. swing feel depending on what the melody players are doing. Don’t impose strong chordal statements if the music “wants” to sound more ambiguous. Leaving out the third interval in chords is always an option there. You’re in a much more secondary role doing accompaniment in this music, not the foundation, so lean into that and follow the melody players. Don’t try to lead them.

Re: Learning accompaniments

I agree listening is crucial, and there are many different styles, but just listening isn’t going to be enough. I truly believe to learn to back you have to put that listening in some kind of larger structural context and that’s where the training materials we’ve offered can be of great assistance. Otherwise, one can learn to mimic another player’s playing, which works to some extent until it doesn’t and then things can go very sideways.

We’ve all been there. Some new player to a session with a guitar shows up and thinks they know how to back E-Dorian tunes because they learned to back Road to Lisdoonvarna. When presented with a more complex tune, for example Mayor Harrison’s Fedora, they’ll just keep playing the same Em/DMaj sequence through the third part with no concept of what’s actually going on. After a few tunes where this sort of thing happens, they’re written off as a clueless backup player, because, well, they are. That’s sad and can easily be avoided.

Learn to listen, learn what to listen for, and learn the theory, in the long run, you’ll be a much better backup player for the work.

For me, it’s also about listening and having enough theoretical knowledge to allow you to make informed choices. The better you get at listening the more often what you play in accompaniment will work.

Don’t fall into the trap of over analysing tune scores and using your eyes rather than your ears. This can be a blind alley. What, on paper, look like the right chords can often be turn out to be rather poor at helping to tell the story of the tune.

The backer job apart from playing the right bloody chords , is to make everyone else sound better than they are by supporting and enhancing the tunes and players Varying the right hand to create musical variation between parts .
So with cooleys you will notice both parts have the same chord pattern… so as a backer you need to vary the way you play those chords NOT changing them in some vain attempt to add variation and be clever but using different inversions or strumming patterns etc etc etc silences, rests , half chords ,bass runs or whatever your personal style and approach is .

When i know the tune , but not the chords , my conscious mind gets involved , like “ oh thats so and so and straight away the flow is interrupted.
Knowing how to play the tune on a different instrument is no help . It can be a hinderance .

The tune is single notes one after the other , the backing is the tune stacked in chords .
Flat picking on guitar is different because the tune and chords are all in the same place so to speak . So as you play the tune the chord shapes are to an extent in exactly the same place , such as the Monaghan jig .

Re: Learning accompaniments

In my experience, knowing the tunes absolutely can inform and make life much easier for a backer.

When it’s clear that a tune is at first clearly in D, knowing that there’s a C-natural instead of a C# might make the difference between backing a D Mix tune with a D Major, or that, in a tune with a few quirks, like The Cook in the Kitchen, knowing the use of the F natural in the A part absolutely would change the choice of chords rather than falling into some automatic pattern, and knowing the last part changes key to D is important, certainly discoverable on-the-fly, but so much easier if you know the tune.

Knowing the tunes is even more useful for those tunes that don’t start with the I chord, like “Providence” or “Ah, Surely”. Those are excellent examples of where a clueless backer will almost always self-destruct, and sometimes, when confronted with a “new guitarist to the session who insists they can back” situation, I’ll throw one of those tunes in the mix to see if they actually know what they are doing, because, well, I’m evil, but in a caring supportive way.

Different horses for different courses, I guess. Whatever works to not have your fellow session players shoot daggers from their eyes at you is fine.

Re ‘busking’ (in the sense of winging an accompaniment ad hoc)
I think musicians, especially pianists, had great busking skills in the past (yes! I know! Micheal Coleman’s pianist withstanding!) A singer would approach the bloke at the piano, start a song and the Joanna player would quickly find the key and extemporise a chordal back drop.
60 or 70 years ago the guitar and blues based pop music became popular and mainstream. Nearly everything was a 12 bar or a I, vi, IV, V! The three chord trick. Skills were lost. As guitar based music developed, the chord progressions became the major road map of music, aided and abetted by the chord sequence informed jamming of the jazzers. Melody based ad hoc chord selection techniques gone for ever!
And that’s where we are now with guitar holders unable to play unless “they know the chords”. The only ‘tune’ they know is the dreaded 12 bar (and most don’t even know the nice variations on that!). As useless as classical players that can’t play the simplest tune by ear and absolutely have to have the dots!

Hi @Tervs and Tunes - harmony, while not integral to ITM, is basically the interaction between two or more notes played at the same time - a D on fiddle, an F# on tin whistle and an A on flute would produce a D major chord. The interaction of those notes is the harmony.

When I refer to harmony above, it’s to distinguish playing backing or accompaniment (harmony), from the lead tune itself (melody).

OP, consider the common theme in this thread: “bad backers are evil!” (but bad fiddlers, fluters and whistlers get a pass). A spherical “bad backer” in vacuum is two-dimensional:

1) “your rhythm sucks”, yes it does, work on the right hand technique with a metronome, and learn all the cool grooves, go to every rhythm workshop out there (including drum workshops) (at the latest Fiddle Hell, Nataly Haas workshop “playing rhythm on cello” was excellent!)

2) “you are playing the wrong chords”, yes, you do, instead, play chords from the Portland Collection (tunes used for contra dances, includes all the common irish reels and jigs) and you will get the least number of complaints: these are chords played by people who fill large gyms and auditoriums with dancers, they earn tens of dollars and are rich and famous. play chords from other sources, ymmv. when people start telling you “I liked your chords”, you know you are getting somewhere.

Re: Learning accompaniments

Dylan,

I hope you’ve gotten something useful out of all this!

I’ve been working away at accompaniment for a wee while now, about 7 years, and I’m coming from a melody player perspective.

Learning the tunes are important, but arguably, not really as important as learning the feeling and drive of what accompanists employ to support a tune. There are a lot of tunes that ask for specific chord changes, but most tunes are really quite open to interpretation.

To me, it’s a balance of respecting the tune (and the people you’re playing with), while still painting a harmonic picture that is true to you.

Listening is the most helpful thing you can do. Listen to the groups that have sets that light you up. Really clue into what the backing members are doing…and then recreate that! Having a healthy understanding of basic music theory is suuuuuper duper helpful here!

At the end of the day, this whole music thing is a big ole process that we will never actually “master”. It’s a way of connecting with ourselves, and likeminded people, and sharing in something that is meaningful and feels good, so try not to get so wrapped up in the bullshit of deprecating remarks. Just keep working away at it, as long as it feels good to you.

Happy playing, Dylan!

“Farewell To Erin” - a 2-part reel in “D” [ #701 in “O’Neill’s 1,001 ” ]. It is Irish.
“Farewell To Ireland” - a 4-part reel in “Ador” [ #805 in “O‘Neill’s 1,001” ]. It is also Irish, but it has its’ origins in the Scottish strathspey “The Highlander’s Farewell To Ireland”.
