Re: Fiddler’s Frustration

Keep going - 2 years is nothing on fiddle, especially
since you say you’ve never played anything else. Your brain
has to learn how to do a few things on autopilot. There’s no
fast way to make that happen as far as I know, except by being
a child again.

Technique is important but more important is listening to what’s coming
out. Are all the notes in tune all the time?
Probably not. Do you play with great rhythm that makes people
want to tap their toes? Probably not. That is so hard to do because
it’s a lot to do with how you interpret tunes - how you think about them,
how you edit them to bring out some kind of a groove.

Technique you have to work on forever - left hand, right
hand, all your fingers, arms, wrists, shoulders, head, neck,
angles, pressure. You can’t monitor all of it all the time. You have
pick on key things. Like for example spend two weeks focusing
on your right hand and fingers - what is each finger and your
thumb doing? Look in the mirror, record yourself.

Re: Fiddler’s Frustration

If you think you aren’t improving, try playing left-handed -- that’s more or less what you were like when you started out. As for your teacher -- after two years, I doubt if he or she has anything more to teach you. There’s not much point in paying to learn tunes, so unless you are actually learning new techniques, I would go it alone or look elsewhere.
Recording yourself is soul-destroying sometimes; but you are an honest and ruthless critic, and it is learning to hear yourself while playing in the same way you hear a recording that is the hard part.
Two years is nothing (bad grammar aside), and although it is all uphill from here, you are clear of the foothills, and the view gets better. Look back at some of your old recordings if you want encouragement. I keep a folder marked ‘My Progress’ with mp3s and bits of video. One end is dark and dingy and no-one goes there. The other has little glimmers of light, and drawers that occasionally get looked in.
Keep going -- it is worth it.

Re: Fiddler’s Frustration

I’ll be two years at the fiddle in a weeks time. I understand your pain.

I have a little advantage over you because I played guitar for many years, and I don’t mean I strummed the thing while I croaked out a few songs, I mean I actually played it.

Actually that’s a large advantage, because it meant that for the most part, I didn’t really have to worry too much about the left hand. A little adjustment of position, and get used to the tuning and that was it really.

Some people say that the fiddle/violin is really difficult to learn, and it is, but that has nothing to do with the left hand. You’d be hard pushed to find an easier pitch interface than the traditional irish fiddle neck. There’s no stretching. For the the most part the hand remains in the same position and the lack of frets means that if you find you’re a touch out - simply slide up or down. Identifying your out a little of course - that requires a good deal of musical experience.

OK so if you find you still have problems with your left hand - I can tell you what to do to ensure you work through those as quickly as possible:

1. Learn as many Tunes as you can remember - this will also help you memory and ability to learn quickly will increase.

2. Play scales - and intervals.

3. Play everyday at least an hour.

Number 2 isn’t so important if you learn a new tune every second day or so - the purpose of doing this is to give your left hand enough diverse patterns of notes to run through that it becomes second nature to do so without thinking, its when your thinking that you make mistakes I find. You want to have it played before your mind has an opportunity to mess it up.

Now as for the Right-Hand that’s what in my opinion makes the fiddle very tough indeed. Also you need to be able to coordinate your right-hand movements with your left-hand movements. Others can give you advice on that aspect of it, my own opinion, and what I do is:

1. Play slowly until I know the tune and have a basic bowing patterns.

2. Play faster to elicit alternative bowing patterns.

3. Play light and hard to develop muscle control.

4. Play different types of tunes (jigs/reels/airs) to develop muscle control and approriate rhythm and pulse.

5. Play with other people.

6. Try to emulate the sound of other musicians (until such time as I have enough control to control the music).

Anyway no doubt some will disagree with all or many of my points - but you asked a question and this is my answer, and simply my approach. Good luck.

Oh and as for plateaus - there only in your head - just make sure you keep giving yourself new challenges to overcome. That’ll cause you to keep climbing.

Re: Fiddler’s Frustration

I read a quote once that went something like:
‘The greatest musician considers every performance to be a failure’.
There will always be things you could improve on, and I think that’s part of the fun. Isolating problems and working on them until they are not problems anymore.

Secondly, plateauing is a natural part of learning any skill. If people just got better exponentially from the moment they started learning, they’d all be amazing within a few years! The tricky part is working through it. I liken it to that feeling you get when you can’t get to sleep; you start to panic that you’re not sleeping, making it even harder to drop off. It’s easy to worry that you’re suddenly pants at playing, which makes you even less inspired to practice. A real vicious circle. Try playing a new style of music. Even a new tune. Or maybe just take a break and listen to a load of CDs to inspire you once again. You’ll know when you get it back :)

Re: Fiddler’s Frustration

Two things:

1) I second (or third) the point about hitting plateaus -- I believe that the better you get, the more discriminating your ear gets, so you hear faults that you wouldn’t have noticed six months ago. But what also happens is that the better you get, the more subtle the faults will become, to a point that the audience won’t notice at all -- but you will. I believe that’s what Fred’s saying referred to: ‘The greatest musician considers every performance to be a failure’. So accept it as part of the process. And that’s the beauty of music, I think -- that you can always hear yourself improve, now matter how old or how “good” you are.

2) I don’t know how much you practice, but if you’re practicing steadily, you might consider taking a day or two off. Interesting things happen when you come back. You may hear yourself differently -- and realize that you sound pretty good, after all, for where you are. And also you might get some new insight into how to fix a problem. I’ve found that I’ll come back and realize that I want to improve a certain bowing, for instance, that I wouldn’t have “noticed” if I hadn’t taken a day off.

Re: Fiddler’s Frustration

Over time the bow hair accumulates grease and caked-on rosin that new rosin will not stick to. The result is a coarser sound particularly on the lower strings. After two years you are probably due a re-hair but you could check it out by trying someone else’s bow.

Re: Fiddler’s Frustration

I haven’t re-haired a bow in ten years. If rosin doesn’t stick to it, give it a wash. I would listen to your teacher on this one.
One other thing that has been hinted at above, and indeed by yourself, Malena -- don’t just look for / listen to the mistakes. If you play a tune and make, say, twenty mistakes, that’s over a hundred notes you got right.
Equally important is to listen for the good things, the unexpected sounds that crop up from time to time, the things you do better than expected -- and remember them. A well-played note in a whole tune can leave you with a feeling of well-being that lasts all day. Learning is not just about eliminating all the bad stuff, it’s also about putting in the good.
