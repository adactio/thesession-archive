I don’t know whereabouts in the world you are, Taran, but I think the usual message here is probably the best to get you going: I would say just turn up at the session, and be prepared not to play anything, or hardly anything, that first night. Listen a lot. If you can take a digital recording device with you, that might help. Then you can listen to the tunes later when you get home.

The point being to get to know the tunes that your local session musicians play. If you go regularly, then you will gradually pick up the tunes they play, and then you’ll be away! :-)

Three right answers in a row. The only thing I’d add to that would be: listen to the records that you like and pick the tunes that grab you the most. Start trying to figure those out. Learning tunes by ear, off the records, will be good practice for when you’re at the session and you hear one you like.

If you really want a list of tunes that you can be sure others will know - it’s a nice security blanket, I know - select the Members tab at the right of the screen and then select “Tunebook”. This will give you a list of commonly-known tunes. Best to use this as a list of tunes to find and learn, though, and not click through to the sheet music. Resist the temptation, it’ll help you in the long run. I know from experience - knowing no better, I started out by learning these tunes from books, and I think it really hampered my progress for years.

Re: First Session

nednog’s advice is no good. Don’t play tunes you don’t know. One thing that most session players agree on is that you should practice at home, not in the session. You may not be making a lot of noise, but trust me, the person next to you will wish he were sitting somewhere else. You will see experienced players learn tunes on the fly, but that takes years of practice and they’re drawing on similarities to tunes they already know.

Everyone spends their first year (or more) listening a lot. If you don’t like listening to the music in the first place, I can’t fathom why you would want to play it.

Ask the session leads for suggestions of tunes you might learn. Every group of players has different favorite tunes. It can be good to record a little if the players don’t mind. Be discreet and don’t disturb the flow of the session recording. That way you can get the local settings and feel for tunes that appeal to you.

Noodling need not be a problem if it’s done right. Once you’ve got the knack of spotting the pivotal notes in a tune, hitting them along with everybody else and then unobtrusively holding them or dropping out will usually add to the overall effect.

But if your best shot is playing something that’s 25% tune, 75% accompaniment and 90% pure guesswork with no idea of the overall structure, please go away for a while.

Since no one has offered it yet, here are 50 tunes, a list first developed by thesession.org member ‘Dow,’ which are pretty common the world around. Some of them are a bit overplayed, or felt to be out of fashion, but generally, when they get played at a session, most everyone in the circle can join in. Learning them is a good place to start.

Reels: The Banshee [James McMahon], The Bird In The Bush, The Bucks Of Oranmore, The Concertina Reel, The Congress, Cooley’s (Luttrell’s Pass), The Cup Of Tea, Drowsy Maggie, Farewell To Ireland, Father Kelly’s (Rossmore Jetty), The Foxhunter’s, The Gravel Walks, The Maid Behind The Bar, The Merry Blacksmith, Miss McLeod’s, The Mountain Road [Michael Gorman], Rolling In The Ryegrass (The Shannon Breeze), Saint Anne’s, The Sally Gardens, The Silver Spear, The Star Of Munster, The Wise Maid (All Around The World).
Jigs: The Blackthorn Stick, The Blarney Pilgrim, The Cliffs Of Moher, The Connaughtman’s Rambles, Donnybrook Fair (The Joy Of My Life), The Irish Washerwoman, The Kesh, The Lark In The Morning, The Lilting Banshee, Morrison’s, My Darling Asleep, Out On The Ocean, The Rakes Of Kildare, Tripping Up The Stairs.
Hornpipes & Set Dances: The Boys Of Bluehill, Harvest Home, King Of The Fairies, Off To California, The Rights Of Man
Slip Jigs: The Foxhunter’s, The Kid On The Mountain.
Hop Jigs: The Butterfly, The Rocky Road To Dublin.
Slides: Merrily Kissed The Quaker’s Wife, The Road To Lisdoonvarna.
Polkas: Denis Murphy’s, Egan’s, John Ryan’s (The Keadue).

And the advice to listen first, and join in slowly, is the way to go. But every journey starts with a single step, so step in there and start enjoying the ride!!!

Re: First Session

Hmmm. It doesn’t bother me if someone quietly tries to pick up a tune they don’t know, especially on something like mandolin, which can be played really softly. So if “mandolinist” were sitting next to me and tried to learn a couple of tunes over the course of an evening, I’d more likely be encouraging, offering help, maybe even quietly playing the tune over again during a break in the sets.

So it might not hurt to ask your neighbors in the circle if they’d mind. But even if they are encouraging, don’t be greedy. Just try one or maybe two tunes, and go for ones that you’ve already heard before, are short and fairly simple, and going at a slower pace.

Being able to play along to variations and different settings of tunes on the fly is a valuable and welcomed skill among good session players. The abilities to do that are basically the same as learning tunes on the fly. The sooner you start learning those skills, the sooner you’ll grow comfortable and eventually fluent with them. Sure, don’t make a pest out of yourself, and do your homework (at home). But if your local session doesn’t mind a couple of exploratory forays now and then, go for it.

Re: First Session

I think the absolute minimum for being ready to try to pick up a tune on the fly is being able to recognize it - “ones you’ve already heard before”, and ones you know when you hear them. “Ah, that’s the one that does that thing in the third part” is a good clue that you’re in a place to start learning the tune. If it sounds like all the other ones, leave it, you’ll get it later. But if you hear one particular bit standing out, you might already know where that part lies - then you can use that bit to get the rest. Jim Ward’s is a good example of that for me - I heard it a few times, and then one times when the B part started I just sort of knew how it went, for a little bit, so I played that bit and then I realized I knew the rest of it.
But the hunt-and-peck method is no good at all for finding a tune, especially when people are trying to play it.

Jon, when someone’s been playing this music for three decades or more, chances are they’ve heard many of the tunes before. And, importantly, they’ve heard all the building blocks. So all it takes is hearing a tune a few times to pick it up, third or fourth time around. And tunes that do something quirky or idiosyncratic aren’t any harder, because the quirky bit stands out from the rest, and so you learn that on third or fourth hearing, too.

(Yes, I realize that my assumptions here don’t likely apply to our OP. Just saying that more experienced players can learn what most people would consider “unfamiliar” tunes upon first hearing them.

Re: First Session

I wouldn’t disagree with you there, Will. I think that that familiarity comes a lot quicker with a depth of experience, and can even arrive in the first or second hearing of a tune (which may actually be the thirty-first or second, or the hundred and thirty-first or second). When you hear a tune that you don’t remember hearing before, you’re likely hearing pieces of tunes you’ve played hundreds of times, put together in some slightly new way - of course it’s easier at that point to catch that immediate sense of the tune.
I was specifically thinking of the experience of the new player, who might think that “getting the tune on the fly” is something you do by just diving into it and trying notes until one or two of them fit. Obviously, this is not the way it’s done, but to the beginner it can easily seem that this is what you’re doing when you hear the Milliner’s Second-Youngest Daughter With the Long Golden Hair for the first time, and realize that the A part is pretty much like Miss Monaghan with a B part that’s more like that other one you can’t remember the name of but it goes like this - and you’re in the tune on the third time through. It looks very simple when an experienced player does it - my point was, it isn’t, and one of the things to listen for is something you can feel under your fingers before you try to play it. At least, at a session, I think that’s the best way to go.

Re: First Session

Meh. If learning by ear is so difficult, why do we all know the melody and words to Happy Birthday as soon as we can talk? :-)

Chris wrote: “Sheet music can help with the finer bits when things are played fast in a pub.” Not likely. Sheet music rarely has any correlation to the tunes (much less the “finer bits”) played in a pub session. Unless the pub session is astoundingly bad.

Sure, learning by ear comes slowly for some people--usually the people who convince themselves that it’s difficult. Really, if you just relax and sing the tunes (either lilting them aloud or in your head), there’s nothing strenuous about it. The more you do it, the more you hear, the better you get at it.

Many people who think they’re slow at ear learning are actually just not fluent yet on their instruments. But it’s never too early to start sharpening your ear skills.
