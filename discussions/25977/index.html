“The word ornament implies a musical element that could just as well be left out, leaving the essence of the music perfectly intact. "When I speak of ornamentation in traditional Irish music I am referring to ways of altering or embellishing small
pieces or cells of a melody that are between one and three eighth-note beats long. These alterations and embellishments are created mainly through the use of special fingered articulations and inflections, not through the addition of extra, ornamental or grace notes."

Re: Now you see it ~ now you don’t

Having the articulation notated in written forms of this music is a somewhat silly thing, IMO. If you’re a beginner, trying to learn how to make it sound right from written notation, then you’re not getting it. And if you’ve been playing for a while, and haven’t internalized how it should sound, and need to rely on written notation to remind you, then you haven’t been listening to enough music.

I like the flexibility of expression in this music. Yes, there are certain parts of certain tunes that don’t sound right when they’re not articulated properly (although, there is still a certain musical expression to be had by intentionally leaving out a common “ornament”). But if you get into the habit of articulating it exactly the same way every time, then your playing is predictable and boring. And again, if you can’t automatically feel those parts that need it to sound right, then you’re not listening to enough of it.

Re: Now you see it ~ now you don’t

I wish Grey had used “articulation” throughout his book, in place of “ornamentation.” A editor more savvy with this music could’ve helped a great deal with the prose.

When I teach tunes, to avoid the dilemma over whether to include twiddly bits or just teach the bones, I usually start simple and then give lots of options for most of the obvious places that beg for twiddly bits. For example:

Tommy People’s Reel
K: Bm
B3c dBAF|B3c dfed|B3c dcdB|ABde fded|

I’ll teach the opening phrase as above, with those dotted quarters played plain. Then I’ll suggest breaking them into quarter/eighth combos:

B2 Bc dBAF|B2 Bc dfed|B2 Bc dcdB|

and then demonstrate how to get that B2 B sound with a cut:

B2{d}Bc dBAF|B2{d}Bc dfed|B2{d}Bc dcdB|

Which becomes the basis for playing a roll there:

~B3c dBAF|~B3c dfed|~B3c dcdB|

Then I might introduce scratch triplets, first on the back beat:

B B/B/B c dBAF|B B/B/B c dfed|B B/B/B c dcdB|

then on the downbeat:

B/B/B Bc dBAF|B/B/B Bc dfed|B/B/B Bc dcdB|

then moving triplets:

B A/A/B c dBAF|B/B/A Bc dfed|A/B/A Bc dcdB|

Then I’ll go through some melodic variations:

BABc dBAF|BFBc dfed|F~B3 dcdB|

By now it’s pretty clear that all these ideas are interchangeable, which creates the opening to talk about personal style and interpretation and building a coherent flow through the tune each time.

Re: Now you see it ~ now you don’t

Rev, are you setting me up? ;-)

The first melodic variation I do is simple: BAB instead of B3. That these forms can swap out for one another is a fundamental trait--a cliche even--of this music. I point out that this “variation” is nothing more than playing the main note (B in this case) followed by the next note in the key/mode scale of the tune below (or, often, the note above), then back to the main note.

The crucial point to make here is that this is *not* melodic variation just for the sake of melodic variation. Sure, you get to feature a note or two that might not otherwise be part of the tune. But what’s really happening is that those “extra” notes allow you to play the phrase with a different sense of timing and/or emphasis on the beats. Because now you have more options for slurring notes together, or playing them distinct and separate, and varying the emphasis on each note.

By now, most people in a workshop are overloaded, so I don’t usually get into how to do more far-flung melodic variations. If we do, I talk about letting the tune suggest other notes. If that’s too abstract and esoteric, then I talk about trying the notes of the triad of whatever chord might go behind the melody in the phrase at hand. In me examples above, I used F sharp--the 5th note of the Bm triad. And if people don’t want to think about chords and harmonic theory, then I show them some simple finger relationships that tend to work well in this music.

Es claro? :-)

Re: Now you see it ~ now you don’t

For me, melodic variation is an ongoing battle between being deliberate and being sloppy. If I relax and let the music flow, there can be some amazing things that appear as part of the expression of the tune, but there is often a bit of sloppiness as well, because I’m not always sure exactly where it’s going, and there can be some hesitancy in my finger movements.

If there isn’t a fair amount of deliberate control over where it’s going, then it can also turn into wild improvisation that loses the musical idea, and is just annoying “key noise”, which may be fine for jazz, but doesn’t work well in a unison environment.

These are things that I think you get better at over time – I certainly have over the last few years. But I also imagine that it’s an ongoing battle…

Another potential issue is that many people think of melodic variation in the way that they might hear in a commercial recording, which will be very deliberate most of the time. So much so, that many of the things that sound really interesting to us are actually leaving the key/mode for a moment (what Eliot Grasso calls “modal substitution”). This is something that all the players need to know about, or you’re going to have a train wreck, which means that it is much more suited to an organized recording than a session.

I remember one time, just for fun, I sat down and worked out sketches of variations for each bar of Star of Munster - each bar could have fit in with the ordinary tune as a variation (well, they didn’t all line up with the bars, but you get the idea) but the whole thing amounted to a sort of parallel version of the tune. It was fun, and there was more room on the paper, so I did it again, and came up with something completely different. I think I ended up with four or five different Stars of Munster. It wasn’t all good, and some of it was just silly, but that’s what it takes some times to come up with an idea. I think I ended up keeping a few fragments of that - I wish I had that piece of paper now, I’d love to see what it was I’d thought of.

Anyway, that sort of thing can be a good “idea pump” for melodic variation, if you’re reasonably familiar with harmony. Just sit down and sketch out ideas, and then play with them. The rule when you’re playing that sort of game is to be very open in what you’ll consider and extremely ruthless in what you discard. No idea should be rejected out of hand, but no idea should be held on to unless it’s sound.

This can’t replace the “aha” moment when you’re playing and the notion just come to yoy to play it this way instead of that, but it’s a fun way to get at variations from a different angle.

Ben, the “stepwise” idea is pretty simple: play a note or two above or below the main note in a phrase, and don’t wander too far from that main note. You can get all sorts of simple melodic variation that way, as I laid out in my examples from the Bm Tommy Peoples’ reel. Intervals of a 2nd, 3rd, and 4th often work well in this music, either in sequence, or in leap-frog fashion, to create variations. Stray too far, and it becomes, as Rev says, jazz.

I can by analytical about this to explain it to a music student, but I don’t tend to actually play this way. It’s easier (for me) to simply sing the tune--and the variations--in my head and let them come out on fiddle.

Re: Now you see it ~ now you don’t

Ornaments, articulations, and such are expressive.
They are primarily rhythmic, not melodic, because, altho some must be played exactly, other notes may usually be substituted successfully.

TIME is the most important thing about musicmaking. Music is fundamentally rhythmic, especially trad music. The Time and rhythm must be in your body, not just your head.

Therefore, play tunes rhythmically, paying attention more to the Time (the pulse, count) and the rhythm (on and around the Time) than other aspects, and the twiddly bits will play themselves.
Happy playing! vlnplyr
