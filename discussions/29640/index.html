Re: What fiddling styles do you like?

hmmm… the thread title asks “what fiddling styles do you like?” but the OP asks “what styles of trad Irish fiddling do you prefer?” two very different questions…

As people have said, good fiddling is good fiddling. I love good Shetland, Scottish Highland, Donegal, Clare, Sligo, Cape Breton, and West Virginian fiddling equally well.

Whatever the region, I tend to like minor tunes and fiddle tunes that spend some time on the low string.

I like the older tunes which seem to exist in most traditions that use the “high bass” or AEAE tuning. Heck, in Appalachian fiddling there are a half-dozen different tunings, all of which I prefer to the violin tuning GDAE.

As far as styles go, I tend to prefer strong rythmic bowing and plenty of droning/chording.

Most important than any of this stuff is something that’s a bit hard to put into words… but I’ll try. Some fiddlers seem to be saying “it’s about ME! Listen to how great I am!” when they play. I don’t care for those. I much more prefer fiddlers who seem to really be exploring and expressing the TUNE, and their “self” or ego is set aside and lost.

Greater still are the very few fiddlers to transcend to a yet higher level, where it’s no longer about them as a person, or fiddling as an art, or even the tune itself… where all of these things become a means to express something higher, something otherwise inexpressable (and therefore impossible to put into words). Great visual art and great music can, at their highest levels, say something about the human condition and the nature of being human… and yes I have heard fiddlers (very very few) who can do that.

Re: What fiddling styles do you like?

Hmm…Thinking about this, I realise that there are not so much two styles as two overall approaches to both fiddle and flute that I really appreciate, but at different times - maybe even in the same session.

One is the powerful, soulful one, which I’ve heard in the playing of Tommy Peoples, Bothies-era Kevin Burke, Frankie Gavin on early De Danaan albums, and Chuck Fleming, who lives locally. (Of course, these gents needn’t be playing like this all the time.)The playing of such people doesn’t only express power (controlled, of course) in the music one hears, but somehow gives the impression there are unlimited reserves of stamina and, who knows, musical and personal and historical memory, pent up in that wooden instrument: that the music is tapping into something eternal, indeed. This impression may be just brain fever on my part, but it’s an impression I do get. It’s the goose-bumps, heightened-awareness thing.

Matt Molloy has been an ultimate exemplar of this sort of thing. But I may have felt it in the presence of musicians who haven’t felt ‘unlimited reserves of stamina’ at all and who have just wanted to make it to the end of a set before their instrument killed them.

The other approach is much lighter, more tranquil, more relaxing: a parallel may be the calls of chiff-chaffs and many other small birds just going on around one when the trees are coming into leaf in Spring and one is dozing outside through a warm afternoon folded over a drink or something. The sound is not soulful so much as soul food: it also has something of that eternal quality, but seems to be more about what the world could, or should, be like as an utterly safe and reassuring place, and less about passion and the unquiet of history

I’d say the fiddling of the late Willy Taylor of Northumberland exemplified this approach. ‘Pastoral’ is a word I’ve heard very aptly applied to it. His playing had an uncomplicated approach to the tunes, which he played at a relatively relaxed pace with a lovely tone and supple phrasing.

Re: What fiddling styles do you like?

It’s a common rhythmic articulation. The short interruption of an otherwise continuous note. People do it different ways, but the intention is not to make another note, but just to halt the vibration (string or column of air) for a split second. The deliberacy of it is without question. It may well be done without conscious thought, but that is a long way from insinuating that it is not thought about.

And, don’t loose sight of the most important aspect of such articulations. It’s not how you do it, but where. It’s about the precise placing of the timing.

Llig wrote: “How do you teach someone to not sound like they’ve been taught?”

I struggle with this all the time.

I learned 99% by osmosis sitting next to better players. It wasn’t a fast process--took years of listening to the music and flailing away with a hank of horsetail tied to a stick. And then forcing myself to buckle down and not accept my own playing when it didn’t have the right feel.

But people who are already musical--whether experienced on an instrument or not--don’t need much help with mechanics. They just do what’s needed to get the music out. So I don’t really teach these people. We just play and they soak up the feel.

Sometimes they ask questions and we chat about phrasing or pulse or even mechanics. But that almost always boils down to “relax” and “listen.”

I think learning to play music is nearly all about learning to listen well. Which is simply paying attention to what matters.

So that’s my answer to Llig’s question.

Synaesthesia is a handicap that only afflicts those with perfect pitch. Irrelevant to this music, of course, because of the prevalence of differently root noted diatonic instruments.

“some folks will loosely refer to ‘brighter’ or ‘brighten up’ for raising the pitch and ‘duller’ or ‘dulled down’ for lowering the pitch”

The effect I had in mind was more key-specific. For “Leaving Lerwick Harbour”, the third is open so you can’t use any vibrato on it, while the B flat tonal centre is stopped so you get less resonance and more potential for pitch variation. Play it in G and it doesn’t just go higher and get the flute players joining in: it doesn’t have the same timbral contrasts. With “Calliope House”, if you do it in D, the half-close in each part is on the open E string, so the full close on a stopped D is always going to sound anticlimactic.

These aren’t simply effects linearly proportional to the pitch you’re using, they’re the result of the unevenness of response across their range that all instruments have (except for pathological monstrosities like Steinways).
