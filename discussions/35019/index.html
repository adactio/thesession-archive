He’s doing a good job on the tune too, and toward the end, it’s clear that he’s able to control the bow fairly well.

Bowing ‘straight’ and bowing an ‘ellipse’ make a very different sound. If you ‘skew’ on the return bow, you lose the bite at the start of the next note, and the next note after each subsequent ‘skew’.

If you compare a straight return to a skewed return when listening at close range, there’s a big difference in the sound. A *big* difference. If you move 10 feet away, it’s less noticeable, but still there.

If you’re 10+ feet away, and there are other players playing along too, then it’s not an issue.

Now listen to that on Youtube, and there’s no problem at all.

I think this is why many people think that sloppy bowing doesn’t matter at all.

Re: How does this guy manage to play while skating his bow so much?

Thanks Jim, I think your explanation does it. Ricci evidently knew exactly what he wanted and how to do it.

“Soundpoints” in Jim’s post are the key, and for those who may not know the term, a soundpoint is the place on the string that gives the best tone, or most useful tone in the circumstances, because there can be more than one soundpoint. The soundpoint position varies according to where the finger is stopping the string. For example, the most important soundpoint for an open string on the violin is at 1/9 of the bridge-nut, measured from the bridge, about level with the top of the f-holes. There are other soundpoints, depending on the sort of tone the player (or music) wants.

For Irish trad the soundpoint is not going to change much from the 1/9 position because the playing is almost always in the first position. If you do a lot of skating around in the first position when playing trad then you can expect a variable tone which probably won’t always be attractive in the context, and usually indicates a bowing technique that needs smartening up.

Ricci, a concert performer from the age of 10, died in 2012 at the age of 94, made more than 500 recordings, was performing up to 2003, and was busy teaching almost to the end. One of his sayings was, ‘A specialist is somebody who plays all the other types of music worse’ - he didn’t like to be categorized.

Re: How does this guy manage to play while skating his bow so much?

Hi fearfeasog,

I find that using a mirror can help with maintaining a straight bow. It’s really the only way you can get the ‘teacher’ view of how straight you are bowing.

You could try this : in front of a mirror - mid-point of bow on the string, very short bows in both directions, and see how you are doing. Just play naturally - don’t look at the mirror yet.

Increase the bow travel gradually until you’re on full bow.

Try all that again, this time watching the mirror, and see at what point you’re going wrong. You’ll probably have to make a few finger/hand/wrist adjustments to make sure the bow travels in a straight line.

Re: How does this guy manage to play while skating his bow so much?

@TomMoore, Here is a supplementary exercise for getting the “feel” of straight bowing without using a violin, bow or a mirror. It was taught to me by my teacher, who learnt the violin at the Suzuki School in Japan, and it helped me with my real life bowing. All it needs is a smooth wall free of obstructions.

My teacher got me to stand upright by said wall with my left side just touching it and then to turn 45° to my left so that I am facing the wall at that angle. Then, with my right hand shaped as if were holding an invisible bow I reach out and gently touch the wall with the back of my right index finger, my right arm being almost straight and reaching downwards at an angle of about 45°. I then smoothly move my right hand up to my chin with the medial joint of the index finger gently brushing the wall until I can touch the back of my hand with my chin. The right arm is relaxed like a flexible rope with the elbow hanging by gravity during this action. This exercise replicates a straight bowing action on the string.

I see you’ve had lessons from John Dipper. A stellar musician, and a pretty good teacher judging by workshops of his that I’ve been to.

Hasty generalizations (like always bow perpendicular to the string, or maintain a contact point about half way between the bridge and the fingerboard) are much over-used. I fear we lose/ruin many promising students with too much pressure to play the fiddle in a certain way -- insisting on boring repetition of mindless exercises and a stifling insistence to maintain specified physical positions. What compounds the crime is that in most cases the teachers don’t understand what is really necessary to create the sounds they are looking for. There are many paths to good music. Isn’t it arrogant to say it must be done a certain way?

Re: How does this guy manage to play while skating his bow so much?

From Galamian:

"Increase of pressure with constant sounding point requires an increase of speed in the bow; decrease of pressure requires a decrease of speed of stroke.
Increase of pressure with constant speed of bow stroke requires the sounding point to move towards the bridge.
Decrease of pressure with constant bow speed requires the sounding point to move towards the bridge.
Greater speed with constant pressure requires the sounding point to move towards the fingerboard.
Slower speed with constant pressure requires a move towards the bridge."

Bow speed is largely dictated by the tempo, rhythm and bowing pattern of the music. Pressure is adjusted in relation to speed to give the required Dynamic (volume). That leaves the sounding point as the only variable left that can be changed to control the tone.

That all sounds very complicated. but in reality, if you practice playing at different volumes and with different bow speeds, and concentrating on producing an even tone, you will find yourself moving the sounding point instinctively.

In the early stages it is vital to be able to keep speed, pressure and sounding point all constant and produce a stream of equal length notes that all sound the same in tone and volume, so a teacher will insist that you play that way, and tell you off if your bow moves about on the string. But that doesn’t make it a rule that will stay in force as you progress, just as initially you are taught to hold your bow in one particular way. Later on your bow grip becomes something that is constantly changing as you play.

Hi fearfeasog,

Following on from Jim’s suggestion of playing in front of a mirror (which is excellent for correcting a lot of problems, including crooked bowing), by far the best way to learn is to have a live teacher who can gently guide your bow when it strays. But one exercise you can do on your own is shoulder bowing - get the cardboard centre from a roll of paper towel or aluminium foil and hold it on your left shoulder with your left hand. Insert your bow into the tube and practice bowing various rhythm patterns. The tube holds the bow straight and lets you feel how shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers all need to work together to keep the bow straight. Another trick some teachers use (which I don’t personally like) is to insert drinking straws in the top eyes and centre notches of the F holes, to form little gates that your bow has to run between.

Re: How does this guy manage to play while skating his bow so much?

You will find as many opinions on bowing technique here as there are fiddlers. The violin is a robust instrument, and within certain parameters, virtually any system will work. Heck, I’ve even seen a fellow put rosin on a coat hanger and play with that as a bow. If you are applying a technique systematically and with an ear to improvement, you WILL improve. Some systems simply work better.

This thread is starting to grow some legs, so I haven’t read all the comments thoroughly, but I don’t think I’ve read the following… bowing nearer the bridge allows the strings to produce more of the complex higher harmonics (which you may or may not want) while playing more towards the fingerboard favours the lower harmonics. (Guitar players do this regularly as well, playing closer to the bridge when they wish to cut through a little stronger.) This is of course, within limits - too close to the bridge and your sound becomes unstable, though there are some classical compositions which call precisely for that effect. As an extreme case, there are some more avant-garde compositions which have the violinists bowing ON the bridge, or even on the opposite side of the bridge, but those are extreme examples.

Another point is that if you vary the angle of the bow along its lengthwise axis, you can control how much of the bow hair is at play on the string. This is useful for controlling the tone and volume of the notes.

As for keeping the bow straight, (if that is your goal… apparently insisting on a straight bow is anathema to some here) working with a mirror does help a lot. Think of your right arm as a series of gimbaled joints. (is that even a word?) Your shoulder, elbow and wrist need to flex freely with a minimum of tension in any of the joints. As you bow across the string, your hand position itself does not change. The joints of your arm work in cooperation to keep the bow moving in a plane (hopefully) parallel to the bridge.

As I said earlier, the fiddle is quite a robust instrument, and will tolerate quite a bit of variance in technique and still produce acceptable tone, pitch and rhythm. I am by no means suggesting that classical technique is required for ITM, but if you find that your technique is limiting you in any way, there should be no stigma in learning a few things from the classical world.

On the other hand, if the coat hanger thing is working for you, why change it? :)

Cheers,
Tom, on Point Pelee Canada

Re: How does this guy manage to play while skating his bow so much?

As usual here, I encourage people to stop looking, and start feeling and listening!
Why should we care how it looks if it sounds good and gives us a good feeling?
Much good-looking “correct” playing is unlistenable, and makes me feel bad.

But, for those who are so visually-minded (minding the visual), it should be obvious that Lucy Farr’s RH is further away from her body and Ricci’s RH is closer to his body than their sound points. Heifitz’s RH is always 90* in line with his sound point. From an audio recording, no one would know, or care.

The bow police did not reprimand Ricci.
Ricci said: ‘They call me “The Aristocrat of the Bow”. Why not of the whole violin?’

‘Straight line’, ‘ninety-degree’ playing is a function of ‘placement’ of Both violin and bow. Farr and Ricci could have slightly changed the angle of their violins (in opposite directions) to instantly create 90* visual playing. But they didn’t, due to factors other than looking “correct” for the bow police.

Obviously, sliding too far will detract from the sound and music. But some here are in wonder that any sliding can be musical. Don’t wonder about it: learn from it. A purposefully sliding bow can be very expressive, and -- starting over the fingerboard -- can also assist with playing 3 or 4 strings at once without harsh ‘attack’. Ask a gambist.

There is more than one way that people can play musically, for each aspect of all instruments, in all types of musics. Your way, or your teacher’s way, is not necessarily the only one. Rid the religious dogmatism. Loosen up. Have some fun. Experience variety for yourselves in your playing and in others’.

Get away from the mirror and the wall. Listen to and feel your music and how you make it. And to how it makes others feel. Then you will discover that this is what it’s about.

Happy playing,
vlnplyr

Re: How does this guy manage to play while skating his bow so much?

I think that when teaching beginners (in any skill, in any discipline) it is important to keep things simple and straightforward, and one thing at a time. Details and variations will come later when the basic skills have been learnt.

In particular, the point about learning to play with a straight bow, and using aids such as a mirror or wall, is that it trains the student in a basic skill of bow control. It is only when the student has reliably acquired that skill that he will be in a position to modify his bowing in a controlled manner when he wants to - as advanced players do.

The violin is probably the most difficult musical instrument in the early stages of learning because so much is not intuitive. Samuel Johnson said, three centuries ago, “There is nothing, I think, in which the power of art is shown so much as in playing on the fiddle. In all other things we can do something at first. Any man will forge a bar of iron, if you give him a hammer; not so well as a smith, but tolerably. A man will saw a piece of wood, and make a box, though a clumsy one; but give him a fiddle and a fiddle-stick, and he can do nothing.” Things haven’t changed today. It takes several years to learn decent fiddling/violin skills.
