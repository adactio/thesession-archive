Repertoires and Associations.

I am happy to announce that I have decided to re-establish the fiddle as one of my official instruments. After a year of physical training, it is evident now that my issues with the instrument really were my flexibility and mobility. Opening up my shoulders and developing a healthier posterior chain has really opened up the fiddle for me. Ironically, as I committed to improving those things for the sake of becoming physically comfortable with the instrument, the less and less I played the instrument and just focused more on physical training. This meant playing more concertina in its stead.

Over this last year, and after going through a period of repertoire building on the concertina, I’m under the impression that some tunes really do just sit better on certain instruments; (For example, a tune that may have been written for the fiddle in the key of AMaj being difficult on the flute because of too many G#’s[is that the one?]. Or how most tunes have a steep learning curve on piano because they weren’t written with piano technique in mind.) However, I don’t know how true that is or what the facts are on paper, and would like to know how others felt about it.

What I would like to ask is:

Is it commonplace for tunes to be associated with certain instruments within the tradition, or is this more of an exception?

Also, do tunes with known composers tend to be associated with the instrument they were composed on?

And lastly, how much of the Irish Traditional Repertoire was written for the pipes or harp, and has that influenced other instrumental associations?

Re: Repertoires and Associations.

Yes, I have noticed that octaves and other large intervals across the octave break that are simple on the flute can take fiddlers longer to get their fingers round. Some flute players use octaves as ornaments. Conversely ornaments that are a few flicks of the fingers on the A string for a fiddler are harder to do neatly on the flute or whistle if they cross the octave break or involve cross fingering.

I haven’t traced many tunes back to composers but I often feel from the ease of playing (or lack if it) something must be a flute (or fiddle) tune. The big repertoire different I notice is between Irish and English tunes in G. In Irish tunes - I guess because of the flute and whistle influence - passages involving C natural and d or e (e.g. bar two of https://thesession.org/tunes/2344) are not common whereas in English tunes - fiddle and DG box dominant, flute uncommon - there are loads of them.

I have a feeling that trad players get practice at creating something different that uses their instrument best - and that classical players have to practice the things that don’t.

Re: Repertoires and Associations.

On the note of semantics:

“Compose” is defined as “to write or create”.

If we’re talking about the importance of word usage and their implications, the “or” in that definition means that writing and creating don’t have to be considered as the same thing; which means that composing can be synonymous with either word in a given context, but not requiring including both words at the same time.

This is what Oxford Languages has to say about the origin on the word.

“Late Middle English (in the general sense ‘put together, construct’): from Old French composer, from Latin componere (see component), but influenced by Latin compositus ‘composed’ and Old French poser ‘to place’.”

My point, in short: “compose” means “made-up” means “create”, especially in a musical context. I am convinced that it is reaching to claim that Irish tunes aren’t “composed” because they were “made-up”. Those words mean the same thing. The fact that they may not have been physically written down on paper is irrelevant.

More on semantics:

I’ve always understood the term “compose” to be the terminological word used by musicians for “create”. A lot of arts and crafts have special words to contextualize what is meant by “create”. Choreograph, draw, build, design, engineer, etc… Our word is “compose”.

I stand by my usage of both words.

Re: Repertoires and Associations.

After I played this music long enough on mandolin and later flute, I eventually started to get a suspicion about what instrument it might have been composed on. The major categories being pipe/flute/whistle vs. fiddle, with maybe free reeds as another distinct category.

Some tunes sit beautifully on flute without much effort, but involve tricky string crossings on mandolin. Some tunes are dead easy on mandolin but require either a lot of octave jumps on flute (not that difficult but not exactly “idiomatic”), or are in modes with notes that aren’t easy or even possible unless you have a fully keyed flute.

And even with a fully keyed flute, we lack the notes on a fiddle’s G string. It’s one reason I still bring both my mandolin and flute to a session. Partly because I haven’t moved all my repertoire from mandolin to flute yet, but also for those tunes with essential notes below the flute’s bottom D that were probably composed on a fiddle or accordion.

I’m not a fan of “folding” notes up an octave on flute to get around the bottom note limitation. It’s a decent workaround in a session where the missing low notes are being played by other instruments, but practicing those tunes at home that way just hurts my ears, when it sounds so good on a fiddle and that’s the version I have in my head. For some tunes I can find workarounds with enharmonic notes close to the missing ones. That works for many tunes. But others like “The Orphan” just need every one of those lower notes to sound right in the key they’re commonly played in. So I only play those tunes on mandolin.

I’ve actually spent time thinking about how to get down into that low range on flute, and while there are big mechanical Boehm monsters that can do it (Alto flutes), they don’t lend themselves to nimble playing in our common ITM keys for the rest of the repertoire.

At any rate, the ITM center of gravity around the keys of D and G with related and nearby modes allows us to mix different instruments together well enough to have fun playing the tunes in sessions. There are just a few wrinkles here and there, for tunes that didn’t originally arrive on the instrument you’re playing.

Re: Repertoires and Associations.

Tunes that go below standard fiddle tuning or well above first position, especially with quirky arpeggios, are often accordion tunes. And some tunes sound not just like box tunes but like a particular box player’s tunes—the more you play this music, the easier it becomes to spot a Finbarr Dwyer or Paddy O’Brien (Nenagh) tune, say.

I suppose the same holds true for Ed Reavy, Paddy Fahy, and Charlie Lennon tunes. And most of Fahy’s, for example, clearly weren’t invented on flute, say, given the key/mode.

Most of Vincent Broderick’s tunes sound and feel like flute tunes. He favors G major with fairly minimal use of Cnats, for one thing. And most of them live within the D to high b range of the simple flute. Some of Broderick’s tunes are a little awkward on fiddle, but a surprising number lend themselves to pulsey bowing—Milky Way, Stoney Brennan, Tinker’s Daughter, Coachman’s Whip, Flagstone of Memories, Haunted House, etc.

I know fiddlers who tend to favor F major when creating new tunes, and I suspect that’s because it’s a sweet sounding key on fiddle, not hard to play in, and tunes build to work in F are vastly outnumbered by tunes built to work in D, G, A, Edor, etc. So for fiddlers, playing in F is a breath of fresh air. Since string changes and fingers differ from the “standard” keys, it may also be easier to come up with melody lines and phrases that vary a bit from the usual stock phrases. Fiddlers from Clare and East Galway also tend to favor Gdor and Ddor, not the friendliest for winds.

All that said, it’s clear that most tunes are designed to be playable on the usual trad instruments and with the expected skillset on each instrument. Of course, you can flip this—most musicians eventually learn the techniques on their instrument that enable them to play the motifs and phrases common to the whole body of music, regardless which instrument was used during the invention of the tunes.

FWIW, Irish traditional musicians do indeed use “composer” when talking about players who invent tunes. In the Companion to Irish Traditional Music, Fintan Vallely and the many contributors routinely use “composer” in the profiles. E.g., “Reavy, Ed. (1898–1988) Fiddler, composer.” Vincent Broderick is described as “a prolific composer.”

When she compiled her father’s tunes into a book, Eileen O‘Brien called it The Compositions of Paddy O’Brien.

Introducing one of your own tunes during a session, it might sound pretentious to say, “Here’s one I composed,” and it would be much more natural to say, “Here’s one of my own.” But that’s a narrow slice of the tradition, not representative of the whole.

Re: Repertoires and Associations.

Jerone, my sense is that in aural traditions such as this one, the connotations of “compose” strikes some people as off the mark because it’s less of a written tradition than, say, Bach and Beethoven. That, and the tunes are changed as soon as they’re played—even those that are written down aren’t written in stone.

But “compose” has several shades of meaning, and it’s not wrong to talk about composing tunes in a traditional style. Certainly the musicians who have been prolific tunesmiths and whose tunes are widely played and beloved qualify as composers. They’ve added greatly to the body of tunes in the tradition, and some of them at least took the trouble to write down their tunes. (As I understand it, Vincent Broderick wasn’t comfortable with written notation, so his friend Antoin MacGabhann (aka Tony Smith) transcribed the tunes from Vincent’s playing to create the Turoe Stone book.)

As rightfully proud and respectful of this tradition as many of its adherents are, that usually comes with a customary sense of humility. So I understand it when someone shies away from saying he or she “composed” or “wrote” a tune and instead says something like, “This tune came to me after a trip to….”

Will Evans, are you familiar with the term “honorific”? It’s used to describe a system in some Asian languages that use certain words and particles to suggest a certain level of respect or relationship dynamic. My knowledge is limited on this, but what I’ve learned so far is that there is humble speech, polite speech, and casual speech. For example, in Japanese when referring to yourself or something you possess, you use a certain word or particle to suggest humility; and when referring to someone else you use a different word or particle to suggest respect/honor.

In this context, I can see how referring to oneself as a composer could sound pretentious; But I personally wouldn’t call myself a composer of Irish music because I’m not a composer of Irish music, and never will be. However, to show respect, I wouldn’t approach a tunesmith and say “what’s that tune called that you made up”… that sounds way too casual to me. To ask them what tune they “wrote” just sounds cleaner and more respectful to me. I acknowledge them as composers to suggest respect.

Here in the west, we don’t have an explicit honorific system in English, but honorifics are still apart of our speech. Mam, sir, officer, doctor, young lady, your honor, etc. It’s an expression of outward respect. An acknowledgment of who someone is and what that may mean to them.

Yes. Some tunes work better on some instruments than others. Ones plainly written around the strengths of the uilleann pipes/whistle/flute are way easier to learn and play and just generally flow better on uilleann pipes and whistle (and presumably the flute). You know that feeling when a tune just happens, and you don’t have to work hard to find that pulse and flow. I like those ones. Many fiddle tunes are also fine, unless they are in silly keys like Gminor or go below the range of the wind instruments, but you can fold them if you need to.

The hardest ones (discounting the silly keys and some awkward Paddy O’Brien and Ed Reavy compositions) are GHB tunes and tricksy Scottish strathspeys. They don’t sit on the uilleann pipes well at all. I play enough of the former to get by at your average Scottish session but don’t bother with the ones that turn into a mess on the pipes. “Many fiddle tunes are also fine, unless they are in silly keys like Gminor or go below the range of the wind instruments, but you can fold them if you need to.”

I should mention there is one quirk regarding tunes in G minor/G dorian and woodwinds. Sometimes you’ll hear a flute recording in those keys with a reach down to Bb as the lowest note, and it’s typically being played on a low Bb flute, with a bell note four semitones below the common D flute.

It’s the lowest pitched flute commonly available as an “Irish flute” in the conical bore, open hole design because anything bigger would have finger holes too far apart and be a real stretch for the right arm (Boehm Alto flutes get around that with bent head joints and mechanical keypads). It’s been used by many artists like Kennedy, Molloy, Crawford, Barou and others on recordings for variety in pitch and timbre, a nice contrast with the sound of the D flute on the rest of the album.

I have a low Bb flute and it’s fun to fool around with at home. Not really a session instrument due to the difficulty of playing in the more common tune keys, and the slower response. Also it’s huge and heavy, with a risk of whacking someone next to me in the head while waving it around. But it does explain why you’ll sometimes hear a flute on a recording playing in G minor/G dorian or related Bb modes with a lowest note of Bb.

Re: Repertoires and Associations.

“Is it commonplace for tunes to be associated with certain instruments within the tradition, or is this more of an exception?”

I listen for patterns, phrases, keys/modes and also range in order to get an idea of what type of tune it is. Tunes that are cemented in keys outside D and G (and their modes) are likely to come from somewhere else than, say, the pipe/flute core repertoire.

Lots of sharps? Fiddle. Lots of flats? Fiddle. A full range from low G to high a or b? Fiddle.

“Also, do tunes with known composers tend to be associated with the instrument they were composed on?”

Probably, yes. Take tunes by Ed Reavy and Paddy Fahy - those tunes scream fiddle.

At the same time, a tune is a tune, and unless you play an instrument which has a limited range and note inventory, you can figure out how to play it. Such a tune might still not be your best party trick but you’ll grow as a musician.

Something else - unless you like the idea of a one-man show, knowing about the “limitations” of other instruments not only shows good manners. It’s also a necessary session skill. Otherwise you may very well start something in a key which none of the other instruments can even play.

There’s much more to be had from scordatura in Irish music than ‘droney rhythmic effect.’ Like I mentioned, many subtler aspects of the music were lost to time. Just using one obvious example: the transition from wire to gut/nylon harp; totally different instruments, totally different repertoire. You can play a bit of one on the other, but certainly not to the same effect.

Of course as we lose things, we gain others anew. Where we once had wire clarsach (played in such fashion we’re unaware), today we have supergroups, modern harmony, exotic percussion, et al. Not passing judgement on any of that, only noting the passing phenomena.
