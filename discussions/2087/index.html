Playing for large audiences

I recently had an experience that I wish I had some good advice for ahead of time. I played flute along side a vocalist for the invocation at a University Graduation (she sang an Aire, I did little diddly things). The audience was over 1500. I was NOT prepared for the experience and was making it up as I went along. I only slept about 2 hours the night before, and was feeling sick for a few days on either side.

Some of the things I did to get ready were:

1) eat a large meal full of protein about 2 hours ahead of time
2) stay alone and secluded until 10 minutes before the performance
3) not look at the audience until up on stage

What else? Any advice to make it “better” next time? How do you do this?

Thanks!

--Eliot

Re: Playing for large audiences

If you’re playing solo it’s a good idea to start off with something so cast-iron copper-bottomed failsafe that you don’t know you’re playing it. It doesn’t matter if it’s something simple and straightforward that you perhaps learnt 10 years ago (probably all the better, in fact) - all the audience is interested is good playing that moves them. It will get you used to the acoustics and the audience - you can actually “hear” when they’re really listening - get your fingers and breathing relaxed and warmed up, and this will give you confidence and put you in the right frame of mind for the rest of the concert.
Eliot, if you’re playing the flute you shouln’t have any problems with sound projection (why do symphony orchestras need no more the two flutes!). But for the benefit of fiddle players, if you know, or find out at the last minute, that you’re not going be playing with the benefit of a mic and amp, it is well to prepare beforehand and learn the technical aspects of projecting the tone. Don’t worry if it sounds deafening and rough in your ears. This type of noise doesn’t carry more than a few feet and the audience won’t hear it, but the rest of your sound will carry to the far reaches of the hall.
If you don’t know the technique of violin tone projection a good classical player or teacher should be able to help you. It’s not easily described on paper; you need to learn it in a large(ish) hall from a teacher.

I’m not so sure that a large meal full of protein two hours before is necessarily a good idea. Protein takes a fair while to digest, and the last thing you want are twinges of indigestion on top of everything else. I’d recommend, from my own experience, a light snack and non-alcoholic, non-fizzy drink. Just something to allay hunger but not enough to induce a feeling of fullness. After all, you’re not running in a marathon, or playing in a 3-hour symphony or opera concert, where stamina is indicated!

I’m sure I’ve told this story on some long-forgotten thread, so I’ll keep it short (okay, this is Wilbur talking, so have a seat).

Some years ago, our band was warming up in the green room before our biggest gig to date, a sold-out house (we were opening for a popular band :o) at a fair-sized concert hall. All of us were a bit on edge. It helped ease tensions when our guitar player came out of the bathroom (after making all sorts of horrible noises) with a mile of toilet paper trailing behind his shoe. So we had a good laugh and careened through a few sets to get warmed up. Then the executioner came and led us toward the stage. I remember walking out to polite applause and bright lights, waving to the crowd and standing for a while to adjust my mic before reaching for my chair. Then, as I bent down to sit, I saw that our musical warm ups had left a huge blotch of white rosin from my bow, glowing in the spotlights, smack dab on the crotch of my otherwise deep, dark green trousers. It had the albedo of a full moon and could not have been more strategically placed. I thought of the toilet paper. I laughed. I’m sure someone, maybe everyone, all the way to the back row, was laughing too. And I completely relaxed--no matter how many mistakes I might make, nothing could be any more embarrassing than what I’d already done, so…. And it helped me play with a sense of humor and vulnerability, “nothing to prove, just don’t split the seat of your pants.”

Re: Playing for large audiences

Some other suggestions:

1) As a general rule of thumb, I’d suggest one should only very seldom play something on-stage that’s at the limit of one’s technical ability. If you’re suffering from any kind of performance anxiety, you want the repertoire you play to be *well below* the limits of your technical execution. 4 times out of 5 correct in the practice room is not enough--it needs to be something you can play infallibly and in your sleep. The stage is rife with distractions and it’s hard to keep concentration together. Playing something that demands every ounce of one’s concentration and skill is, in my opinion, a bad idea.

2) Visualization. Spend time in the practice room visualizing the performance situation: setting, lighting, repertoire, personnel, audience, and, most importantly, all the stuff that can go wrong. If you do it right, you can actually give yourself the performance shakes in the practice room--and then make friends with that, and learn to function despite it. Having one’s only experience of playing-through-stage-fright actually be on the stage is a bad idea; why not get used to the sensation, and holding it together through the sensation, in the practice room where it doesn’t matter? I used this when taking a doctoral examination in piano sight-reading from orchestral scores (at which I’m terrible), and the visualization in the deserted practice room worked so well that I’d actually get dry mouth, sweats, hands shaking with no one present. It was good to become familiar with those sensations so that I wasn’t blind-sided by them in the actual exam.

3) Practice in distracting circumstances. In other words, though of course it’s a good idea to have lots of undistracted and high-concentration practice time, it’s also good to spend time practicing, and concentrating, when conditions are less than optimum. Watazumido Susho, a Japanese zen master and shakuhachi flute player, used to practice in the middle of Tokyo intersections because it helped his concentration. One year teaching at a summer workshop, the Dumpster-collection guy used to show up right outside our window about 20 minutes into each day’s class. I’d keep talking at the same level, and when people fidgeted, suggest that concentrating through the distraction might be a good exercise. Not suggesting that you practice in the middle of an intersection--though pushing yourself to concentrate in adverse conditions is good practice for a like experience in the session--but try playing tunes while holding a conversation, reading a book aloud, listening to a different tune, and so on. I know players who in the session can be playing one tune while simultaneously cuing someone else by singing the next tune--that’s concentration.

chris smith

Re: Playing for large audiences

Sorry: this followup got eaten last night. Addenda to previous post:

I don’t think it’s advisable or necessary to “stay alone and secluded” before a performance. Yes, it’s good to be able to focus in, and yes, everybody has different quirky ways of doing that (I tend to wash my hands 4 or 5 times before a high-tension gig). But secluding oneself so rigorously tends I think to make you focus *too much*, or too exclusively, on your own part. In other words, isolating yourself so stringently from the other players prior to the performance doesn’t help you connect with them during it.

It’s analogous to playing with your eyes closed, which I also think is a bad idea. Beyond the fact that it makes it impossible for anyone on stage to have eye contact or to give you cues (nothing more frustrating than trying to cue someone for the next tune in the medley when that person has eyes closed), it also cuts you off from the audience and in my opinion tends to make you tune out the other music going on. I think that, no matter how arduous the situation or how high the distraction, it’s important to consciously make eye- and therefore communicative-contact with the other players, AND with the audience.

Rule of thumb: if you can’t play something demanding onstage while at the same time keeping eye-contact with other players, then you’re playing beyond workable limits of your technique. Playing right at the limits of one’s technique tends to lead to “blocking out” the other parts. You need to be able to execute your own part, *while at the same time* hearing how it fits into the musical whole. Eye contact helps this; absence of eye contact (and, I would argue, sequestering oneself) tends to preclude it.

chris smith

Re: Playing for large audiences

Chris, excellent advice.

There are 2 things here - playing with others and playing to an audience.

For the first, you need to spot the cues, but more importantly watch the rhythm (fiddling arms, squeezing bellows, strumming hand, etc.). For me as a bodhran player this is so important in playing with others, familiar or new.

For the second, if you’re being payed to play you need to communicate with the audience. As well as maybe having someone who can say “Hello! The draw numbers are now going to be announced by Little Gladys” you also need that eveyone in the band catches eyes with people in the audience. Are they interested, enjoying? Even though you’re desperate to keep it together with the rest of the band and are making mad eye-contact, remember the punters. I have to remind myself to do this sometimes. Regular performing helps this to happen.

Just a thought.
