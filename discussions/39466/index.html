Coaxing someone off sheet music

I know a player - and no exaggeration - an excellent player. They teach fiddle and tunes and play excellently. The only thing is that they continually play using sheet music.

They’ve been playing for decades - yet regularly get surprised that they ‘actually’ remembered a few of the tunes that get played at the sesh.

Clearly this person knows many tunes and proves it week after week - but they are convinced that they just need the sheet music and to remember a melody is a mind bendingly difficult thing to do…!?

Even Atholl Highlanders is surprising when played along to without the dots.

It is apparent that they can play well and, unsurprisingly, after decades of playing these catchy melodies, remembers scores of tunes.

But how is it possible to give them the confidence to play, without feeling bound to searching through folder after folder to play any tune?

Any tips for this quandary, as it always gives me palpitations when this person gets into a tis when trying to hunt out the Kesh jig or some other well remembered standard…

Maybe I should just chill but I want to help, as I feel this person feels left behind if any new combination of tunes is played.

Re: Coaxing someone off sheet music

Matt Cranitch - The Irish Fiddle Book - page 59: “Remember that all traditional musicians learn to play by ear: The sooner you can do that, the better! This is not as difficult as it seems because, within each tune, there are recurring bars and phrases. Generally, each part consists of two 4-bar sections that are often similar and sometimes identical. … Because of this, the tunes can be easily memorized, particularly if they are practiced two of four bars at at time. With experience, this will no longer be necessary and the tunes can be learned very quickly.” One of the many great but simple truths in Matt’s book.

I might add, as student and believer in this great book, that there is a large gap between playing with the sheet music and playing as if by auto pilot purely from memory. I believe that being able to play the tune without the cheat sheets mandates a real commitment to really learn the tune: The work of learning the tune is qualitatively different that playing along from printed notation. It’s all about the work of learning the tune in order to own the tune.

I notice there are different ways in which sheet music fixations develop & manifest.

The spectrum ranges from those who cannot turn their gaze away from the page & when they do feel a wee bit of freedom to permit their eyes to become unglued it quickly reverts to panic if the wind blows or their musicstand gets bumped … to those who incessantly rant about their need for sheet music, always asking for a transcription of some tune and typically have some paper in front of them when playing; yet she often has her eyes closed, or is not wearing her glasses and you know she cannot see very well w/out them; still she manages to play the tune better than you on a good night.

Re: Coaxing someone off sheet music

If this is happening at a session, it does that mean that there are pre-determined sets so that the player has time to set the sheet music out ahead of time? The passive-aggressive way to handle it is to play tunes in a different order or unannounced, so that the person gets frustrated trying to keep up. That will either drive them away, or, if they’re really committed to wanting to be part of the session, drive them to stop relying on the dots.

I’ve never met a good trad player that needed dots to play. (Doesn’t matter if they used the dots to learn the tunes… they need to be able to play without the security blanket of the dots sitting in front of them).

Another way to maybe inspire them is to take a good recording from a great player, where the tunes are expressions of musical ideas, and not just played the same each time through. See if you can find a tune that the person “knows” and get them inspired to feel the music instead of reading it. They’ll never be able to play that way with sheet music in front of them…

Probably best to get them to buy an iPad at first, at least they’ll save hauling the binders about.

Playing from memory means you own the tunes inside, they’ll probably be in there for your friend too, but accessing them can be an issue. I’m not very good at remembering without some trigger to kickstart me but I know the tunes once I start. For me it’s because I spread my attention too thinly between different styles & instruments, but I really enjoy that too. So the sheet music becomes a prop to hold the whole rickey edifice up. One related part of my problem was that I live so much in the tune being played at the time, it takes me time to think of a tune and that can be even worse if it’s my turn to kick off a set. Zip, blank; no more efficient memory degausser known to mankind than having to start one off. But it can be overcome and I’m working out tricks to fool myself into remembering.

First part is remembering the tunes and being able to play them through. I’ve found it takes me no more effort to remember three than it does to remember one. So I’ve been learning them as sets, with short names (ie; Drowsy, Cooley, Lads) once in they can be chopped up to mix with others.

Then there’s the long note to start. If I draw out the first note or the root note/ drone in a big confident pull on the fiddle bow/ tremolo on the mandolin, it sets my memory up and I can fall into the tune with thar tone erasing what went before until the memory kicks in.

Another cheat I have done to get over the set starting one is to record the first bar of tunes or each of a set then make little mp3 s of them with the title. I stick them on my old iPod mini and stick the earbud in, hit play, get the name and the first bar. Then away I go. I purposely do this without consulting a screen or anything, I just go with what’s next. Like the wall builders adage, ‘if you pick it up use that one’. This helped a lot, but it probably would be limited in a very noisy session. A bit sad to have to delegate my memory to a machine, but if it gets me off to the races I don’t mind looking a bit thick.

For your friend they’d need to specifically practice without the dots inorder to be convinced they could. Confidence isn’t something you can bully people into having, but it can be drawn out by encouragement to have a go in a safe space. You could suggest one or two sets as a target for the next month. Offering to pick up or play along where they may falter so they continually have a guide-track in your playing.

We lead such busy and complex lives now and try to pack in so much that we normally don’t spend long enough on slow-time skills like long term memorising and delegate that role to our books and devices. I think for me part of the attraction of learning trad and folk tunes & songs is the reconnection with that. Maybe someday I’ll wean myself off mechanical memory props, perhaps not, but at least by trying then it’s more likely.

Fun fact time!!!

I just happened to run across an article about James Galway that y’all might find interesting relative to this thread. The portion below covers his audition for principle flute of the Berlin Philharmonic. I start at the end of his audition piece:

“Then, a voice from the crowd (probably that of the conductor, Herbert von Karajan) called out, ‘Play William Tell’. He did - from memory. Then, ‘Play L’Après-midi’, then more orchestral excerpts. Whatever he was asked, he played from memory. When this was over, there was a break during which he met the other candidates. After a time, they were all called onto the stage where one after the other they had to play orchestral excerpts on demand. Sir James still can’t believe some of the other candidates had to step up to the music stand to play. He played everything from memory.”

Thus, the argument can be made that the purpose of a score is not to provide a means by which to play a piece, but rather a means by which to learn one - an approach hardly confined to devotees of ITM.

Whether one subscribes to that notion is an individual choice. I have always preferred it, but I couldn’t care less what others do unless it becomes a nuisance.

I just got a kick out of the anecdote and thought I would share.

Re: Coaxing someone off sheet music

It’s tough to wean somebody off sheet music, if that’s the way they were raised.

I had somebody who had a background in Recorder come to me to learn Irish flute. She could play any sheet music I put in front of her. After we did that for a few lessons I told her now was the time to get away from it.

I think it’s not all that productive to merely tell somebody “get off the music” without giving them any strategies to learn tunes by ear.

What I did was to pop in a CD that had flute, fiddle, & pipes playing reels and jigs with no accompaniment, and I began playing along with the album. Since they were tunes I didn’t play myself, what I was doing was demonstrating strategies for picking up tunes on the fly. She was pretty good at it, the combination of hearing the music and watching my fingers having both of us tooting along with the CD straightaway, having most of a tune down by the 3rd playing (just in time to switch to the next tune!)

My goal was for her to begin building up her repertoire of memorised tunes, by acquiring them and practicing them without ever seeing sheet music for them.

Now, sightreading is a great tool for sure, and I’m not a dot-hater. When I used to go around teaching Irish flute workshops I loved it when a class was full of readers. I could hand out a load of tunes and we could cover a large amount of material in an hour. Painful when you have non-readers who also are slow at picking up tunes by ear- you can spend the whole hour and not even get through one part of one tune, a massive waste of time for all.

As an aside I’ve found, to my surprise, that people have more difficulty picking up airs by ear than jigs or reels. I would have thought the opposite.

Re: Coaxing someone off sheet music

For sure at a session it’s a big help if there’s a piper or fluter or whistler so that I can watch the fingers.

But even sight unseen- listening to a recording- one ought to be able to hear if they’re matching the notes or not.

Above was mentioned identifying arpeggios. That’s one strategy. For me picking up reels and jigs by ear starts with identifying the rolls (or roll-equivalent spots). This is because a long roll is the tune parking on a note for three 8th-notes, a pause in the movement. Then the arpeggios, which stand out and are easy to latch onto. Ditto scalar runs, and the thing where three notes pop out medium/high/medium above a pedal tone (and its inverse). Once these motifs are got, you usually only have a few linking notes here and there.

Thing is, reels and jigs are nearly entirely composed of stock motifs. Once one has a number of the stock phrases under one’s fingers, learning a new tune is simply figuring out which motifs go where. It’s much easier than having to figure out an entirely unique melody.

One is that no-one has a right to insist that another person change just to satisfy their often fuzzy notions of the “right way to do things”. No-one. A lot of the world’s problems, big and small, stem from just that.

Conversely there is no imperative for the rest of us to wait for them. When the problem of playing catch-up at sessions, or when it’s clear that sheet music just isn’t welcome, the one’s who must read needs to make some decisions. There are options. Most importantly, those decisions are exclusively their own to make.

Ideally everybody could meet somewhere in the middle. A little patience, a little understanding, a little kindness, goes a long way. So does a little effort to join in. Yeah, one person (who will make no effort to adapt) can ruin a session, but so can an impatient session “nazi”.

So yeah, leave your friends free to handle things their own way. That or accept your culpability when you lose the friend.

I’m a pretty good sightreader and I have loads of tunes transcribed but I’ve never taken sheet music to a session, I don’t think.

The spontaneous nature of the session, where people just go into whatever tune feels right at the moment, and you don’t know the name of a tune until after it’s played and you ask (if then) makes having music arranged by title useless.

What I could see sort of working would be if you had your music arranged by key. You hear everyone start playing a G Major reel, and you flip to your G Major Reel section, and maybe, just maybe, you can find the right tune before they’ve hit the third time through.

But I myself am better off watching and listening and by the 3rd time through I usually have most of the tune. I don’t audibly noodle (I hate that) rather I silently finger until I know I have it.

Re: Coaxing someone off sheet music

I can relate to the person who likes to use sheet music because I prefer having sheet music.
I don’t bring sheet music to sessions ( unless others do ) and I have to be content with playing
at about half of my potential ability because of the strong dislike of sheet music at most better sessions. I have identified myself as a very strong Visual learner ( as opposed to Auditory ), and
I became aware of this through being able to play much better on fretted instruments than on
whistles while not using sheet music because I can rely on Visual and Tactile Memory while playing banjo, OM, and guitar. You can’t easily look at your fingers while playing whistle without going cross eyed and getting dizzy. I can play well on whistle with sheet music, but only marginally well without sheet music. I still enjoy sessions without sheet music very much and I look at part of the experience as being therapy for a Visual Learner to integrate Auditory, Tactile, and Visual Learning. I’ve been playing for 30+ years and hope to play another 20 or 30 years, but I don’t expect myself to be a stellar player and lead sessions unless I have sheet music, it’s just how my brain is wired. I am aware of the limitations of my thinking and learning pathway and I have found playing without sheet music to be the best possible therapy. I have been drafted into a few performing groups over the years and bandmates have allowed me to keep reduced musical scores on mike stands. The best part of it all is that almost all of the musicians I play with know I have limitations and still offer support and encouragement and make me feel part of a loving community.
