Re: Learning tune from “dots” on different key whistle?

Hi Agnar, and welcome.
It depends what you want. You can pick up your C whistle and play it exactly like you play your D whistle. It’ll work fine but the sound will come out a tone lower. You will have transposed a D tune into C or a G tune into F.

If you want to play a D tune on your C whistle and have it sounding in D you’ll have to adjust all your fingerings. You’ll have to play F#s instead of F naturals and C#s instead of C natural. This is not so easy!

In general you can pick up a whistle in any key and use the same fingering that you use on a D whistle. The tune will come out sounding fine, but at a different pitch.

Re: Learning tune from “dots” on different key whistle?

“ … if written in key of C do I play a D-dot as xxx xxo instead of xxx xxx?”

Already answered by TomB-R above. But yes, reading for a differently-pitched whistle can be a bit of a challenge
when you have only ever played a D whistle before, especially if you are not already an experienced reader of music. To save you having to mentally convert every note before playing it, the ‘shortcut’ is to learn to read by *intervals* rather than notes. What I mean by this is, learn to visually recognise where the tune jumps from one note to the next one up or down (a 2nd), from a line to the next line or space to the next space (a 3rd), from a line to the next-but-one space (a 4th) etc., irrespective of the actual pitches. Similarly, learn the fingerings of the whistle as steps of the scale of that whistle, whatever its pitch – you can think of them as Do, Re, Mi etc., as numbers (or ideally, just sounds – cut out the middle man), whatever works for you.

Calum “… many tunes … can’t actually be played on whistle at all, often because they are fiddle tunes that have too big a range for the whistle.”

I think a lot of whistle players would take issue with that statement. It may be true that most whistle and flute players, when choosing tunes to play, favour those that sit easily on their instrument. But when it comes to playing in sessions, many would sooner play a slightly modified version of a tune than simply sit out; this most commonly involves ‘folding’ the tune, whereby any notes below the range of the instrument are played an octave higher – so the resulting version is compatible with whatever the fiddlers and box players are playing.

The Boy from County Cook: “Get yourself a C whistle to play that tune in C.”

Yes, a C whistle is good for tunes in C –  also F, Dm, Gm and Am

“A D, a G, and an A will get you through - 60%? 75%? - most, anyway, of the tunes you’ll hear at an Irish session.”

I wouldn’t like to guess at a percentage – it depends on the session and who the dominant players are (if it is led by a flute player, whistle player or piper, it would most likely be 100%). I have come across a few players that will happily play in any of the ‘outlying’ keys on a D whistle, so confident and accurate are they with half-hole fingerings. Great if you can do it, but playing the whistle doesn’t have to be a display of bravado – first and foremost, it has to sound good.

Re: Learning tune from “dots” on different key whistle?

Maybe the way into this, as per Calum’s advice above, is that it is ‘useful to learn the basics of how scales are constructed’. I wonder if it would help to set out one or two pointers?

An octave comprises 12 semitones in Western music. A scale is simply a selection of some of them played in order: usually in a series of 8 notes, but other sequences are available (e.g. pentatonic - a five note series). The character of the scale is set by the particular selection of notes used out of the 12 that are available. In an 8-note scale, clearly some of the semitones are omitted. A Major scale, for example, goes up by whole-tone intervals EXCEPT for the 3rd and 4th notes and the 7th and 8th notes, both of which are only a semitone apart. Minor scales place these semitone intervals at different points in the scale, creating a more ‘sombre’ feel. If you have access to a piano keyboard, check out scales starting on different notes and see where the semitone intervals lie.

An instrument that can play all 12 semitones (i.e. ‘fully chromatic’ - like a fiddle) can play in any key, but a whistle is not built to play all of them. The available notes limit it to certain keys: the D whistle is particularly good for the keys of D and G Major and E and A Minor, and can cope fairly well with some tunes in A Major and B Minor*. These are the keys whose scales use the notes a D whistle is built to play, and which are commonly used for much traditional music . To play in other keys on the same whistle requires using notes that are not normally available, and maybe an adept player can get some of these by nifty fingering, e.g. ‘half-holing’.

Hence the usefulness of having whistles built for other scales, so that playing in other keys can be done using familiar fingering - just on a different length of pipe. (I’ve noticed that blues harp players tend to carry around several harmonicas, each pitched in a different key, for the same reason).

*Someone more knowledgeable will no doubt add information about available scales in Mixolydian, Dorian, etc. modes. Meanwhile, I apologise if this is all too basic an explanation, but I hope it’s of some use to folks new to whistles.

Re: Learning tune from “dots” on different key whistle?

Put as simply as possible, every whistle has two Major keys available, one starting on the “six finger note”
xxx xxx
and one starting on the “three finger note”
xxx ooo
with the fipple of the whistle pointing to the left, and the open end of the whistle pointing to the right.
Whistles are named from their six-finger-note xxx xxx but for many tunes the three-finger-note xxx ooo works better because it gives you three notes below the key-note of the tune.
Be aware that you use the open C# ooo ooo for the six-finger-note Major scale, and the crossfingered C natural oxx ooo for the three-finger-note Major scale.

So, a D whistle plays D Major and G Major.
C whistle: C Major and F Major
Bb whistle: Bb Major and Eb Major

and so forth.
But most whistleplayers, regardless of what size whistle they’re playing, think of the notes as if they’re playing a D whistle, for example calling this note “G”
xxx ooo
regardless if they’re playing a D, C, Bb, or any other size whistle.

About “the dots” when I write out whistle/flute/uilleann pipe music I write everything as it would sound on a D whistle, D flute, or D chanter, even though I might not ever be performing the tune on a D instrument.

Let’s say I’ll be performing a tune in A Major. I write it out in G Major and play it on an E whistle.

Or I’ll be performing a tune in Eb Major. I also write it out in G but play it on a Bb whistle.

In other words I always write things out in D-instrument-friendly keys, and play the tune on whatever whistle is required for the correct key to be sounded.

Re: Learning tune from “dots” on different key whistle?

About other scales, here’s a quick guide, in D whistle terms, giving the fingering of the note the scale begins on:
xxx xxx (using C sharp) D Major

xxx xxx (using C natural) D Mixolydian (like a Major scale but with a flat 7th degree)

xxx xxo (using C sharp) E Dorian (like a minor scale but with a sharp 6th degree)

xxx xxo (using C natural) E minor

xxx ooo (using C natural) G Major

xxo ooo (using C sharp) A Mixolydian

xxo ooo (using C natural) A Dorian

xoo ooo (using C sharp) B minor

(I’m not going to get into Lydian, though there are traditional Lydian-mode tunes.)

Re: Learning tune from “dots” on different key whistle?

Richard Cook’s last two posts are very useful and informative. I would just like to add a comment about which key(s) of whistle are suitable. The many keys of whistle available give beginners the impression that if you want to play in a different key, you just switch to a different whistle. However, the structure of most tunes in ITM does not favour that approach.

For example, most ITM tunes in G major will make use of the octave from G to the g above it, but they will also often use notes above that octave (a and b) and some below it (typically F#, E and D, and sometimes even C and B for fiddle tunes). The G whistle will give you the two octaves from G to g’, which is fine all the way up to the top notes, but then there are no notes available below the G. Shifting up an octave (i.e. taking g as the keynote) does not help, as you cannot then get the high notes that you need. Tunes in A major are also problematic as they often include the F#, E, D and C# below the keynote.

The solution adopted by most whistlers playing instrumental music is to use the D whistle for most tunes, since that will give all the keys/scales set out by Richard in the previous post. Using the D means that you can cope with a fair number of key changes without having to change whistle.

Accompanying songs is a different matter, since you may well need to find a key that will suit the singer’s voice. But most song melodies don’t have the same range as tunes, so this is usually easier to accommodate.
