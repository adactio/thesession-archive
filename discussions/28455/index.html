Yes, and you are correct in thinking this is a problem if you just pick up a standard chromatic. Trying to play one of these in D is a nightmare. They work well in C, though, if you want to play melodeon-style, or in E flat if you want to play as though you’re playing a B/C box (you’d reverse the slide in this case for the best effect)
If you only have a C chromatic and you want to figure out how to work it, these are two good things to try.

↳ Posted by Jon Kiparsky on September 19th, 2011.

Re: Chromatic Harmonica ..

Caveat emptor, if you are contemplating buying a second-hand chrom. There’s many a potential pitfall: abused harp (it only takes two minutes in the hands of some kid and you can have damaged reeds and valves), harp played very little but put away dirty for years, slide mechanism seized and corroded, harp not kept in ideal conditions, harp played by smoker (irredeemable if it has a wood body), disease (you suck half the time, remember), very difficult to clean thoroughly, little or no comeback. Still, if lower prices stir your loins, who am I to gainsay! Some people do buy second-hand chroms and many of them are very happy. If I did buy one it would be for spare bits such as reeds and valves, and I would never pay much.

To start with, you can play the vast majority of Irish tunes on a ten-hole diatonic. Three harps - a D (low D is my preference), a G and an A will see you sitting pretty. The G and A at least need to be in Paddy Richter tuning, which is just one reed, the 3-blow, tuned up a whole tone. All my diatonics are paddied (I do it myself).

Chroms are handy, as you say, for music of other genres and for the occasional tune with an awkward accidental (usually that pesky Cnat in the likes of Jenny’s Wedding and Ashokan Farewell, etc.) and for people who just like chroms. You could consider, as an alternative, Hohner’s XB40s. They are ten-hole harps with lots of auxiliary reeds which don’t actually sound but which enable you to bend every note on the harp. Slightly cheaper than chroms, they have the advantage of being twice as loud as any other harp, honest. They are slightly hard to fall in love with, but I always take my G and low D out with me.

The easiest way to get going on Irish tunes in the “right” keys on chroms is to have two stock instruments, one in D and one in G. The commonest chroms around, and I would think the vast majority of second-hand ones would be in this category, are in C, which is actually a very poor choice for playing Irish music (unless you don’t mind playing in keys that no-one else will be in). We’ve been here before, but to cut a long story short, with a C chrom you will spend much of your time pressing the slide to get notes in the keys of D or G instead of being able to use it for ornamentation, and the ornamentation you can get will sound rough. You won’t find many accomplished players using C chroms in session-type contexts.

As for slide-flipping (easy to do, and it results in your getting a lower note instead of a higher note when you press the slide), it’s a very good thing to do because it enables you to get far better ornamentation. The only thing here, again, is making sure that you are playing in the “right” keys with the slide out. You would need to buy a C sharp harp and an F sharp harp in order to be able play in D and G respectively, slide out, once you’d flipped the slides. I’ve just had a look at Eagle Music’s selection of harmonicas (that’s where I get mine from), and Hohner USA, and I can’t see C sharp or F sharp chromatic harps anywhere. Maybe you’d have to go a specialist customiser such as Brendan. Unpadlock that cheque-book!

I’ve only ever had Hohner Super Chromonicas, and a Hering 5148 in G, so I can’t speak for brands, but definitely avoid cheapos. You should be happy with most stuff from Hohner (though never those horrid Chromettas), Hering, Seydel or Suzuki. I shouldn’t say it, but I avoid the name “Huang” like the plague. Low price, questionable quality control.

Brendan Power has written a really excellent tutor with CD on how to play chromatic in Irish music. He assumes you’ll be using stock D and G chroms. Have a look on his website for further ideas. When listening to me, remember that I’m really a dyed-in-the-wool diatonic man!

Re: Chromatic Harmonica ..

“As for slide-flipping (easy to do, and it results in your getting a lower note instead of a higher note when you press the slide)”

As a caveat, some more recent hohner models are harder to flip. The CX-12, for example, is a fine harp and well made, but the way the slide is configured, flipping it would be difficult without serious surgery - there’s a knob on the end that only allows the slide to go in one way.
All of the classic chroms that I’ve seen - the Chromonica, the Chrometta, etc - are easy. These are the wood or plastic combs and the tin plates.
Anything with molded plastic “plates” (shells, more like), you might want to look at it a little carefully if flipping the slide is important to you.

Re: Chromatic Harmonica ..

Warping of the timber? Not impossible, I suppose, but not an issue I’ve come across. The pearwood used in various Hohner harps (that 270/48, aka Super Chromonica, Echo tremolos and the traditional Marine Bands) will swell and shrink like mad if soaked and dried, and the first two at least should never be immersed or rinsed in water. It would mean instant death to the comb (I know - I’ve done it. :-( ) But you wouldn’t wash your nice fiddle in the bath either, would you? A correctly-stored harmonica, which means out of the hot sun or steamy places, should give no problems. Those nails are an issue (they attach the reed-plates, not the individual reeds, which are attached in the usual manner with rivets, by the way) but not as much as some would have you believe. You just have to be careful when you dismantle, that’s all, and they’ll go back in just fine even after several dismantlings. If they do work loose eventually (and why are you taking your harp apart so often!), you can always tighten the holes by putting thin slivers of wood in them. I’m not saying they’re the best on the planet but they work well and are very nice harps for the money.

↳ Posted by Steve Shaw on September 20th, 2011.

Re: Chromatic Harmonica ..

I couldn’t find that Hohner Educator anywhere in the UK after a quick scout around. From what I can see, it ticks none of my boxes: it’s only available in C so is unsuitable for Irish sessions, it is 10-hole so has a limited range for a chrom, and it is valveless. The latter sounds good (no sticking, no maintenance, etc.) but you should be aware that valves are what make chroms playable. The layout of the reeds in the comb channels in a chrom means that, unless the unused section is closed off by a valve when you’re blowing/drawing, you have to use huge amounts of breath to get a decent sound to come out. It isn’t for nothing that the alternative name for valves is “windsavers.” I’d steer clear if I were you.

Re: Chromatic Harmonica ..

You should be OK as long as you don’t go too cheap!!

Wood combs have a sort of aesthetic appeal, but, as far as I’m concerned, they are the least practical: prone to distortion, hard to clean and uncomfortable for tongue-blocking are common gripes. Plastic bodies are cheap, waterproof and stable. That’ll do me. You don’t actually come into physical contact with the wood in a Hohner chromatic. You do with diatonics, though. Hohner use a different wood, far less prone to swelling, in the modular system (MS on the coverplate) blues harps to the pearwood previously mentioned for chroms and the older, nailed harps. The Marine Band Deluxe (pricey in my view) has a sealed wood comb that should give no trouble. As for whether comb material affects sound quality, I think not, but don’t listen to me, read this: http://www.patmissin.com/ffaq/q6.html
