Added by Michael Dineen on October 30th, 2014

Is Playing Traditional Music Fast A Skill, Or Is It An Insult To Ireland’s Instrumental Heritage.?

Trad music has changed over the years. There were many changes, some good and some bad. Let me add, it depends on the different opinions. One change, has been the demand for fast traditional. In my opinion, playing fast is a skill which few people can master. The other side is, it has to move forward too, and playing trad music fast has more excitement in it. It also shows, that a musician has some sort of technique in their playing. I also feel, it has to do with the mood of a musician at that particular time. Yet another view is, if trad players played it the way it was written, it would get boring. People such as Vinnie Kilduff, Finbar Furey, and Micheal O‘ Suilleabhain, have demonstrated their talent by playing at a fast pace. The Bothy Band became known, for their wild arrangements of trad tunes. Even Tony Mc’Mahon sometimes played very fast, which made it exciting for me. Is playing fast, a skill.? Is it an insult to the music of Ireland.? should trad players, play the tunes the proper way.? What do other members think.?

Re: Is Playing Traditional Music Fast A Skill, Or Is It An Insult To Ireland’s Instrumental Heritage.?

It all depends what you like.
Those of us who have played for dancing know that danceable speed is quite a lot slower than “session” speed. There is also a question of accent and phrasing. The faster you go the more these other vital components fly out of the window.
Young people, once they have mastered basic techniques, often play very fast, but I think they are missing out on one of the joys of playing together, if the only skill is keeping up with the others.

Re: Is Playing Traditional Music Fast A Skill, Or Is It An Insult To Ireland’s Instrumental Heritage.?

So people like Patsy Touhey and Michael Coleman who played quite fast, and are some of the earliest recorded examples of trad music, weren’t playing the music the “proper” way? In my opinion there is a range of tempos where the music sounds good. You get too slow it is boring and too fast it loses the rhythmic nuances. I personally like to play on the lively side but I keep it in check and don’t go over board tempo-wise. Although at times I do like to slow things down; really it depends a lot on the tune. For instance I would play a tune like “The Musical Priest” a good bit fast than I would play “Maud Millar” simply because I think the first tune is better suited to a faster tempo and the second a slower one.

I have known players that played very slow and I couldn’t stand to listen to them or play with them. I have been in sessions with said players who would give out to me or others if someone played what they deemed “too fast.” Needless to say I avoided sessions with those players as it took away the spirit of it for me. There is no point, in my mind, in playing reels at ~80 bpm and giving out if someone went faster.

Plus, there is a stylistic aspect to it. Reading the bio of the OP he is from Kerry; well in terms of regional style Kerry might be on the slower side of the tradition. People like Dennis Murphy and Julia Clifford certainly played a bit slower than Sligo players like Coleman and Morrison or Donegal players like Johnny Doherty.

I think it is more insulting to the tradition to think that one style of playing is the “correct” style and everything else is wrong. Even when there is historical precedence for playing in said “wrong” styles.

I feel like asking this question is like asking; is the current society in Ireland an insult to Ireland’s cultural heritage? Which inevitably will issue similar responses. The fact of the matter is that things change; sometimes for the better and sometimes for the worse it is all about perspective. We can either adjust to the changes or complain about how much better things used to be.

Fast is not the same as lively.

↳ Posted by kmforst on October 31st, 2014.

Re: Is Playing Traditional Music Fast A Skill, Or Is It An Insult To Ireland’s Instrumental Heritage.?

In 2002, I embarked on my “Self-Indulgent Flutemakers Tour” where I visited 25 places in the US, Canada, England, Ireland and Scotland over the period of two months. That included attending 32 sessions! I found and noted an enormous diversity in speeds and friendliness of sessions - the fastest (and generally most competitive) sessions being in the major cities, the more relaxed and more friendly sessions being in regional centres. 32 samples may not be enough to form a thesis on; perhaps someone would like to extend the study and report back! But it suggests that speed is not an independent variable, it might be linked to social considerations as well.

I remember commenting to flute maker Sam Murray that I had found the previous night’s session in Belfast too fast to enjoy, and he responded brightly: “Ah yes, Belfast Power Diddle”. Good to have a name for the syndrome, perhaps a cure is just around the corner….

I was amused to see recently the style instructions on an 17th century piece of harpsichord music: “To be taken as slowly as talent permits”.

I think it was old Tim Whelan, whistle player originally from Dublin but later living in South Australia, hearing some young buck fracturing a nice tune at breakneck speed, who summed it up nicely. “Ah, he’s interfering with something he doesn’t understand…”

For my own part, I enjoy a range of speeds, generally avoiding the extremes at either end. Some tunes (e.g. The Sailor’s Bonnet) can clip along at a great rate without losing the rhythm, others can’t, so I think taking them above that critical point is to do them a disservice. And some tunes are just so full of beautiful “nuance” (e.g. Mist on the Mountain), that they can support being taken slowly, and benefit from it. I’d say let each tune set its own pace.

Scales, Scales, Scales! (and arpeggios)

Yes playing fast is a skill, but you can do it. You just need to start slowly. Practice 2 notes at a time until you can play the whole bar perfectly, then add another bar and another until you can play the whole A part without a mistake. Same for the B part. Then practice the whole tune at a slow tempo, say 80 on your metronome. Each day, increase the speed by a few notches. By the end of the week, you’ll have it up to a good tempo. Do this with every tune you learn.

Depends on the context doesn’t it? If you’re at a session where everyone is rattling through farewell to Erin at a thousand miles an hour, it’s probably not the time to play the drunken sailor as slowly as you can while trying to milk the tone out of your instrument. And vice-versa.
If you’re playing for dancers, ask them what sort of speed they want. (In my admittedly very limited experience they want reels to be really fast and hornpipes really slow, with jigs at about the speed I’d normally expect to play them, but that might depend on where you are and what sort of dancing they do.)
If you’re just playing for yourself, or other people expect you to set the tone for a session, I guess you should experiment a bit. Ideally you should play at a speed where you’re comfortable and relaxed enough to do the tune justice, but if you don’t push yourself occasionally and leave your comfort zone, you’ll find progress is pretty slow. It’s all about paradoxes innit.
In my (again, limited-we’re mainly talking busking here) experience of playing for money, especially for people who aren’t already into the music, you want to differentiate the types tunes as much as possible to avoid the dreaded ‘it all sounds the same’. You can do that to some extent by emphasising the differences in the rhythm patterns, but differentiating the speed helps too.
The tradition can survive any amount of messing around with tempo, so do whatever you want at home. Don’t derail other people’s sessions though.
