Hate my own playing and i’m fed up making excuses for it

All i have to go on is what i record of my playing, when i bother to, but actually there’s a lot of that, and it’s all hopeless.

Rhythm, other people have it.

Lets talk about phrasing. Just breathe any random where and if by some chance, before that point the rhythm is decently established, lets immediately lose it! If it is to be re-established, then have the feel to be different than before the breath, as a preference ‘flatter’ if its a reel, so that overall the effect is a janky patchwork of discrete lumps between breaths with a different ‘feel’ to each.

Above all, where-ever possible get nervous and rush, by which i don’t mean start at 170 bpm or speed the tune up, no, but keep the speed chugging at a sensible moderate pace - that way there’s all the time in the world to appreciate what an inhomogenous, unmusical mess it is. Just squeeze all the space from between the notes, but NOT ALWAYS, to make it stand out, occasionally you have to do it right for a little bit to achieve a properly jarring effect.

That i’m actually good at ornaments and tend to give them the right space is not a feature as much as a bug because the relative precision at random points jars horribly with the fact i can’t do anything rhythmically-correctly on the bare bits of the tune.

So fair enough i’m terrible, but i still believe(d) despite mountains of home-recording evidence to the contrary that ‘its just nerves’ or whatever. Justified because nearly always i’m unhappy with how i;m playing when i record it (versus when i’m relaxed) and can definitely do better. Unfortunately recently - just once! - i was pretty happy with some things i recorded at the time (3 sets the other day.) But they were terrible when i listened back as well. You’d think that if i was any cop, there’d be at least 1 track of decent recording (from over 100 hours of recording in all) but no. Literally not one.

I listen and i cringe. It’s not enough to just disapprove of what i hear, i hate it on a primal visceral level that makes me want to take a hammer to my instrument or my hands.

But what’s hilarious. (I mean mortifying and upsetting), is that i really (and i mean this unironically) sound great in a session, not just competent, i sound way better than i actually am - i guess the caveat being that nobody can properly listen in that format even if they wanted to. To the extent where i’ve been in sessions with all ireland champions and pros & had people come up and say nice stuff after. So being a normal person, i tend to want to default to thinking that i can play a bit, versus regarding myself as a hopeless case. In fact its just stringing me along and falsely building up my confidence, where at some point my mechanics and basics are fundamentally flawed. Or i just pathologically have no musicality and i have dexterity but without any ear for music? Both?

If that wasn’t enough, my hands are packing in some sort of tendon thing where i can’t grip the whistle right on my bad days and my fingers have little feeling or are tingly. Yesterday was like that and (even by my standards) i couldn’t - do - anything. It was a lot like how it is when you try and play outside when it’s zero degrees Centigrade, only at least thats only super logy finger speed. Not wierd hand shape like you never held the thing before and it feels wrong no matter how much you faff around trying to get comfortable.

And now i’m pathologically invested in ‘proving’ to myself i can play, which means at a minimum recording 1 jig, reel, hornpipe and air with (at the very least) solid rhythm. Having a day when i think i’m playing alright is no longer sufficient to prove the case for the defence, (since i have previously thought something was decent before listening back and it turned out to be cack.)

Somehow I make it into rocket science when trying to record something - it’s creative how many ways i end up trying to interpret rhythm, trying to CREATE rhythm, and not in a good way. Also listening back, i usually know when it’s wrong, but i find it hard to eg differentiate which are my better efforts (the shiniest of many turds?), and to an extent, i feel that when i’m listening to myself critically, my subjective assumptions weigh into what i think i’m hearing way more than they should. If i’m stressed, and i get stressed waiting for the other shoe to drop listening back to my own stuff, i will tend to hear it as rhythmically inexact regardless and it won’t be a wrong judgement wrong per se.

So, its just a bit shaky rhythmically, what’s the big deal? It’s not just a bit inexact, it’s a complete shambles. I’m not after being Mary Bergin, just to have an intermediate player level of rhythm, if i can’t do that, then none of the rest of it matters, and since i dislike and am stressed by my own playing, since my hands are packing in (because of course, why wouldn’t they be? Anything to add another indeterminate variable tyo make rhythm impossible) then i might as well pack it in.

The fact that i can fool a lot of the people a lot of the time in a session doesn’t help, in fact all it’s done is make me complacent about my playing for YEARS when i should have been tearing it apart and learning to do it RIGHT.

“i really (and i mean this unironically) sound great in a session … i sound way better than i actually am”

This puts me in mind of the variously attributed ironic quote, “Wagner’s music is much better than it sounds.”

Music can only be as good as it sounds, because *sound* is what music is made of. If you sound good in a session, you *are* good – maybe better at some things than others (i.e. better at playing as part a group than playing unaccompanied), but that’s life. It is frustrating and disheartening, no doubt, to be acutely aware of your weaknesses yet not know how to overcome them. Keep playing in sessions, since you evidently enjoy that (and, presumably, so do others), but perhaps you should ease off recording yourself at home for a while, since it always seems to make you unhappy and anxious – if scrutinising your own playing does not lead to improving it, then all you are doing is torturing yourself.

Yes, taking up the mangocello is a good idea – or maybe the mandarin, tangerine or fruit.

Re: Hate my own playing and i’m fed up making excuses for it

Self criticism is the driving force behind all musicians. If you ever stop seeing the faults in your playing and just think ‘I played that brilliantly’ that is the time to stop, because you’ll never get any better.

Your specific issue at the moment seems to be timing, so practice with a metronome, make your recordings with a click track (it’s not cheating, most professional bands record that way nowadays). Practice consciously over-emphasising the beat - if you are just playing strings of quavers it is inevitable that the timing will slip, but if you are deliberately punching out the beat you’ll be bringing it back under control once or twice per bar.

Re: Hate my own playing and i’m fed up making excuses for it

This reminds me of my playing up until the last year or so.

The solution is to sit at home when no one is listening and start over from scratch. Take your favorite tune and play it super slow and play it perfectly with a metronome at a very slow pace. Keep doing it until it becomes natural and slowly speed it up as you gain more control.

Often we’re playing in public and are too eager to play fast and never take the time to firm up our playing. Then we want to learn more tunes when we haven’t ever firmed up our original tunes.

The good news is that after a while it just becomes natural and it will carry over into all of your other tunes that you never worked on as well as new tunes.

You just need to retrain your fingers. Same with the breathing. Slow it way down and pick some good spots at which to take a breath and stick to them. After a while it will become natural. Also you may not be taking enough breaths. For instance if you’re trying to hold out too long you’ll be forced to take a breath where it doesn’t sound good or such a big breath that it interferes with the tune.

I think you’re way ahead of the game though in that you recognize the flaws which is half the way to fixing them. Many have the same issues and are blissfully unaware.

The CREATIVE PROCESS needs to be clarified and engaged with. You must tantra the music, yoga the riffing, swim, scuba and surf in the dynamic oceans of rhythm, motion, feel, experience, sensation, mood, energy flows, lung activity and melody. I don’t know where Melody is since she went on holidays, so we’ll leave her out of this for now.

You need to both entirely relax your analytic mind and engage everything else in the music directly and completely. Turn off the analysis. You seem to be standing far back from 1) yourself, 2) the music, and 3) the instrument, with a sense of high anxiety based on what I don’t know. It doesn’t matter any more. Throw that approach out, it doesn’t work.

So, I’ll mention some ways to get there.
1) Play some recorded music you like and play along with it, but spontaneously and just wing it. Don’t try to closely copy it. Instead, try to get into the feel of the music, sense the phrasing, sense the rhythm, move your body to it, and get the lungs working in rhythm to that. So in this case your fully immersed in the situation but not as a student or copyist but as a seagull flying all over the beach area where waves and wind are happening but you’re in full control and just follow your nose to explore everything. Sail! Fly! Be free! Or be the musical dolphin who swims masterfully all over that ocean no matter how calm or stormy it is.
2) Improvise with no music playing, just out of your own head. Don’t play familiar tunes, don’t play anything you’ve heard before, just spontaneously get the instrument in action to find very simple musical ideas and aim to get the maximum FEEL of them and make them happen, then repeat them but with some slight variations each time. Variation and repetition are at the core of all classical music and much else too, including fractals, DNA, and why sports betters lose so much money. You MUST be fully committed to the FEEL of the music or you’re being phony to the CREATIVE PROCESS and have no chance of finding authentic presence in the act of self-expression. Think about that.
3) Practice the instrument, to master your ability to carefully control the notes throughout the full range of the thing, whether it’s a whistle, violin, harmonica, guitar, drum or a rental calliope. Feel the lung to instrument to fingertip relationship as an energy flow and learn how to get that happening like wind through a national flag up above a public library. If the lungs and the mind and the mood you’re in and the instrument aren’t all a unified process then something is going to cause roadblocks. Practice the lowest range. Practice the highest range. It’s like learning to drive a car; learning to deal with the mechanical/physical part of driving MUST become second-nature (subconscious) if you’re ever going to actually drive on the streets and deal with traffic safely. If it’s a whistle or flute or just about anything, practice some simple trills or chromatic runs, to get the fingers in control, and to get your musical imagination flowing smoothly with a sense that from MIND to FINGERS is a unified thing. Eventually this can lead to >
4) Being able to improvise with great agility, so that you can hear the music in your head and transfer it to instrument simultaneously. There’s no delay, and with practice, less and less error, with ever more feel and conviction.

You’ve been analytical and standing back from it all. You need to go to the MUSIC BEACH and go snorkeling. Enjoy it all. IF you scuba regularly off that beach, you can develop your lung capacity by learning to hold your breath and exercise your rib cage and diaphragm muscles. Stretch it out.

Music is the energy flows of the universe. It’s natural and wholistic. Analysis is an isolated, obstinate, irrelevant and culturally rotted stone in your shoe. Stick with the music.

Re: Hate my own playing and i’m fed up making excuses for it

If rhythm is your main issue (and it sounds like it is), then practice playing with a metronome a lot i.e. all the time, for a few weeks. And play along with recordings - steady paced recordings.

Recording yourself is good, it shows you are invested in becoming a better player. But yes, it is super-demoralising. Remember that solo recordings put your playing under a microscope in a way that no other form of music making does. I think solo recording is tougher than playing a solo gig, because the audience is on your side, and are there to enjoy themselves, and they don’t notice or care about the odd mistake.

I would say, pick some simple tunes. Not necessarily Irish trad tunes. Go as simple as you need to, even ‘Twinkle Twinkle Little Star’ if need be. Record something simple and record it well.

I recently joined one of those ‘tune a week’ groups on Facebook. ‘Tunesday Tuesdays’ it’s called. A new tune is chosen every Tuesday, and we all post up a video of ourselves playing said tune the following week. It’s been a really good discipline for me - forcing myself to learn and record a tune each week. It’s always taken me several (often over 20) attempts to get a good take of the new tune I’ve learned, even when it’s simple. But having a deadline is a very good thing.

You come up with workarounds (e.g. leaving out the fancy fiddly bit that you can only get right once in a blue moon) and learn which note you always play a bit sharp and which part you always tend to speed up in. You also learn that perfection doesn’t exist - ‘done’ is better than perfect.

I agree with much of what others have said, but I wanted to throw this in too.

Here’s the Wikipedia page that explains dermatomes. These are the areas of skin that are enervated by individual or groups of nerves, and when it comes to unpicking the reasons for hand or wrist pain, they’re very useful.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermatome_(anatomy)

Near the bottom of the page there’s a strip of additional images; look at the first one, which gives the dermatomes of both sides of the hand. When you next get the tingling from playing, think about the area that’s affected and check it against this. If it’s clear which nerve is involved (and it often is), it gives you a head start on how to work out what’s causing the impingement. Usually it’s the elbow or the wrist, but it could be a high up as the the neck or as far back as the shoulder blade. It’s hard to address behaviours or postural issues that might be causing the numbness unless you know which nerve is the problem.

If you can sort this out, it might resolve half of your difficulty. The other half is more difficult, because there’s no helpful diagram on how to give yourself a break. I do hope you can!

Re: Hate my own playing and i’m fed up making excuses for it

I play the flute and only rarely play the whistle, but am also rhythmically challenged and it requires a constant effort to make my playing sparkle rhythmically. Three thoughts: (1) I think the whistle is a particularly difficult instrument to play with good rhythm. The reason is that, for reasons unknown to me, whistle players tend to slur more than players of other instruments. This can be seen in the playing of players who are highly proficient on both instruments such as Kevin Crawford, whose whistle-playing is much more ‘slurry’ than his flute-playing. (2) I am blessed with a good friend who also plays the flute and who has fantastic rhythm. When I am discouraged with what I am doing with a tune, I can ask him to send me a clip of his own playing. What I hear are a wealth of sharp articulations -- breath pulses, cuts, pauses to breathe that are a bit punctuated, etc. -- that hadn’t occurred to me. I don’t know if it would help your playing to try these devices (and I don’t know how well they would work on the whistle), but give them a try. I believe some well-known whistle players tongue notes on occasion as well. Try that. (3) I listen a lot to box and concertina players as their instruments seem to lend themselves to well-punctuated, rhythmical articulation and I hope some of this rubs off on me. Chet

When I was a kid, if I strung a rubber band over a match box and twanged it (as I really did) it wouldn’t have bothered me whatsoever if people didn’t like the noise that I was enjoying. It’s the same now when I play the fiddle. If you don’t like the noises that you make yourself then just don’t make them. If you do, then just keep doing it. Personally I just can’t help it;- give me a stick and some railings and there’s a tune in my head as I walk along.

The Sean sa Cheo was interesting in particular Kenny. I’d actually been trying to record that tune off and on, and felt ‘stuck between gears’ if you follow me. Most whistle players seem to play it dead fast, i’ve listened to stacks of versions, and that’s the way i’d naturally play with others. But solo, just implicitly it felt wrong trying to do it that way and so every time it turned out different - different speed and different feel which at some point went wrong and jarred with me. Like the Curate’s Egg - good in parts. His was v. nice tho.
