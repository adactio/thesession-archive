It may come as a surprise to you that there aren’t (apart from a few Early Music intellectuals who are deliberately re-creating music from the past). Re: Successful classical-Irish Music Fusion

The integration of different, new instruments is in my humble opinion mere window-dressing. That’s just the tools used, not the building itself. It’s how they are played that makes a difference, not what the instruments are. The tunes and the aesthetics remain the same: when a new instrument enters the realm of traditional music, its first task is to find a way of fitting in.

I don’t think anyone would suggest that the way this music has been played has remained COMPLETELY unchanged IN ANY WAY for centuries. Not least cos one player rarely plays the same thing the same way twice! But, listening to O’Neill’s wax cylinder recordings of Patsy Touhey or James Early, some of which date from 116 years ago, what strikes me more is the similarities than the differences. It does not sound like alien music.

So I think it’s highly contentious to say: “if people refuse to adapt to modern tastes the music will lose its appeal and die”. Not least because, well, who defines whose “modern tastes” these are? There’s nothing especially “modern” about Goat Rodeo – their sound and line-up could have come about at any point in the last 50 years. If you really wanted to adapt Irish trad to modern tastes, you’d be fusing it with hip-hop or something. But that would be naff and probably sound awful. (Never say never though, who knows, anything’s possible…)

Traditional music, unlike pop music, can exist – and does, via things like sessions – completely outside of the commercial music marketplace. Irish music - unlike many other national folk traditions - is very healthy, no risk of it dying. Irish traditional music doesn’t need fusion; some people might like it, fair enough, but I think it’s simply wrong to suggest that fusion’s existence is crucial to Irish traditional music’s health.

Re: Successful classical-Irish Music Fusion

“What you seem to be missing is that the traditional music you are playing is created entirely out of a series of fusions. The instruments used, the tune types, many of the tunes themselves and even the social setting where the music is played have all been brought in from elsewhere.”

I’m well aware of the fact that plenty of Irish tune types weren’t indigenous. I play a lot of English music too and own tons of old tune books. But that’s a very different process: that’s a process of assimilation and adaption and absorption. You could call it fusion, but I don’t think that’s as accurate a term as, say, “naturalisation” or “adaptation”. A series of millions of very very tiny fusions, if you must: very slow, tectonic, incremental fusions. Like barnacles on a massive rock. All of which is in the nature of being a musician playing music. Or even being a human being existing in the world.

That’s all very different to, say, choosing to put rock drums on a track, or teaming up with a bunch of classical musicians etc etc. “Fusion” in the Kenny G sense of the word. Things like Goat Rodeo, or cheesy classical musicians, or soupy middle-of-the-road harp playing, or Irish music with a naff funk rhythm or whatever. (No disrespect to Goat Rodeo: they might not be my cup of tea, but they’re way better than the other sort of stuff I’ve just mentioned.) That is a very self-conscious, inorganic decision. It’s not interpolating one small thing into one big thing. It’s a mishmash.

De Dannan or Planxty popularising stuff is, again, a completely different process to the assimilation of different tunes and, less often, techniques or instruments from other folk traditions. One testament to how powerful (or perhaps how conservative) Irish trad music is is how relatively FEW instruments are generally played within it. Despite 70-odd years of rock music, you still don’t generally hear many electric guitars in Irish trad music. And that’s a GOOD thing, because they tend to sound bad. Worth stating again: listen to the earliest recordings of ITM, and they don’t sound alien to a solo instrumentalist playing today.

For what it’s worth, I have no problem with Irish music - or indeed any traditional music - existing purely as old men in kitchens, or just as pub sessions etc And I genuinely don’t believe that it’s Planxty or De Dannan that I have to thank for the fact that I play and love Irish trad music today. I like some of their music, but it has no bearing whatsoever on the music I listen to and the music I play.

>>“I’m also finding it hard to believe that trad was going to die if trad didn’t become fused with other popular Music genres. If the fusions didn’t happen the only thing that was going to happen to trad was that it wouldn’t have been known to the masses.”

Music that isn’t played is dead music. If you think you are preserving the music by sitting in your kitchen playing to yourself just like your grandaddy did you are wrong, if that is all you do the music will die with you. To preserve it you have to make it appeal to the next generation so that they take on the mantle, and if that means fusing it with styles they are more familiar with, so be it.

I want to talk about cars. Some people devote their lives to preserving vintage cars. They do it with a real passion, and go to extreme lengths to ensure their cars stay exactly as they were the day they were made. What they do is important, It lets us see how cars were in the past, but I’ve never heard a vintage car enthusiast lamenting the fact that most people drive modern cars. And that is how it is with traditional music. Yes, if you want to preserve the music and play it as it was played at some particular point in time that’s great, go for it. But it’s not a reason for lamenting the fact that other people are willing to experiment and move the music forwards. The more people they expose to the music, even in corrupt forms, the better because a small percentage of those people will go on to discover the pure drop. I understand that you are one of the lucky few who come from a long family tradition, but if you ask other members on this board, even the hard core pure drop exponents, I think you’ll find for the vast majority their first exposure was to commercial bands and their corrupt, fused forms of the music.
