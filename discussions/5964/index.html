Re: TV definition of Traditional Instruments

“Wasn’t it an import too, just like the banjo?”

Ah, the irony. The wooden whistle, which many tend to look down on now as not being properly Irish is, in fact, the “proper” Irish whistle and the “Tin” whistle the modern English import invented by Clarke, basically because a good wooden one was too expensive and didn’t last if not treated with extreme care.

But here’s the thing, not only do the wooden ones rot nearly as soon as they’re tossed out, thus leaving no archeological remains (The stone at Stonehenge survives, but it took them a long time to figure out that there used to be wooden posts too, because they had to deduce that from the post holes. The wood is long gone), but only the well off could afford one.

The vast majority of Irish whistles were made on the spot from whatever hollow grasses and reeds were growing in the neighborhood, used until they no longer served, then just tossed aside. A bit of grass in a roadside ditch, rottting away with all the other bits of grass. No archeological record.

Thus the bamboo whistle/flute has greater traditional provenence than wood (which itself has greater provenence than metal) or metal, even though bamboo itself is an import. (The wooden flute as we know it became traditional during the potato famine in the mid 1800s. This happened to coincide with Boehm’s invention of the modern flute. The wealthy flute owners (and you had to be wealthy to own a keyed flute in the first place, which is why the flute is a relative newcomer to Irish music) discarded their English style flutes to replace them with German style flutes and the shops filled up with English flutes that had originally cost a thousand pounds, but were now considered “worthless.” The Irish bought them, because they were perfectly dandy for playing Irish music on, after you knocked the C keys off the end to turn it into a D flute. Hence the invention of the “vented” flute which so many people still treasure, even though the “vents” are a historical oddity that have no effect on the sound of the flute)

But we do have the historical record, and the Irish reed whistle predates history. The practice of making reed whistles also survived until the 20th century or so, because there were often people who could not even afford the “cheap” tin whistle or “worthless” English flute (nowadays there are so many metal whistles just lying around that the poor can obtain one for free, or maybe a penny)

The harp craze that swept Ireland for several hundred years made the harp the national symbol of Ireland, but it was the whistle that could be found in just about anyone’s pocket (harps were expensive and took years to learn to play) from the dawn of history (and beyond!) right up until today, and it’s only since the time of Cromwell (when everything identifiable as Irish was frowned upon, unto death) that the whistle began to be looked upon as a lowly and/or “beginner’s” instrument.

Pull the fipple off one of your C whistles (because the C is “big”). Now take it outside and find a bit of dried, hollow stalk (rush, cat tail, oat, wild bamboo, some weed or other) that can be cut to fit in the fipple. Now poke some holes in it with a pen knife and slowly bore them out (working from low note to high) until it sounds about right, by ear (if you can’t afford a pen knife you can use a rock to bore the holes. I’ve done it. It works)

Now, and probably for the first time in your life, you have an instrument that can be declared truely and properly “Irish.”
