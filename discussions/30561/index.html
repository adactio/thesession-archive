Flute consensus?

This is going to sound like a trick question & there may be a number of ways to answer it.

The question is about so-called *D* flutes. As I understand it flutes which were made before (mechanical) keys were introduced were named according to the lowest note which can be played. i.e. *D* flutes would be simple system, six-hole (I’ll leave that to you which holes I’m counting) flutes which sound a D note when played with the six holes covered. Dispute the details all you want, but I think it’s “true enough”
for your standard D whistle & D flute.

Now here’s the bit about the flute I’m playing these days. It is a variation of the six-hole, keyless flute in that it has a lower foot with 2 additional holes. The question is would you say it is “a simple-system D flute with a long D foot”?

It’s not a trick question. I’m posting it because I think it may be one of those rare instances where there is some agreement.

So, why am I hesitating ….

Re: Flute consensus?

I’m not sure there is agreement, Ben.

This should be a long answer, but here’s my attempt at a short form. I’m sorry if it’s confusing as a result.

In the baroque era, flutes were made in a number of keys. However, the flute that us traddies think of as a ‘D’ flute, with 6 holes, the bottom note of which was a D, was called a ‘C’ flute. Read Quantz if you don’t believe me.

So, to a classical musician, the flute that we call a ‘D’ flute is a ‘C’ flute. BTW, it has nothing to do with the notation of the music written for the instrument, as some often suggest. It’s simply the convention for the instrument.

A FAQ from Doug Tipple

http://tippleflutes.com/
https://sites.google.com/site/dougsflutes/frequentlyaskedquestions

What is the difference between a 6-hole and an 8-hole flute?

The 8-hole flutes have two non-fingered exhaust ports. This allows the flute to be longer without changing the pitch of the flute. On many Irish flutes these exhaust ports are in the position of lower notes on the flute. For example, on a low D flute the first exhaust port, if covered, would play the C#, and the second exhaust port, if covered, would play the low C note. On some flutes these ports can be fitted with keys if desired. The exhaust ports on my 8-hole flutes are not in the positions just described. On my flutes, at least, I cannot detect a difference in tone or volume between a 6-hole and an 8-hole low D flute, and the fingering is exactly the same for both flutes. Some players feel that the longer 8-hole flute looks more like a traditional Irish flute, and, depending on the weight of the headjoint and tuning slide, it may feel more balanced in the hand. Some players can sense an additonal blowing resistance because of the extra three inches in body length. With the lightweight machined pvc tuning joints that I construct, I don’t think that a longer flute is needed for balance, and I prefer the shorter and lighter 6-hole flute. If you are interested in reading about this in greater depth, the topic was discussed on the flute forum at Chiff & Fipple.

Re: Flute consensus?

I play both a Boehm system ‘C’ flute and an Irish ‘D’ flute. The difference is totally in the terminology used by different communities of musicians. On both flutes, if you put down the 6 main fingers, you get a low D. The reason the Boehm flute plays an Fnat with four fingers down is because, with the cylindrical tube, the F# is too far above the E to reach easily, so the Fnat and F# holes are covered by keys which are joined together. To get an F#, you have play G and use the 6th finger to make the F# close on it’s own. (This sounds more complicated than it is in my head!).

The ‘C’ instruments in the orchestra all sound a C when they read, and play, the note C on the music score. Other instruments will sound a different note when playing a C, such as the clarinet, usually sounding a Bb (or, less commonly, an A) when playing C. That is the reason for calling them that terminology. ‘C’ instruments can all read the same music (ignoring the bass/treble clef difference), and Bb instruments can all read the same music, and Eb instruments can all read the same music - makes life easier when writing out the individual parts.
