So … preconceptions?

We are thesession.org

Here are my preconceptions about what a session is.

- You play tunes.
- The tunes may be old “traditional” tunes or they may be relatively recent ‘compositions’.
- There are different styles of playing regarding phrasing, articulation, type of tunes & such; each session tending to favour it’s individual style (or styles).
- To a certain extent players in a given session come to learn (or “know”) a set of core tunes,
played in a setting which *that* session chooses, over time. [Or not].
- Sessions (in general) are neither welcoming nor exclusive. i.e. ~ each session develops it’s individual way of working with it’s own particular needs & desires.
- Sessions always have a degree of slagging, banter & crack (except the latter is sometimes written as craic).
- A session may only happen once or periodically.

I refuse to address the issue of what a session *is not*, because to go there is pointless & it is the place from which no one has ever returned.

I ask the board, “What are your preconceptions regarding what a session is?”

Re: So … preconceptions?

We’ve already had this discussion here before - I would refer you to page 91 of Fintan Vallely’s “Companion To Irish Traditional Irish Music”. 11 years ago Zina Lee asked Fintan about this, and posted his reply here , copied below :

Okay, I wrote Fintan Vallely, asking him if he was indeed pulling legs with his entry under the “craic” category in the Companion to Irish Traditional Music, and here’s his reply — enjoy, I certainly did! (And yes, he gave me permission to pass this on. :)
___________________________

Zina:

Yes and no … But mostly, emphatically, NO. The pisstake is only a device.

When I asked Caomhín Mac Aoidh (author of Between the jigs and the reels, on Donegal fiddling) what his opinion was, he gave the quote (which is attributed to him) Rather good I thought.

Yes, for the reasons outlined in the Companion article, the spelling ‘craic’ causes serious nausea among intelligent people. This glib spelling of the word was invented in the 1970s - All of the rest of the article is deadly serious (if you call railing against stupidity serious).

The article also makes it clear that it is the context of the use of the (recent, modern) Irish spelling of the word that is the issue - if ‘craic’ is to be used it should be used while writing in the Irish language, OR placed in parentheses or in italics when writing in English. I stress that this is a word which was NEVER in the Irish language (but cráic, meaning arsehole, or creac, meaning herd, are). The original word, crack, IS in fact old English and is still widely used in England and Scotland, and had moved from there to Ulster with the plantation in the 17th century.

I grew up using the word in the 1950s. When I went to Dublin (from Ulster) in 1968 NOBODY I met in Dublin used ‘crack’, but people from down south used ‘gas’ (a corruption of the Irish ‘geas’ meaning spell, or wonder, effectively the same thing). Crack only began to be used with the influx of northerners and in the context of music, it travelled with northern influence (at the fleadh cheoil, etc) until southern people began to believe that they had invented it. Ciarán Carson is particular enraged by the ‘craic’ spelling, so too Desi Wilkinson and many other otherwise tolerant souls. It is used largely by the kind of people who put signs outside bars such as “Chips and beens today”, or “New potato’s”. But alarmingly ‘craic’ is used by younger journalists who cull their education from pub signs and only read press releases issued by CCÉ PR officers who because of the fact that many of them don’t play actual music (ah, sure why should they have to?) they are obliged to think that at least some of the words that they speak are surely Irish - on account of the fact that CCÉ gets the bulk of its funding from the Irish government’s Irish language budget. Another such word is ‘seisiún’, equally nauseating if used without italics in the English language. The implication of its usage is that ‘seisiún’ in its music sense is an Irish tradition. It is not. It probably started in the USA, and became a feature of Irish music life only in the revival years (post 1952). ‘seisiún’ is likely constructed from the English ‘session’. It should be used properly only as a prefix to ‘ceoil’ (= a music session, as opposed to a drinking session, or a courting session) Originally, anyway, it is a borrowing of the word used for such as a ‘court session’, and like crack, intelligent reason should have demanded the usage of a proper Irish word for the concept of music session.

Someone else, who probably now is a fanatic agin smoking in bars, remarked to me that the English language in Irish journalism ‘had’ to re-spell crack as craic, to avoid confusion with the crack cocaine. Dear me! Using that as logic we should be changing half the words in the English language into babby-irish. I mean, look at what ‘bush’ means - literally and colloquially, And Irish music has ‘the old bush reel’. Which do we re-name ‘búis’ or whatever. Anyway since the drug ‘crack’ is now found on Irish streets, and the Irish language is obliged to keep up with modern trends, the linguists have in fact gratuitously wasted an important word in their permission for the borrow-usage of ‘craic’ - rather a stupid thing since there are dozens of perfectly good and much more expressive words already in the Irish language to describe ‘good fun’. I’m getting nauseous again.

Sincerely,

Fintan Vallely

Re: So … preconceptions?

…and ‘accordion’ is a loanword from Irish ‘cairdín’, meaning ‘little heart’. It just happened to sound a bit like ‘accord’ (the word for ‘chord’ in French - and similar in many other European languages) which spawned erroneous folk etymologies relating to its facility for playing chords.

The cairdín, by the way, was invented in 1716 by Connemaraman Paul O‘Soprani. It was not until over a century later, when the English police reached Connemara, that the cairdín found its way to Great Britain and continental Europe, by way of being seized by police officers and sold elsewhere. (Corruption in the police is nothing new.) Inevitably, some of these were sold on before they even got across the Irish sea, thus enabling the cairdín to become established elsewhere in Ireland. As the cairdín problem in Ireland grew, it was decided that drastic measures were needed and, in the late 19th Century, the police force employed their IT experts to prevent the emergence of any new players by rearranging all the buttons. Meanwhile, the cairdín problem had taken hold across the pond and, without the civilising influence of the English police, there was no stopping it. Furthermore, it was only a matter of decades before a smart young Tipperaryman, Paddy O’Brains, using a combination of Faurier transforms and strong tea, managed to decipher the new ‘scrambled’ tuning and create a whole new way of playing. The English police, surrendering to the fact that *even they* were not clever enough to break the pulsating heart of Ireland, retreated and were replaced by the (often cairdín-toting) Gardaí.

Re: So … preconceptions?

Your list does not stand well as a list of what you think a session will be, since your points either offer possibilities within a range or they get into things that are not what sessions are generally about. It is easier to say what a session is not, but you didn’t want that. As a result, the only preconceived notion I can offer is that a session is a group of people gathered to play Irish trad. Whether it is a closed or open session, a place to learn new tunes, involves different styles, is a place for banter, etc., is nothing about which you can have a preconceived notion. Except for what I have already offered, you can have no additional notion unless you are told or go to see for yourself.

Now, I can tell you what you can safely put out of your mind as to what to expect, at least generally, if that helps:

Sessions do not include
Improvisation
Tunes that are not dance tunes, songs or airs, except for O’Carolan tunes which I tend to think of as being none of these
Instruments other than fiddles, instruments related to accordion or guitar, flute, whistle, bodhran, bones, or hammered dulcimer
Amplification
Written music

I won’t get into notions of etiquette, but will leave this as a pretty comprehensive outline of what to expect. The etiquette questions are best answered by asking or observation.

Re: So … preconceptions?

“but you left out Dean O’Bafferty’s part of the tale.”

That’s another strand to the story entirely. But in brief, O‘Bafferty was a double glazing salesman and occasional drinking companion of O’Soprani from Boxford, Co. Mayo. Once, when he was doing his rounds in O‘Soprani’s home town of Lettercrack, he would met with his friend for a few drinks in the local hostelry. Being a salesman, he was well versed in the art of persuasion and, having had a successful day’s window selling (window shopping being a popular pastime in Connemara), he was able to afford to ply Mr. O’Soprani with enough poitín to have his eyes thoroughly glazed (enough, in fact, to glaze a lesser man’s eyes twice over). Meanwhile, O‘Bafferty, for every gill of an créatúr that O’Soprani drank, took the equivalent amount of water and - perhaps not the ‘friend’ he appeared to be - persuaded O‘Soprani to divulge the secrets of his cairdín making. O’Bafferty delivered the poor, helpless O‘Soprani safely to his home and the very next day, sailed to Italy, where he Italicised his name and set up a rival accordion factory. (So it was not the English police alone that were responsible for the international spread of the cairdín.) Still, many of O’Soprani’s fellow countrymen, knowing this story, have remained faithful to the man and his cairdíns are still widely played today (although he too, for some reason, eventually succumbed to the peculiar fashion for making Irish names look Italian).

Re: So … preconceptions?

“In the final bit, not getting into notions about etiquette, it seems you already did by stating what sessions do not include.”

I think we’re operating under definitions that are not the same. When we speak of session etiquette, what is meant are things like asking to join (or waiting to be invited), not having a raft of bodhrans at the same time, not playing faster or slower than the general group, etc. The things that sessions don’t include exist within the realm of what most in the tradition consider the make-up of a session, having nothing to do with what is polite or considerate.

I think the way I defined a session is as much concept of what will occur as you need. Sessions will vary, but not much. To the extent that they do, with the exception of closed sessions, having advance notice of the peculiarities will probably not be important.

“a session is a group of people gathered to play Irish trad.”
Why does it have to be Irish and why does it have to be trad? No Calliope House then.

It’s very difficult to say what a “session” is not. Perhaps easier is to say what one particular “session” is not (though that suggests in some cases having rules that are enforced). So from Ailin’s list of no-nos I can say that more than one of those is broken at my local. We have (GHB) pipers who come along and play through electronic chanters linked to little amplifiers. And we sometimes play from sheets when working on new tunes.

As has been said, a session is “a group of people playing tunes”. The best ones, in my opinion, are usually the regular ones where you have to work hard to learn the particular repertoire. You’re playing with a core of regulars (who are pretty much of similar ability) plus a few visitors now and then (when usually allowances are made to encourage them to play). A core of six to ten seems to work well. At festivals you often get another type of session where a large number of players, very few of whom know each other well, play through mostly well-known tunes, usually moderately paced. Much prefer the former. Harder work but more rewarding for it.
