Re: why accompany?

There was of course the recent thread about what melody-line players want out of backers………
Well, for a start, we’ve been hearing it for a long time - I go back to listening to Carthy and Swarbrick, but long before that there was the traditon of someone “driving the piano” behind some of the early fiddle greats, sometimes, alas, added later in the studio without checking that the piano was in tune with the previously-recorded fiddle ! Margaret Barry and ?who was it? had this wonderful technique of not actually knowing what we would now consider the appropriate chords for her banjo to accompany a fiddle. It’s been pretty standard in US music for several generations now, long before Bill Munroe et al standardised the lineup with a fiddle, a mandolin, a banjo (5-string and finger-picked of course), guitar and a big double-bass for the smallest member of the band to dance on.
And yes, why do we do it ?
Well, even though I do acknowledge that playing the tunes is a higher art, yet, even my 3-chord trick on the ’zouk does seem to add something to the fullness of the sound and the punctuation of the rhythmn.
And it’s fun.

↳ Posted by bodhran bliss on July 18th, 2005.

Re: why accompany?

It’s as simple as this:

Picture a session of 4 or 5 flautists, a few accordions, 2 tenor banjos, 7 or 8 fiddle players, and maybe a piper…all playing the melody. True, everyone has their own interpretations of the music, but with 20 people playing melody, all we hear is one collective interpretation.

That’s all well and good, but I’ve always felt that to listen to a sole flute player or fiddle player play for more than a few minutes just gets boring. Heck, I love Martin Hayes to death, but without Dennis Cahill, there would be only the melody to listen to.

***Experiment***

Materials:

1) One fiddle player
2) One guitarist

Procedure:

Fiddle player plays tune in D major by him/herself. Repeat with guitarist playing D, G, A chords where appropriate. Then repeat with guitarist substituting F#m and Bm chords.

The “color” of the tune changes and grows with the addition of more complex or interesting chords. It’s amazing how one’s idea of a tune can change instantly after hearing a great accompanying chord. Even the pain of the friggin’ Rakes of Mallow can be reduced with some good chording.

I’m not big on piano accompaniment (chiefly because all the piano accompaniment I’ve heard sounds monotonous and has this annoying “chord-THUMP-chord-THUMP” sound), plus I think it’s just wrong to roll a Casio keyboard, complete with amp, into a session. And house pianos are old and out of tune. Yuck. Never send a pianist to do a guitarist’s job.

Irish music is living. It is organic. We play tunes written decades and centuries ago. The authors of the tunes in those days, as (hopefully) so with today’s tunesmiths, were inspired by love, nature, oppression, death, other people’s “fancies” or “favourites” (George White’s, for example), roads whose names would later become tune names, prostitutes (The Maids of Feakle? Yes, I’m afraid it’s true…), Trans-Atlantic travel, currency (Tenpenny Bit), etc. THE MUSIC IS A REFLECTION OF LIFE! Life never features “one thing that’s happening all the time without ever changing.” Life has happy moments, sad moments, moments of intrigue, moments of haste, moments of relaxation; all kinds of little things that together make up “life.” Was it John Done who said, “No melody is an island, entire of itself…”?

That is why accompaniment is interesting to me. It provides the “little” things to add spice, color, variation, and interest to the melody. I live for music, and music lives. I want to hear music that makes me want to laugh, to cry, to shout in jubilation, to remember old friends or mentors who have gone on, to visualize places that you can’t get up and go to any time soon. What is life without all these things?

… a melody. Bittersweet, happy-sad, briefly eternal, but a melody all the same.

Sean Earnest
Camp Hill, PA

Re: why accompany?

Nice reply, Sean Earnest!

When I joined in sessions, I had some idea of what accompaniment styles there were that had been recorded, but it quickly became apparent to me that each melodist would like something different from a chordal accompanist.

So I asked some of the local melodists to spend time with me out of the session, just one-on-one, so that they could teach me what they felt they wanted. It was wonderful and difficult, because I had to let go of what each of the previous ones had said for each new one with whom I played!

But if some of the folks who have so much splenetic fun slagging off what they call poor accompanists, took an aspiring chordal accompanist off for some one-on-one and taught them their view, their world would be a bit different, eh? The melodist may even find some previously unknown perspectives on the music!

And then there’s the dirtly little secret that many melodists, when playing with a single accompanist, get a huge charge out of it. Not just ego, but the delight of the result, an intimate collaboration which can be really magic. There are recordings which confirm that this can happen.

Yes, there are ‘pure’ solo recordings which are glorious as well. But not -all- the joy is in playing the melody, there’s plenty to go round.

And it is true that not all ITM should be accompanied, also.

Re: why accompany?

This question seems to come in on two levels. 1.Why do I (or anyone else) do it? - personal reasons usually and different for everyone.
2.Why do people feel it is needed (or not, as the case may be).
Each person can give their own reasons for the first. In answer to the second it is in the ear of the beholder. If a tune sounds full in it’s rendition or too ambiguous to be tied down to one specific chord then the tune will be allowed to fly away unfettered. But if (to the ear of the beholder) it seems to beg a chord to anchor the tune or a moving base line to keep a repetitive phrase moving along; not to mention the rhythmic elements that an accompaniment can bring. Then this is what gives the accompaniment it’s raison d’etre.
Unfortunately we all have different ideas of what is right and wrong so only the best of us will get it right in the eyes of more than 2/3 of the melodists, and that is only taking the melodists who are partial to some form of accompaniment. The rest are never going to appreciate even the subtlest sympathetic accompaniment.

Re: why accompany?

I play guitar and I can say that I for one don’t find accompanying easier that playing tunes. I admire and envy guitarists who I see confidently and intuitively applying both harmony and percussion to tunes (let’s not forget the percussive quality of strumming chords). By contrast I’ve found it pretty easy to learn a single line of music (though I don’t claim mastery and ornamentation has some way to go). So that gives me a problem. I know quite a few tunes, but if I play just the melody in a session the sound of the guitar gets swamped. I can’t even hear myself sometimes. I’m trying to develop a style of plyaing that is a bit of a mixture, with melody riffs emerging from chordal punctuation. That way I maybe can ‘drive’ the guitar a bit harder, achieving more volume, and actually contribute something to the overall sound, rather than getting submerged. But I’m having to work out the basic technique for each tune individually. Maybe there’ll be a time when it comes a bit more naturally.
