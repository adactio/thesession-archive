There is no one interpretation of a roll that works all the time. It depends on the phrasing and the effect the performer wants to create. A ~G2 in one tune might be very different from a ~G2 in another tune. I find rolls in jigs tend to be timed differently from rolls in reels.

What are you doing about grace notes? There can be some flexibility with how you attack them as well.

If you want a solution, you might try to slow down a performance of a ~G2 or a ~G3 from an instructional audio file and deduce some approximate timing. But I expect it will sound wrong in a fair fraction of the tunes.

Re: ABC Rolls

The real issue in the OP question I think is what did the transcriber mean by ~G2 or whatever. Many many folks use the ~ just to indicate that some sort of “ornamentation” (let’s not have that discussion) happens. So on ~G what might be implied is a cut or a strike. On ~G2 one might mean a short roll or a triplet (using three same pitches or inserting the note above or below as the center pitch). Or, It might simply imply a cut or strike. With ~G3 it might be any of a whole much of things. Because the transcribers are so inconsistent I don’t see how one could possibly create any consistent meaning. Maybe this is one of those “A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds” situations… But I’m not implying the OP has a little mind.

If you must find one I’d suggest

~G either a cut or a strike
~G2 short roll
~G3 long roll.

In all cases i’d try to divide the note into equal parts by inserting the needed graces between the equal parts where the graces are as short as possible and take whatever duration they need from the preceding note (so that each note of the equal parts falls on it’s own eighth value). The result will be too mechanical, but would at least provide a starting point from which to work. Check out the discussions in the Flute/Whistle books by Grey Larsen for ideas.

But I still think it is not really possible to produce anything like what a fine player might do.

Re: ABC Rolls

“if you are going to take the trouble to finger notes, be they main or grace, for whatever reason, in whatever context, they should be heard. If they can’t, then it’s either deliberate, un-thinking, accidental, or the simply part of the efn tradition”

One function of gracenotes is to maintain rhythm. To the listener they serve a percussive function, and to the player they organize the movement of the fingers into rhythmic patterns.

If they are functioning as percussion their specific pitch isn’t relevant to the music.

What they do for the player is more interesting. It may make a haptic difference which of two gracenote patterns you use despite there being no audible difference between them; one may be easy to play fluidly in the melodic context, the other not. Which is why Highland pipers make extensive use of written notation and cainntaireachd (which is logically equivalent to a written notation) - they have to transmit information that is not perceptible from any audio recording.

@thekerryman; A crann is an Uilleann piping ornament whereby One note is broken in to three smaller note [triplet] with a percussive break between each formed by rapidly tapping[or removing] a series of fingers. Based on bottom D and E
There are also Cranns for Border/Pastoral/Highland pipes based off different notes and finger patterns.

Jack is well informed but id have to add a caveat as a piper myself, that being the individual grace notes ARE pitched and that pitch dictates the effect of the grace, so on the Piob Mhor a High G is the strongest grace and is generally used as ‘1 in a 123 triplet . useing random gracenotes would destroy the rhythmic effect as 1’s 2’ and 3’s get mixed up unless it is deliberate syncopation.

On the UP the situation is similar in that each version of crann actually sounds different and can be identified by ear and so once again its clear that the order of graces makes a big difference within the context of fine detail!
