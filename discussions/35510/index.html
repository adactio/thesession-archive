Re: How long does it take to make the low notes on a low D whistle “sound” properly?

It won’t sound unless you are covering all the holes properly. You’ve probably heard of the pipers grip thing - which means using the flats of your fingers to cover the holes, and angling your bottom hand downwards. You may be very surprised to realize how far you need to stretch the fingers over the holes (and Susatos have big holes as far as I remember). Try using the third joint of your index finger and the second joint of the middle and ring fingers.

Re: How long does it take to make the low notes on a low D whistle “sound” properly?

At least with a Susato you can be pretty sure that any issues with those low notes are the player, not the whistle. I’ve owned Susatos in many keys, including Low D and Low C, and one thing Susato always does very well is have solid powerful “bell notes”.

Yes as was mentioned above Hole 5, the hole from which the note F# emits, is exceptionally large on the various Susato Low Ds I’ve owned and tried, and might take some getting used to, to seal it properly. This is the probable issue, with getting bad Low E and Bottom D.

The “pipers‘ grip” thing is probably essential- it’s rare for somebody to be able to play a Low D by using the fingertips, and their hands will never be as comfortable as the hands of a person using pipers’ grip, especially on the lower hand.

What you do is this: First, note that each finger has three joints, and on the underside of each joint is a fleshy pad. Better results are got using one of these, rather than the thin-skin-over-bone of the joints, for getting a good seal on a large hole.

Second, decide which pad of each finger will be used. Pipers’ grip takes advantage of the fact that the middle-joint pads of the index and middle fingers line up nicely with the end-joint pad of the ring finger, if the hand is held at a slight downward angle.

It might be diagrammed, crudely, like this, with a - indicating each unused pad and a + indicating a pad used to seal a hole, on the lower hand, index finger uppermost, from the player’s perspective, a ‘righthanded’ player, left hand on top, hand to the right of the screen.

Lowerhand pipers’ grip
- + -
- + -
+ - -
- - -
(the little finger isn’t used.)

Note that people speak of ‘straight fingers’ but you don’t want them stiffly straight, but relaxed.

With the upper hand, I, because I have big fairly big hands, can use either normal grip or pipers‘ grip, though on my Low C and some Low Ds the pipers’ grip is much more relaxed.

The two grips could be diagrammed like this, this time with the hand to the left of the screen.

Upperhand pipers’ grip

- + -
- + -
- - +
- - -

Upperhand normal grip

- - +
- - +
- - +
- - -
About your original question, I was a piper before I took up the whistle, and I was able to use good pipers’ grip and seal all the holes on a Low Whistle immediately, but I know that people who don’t have such a background often have great difficulty getting their hands used to these new positions.

Re: How long does it take to make the low notes on a low D whistle “sound” properly?

Piper’s grip is not for everyone - I need to play with the pads of my fingertips to seal properly and more importantly to feel the holes. I sometimes play an #F transition to G pushing my finger across the hole starting with tip then across to second joint as it rises from the hole to the G. My lower joints on my fingers will not seal the holes on my whistles and even though I know it’s the promoted way to play low whistles it is not the only way.

Returning to the original post, if you repeatedly play each lower note and follow each time with a G (right hand on then right hand off) starting with the #F then after 5 mins E then after another 5 mins D you should see improvements. This should work with either form of grip. Additionally lifting your right elbow will give fuller coverage as it alters finger position slightly (pipers use this elbow to pump their bellows so it comes with their piper’s grip).

Breathing for good Low D playing

Grip & hole coverage are indeed crucial aspects of playing, and it is no coincidence that you are finding it most difficult to play E & D, where the most stretch is required.

Also to get a good note, the breathing needs to be strong and constant, at a lower velocity for the lower octave. As for all wind instruments, consciously breathing out steadily out from ‘lower down’ (i.e. the diaphragm) to produce the best quality note. It can take quite a lot of air to keep the Low D operating, particularly at the upper octave, so it helps to be fit and have plenty of lung power. Something I need to work on more myself.

After a bit of time on the Low D, it seems so easy to play the high D whistle, with it needing only a little air and no stretching of the fingers!

I recommend some of the ‘Scottish Islands’ repertoire as great starter pieces for the low D, as they are mainly quite short & easy, and are played at a slow and atmospheric pace:

Mingulay Boat Song
In praise of Islay
Skye Boat Song
Eriskay Love Lilt
The Arran Boat

The first one only uses D, E, F#, A & B and is therefore great for practicing the lower notes.

When you begin to get the hang of the upper octave, have a go at ‘Jesu, Joy if Man’s Desiring’ by Bach. It is a lot more challenging but an excellent steady piece for finger work practice, as there is a lot of jumping about, up as far as high G. It is a wonderful tune on the Low D when played well. I’m still getting there…
