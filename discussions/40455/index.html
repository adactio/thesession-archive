I don’t believe that you have to know the difference between a jig and a slide beforehand and then apply it. But now I’ve had a sleep, I will attempt to explain that while jig is primarily three quavers to the beat, a slide has more swing and is mainly a crotchet and a quaver to each beat with three quavers sometimes mixed in. It is hard to explain without listening to some slides. Have a look at the notes, or have a listen to Mick Dugan’s slide, which has a time signature of 12/8 (a slide) rather than the standard double jig which is 6/8. I still think though, that if you have a good tune in your head, then play it how it feels to you, and then worry about what it is. The time signature will tell you what it is.

Re: Composing: Is this a slide or a jig?

Listen to Johnny O’Leary playing slides too. And also the Star Above the Garter album by Dennis Murphy and Julia Clifford. Both would be on Google Play, I imagine.

Someone above mentioned ‘Humpty Dumpty’. I always use that as an example. It’s not an exclusive template, by any means, but it’s an excellent place to start. ‘Humpty DUMPty sat on a WALL/Humpty DUMPty HAD a great FALL’ is the rhythm, but a fair bit faster than you’d normally sing the nursery rhyme. The capital letters are the emphasis, and generally I hear the emphases as being played much louder and more pronounced in the second line, than in the first. A big big characteristic of slides for me is that it is for me very much a two-statement phrase, with emphases a bit more pronounced second time around and, depending on the tune of course, judiciously differently placed. That’s how I hear them at the moment, anyway - that might change as my listening does! It’s how I hear Johnny O’Leary placing his slide emphases, most of the time.

When I was flicking through the O‘Leary book of tunes, I was delighted to have the ’Humpty Dumpty‘ analogy affirmed when I found ’Off She Goes‘, the jig with the same tune as Humpty Dumpty – which is classified as a jig on this site, but which O’Leary plays as a slide, and which seems to my ears more like a slide anyway. (If you wanted to sing ‘Humpty Dumpty’ exactly like a slide you would probably change the last line from ‘Couldn’t put Humpty together again’ to simply ‘Humpty Dumpty Dummmp-ty’ to get that two-long-notes ending, so characteristic of many slides.)

Some people say slides should be played “like hornpipes”, and I can sort of see what they mean – when you play a slide very fast, there is something of the hornpipe’s ‘jerk’ in the leaning-into and articulation of beat 2 and beat 4 of each bar (if you were counting in four that is – beats two and four would be “DUMP” and "WALL’ in our Humpty Dumpty model).

But I’m a bit chary of stressing the hornpipe similarity because it risks obscuring the ‘danciness’ and daintiness and “tippetty-tappetty” nature of the shorter notes. Slides like the ‘Clare Slide’ (or Micho Russell’s) or ‘the Star of the Garter’ or ‘The Cuil Aodha Slide’ have a sort of airy, lyrical quality, both in the 3-short-note groupings and also in the ‘slideyness’ and space of the long-notes.

A triple time tune can often be either a jig, 6/8, or a slide. 12/8 depending on how it’s played.

Re: Composing: Is this a slide or a jig?

I would say that you’ve taken sufficient liberties there, jeff, to change the slide I posted. Sure you could change almost any tune and get another quite easily, and I doubt slides would ever have evolved without polkas. The well known Ballydesmond Polka that starts EA AB is really named after Maurice Manley, who played it as a slide, as it was originally. Its prevalence as a polka is due to Padraig O’Keeffe’s deliberately changing it to a polka, and giving it to Denis and Julia that way, who then recorded it.

Playing for sets, there have been a few times when a slide has accidentally been polka-fied without anyone noticing until the figure has been started.

My advice for composing a slide would be to listen to more of them, but more importantly, learn to play more of them. I would say slides are some of the trickiest tunes to compose because of how strong their melody has to be. In reels, jigs, and hornpipes, you can almost compose a tune off a formula. Much of it is scales and interval patterns. Good slides and polkas tend to be very melodic and feature somewhat dissonant notes.
Some of the best slides out there are two collected from Danny Ab O‘Keefe (which bear his name). They’re thought to be loosely based off some old forgotten Welsh tunes. Notice how large a range them have. The jig you posted, Kellie, and the slide I posted really tend to stay in the same place. If you look at the slide Jackie Daly plays called ’Where’s the Cat?‘, it’s another diddy tune with words to it. Same with ’The Hair Fell Off Me Coconut‘, ’Chase me Charlie’,.. melody is big in slides.

Hi,

Don’t worry about what technical definition you give a tune just write it and play it ;-)

Also don’t listen to anyone who says composing is something you can only do once you have learnt loads of tunes. Obviously learning tunes will likely impact how you compose. However if you have a knack for invention the compose and compose.

All I would say is that with composing it is very easy to get stuck in a rut of where you are mechanically capable. i.e you end up not really challenging your fingers/breath/arms to do something they are not comfortable with. So make a point of trying stuff that feels hard. It’s good for the muscle memory and you might just stumble across and interesting melody.

Also play along to the radio or other songs you like, they don’t have to be traditional, and you will learn a lot.
