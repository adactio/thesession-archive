How Do You Deal With Music That’s Above Your Level?

What I mean by “above your level” is music that you have very clearly learned, but you can’t seem to bring it to a performable standard. Music that you’ve been so intimate with, that you could probably recite the melody pitch for pitch, and call out all of the chords and their inversions; But when it comes to playing it, it’s just not there? From the tempo, to overall accuracy, to fluidity and clarity, let alone dynamic and artistic expression.

For me, the answer has always been simple. Learn more music of the same level. The more you expose yourself to the challenges in different forms, be present in the discomfort, and work through the technical difficulty, the less of a choice you have to do anything but improve. So what happens when there is no other music? All you have is what’s in front of you. That’s what I’m dealing with now for a classical piece that I’ve been studying for well over a year now. I may be classically trained, but that only means I was taught formal western music theory. It never meant that I was familiar with, or skilled at, actual classical music(I can’t even say that I like classical music, and that’s certainly not from a lack of exploring and trying). The piece in question, Chopin’s Etude Op. 10 No. 5, is unique enough that it’s the only piece of it’s technical ability that I’m even interested in learning. As difficult as it is, I think it is absolutely lovely, and it has some of the most beautiful passages I’ve ever heard in piano. That’s the reason why this is even a conversation. The only other option that I know of is to create my own exercises that use the same skills in different ways(for example, learning the same phrase in all twelve keys…) but that is a discipline that I have not subscribed to.

When it comes to Irish music, I don’t believe there is any music of such a high technical requirement that anyone would be unable to perform it technically after having learned it. Some of the music is so simple, you could play it without having ever practiced it if you’re familiar enough with your instrument, the music, and of course are familiar with the tune ear-wise. For myself with Irish music, I still get to experience music above my level because I learned the music while I was learning new instruments. But alas, there are so many tunes, that no matter what technical requirement a tune called for, there is undoubtedly many many more tunes that have the same requirement that you could learn with it, and supplement the skillset with. So this issue I’ve had with the Etude is a peculiar one.

Has anyone else dealt with this, or something similar? For me, I resolved to create my own arrangement of the piece, in a different key, that focused on all of it’s main themes and motifs. It took me over 36 hours to put the arrangement together, but it was a fun project and I’m proud of what I was able to come up with. Just started practicing it last night and it’s already boosted me off the plateau I’ve been on for quite some time now.

On a different note: I’ve been taking piano lessons for arranging Irish tunes from one of the members here, so lately I’ve been having a lot of fun finally getting to create original arrangements of fully self-accompanied tunes for piano. This endeavor is what motivated me and gave me the confidence to take up the challenge of arranging a classical piece, of all things. We’ve only gotten to do one lesson so far, but their guidance and advice has truly made a difference in my efforts. So, I just wanted to give them a shoutout to show my appreciation.

Re: How Do You Deal With Music That’s Above Your Level?

I’ve played other genres that had more than their share of challenges, but even with Irish trad tunes, I often run into music that’s “above my level.” (No doubt that says a lot more about my gimpy abilities than whether the music is easy or difficult.) Maybe it’s just a phrase or two out of an otherwise playable tune, but sometimes it’s most of the tune. If I really want to learn the tune, I channel that desire into many hours of woodshedding. But instead of just grinding through the tough bits, my strategy usually goes like this:

First, I figure out exactly what the trouble is. Is it my ear or conceptual? In other words, do I accurately understand the music? Or is it something that begs for a technical skill I’m lacking? Or is it one of my own physical/neurological limitations (hence my user name) getting in the way. Regardless, I identify the specific source and nature of the challenge *before* I put in the hours to overcome it.

Depending on the nature of the problem, I might start out by precisely transcribing the piece from some excellent, inspiring aural source. I usually do two transcriptions: a bare bones melody line, and then a fully annotated one, marking every articulation and nuance. This step is less about having the dots or abcs in front of me and more about forcing myself to listen to every note and the pulse and how it’s produced. Listening that closely often reveals new challenges I didn’t hear before. But sometimes it clears up problems that I had misheard before. Either way, it helps plant the music in my head. It’s part of what I consider woodshedding.

Then I’ll sit down and play the whole tune, noticing every spot where my playing isn’t giving me the results—the sound—I want. If it seems to be an issue of technique, mechanics, or ergonomics, then I’ll pause to take a few deep breaths, release any tension, and remind myself that my goal is expressiveness, not perfection.

The next step is where the heavier work comes in, but to me it feels like genuine, enjoyable play. Fully relaxed, I’ll play the tricky part with my default abilities, and then I’ll try every way possible to play the phrase, a process of trial and error, but keeping track of what feels “natural” and what feels “awkward.” I don’t label these as “good” and “bad.” I just notice how they feel. Sometimes it’s the awkward approach that gets closest to the sound I’m after. And I remind myself: the only difference between stumbling blocks and stepping stones is in how you use them.

I do this slowly, attentive to all the details, working on one element at a time so I’m not juggling too many variables at once. But I’ll play through all the elements and variables I can imagine, trying anything that comes to mind, no matter how off the wall it seems. Some of the most counterintuitive approaches lead to the greatest leaps forward.

Usually, I end up with a short list of options, some that feel “easier” even though they might still need a little work, and some that feel hard or clumsy or unlikely to ever work. Keep an open mind—the more you experiment, the more you find links between seemingly unrelated ideas and skills. And the more associations you can weave, the more likely you’ll find a way through. I’ll keep playing *all* of the short list, focusing less on how smooth or awkward they feel and more on “does this get me closer to the sound I want?” Over many years and plugging through tons of music (Irish, but also bluegrass, Appalachian, rock, blues, etc.), it’s the awkward stuff that carries your music to a higher level when it finally feels natural. It’s also a great way to really immerse yourself in a piece of music.

Of course, it helps to be patient. I like setting goals when learning something new, but time—how long it takes—doesn’t matter. Progress comes easier when I don’t set a deadline or worry about how long a concept or skill takes to become natural and ingrained.

Transposing the piece into different keys is often a good way to change up the mechanics—finger placement, or moving to a different string or button or hole—to better understand whether the tune itself is tricky or that it’s how it lays on your chosen instrument that’s at the root of your struggles.

Throughout this process, sometimes I stumble on elegant workarounds that I prefer over the original. But I’ll keep working on the original phrase too. When I play, I want to have options, not constraints.

At some point, you find what works. Then starts the longer process of getting it to work consistently. It may take days or weeks to finally get, say, a roll to sound “right” just once. Then it takes years before they always sound right. That’s okay.

AB, the story about the Etude’s(there’s 2 series of them) is that they all explore a specific musical technique for piano; The ultimate technique being legato playing. Chopin took the musical form of Etude, which is supposed to be focused on technique rather than artistic expression, and turned it into artistic expression. And that’s how people like me, who are not at all interested in music for the sake of technical difficulty, end up being compelled to learn something that’s technically focused. The brilliance of Chopin can not be overstated.

Also, your idea wasn’t stupid. There’s no need to self-deprecate. I think it’s an interesting idea, and it sounds like pretty much how I started off. The only difference is that I changed keys, and I used a simpler classical piece instead of an Irish tune.

When dealing with music above your level, the first step is to find out what exactly is wrong, which is usually a number of things when it gets to that point. When the situation is that bad, I believe it comes down to physical limitations that have to be pushed past. The best way to push past those limitations is to be forced to do the same thing but in a different way, so that instead of using a subset of your capacity, you’re forced to use all of your capacity. When changing keys, not only does it change your fingering, but it changes the dimension that you perceive the piece through, forcing your brain to do a lot of work in an effort to acquire and normalize what you’re doing. That’s why I always recommend for tune playing, if you’re ever struggling with a piece after having learned it, learn it in a different key and watch the issues dissolve away before your eyes.

Re: How Do You Deal With Music That’s Above Your Level?

Jerone, it sounds like we share a similar approach, so I didn’t type anything you don’t already know. I had a hunch that would be the case. But I spelled it out in some detail with the hope that it might help anyone grappling with a tune that’s over their heads. We all experience this.

Trad tunes are etudes, too. They teach techniques that then show up in other tunes. But progress isn’t just about picking up a new technique and stuffing it in your bag of tricks. Real progress is as much or more about the bigger, overarching principles and various approaches *to how (and why) we learn.* Those are the ideas and ways of being musical that permeate *every* tune we play, every tune we carry in our head.

If you set out to learn a tune as a sequence of notes and techniques, that’s probably what you’ll end up with. On the other hand, if you set out to learn a tune to better understand the emotions it carries and the story it tells, then those qualities will come out in your playing, even if your technical chops are still a work in progress. And you’ll have all the motivation you need to keep honing those chops, in service to those emotions and stories.

Re: How Do You Deal With Music That’s Above Your Level?

“And if none of that works, you can resign yourself that they would always sing it wrong. Make it part of the interpretation.” Yes, the old advice that if you make a mistake, repeat it and everyone will think you meant to do it all along and it’s a genius variation.

That might work for solo music but not so well in a session.

In Irish trad, it’s acceptable—welcome, even—to play variations, ideally that blend with what others are playing. But playing something that clashes, and doing that every time, will likely leave you standing in the rain outside the pub while everyone else gets on with the tunes and craic.

Any habit is breakable. The key is to replace it with something else. In the example of the errant singer, that would entail identifying in specific, excruciating detail, what they’re doing wrong and then replacing it with the thing that’s correct. E.g., if a note is sung or played habitually flat, provide the correct pitch and ask the student to match that pitch. Repeat as much as it takes to get it right, really paying attention to the pitch and sound quality and how it physically feels to produce it. Then insert it in the problematic phrase for more woodshedding.

In music, bad habits usually come from not focusing our attention specifically enough on whatever’s off. Sometimes this happens because we’re simply unaware that anything’s off. Other times, it’s more a matter of allowing ourselves to be off. If you want to fix a bad musical habit, pay deep attention to what’s “off” (and ask for help of you’re having a hard time hearing it) and where it needs to go to be “on,” and quit giving yourself permission to settle for less than spot on.

Or take up basket weaving. ;-)

Bad habits may hold us back when attempting music that’s “above our level.” Just as likely, though, challenging music asks us to do things that are simply unfamiliar—we’ve never done them before. Spend some time with those things, get to know them, internalize them, and the music’s no longer so challenging.

Canon in Opus 10 Number 5

NylonFlute, I decided to go ahead and upload the rearrangement to Musescore, since I used their app to make the arrangement. You should be able to listen to it at this link. If you have any trouble, just let me know.

www.musescore.com/user/7247256/scores/7633358

To give you an idea of how I came up with this, the rearrangement of the Etude is pretty much a mash-up with Pachelbel’s “Canon in D”. I had reached a point when I began obsessing over the Canon again(happens every few years, must be some kind of medical condition), and I wanted to do more with it. Give it a different personality, especially since it’s one of the most overplayed “tunes” of all time. So this issue I was having with the Etude gave me a potential outlet to expand the Canon on.

But I wanted it to be an intelligent mash-up, and that’s where the 36+ hours come into play. Now given, there was a lot of copying and pasting. It would’ve defeated the purpose of the project if I didn’t use the exact themes and motifs from the Etude. So instead of a simplified Canon bass line, I used the chord patterns from the Etude over the Canon progression. This was also to mitigate some of the redundancy that would inevitably come from playing the repeated melody phrases of the Etude in the treble clef.

It starts off by introducing the Canon progression on double-time to set up the ear for the rhythmic difference, and then immediately goes into that initial main theme of the Etude adjusted to the Canon’s chord progression. From there, it goes into my favorite themes and motifs from the Etude, mostly in AB patterns, over the next 6 parts. Some of the melodic phrases were on the tonic, which allowed me to use them repeatedly with no changes. Other melodic phrases had really complex harmonic relationships with the bass line, so I had to either sync them with the chord progression or create a variation that would fit with the chord progression. To my delight, this actually ended up creating a handful of new melodies, and in some parts, some really neat cadences that helped with the flow of the piece. It ends on the same dramatic theme as the Etude, the last two measures being a note for note transposition, which I feel fits perfectly for the arrangement while acknowledging the original piece in a way that the central parts don’t. All in all, I think it comes across as a “background theme”, it lacking any distinct melodic character and being structurally simple. Though, that’s kind of the intention behind the sound that I wanted for the Canon. And it still sounds like it’s going to be a lot of fun to play when i’m done practicing it.

Practice the tune at a reduced speed, focusing alternatively on playing the tune very legato or very statcato to get a feel for the flow of the rhythm, and alternatively on forte and piano to get a feel for the expression. Also look for the swung phrases within the tune and how they fit into the rhythm. Also, find places where notes can be held, omitting other notes that impede the rhythm, until your confidence increases and you find the space and the findering to play more complex phrases. As you are playing the tune slowly, think about emotional content.

@cancion
I find your choice of Bach on guitar to be an interesting example of challenge and approach by practice. This was incidentally one of few pieces that I once learned to play in totality, sometime in the distant past.
The guitar is particularly challenging, as there can (when considering permutations of choice of left-hand finger, string, and right-hand picking finger) be literally dozens of ways of playing just a single note, which are only compounded when considering transitions of position in moving smoothly between phrases.
On revisiting the piece again now, I have to work out how to approach it completely from scratch, as my memory fails to recall a final choice from the many possibilities attempted and practiced at particular times in the past.
Some observations of my own attempt to revisit it now :-
- Start at the beginning!
- Work through the piece, 4 notes at a time
- Consider the precise duration/sustain required of each note
- Minimise left-hand effort; fret/hold a given note only when and for as long as required
- Explore higher neck positions utilising open strings
- Explore the transitions between successive 4 note phrases, again with particular focus on minimising effort - this may reveal further possibilities for playing a phrase
- Iteratively extend and revise the above to create longer phrases with smooth transitions
- Practice, practice, practice

To my mind, the real goal is to erase a limitation so you’re free to express the music’s beauty and emotions.

It’s a genuine joy when a once-difficult skill becomes so ingrained and intuitive that it just happens subconsciously when the music asks for it. Isn’t that what happens with cuts, rolls, triplets, and the rest? You come out of the flow afterwards and think, “Wow, that sounded almost as good as it does in my head!” ;-)

Re: How Do You Deal With Music That’s Above Your Level?

I’m surprised no-one has mentioned lessons.

My usual approach would be much like everyone else’s I think. Slow down. Take difficult phrases or passages apart. Try out different fingering or bowling options and see what works. Play through the melody line without the extra chord notes. Then play through the chords. Then combine them. That kind of thing.

But sometimes it’s just really complicated and you need the advice of someone who has already figured it out. Online masterclasses on YouTube or similar can help. Or just find a good teacher who knows that repertoire.

One thing you can do as a non-classical musician who doesn’t have to stick religiously to the composers intention is to just rearrange the piece of course.
