If you can get a tune, any tune, out of a whistle after only 10 days, you are doing very well.

You should probably stick with whistle full-time for now, to keep building on your progress. Trying to learn additional instruments at this stage is not helpful.

Re: one of those newbies :-)

“Mary Had a Little Lamb” is a great first start, especially if you have the tune already in your head, can sing or lilt it. Those should be your first explorations of the instrument, what’s already in your head. Have fun and if at any point you feel any tension or frustration, take a break. It is better to laugh through the experience, which is often easier with age, than to fight it and learn the bad habits of tension and a poor sense of humour. ;-) “Mary Had a Little Lamb” can take you 4 ways ~

1.) BAG = the top three fingers, usually the left hand
2.) FED = the bottom three fingers, usually the right hand
3.) fed = as above but the second / high octave
4.) bag = as above but the second / high octave

There is also your first introduction to ABC notation ~ BAGFED / bagfed ~ starting with all fingers down you get D, the lowest note. Make sure your hands and fingers are relaxed, not curved like a claw, shake hour hands out and let them relax, that’s how the hold should be, not the finger tips but the fatty pads of the fingers, where the whorl of your fingerprint is, approximately, wherever you can fully close the hole and still be have that relaxed curve and hold. It is best to take the learning of the basic scale by playing it descending, from the top down, starting with your index finger over the top hole and blowing, then lowering one finger at a time, and here’s the ABCs again ~

B ~ A ~ G ~ F ~ E ~ D

And if an octave higher ~

b ~ a ~ g ~ f ~ e ~ d

If you can shake any worry about vocal quality, it helps to learn a tune that way first, to be able to sing or hum or lilt the tune, get it in your head first, become familiar with it, take it in, like “Mary Had a Little Lamb”. It makes a great difference if you get it inside first before starting to learn it on an instrument. Hum to a recording. Dance to it around the house. Then learn it a bit at a time, and don’t go faster than you are able to play it at a steady rhythm. Take things slow and don’t rush it.

Sorry, now I’m away… 8-)

Re: one of those newbies :-)

I suggest you practice a D scale, using cuts, then using pats, then rolls. Also Grace notes. So;
D ‘cut E’ D etc
pats; start E ‘pat D’ E. etc.
grace notes start with the note above or ‘a’ note above so; small E toD, small Fto E etc,
Rolls; E---- FEDE----, F-------GFEF------- etc

great description from C here;https://thesession.org/discussions/16504

With ornamenting, take your time, learn the tunes. Practice the ornaments separately, they will come naturally in their own time. A tune played cleanly with no ornaments is fine, a tune played badly with ornaments is not. Remember Rhythm is paramount.

Also try not to bring your fingers off too far. The closer you are-within reason- the shorter the time taken to cover the distance.

To stress C’s advice, relaxed fingers not straight but gently curved. Closer to straight than curved if you see what I mean.

Also….. posture open chest, relax shoulders, balanced , centred.
Try taking a deep breath and controlling the outflow, see how long you can blow one note. Use as little breath as you need, work out your phrasing with your breathing.

Listen to lots of ITM! I recomend ‘the Clare set’, and the pipering and whistling of Willie Clancy.

Vetgirl - it’s been said elsewhere before, by better musicians than me… but I can’t help repeating it:

Ditch the sheet music. I know some people are able to learn tunes and play them in an authentic style - even from ‘the dots’, but I really do think that if you want to understand the rhythms of the music - sharpen your ear and use mimicry.

I used to try to learn from dots - but I only really started to improve and have my playing sound anything NOT like a string of dots when I listened hard to my favourite recordings and tried to playlike them. Now my own variations come in and I think of interesting little decorations myself… because I’m freed from the strictures of the dots.

There is no one way to play these tunes… which is what the dots can make you believe. They’re like a road map (that someone decided upon somewhere, somehow) but we all have powerful SUVs that are intended to go offroad and discover new terrain….

Anyway - you’ve only been playing for 10 days - so no need to think you must solve all the little dilemmas immediately. If you are like any of us here… you’ve just walked into a lifelong obsession!!

Hi vetgirl3493,

There are far more qualified “real” (tm) musicians than I who have already replied to this thread, but in response to your question:

“…how can you teach good rhythm without explaining about note values and time signatures?”

Stick with it, and enjoy this wonderful journey (a.k.a. labor of love) that you’ve set out on, and in time, the answer to this question will simply come naturally to you.

One point that no one has mentioned yet is the time-worn cliche of “The three most important things you must do are: 1) listen, 2) listen, 3) listen”… to the music, that is.

It has been mentioned in past threads that after you have listened to Irish music enough, the melodies -- and their timings, of course -- will be in your head, and you’ll be able to sing, whistle, lilt them to yourself as you are walking, driving, hanging laundry, whatever. When you reach the point of a melody being so stuck in your head that you know it by heart before ever trying to play it, it will be time for you to begin working out the tune on your whistle. You, your husband, brother-in-law, and friend will then naturally know the answer to, “…how can you teach good rhythm without explaining about note values and time signatures?”

(There are even those who would maintain that knowledge of sheet-music-style note values and time -- and key -- signatures would be an impediment to learning Irish music, but I won’t light that fuse! ;-) )

Having said all that, I can read sheet music, and occasionally refer to it, but get a much better sense of satisfaction (and have much better retention) if I grind out a tune the hard way, as you describe, wearing out the pause & rewind buttons of my tape player. :-)

Best of luck to you, and please keep us posted on your progress!

“…how can you teach good rhythm without explaining about note values and time signatures?”

I knew I’d forgotten something in the rush… Make it physical! Even if it is just the walk of, in your realm, a New England contra or an old time square / quadrille, or just walking around ~ get physical with the music. It is ‘dance music’ on the main, and the rhythm and phrasing are there to assist the dancer. The average is 32 bars, 64 steps if you’re walking it, both for the music and the dance, and that is broken up in the largest chunks as AABB, so is the dance. Further, the usual basic structures are the same way to learn the tune ~ 1 bar ~ 2 bars ~ 4 bars ~ 8 bars ~ etc… The dance fits that too, from short 2 and 4 bar phrases. Something shared all over is the simple hand turn (allemande left) of four walking steps, 2 bars, to a 4-hand wheel or star ~ R-hands in and circle for 8-steps, turn to put L-hands in and circle back for 8 steps. MAKE IT PHYSICAL! It’s a kick, you could even dance a bit around the house ~ do-sa-do (back to back) ~ face your partner, pass right shoulders and pass back to back to your right (4 steps/2 bars) then back up to return to place passing left shoulders (4steps/2bars) ~ etc… And if you’re laughing and stumbling about, that’s a good sign… ;-)
