The guitarist doesn’t need to ‘capo-up’ -- he can just play in G.
As for ‘re-learning notes’, the usual practice is to select a whistle according to the key you want and ‘think in D regardless’ (meaning to play as thought it were a D whistle, not as though the tune is in D).

Re: RE: Guitar Capo & Non-D Whistles

Sebastian is correct.

2 musicians: 1 whistle player with a Low G Whistle & 1 guitarist with a capo reading from the same sheet music. With the aid of a capo, the guitarist and Low G whistle player can play in the same key and read from the same sheet music…both imagining themselves to be playing in the key of D, for example. If the capo is on the appropriate fret there’d be no need for the guitarist to transpose chords “as the tune progresses.” In essence the capo has already done the transposition for you.

If (instead of using a capo) the guitarist transposes chords “as the tune progresses” (dependent on the pitch of whistle chosen), then the guitarist will more likely to encounter difficulties. Some keys and chords - especially ones that require bars - are just bad for the guitar because they don’t easily allow “twiddly bits” (ornamentation), or don’t made use of many strings (as full, open chords - like Gmaj - do).

Bbmaj might be just the right key / pitch for a whistle playing Kwela music, but it’s a damn awkward to play that chord barring the first fret with your left-hand index finger. ’Much better to capo to the first fret and position your fingers as though you were playing an open Amaj chord, then you could most easily strum with sweet sustain, embellishing as you go!

For the musical novice, one of the MANY advantages whistles, fiddles, concertinas, and mandolins have over many other instruments is the shear VOLUME of FREE sheet music and videos on the web. With the aid of a capo, a guitarist and a whistle player can play from the same page, in the same key - even if it is not the “true” or “actual” key for either of them…Does that make sense? :^)

Re: RE: Guitar Capo & Non-D Whistles

If it was a Bflat whistle, I would say a capo would be required, but a G whistle plays tunes in keys that are pretty accessible to most session accompanists without a capo being needed.
G is 2.5 steps above the key of D. Each capo represents a half step up. So if you switch to the G whistle, and want to play from the same music you were reading on the D whistle, the guitarist could capo on the fifth fret, and also read from the same music. Although since the G whistle is lower, and the capo moves the guitar higher, you will be an octave apart from where you were before.
But that being said, for this music, generally the sooner you ditch the written music, the more fun you will have!

Re: RE: Guitar Capo & Non-D Whistles

The answer to question 1) is for the guitar player to capo up to the fifth fret. The chords D,G,and A played with a capo on the fifth fret would in fact be G,C, and D respectively, thus matching your low whistle tuning. The other option would be for the guitarist to transpose down a fifth(seven semitones). Personally if I was the guitarist backing you I would choose not to use a capo but transpose instead, purely for tonal reasons. Transposition is not such a difficult exercise.

For example, you say that playing a Bflat Major chord by barring at the first fret is difficult. But it’s only difficult till you learn to do it (not hard at all really), and you could always play that same chord in other places, such as, by playing the standard F chord pattern up on the sixth fret (top 4 strings) or a full barred E position at the sixth fret. The advantage of this is (a) you an easily transpose keys by learning the same standard patterns, and (b) by learning at least a couple of different voices for the same chord you can add a bit of flavour to the tune, For example, the music may well require 4 beats of a D major, but you can always play in two different ways if it adds to the music. From what I understand of “Western Classical” Music - in its early days – it was neither.

Re: Celtic Music enthusiasts

“Newcomers remind oldcomers of how and why they got to be who they presently are.” & vice versa

Celtic music:

Fanciful term which expresses a world-view or record-shelf category rather than actual links between music genres.

1. Indicates ‘Irish’ or ‘Scottish’ musics, but is increasingly used in Britain and the US to denote ‘Irish’, this suggesting discomfort with ‘Irishness’. In Europe it may denote Breton or Galician music in addition to Irish, Scottish and Welsh. The music of Brittany is different to Irish music, but is within the playing and listening experience of many Irish traditional musicians. Isle of Man, England and Wales are connected cultures, but Scotland has particularly strong linguistic and music links with Ireland, as has the Scots-Irish diaspora in Canada (Cape Breton, Newfoundland, etc.)

2. More superficially the term ‘Celtic’ has come to apply to an easy-listening, ‘mood’ music with dreamy, non-specific but Irish/Scots flavour, marketed as ‘relaxing’, ‘evocative’, etc. Such albums are legion, and enjoy a large sale in the US where the Narada company produces many compilation and re-licensed collection —including the playing of such as Máire Ní Chathasaigh, John Whelan and Joanie Madden—while the Mercury label’s ‘Secret Garden’ features Davy Spillane. Traditional players sometimes use the term also, probably to appeal to the pre-formed audience. (Seán O‘Driscoll’s solo album is titled Celtic Music, Shanachie’s 90 per cent Irish song collection is Celtic Love Songs, Green Linnet’s, with similar composition, is Celtic Women in Music and Song), but few players would describe themselves as playing anything other than ’traditional‘ or ’Irish’ music.

Fintan Vallely, ed. The Companion to Irish Traditional Music pp. 64-65

To Jim & Gobby: Thanks for your encouragement. I’m sure I’m no brighter or dimmer than just about anyone else carving words on this wall.

To Na éisc: If you plug the title of a composition into a search engine and add either “pdf” or “abc notation” at the end, then you’ll often come up with what your looking for. “Jimmy Ward’s Jig abc notation” or “The Yellow Wattle abc notation” will take you right to the musical doorstep. The front porch light is on.

With abc notation you’ll need a converter such as http://www.concertina.net/tunes_convert.html (ABC Convert-A-Matic) or http://mandolintab.net/abcconverter.php (Mandolin Tab Converter) to turn the tune into a PDF doc. Before the final conversion of a tune into a PDF doc you may need transpose the tune into a key that your chosen instrument can better handle; this is the great advantage of abc notation. Though there are likely other transposers (Recommendations anyone?), this one has served me well: http://www.8ung.at/abctransposer/ (Jens Wollschläger’s ABC-Transposer). It does a great job with tunes, but it struggles to recognize lyrics and chords (especially minor ones) so you may have to play around with the abc notation itself. It’s sometimes a painstaking process, but the good news is that you’ll be learning a bit about how to read & write abc notation as you go! That’s very perpetuating stuff to The Session. Many have mastered the art & science of writing in abc notation; I’m still working on it. :-)

As one tune can often be played on various instruments I’ve sometimes found my way to a composition by tacking any one of a number of words to the end of a title, for example, concertina, fiddle, mandolin, or even recorder, or flute. As a novice I like to see the fingerings others suggest, so I sometimes attach “tin whistle tab” to the end of a tunes title. That said, sooner-than-later you may no longer feel a need to read from tablature (though tablature is a form of notation that’s been around for centuries and certainly has its uses).

I hope this helps!
