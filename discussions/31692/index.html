Re: About BC Melodeons

BC boxes can play in any key including D and G, there are two rows of buttons, one having no sharps or flats ,(the C row and the other having 5 sharps (the B row,) the sharps can also be perceived as their enharmonic equivalents in the flat keys, thus the last sharp On the B row (A) can be used as a B flat etc.

The BC style is generally regarded as being smoother than the DG as there is less bellows work to be done to get the notes, the DG style of playing is generally perceived as being more punchy than the more flowing B/C style.
It is a bit of a misperception that BC is the box of choice in Ireland, you will find that there are plenty of DG players as well.

The standard left hand systems on b/c boxes are are bit limited for the keys we use, some b/c players get their basses altered to ‘Irish basses’.

Re: About BC Melodeons

Sure, there are boxes in C#/D (the system that I was told to learn), and C/C# (a semitone above B/C), and D/D# (a semitone above C#/D). You won’t be using the basses that much, regardless of system. If you want to play Irish tunes in a somewhat “Irish style”, choose B/C or C#/D for the normal keys - which are more than a D/G box can handle. D and G might be the most common keys, and as long as a D/G box has notes like G#, F, Bb, Eb you might survive. If not, you’ll need another box to be able to play quite common tunes.

Re: About BC Melodeons

«BC boxes can play in any key including D and G» Yes a B/C _can_ play in any key, but it’s my guess that only people who don’t actually play a B/C will tell you that. In practice venturing outside more than a fairly small handful of keys is challenging and very few players bother. The same applies of course to C#/D, which is what I play.

However this small handful of keys is a bit larger than what you can do on a D/G box, as Jeff has mentioned. To my mind the great beauty of the “semitone apart” systems is that they give you every accidental, placed just where they are handy (right next to the uninflected note).

One of the main reasons that B/C became so popular is that it allowed box players to produce something they could pass off as a roll (as played on fiddles, pipes, etc.). This cannot be done on a D/G. The fact that this “roll” sounded pretty ghastly didn’t deter anybody from larding every tune full of them. Fortunately they have gone out of fashion somewhat, and people who still play them seem to have got a lot more subtle at executing them.

Re: About BC Melodeons

No Dick, the rolls that Paddy O’Brien started doing cannot be done on a D/G. They are all played using any note from the inner row as the main note and a note from the outer row - a semitone below the main note - as the final grace. These are what Joe Burke and many other players called “fiddle rolls” and are really a major part of the B/Cs attraction back in the early 1950s.

I’m not saying the B/C is better than D/G. I’m not saying that these rolls are desirable, let alone essential, and of course you can do very suitable ornaments of different kinds on a D/G. What I am saying is that on a D/G you do NOT have the option of a semitone-lower grace on all the notes of your main row. As you said, examine the fingerboard.

PS The D/G was only “invented” in the 1950s for the English market, so it’s not as if it was an option in Ireland at that time. Again this is in reference to why the B/C is the main box for Irish music, as the OP asked.

Re: About BC Melodeons

The BC box has a number of phrases that you can play as ‘triangles,’ with the bellows moving in the same direction, such as EFG and BCD, phrases which would be played with bellows pumping back and forth on a C#D. It is like a piano in being fully chromatic, which came in handy recently when I learned Winnie Horan’s Daisies in December, which has Fs, F#s, C nat, C#, even Bflats. And it has a lot of double stops available, many served by having a B or E on both push and pull. And that pull E makes it surprisingly easy to play A major tunes on.
(Oh and speaking of Daisies in December, there are lots of lovely chord triads available on it that go well with D minor, like Dmin, Cmaj and Bflat.

Re: About BC Melodeons

To Mark - the OP -
Your question is a logical question that many people interested in moving over to the Irish side ask.

There are some great D/G players in ITM - I’m sure they can disprove my observations - I am just basing my opinion based on my experience as a B/C player and from what I have observed.

The reason I went with BC over D/G initially was because of the high percentage of players who play BC or C#/D. Most recording artists are either BC or C#/D. The fact that the majority of players in ITM are on a semitone box indicated to me that there must be something about the semitone box that lends itself to the type of playing required in ITM that the D/G can not do.

I am not a D/G player, but from what I can ascertain from the D/G system, playing on one row would involve a lot of rapid bellows changes in/out/in/out ect - what has been referred to as chucking the box - this does not lend to smooth fast playing -

The semitone systems afford more smoothness in playing because of reduced amount bellows changes required - due to the layout and pattern of the notes - longer series of notes in one direction, as opposed to in/out/in/out.

The semitone systems are no harder to learn than the D/G system - it’s just a matter of knowing when to drop down to the 2nd row for the accidental - not difficult at all. Crossing rows is what lends to the smoothness and speed of the semitone boxes.

All this being said, there are some great D/G players in ITM - I’m sure they can disprove everything I have said - I am just basing my opinion based on my experience as a B/C player and from what I have observed.

Re: About BC Melodeons

all this stuff about smooth sequences on B/C boxes makes me reflect. Whilst it’s true that there are parts of the fiddle key scales that can be played with consecutive notes on the press or on the draw on B/C, the fact is that there are also big parts of fiddle key scales that involve a lot of bellows and row changes.

unsurprisingly, the composers of Irish tunes were ignorant of where in the scale the box works smoothest when they wrote the tunes so most tunes involve the use of both “smooth” and “jerky” parts of the scale.

you need to overcome the natural dynamics of the instrument to play dance music properly on it. To sustain the rhythm within any sequence of notes there needs to be a difference of accentuation. All notes must be played just as the rhythm of the piece demands, and the player must be able to place the correct stress on any particular note as required, irrespective of what precedes or follows it.

as a matter of interest A major isn’t my favourite scale on B/C because it offers fewer opportunities than any other scale to use bellows direction change to help place stresses on a note. This means using other techniques that are more cumbersome and difficult, just to get bit of lift into A major tunes. Being mostly on the draw, it also means that there’s more work for the air key to do.

that’s my take on it anyway, so far

I think part of the problem with A is the unfamiliarity of playing the box with the bellows extended to any significant degree. In A the bass side is forever creeping away to the left. Playing in other common keys I rarely open the bellows more than a few inches. This probably helps keep the treble side stable. It always seems a bit harder to hit the right button in A - perhaps because the keyboard’s bouncing around more than usual.

I use every trick I can to conserve bellows capacity when playing in Amaj, using press B and E as much as possible.

The most breathless passages in my modest repetoire are in the 3rd part of Athol Highlanders. We play it in A at full tilt as the last tune in our Cumberland Square 8 set. The speed we play the tune at militates against the use of press B and E. There are odd press F#’s, but they’re swift passing notes and not long enough to make much difference. I have to open the air key briefly to fully close the bellows at the end of every bar in that part of the tune. If I don’t, I run out of bellows extension well before we get to part four.

BTW I never play basses because (a) it’s hard (b) I’m too lazy to learn how to do it properly and (c) I like to get lots of air through the treble reeds. I can’t imagine how I could play the tune with basses, and still be heard.

@Al et al, I think it’s daft the way people talk to potential Irish-box players about changes in bellows direction as though they were some major roadblock, and that any opportunity not to change bellows direction is somehow a gift from God. Bellows changes are really the soul of the instrument, be it in B/C, C#/D, D/G, or a one-row. Just as the bow is the soul of the fiddle. Imagine music all played in a single neverending bowstroke. Imagine life without bellows changes: yes, it would be like that Far Side cartoon about “welcome to hell, here’s your [piano] accordion.” Bellows changes are your friend. Embrace them, perfect them.
