Where on earth would you get the idea that we were somehow supposed to learn ALL the tunes? I also don’t think it’s a bad thing to know who composed a tune, it helps flesh out the story of the tune.

If you want to treat the traditional music as a historical piece that should be preserved, then I personally think you’re looking at the tradition wrong, but by all means, find yourself a session that only plays tunes from O’Neill’s in the same sets every week, and leave the modern stuff to the rest of us that have vibrant, thriving sessions, and not dusty old preservation society meetings every week!

In my mind, the tradition is more about sharing love of the music, playing it with other people, and having it enrich our lives (which might otherwise be filled with nothing but conflict, politics, poverty, and other such stresses). Part of that is (and has always been) the composition of new tunes! The good ones stick, but that doesn’t mean that the rest of the tunes are worthless. At the very least, they were worth it for the composer, who expressed themselves through music, whether it be just for sharing with their friends, or trying to become the next Paddy Fahey. Why would you want to censor that?

My sessions tend to feature at least a few tunes that were composed by members of the session, including a few of my own, and it adds another layer of camaraderie between the folks there! And we play quite a few modern tunes from the current crop of great composers, McGoldrick, Carroll, Farrell, Finnegan, etc. If Reavy, O’Brien, Wynne, Fahey, et al were the Rat Pack, then these folks would be the Brat Pack, and I’m all for it!

And why would it matter if the new tunes we love to play live on in the tradition for fifteen years or more? What about right now, and our love of playing them?

So embrace the amazing times we live in with an abundance of great tunes! ;-)

I love this thread. When creativity levels explode, something has happened, environments where tradition was secure have their integrity fractured, people who play the music themselves explore all the options, and currently it seems ITM is in the crosshairs of wider environmental FATES, clawing at the doors of session pubs and ………..

There are generally two types of creative work: 1) the quite original in most aspects, and 2) something of a remix or application of “variation” on music already known.

Things that can cause a musical creative explosion: 1) rapid and profound cultural change, 2) sudden and widely adopted technological change related to creating, playing or listening to music (like amps, microphones and new instruments or major instrument evolution. The invention of the electric guitar radically changed music. Music creation software is now inexpensive, widely adopted and millions are using it, so the imagination has been set free and supported in full at every step.). 3) widely adopted new instruments or an improvement in instrument quality, such as what’s going on with whistles over the last 15 years. Concertina tech too? Are bodhrans far more common and consistent quality than 20 years ago? If you get some of these factors in play, having major impacts, then the next ingredient shows up: 4) HIGH TALENT PLAYERS who become pro and record, or stay amateur and yet still have high impact. That serves as more exposure and training for more players and creators. Celtic and ITM is exploding in popularity. It’s 40+ years past the time when The Clancy’s and Fairport and The Chieftains were the only Celtic/ITM that areas outside Ireland/England witnessed. For every Mary Bergin and Matt Molloy who is recording, teaching and touring, there are also the 1000’s of amateurs serving as local hosts, coaches, instructors and session players who build and maintain the backbone of it all.

Players who are also the great innovator creators, like the makers of Killarney, Kerry Whistles (Buskers, etc.), Alba, Nick Metcalf, Goldie, Tony Dixon, Howard, Humphrey, etc. And as this wave of improvement happens, also arising are the total phony market frauds supplying poor quality instruments for low prices that scam you out of money; and yet music fan websites serve to keep people warned of those too! Think of how many hundreds of thousands of whistle buyers have been greatly helped by CutiePie, Nathaniel Dowell, WhistleTutor and others.

Maybe ITM is reaching it’s phase of tradition being a launching pad for all kinds of things. It’s own version of jazz will show up? Everything stretching all possibilities! ITM improv, finding its own style and practitioners? ITM Fusion, probably well developed in some areas long ago (The Chieftains picked up on that long ago), yet that’s always an area wide open for opportunity.

If, as various comments have indicated, you get to witness some foreseeable aspects of creativity that aren’t so desirable, such as 1) shallow academic technicians masquerading as snobby authoritative peacocks and relying primarily on “variation” of standard music tools to vary old material, or 2) session players showing up to join in yet finding far too much music a total surprise so that few can actually play along well at the time, or 3) people who are quite new to creativity and bringing in every scrap of new music they write, to play for everyone, taking up lots of time doing so, yet the quality of the music is generally poor to mediocre, when they should have learned REFINEMENT and CONTEXT, and brought in only the better 25% of their music. ……….. then some sessions may have to create some participation guidelines, and do what works for those who want to attend.

Re: A plea to people to stop composing new tunes

Brendan, many of the tunes named for a musician were simply a matter of naming the tune after who you heard it from. Not necessarily “celebrating” the player, but acknowledging them as the source.

In fact, most of those tunes already had names, but the name might not have been mentioned at a session. So it is that a particular reel in G widely cited as “Tommy Peoples” was one that Tommy played and other people picked up. But Tommy himself learned it from an uncle who called it The Milkmaid.

Also, the abundance of “Paddy Fahey” tunes runs counter to your argument. They were all composed by Mr. Fahey. Yet I suspect his focus was on neither the musician nor the composer but on the tune itself. Which is where it belongs.

Yes, even today tunes get misnamed after some musician or other red herring. But it’s perhaps less likely because now there’s an entire industry (and monetisation) around the penning and recording of tunes. People tend to keep better track of tune titles and composers’ names. As the Lunasa lads found, you can land in trouble with the known and living composer when your sleeve notes credit it as The Wedding Reel but it’s really MacLeod’s Farewell. :-)

Re: A plea to people to stop composing new tunes

Brendan, I suppose my point is that naming a tune after a player is more about not having the tune’s pre-existing name. Yes, tunes get named after musicians who play the tune often and well enough that you want to learn it. There are tunes known as Micho Russell’s or Joe Bane’s for just such reasons. Sure, it’s a sign of respect, but it may as well be that Micho or Joe didn’t have a name for the tune.

Another way to look at this is that we’re not seeing tunes called Cathal Hayden’s or Edel Fox’s because today’s musicians provide tune names in their sleeve notes. And if you hear an ‘anonymous’ tune at a session, you can suss out it’s name by using Tune Pal, or remembering the hook and looking it up on any number of ABC search engines, or—as is a regular past time on this site—posting a snippet of it here and asking the hive mind to i.d. it.

Fifty years ago, there were far fewer recordings. People (myself included) picked up tunes at sessions, where noise and alcohol conspire to muddle tune names, when they’re mentioned at all. Even one on one, the old timers might not name a tune. I remember sitting in a kitchen with one of my mentors, and him starting to teach me a new tune, saying, “Here’s one of the Big Tunes,” and away we went, never a name. It’d turn out to be Farrell O’Gara or Jenny’s Welcome to Charlie, but I’d find that out only later.

“…were it not for composers of fresh shiny new tunes, there never would have been any ITM in the first place.”

In his book Traditional Music In Ireland, Tomas O Canainn says:

“The whole process of composition and transmission within the tradition makes an interesting study.

In the past, and even yet, original composition as such is not the norm.

One finds old material being reworked to provide the setting for a quite new song.

The jig Tuirne Mhaire becomes the air for the song An Brianach Og.

The well-known set dance The Blackbird is quite clearly related to the song A Spailpin a Ruin.”

Re: A plea to people to stop composing new tunes

I’m with Michael Eskin on the “Hey look at me!” aspect. Not that I’d want to forbid new compositions at a session - but I think it’s better if they are rare events.

I’ve been pondering the difficulty in introducing to our sessions even a genuine trad tune that others just don’t know. I might show up at a session where nobody knows (say) Langstrom’s Pony. If I rolled into that tune after another compatible jig, I’d be suddenly doing a rather uncomfortable solo. But at least in that case, I could give a title, any who were interested could find it and learn it, and it might be played in the future. None of that is likely with a “Look at me!” composition.

A compromise tactic might be to first teach a tune to a few friends. They could give honest feedback, and could join in at an open session, as an attempt to have a critical mass. But even that would work far better with existing tunes.

There’s certainly no shortage of extant tunes. Since the heart of a session is playing together, I think introducing a new composition at a session should be done only very rarely.
