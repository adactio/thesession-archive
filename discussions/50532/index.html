The slow reel treatment

Sometimes the need for speed is vastly overrated. Lately, some friends and I have really been enjoying playing some of the more moody reels slowly. My personal, most recent favorite is The Maids of Mitchelstown (can’t get enough of that tune. we play it in a low register,then a little higher), but Martin Wynn’s #2 and Dunmore Lasses are a couple more we like to do this with.
Obviously any reel can be played slowly, but I am wondering if anyone has suggestions for other reels that especially benefit from the slow treatment.

Re: The slow reel treatment

Personally, I think any tune worth playing is worth playing slow. If it has to be fast to be good, it’s probably not good. I also appreciate it when the tune itself is allowed to shine, very often I find that players can get a bit more creative with a tune than the tune can really take.

In that vein, one of my favorite reel pairings is the Ballykett Courthouse and the Old Maids of Mayo (which is basically the Old Maids of Galway, but a fourth up in a G-ish rather than a D-ish key), played at a moderate pace with a solid rhythm and minimal embellishment. For contrast, sometimes I’ll play the Humors of Lissadel following, and let that one get a bit fancy.

Re: The slow reel treatment

Jon Kiparsky: “Personally, I think any tune worth playing is worth playing slow. If it has to be fast to be good, it’s probably not good.”

I do not entirely agree with this. The music we are talking about is fundamentally dance music and, as such, the tunes are, in essence, vehicles for rhythm. A tune can be much more than that, of course, but dance tunes are designed to be played at dance tempo or thereabouts and, if a tune *only* works at that tempo, that does not necessarily make it a bad (or less good) tune.

‘Good’ and ‘bad’ are, of course, entirely subjective – as are ‘fast’ and ‘slow’. I would happily play any reel slower than the typical dance tempo – say 80-90 bpm, rather than the 110-120 bpm used for sean nós and set dancing, at which tempo it can still have lift, still ‘feel’ like a reel and get people’s toes tapping. But for playing at slower tempo, in a way that does not bring about physical movement in the listener, not all tunes, however good they may be as dance tunes, contain enough melodic interest to hold their own. Examples that come to mind are Last Night’s Fun and The Humours of Tulla* – both great, uplifting, dance tunes, but, to me, are a bit pointless played at a slower tempo – as opposed to, say, The Wise Maid or Dr. Gilbert’s, both of which are full of interesting twists and turns, with a kind of narrative quality.

Tunes are a bit like jokes in this regard: there are the spontaneous quips and one-liners, which have to be quick and snappy, or they fall flat; and there are the shaggy-dog stories – the more drawn-out they are, the more packed with engaging but irrelevant detail, the more powerful the punchline when it arrives.

*It is almost guaranteed that somebody will point me to a recording of one or other of these two tunes played slowly to great effect – in which case my argument comes tumbling to the ground.

Re: The slow reel treatment

@CMO - “Slow” is consistent with “rhythm”. I didn’t mean to suggest that all reels should be played as though they were airs! My feeling is that there’s a right tempo range for most tunes, which is where the rhythm happens, and that playing too fast kills the rhythm of the tune and replaces it with a sort of generic “runaway reel” rhythm and it just sounds like “applebutter applebutter applebutter applebutter”.

Basically, when I say “slow”, I mean a moderate tempo - possibly in the range you talk about, but I don’t really speak bpms - and playing either right on top of or a little bit behind the beat. Often on the last tune in a set, I’ll lean forward over the beat a little bit, maybe even get out in front of it, but the tempo doesn’t change. (unless the bodhran player gets confused and starts whacking faster, and everyone else follows them, and the whole thing goes to hell)

Re: The slow reel treatment

John Kiparsky: “I didn’t mean to suggest that all reels should be played as though they were airs!”

That is pretty much how I interpreted ‘The slow reel treatment’ – playing a reel ‘as an air’ rather than simply ‘at a moderate pace’. ‘Slow reel’ as I understand it, has become a kind of subgenre of its own, where a reel (or a purpose-composed tune with the structure of a reel) is played at perhaps as slow as half the customary tempo, without any sense of ‘dance’ (although it can still be rhythmic). A good example of this would be Frankie Kennedy and Mairead ni Mhaonaigh’s ‘The Sunset’ https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3JONf5BoBxY

I agree entirely that reels do not always have to be played from setdancing tempo upwards to sound good (I find playing at those speeds physically exhausting – even painful – if I can manage it at all, and hence, not enjoyable). But I consider this just to be playing reels at a tempo that is comfortable to me, not making a point of playing them slowly.

“… playing too fast kills the rhythm of the tune and replaces it with a sort of generic “runaway reel” rhythm …”

*Too* fast, yes – but what is ‘too fast’ varies considerably from one musician to another. There are some brilliant musicians that play at tempos way beyond what I could ever dream of, yet still manage to include all the lift, phrasing, nuance, melodic variation etc. you could want – and not sound rushed or cluttered. I have found that when a tune is played very fast *and* very well, it can give a different perspective on the tune: like viewing a place from the air, you become aware of things that are less perceptible ‘close up’.

↳ Posted by CreadurMawnOrganig on September 22nd, 2025.

Re: The slow reel treatment

I would say that “too fast” is also in the ears of the listener. A few years ago I suggested that a flute-loving friend might enjoy listening to Molloy’s Black Album. His verdict? “Too fast”. At first, I was aghast - what sort of philistine was this, and could we really be friends? But then I decided to open up my ears and try to listen to the record as a novice, and I realized that he was right: if you don’t know the music, I can see how it would feel like a lot of mush, despite the fact that Molloy is (obviously) in complete control at all times.

At the same time, yes, excessive speed can reveal new things about a tune if the ear is properly attuned to the music. I find that slower tempos are much more revelatory, myself, but sometimes I’ll take a “slow tune” and give it some gas, and that shows me something about what’s essential to that tune, which helps me play it better at the tempos I prefer.
