Fiddle questions

I’ve had a fiddle for about thirty years, but it’s usually been number three or worse behind fretted instruments, so I’ve probably done three years worth of playing scattered over thirty years. As a result, while I’m familiar with countless arcane details of guitars and how to play them, with the fiddle I’m basically a moron. So I will be asking some ignorant questions.

I’ve been using Prim medium strings for maybe twenty-five years. I had tried only two or three others and (Alabama fiddler) James Bryan recommended the Prims. Since I admired his tone, I tried them and thought they sounded pretty good. They are steel-core strings and nearly everybody else I know uses strings with a synthetic core. I tried some kind of Perlon string and didn’t like it on the instrument I had at the time, but that was a long time ago and I have a much better fiddle now. I like a dark, warm, even slightly nasal tone to a fiddle. I know some large part of that depends on the instrument and bow, but how does the setup contribute? And what kind of string is likely to go in my direction?

Okay, I didn’t start with the most ignorant questions, but this is what’s on my mind right now. More ignorance later.

Re: Fiddle questions

Setup - very important. Height, material, curvature (profile) of bridge. Action (height) and elevation of strings above the fingerboard also. Assuming an average quality fiddle and bow, I would recommend something like Corelli Crystal strings - they are synthetic core, and have the advantage of giving a neutral sound to an average or below-par fiddle

One of my students bought a £400 Czecoslovakian fiddle which sounded a bit harsh and ‘wolfy’, but after fitting the Crystals the tone settled down nicely.

Sit tight, I’m in the process of demo’ing a good fiddle setup on my site - http://www.worldfiddlemusic.co.uk/ . I’ll show a good setup, and and some audio and video to back it up.

Bob, I’ve heard that Prims are no longer around (someone please correct me if I’m wrong!). If you want to stick with steel core strings try helicores (quite common in my part of the world - every other player and his/her dog seems to use them), or Thomastic Spirocore. Currently, I’m using Spirocore A,D and G on both my fiddles (a very old one and a new one) as an experiment. The spirocores seem to have more response, power and penetration (very useful) in sessions than the helicores, particularly on the old fiddle. The helicores, in comparison, seem to have a slightly softer tone.
At the end of the day tone and playability depend on so many factors - the fiddle itself (and its setup), the strings, the bow (each bow will give its own individual coloration and power to the tone), the type of music you play, and lastly (and certainly not least) the player. It’s a matter of experiment - as time goes by the instrument changes its tone as it gets more playing, and the player acquires more skill in tone production. Four years of fairly intensive playing and I’m still experimenting with strings and bows, and discovering new things.
I’ll also confirm that my new fiddle (3 years old) has a slightly nasal quality compared with the richer and darker tone of my old fiddle. I think this has a lot to do with the wood changing its properties over the years - drying out perhaps and getting a lot of playing. My new fiddle doesn’t sound as nasal as it used to, so the tone is obviously starting to change with time.

Re: Fiddle questions

A word about steel core strings - some brands do have an attractive tone, but there’s always an equivalent in the synthetic core range. There are a few pros and cons for both types.

Steel cores can appear brighter and tonally nice, but the downside is that some things are physically harder to play,eg 3-note chords (ie 3 string hit all at once), as there is not so much ‘give’ in the strings - they do not move so much towards the fingerboard under normal bow pressure. Some players use this to their advantage, like being able to have a slightly flatter bridge, thus requiring less bowing effort in crossing strings.

Do they produce more volume than synthetic cores? A bit of a myth here. All other factors being equal, the sound seems louder to the player, but the amplitude of vibration is much less than with synthetic or gut core strings, so the sound does not carry as well, so a lot is lost on a listener 10 feet away.

Are they more responsive than synthetic? Certainly not. Are they more robust? Probably yes to that one, at least in the sense that the winding rarely gets damaged (unlike the A-string of the Dominants synthetic core). One final point - I’ve seen old fiddles severely weakened, ang neck put out of alignment by the constant use of steel strings, what with the extra pressure on the bridge.

Well, each to his/her own, just my observations here.

Jim

Re: Fiddle questions

Steel strings are definitely more responsive and crisp for ITM than synthetic. Some synthetics can come close. Evah Pirazzis particularly, but steel is still more responsive due to the slightly higher tension and smaller diameter. That said, synthetics are fine for ITM if your playing style suits them. Just depends on the type of bowing and ornamentation that you prefer. Another thing to consider is the quality of the fiddle. Poorly arched or graduated boxes aren’t going to work as well with synthetics because the fiddle itself is less responsive and flabbier in tone. Bridge cuts are also tailored to the type of string. A bridge cut for steel strings will not work as well for synthetics and vise versa. As far as tension and stress on the fiddle go, good quality rope core steel strings such as Helicores actually are only higher in tension by 3 pounds or so. Older fiddles don’t have a neck block, so that’s why you can have neck set changes more easily. A healthy , newer fiddle will handle rope core steel just fine. The other thing is that synthetic strings will get flabby and lose tone a lot quicker. For ideal performance, they should be changed every couple months. Most people leave synthetics on about 3-4 times longer than they really should!

Re: Fiddle questions

Many fiddlers/violinists (the music genre is immaterial) don’t realise that too much rosin on the bow is counter-productive. Many’s the time I’ve seen players in orchestras and in sessions spend a couple of minutes industrially, and quite unnecessarily, scrubbing away at the rosin with the bow. If the bow needs that amount of rosin to get a response out of the string then that is a sure sign that the bow is in urgent need of rehairing. If you have a rosin-free string and apply the bow to the string and see a light dusting of rosin on the string, then you have at least enough rosin on the bow and do not need to apply any more. Too much rosin can slow the response of the string and slightly deaden its tone because of the extra weight that is now part of the string – the strings I’ve seen caked with the stuff…! BTW, I do practice what I preach - I typically rosin a bow about twice a week, and then only 3 or 4 light swipes of the rosin on the bow hair.

It is good policy to clean rosin off the string after playing. If you use a lint-free cloth to wipe the rosin off (including the underside of the string) every time after playing then you don’t need to use string–cleaning fluids (e.g. isopropanol) very often – once every couple of weeks perhaps. Also, after playing, clean rosin dust off the fiddle’s belly and off the bow stick with a dry cloth. A build-up of rosin over time isn’t good for varnish or, eventually, the underlying wood. In the case of the fiddle belly, too much rosin can deaden the tone. While I’m on this topic, anything that touches the belly of the fiddle will deaden the tone; in particular, an incorrectly fitted chinrest is a notorious and common culprit. I position my chinrest so that it rests only on the purfling (which is very slightly higher than the belly); this means there is an air gap between the belly and that part of the chinrest overlying it, and so no contact with the vibrating wood of the belly.

Trevor

Re: Fiddle questions

I think most guys would get funny looks if their fiddle cases reeked of eau de cologne :-) A serious point I’d make, though, is to be careful about using perfumes and the like for cleaning strings because some may contain oils which leave a residue on the strings – not a good idea. Test beforehand. Note that surgical spirit contains 2% castor oil, and methylated spirits can contain all sorts of nasties, depending on the quality, intended use, and the supplier. That’s why I stick to isopropanol (“isoprop”), which is the basis for some tape head cleaning fluids. But whatever stuff you use, keep it well away from the instrument varnish.

Re: Fiddle questions

Bob, anything to do with the soundpost and bridge is a very complex business and must always be left to the expert luthier. The soundpost has to be of the correct dimensions, be made of the right sort of wood, be profiled correctly at its ends, be of precisely the right length (neither too long or too short – it shouldn’t fall over when the strings are removed, and neither should it be a compression member between the front and the back), the grain should be in the correct orientation relative to the axis of the fiddle, and it should be located in exactly the right place (to the nearest fine fraction of a millimetre – it’s that critical). Get any of these wrong and the fiddle will not be producing anything like the tone it is capable of. A tall order for an amateur to achieve!

The bridge isn’t a much easier proposition -- except that it is physically slightly easier to install. The feet of the bridge have to be in intimate contact with the belly of the fiddle, the flat side of the bridge (the one facing the tailpiece) should be exactly perpendicular to the belly at that point, the thickness of the bridge should change slightly across its width from the E to the G, etc etc. The bridge may look pretty and a bit ornate, but those intricate cut-outs and little notches have been developed over the centuries and are there for very good purposes. The bridge isn’t a passive transmitter of vibrations from the strings to the belly; it is a vibrating member in its own right (and emits sound frequencies which can be detected with the right equipment) and so has a real effect on the tone. The soundpost and bridge complex should be treated as an acoustically matched system – it’s not for nothing that the soundpost is known as the “soul” of the violin.

I don’t want impugn the three people who have worked on your fiddle, but I should have thought that if at least one were an expert luthier your problems would have been solved. With a fiddle such as you have described (unplayed for 30 years, bridge and soundpost down, and so on) a luthier should give it a thorough overhaul, including checking for cracks and that the bass bar is still doing its job properly. The bass bar is glued to the underside of the belly and extends a good part of the length of the belly, passing under the bass foot of the bridge, and running almost parallel to the axis of the fiddle. Its function is to distribute the vibrations from the bridge over the belly, and so to control the volume of tone produced. With time, the glue can deteriorate and bass bar then needs to be reset.

I had a similar experience to yours with my old fiddle. It came to me five years ago from my mother, and had come down to her through the family from her great-grandfather. When I received it, it hadn’t been played for 60 years and was in a dire condition – no strings, broken bridge, soundpost rattling around inside, stuck pegs, cracks in the belly, and black caked rosin over a large portion of the belly – but fundamentally a fine instrument. A luthier in Bristol did an excellent job (not cheap – I could almost have bought a decent beginner’s fiddle for the price) and restored the instrument to a fine condition. It naturally took quite some time for the tone to return to what must have been its original richness, so this is something you’ll have to be aware of when a fiddle is thoroughly overhauled after several years of non-use and damage.

Trevor

Oooo…I don’t like Wittner’s at all. Tend to thin out the sound on fiddles I’ve had with them.

I also don’t like add-on fine tuners. They shorten the string length from what it should be, and this affects both tone and intonation. Plus, few o them work very well--most are stiff and hard to turn.

The best solution I’ve found so far is the Pusch tailpiece with built-in fine tuners. It’s a high-quality ebony tailpiece with carbon-fiber fine tuner’s built in, so the strings nestle right where they should on the tailpiece (each in its own little grooved lever). Every fiddle I’ve put a Pusch on has sounded better for it. And the fine tuners work nice and smooth.

Only about $40 USD, but they’re becoming harder to find--often backordered, even online.
