Unwanted singers in sessions

I’ve just come back from a day at the Irish Music Festival at Mells in Somerset. Problem: how do you (politely) deal with a member of the public who is under the impression that a group of musicians in session is his or her personal karaoke machine?

We’d not long started an afternoon session in the courtyard of the Talbot pub on the main street when a large genial middle-aged gentleman in shorts, in appearance not too unlike the naval “captain” in those fishfinger ads, came out of the bar and tried to persuade us to play the Raggle-Taggle Gypsies for him so that he could sing it. Of course, we all denied knowing the tune or even having heard of it (lying through our teeth, we were), and he tried singing part of it, which he obviously couldn’t remember properly. That didn’t work, so we continued playing our tunes and he disappeared back into the bar. 10 minutes and a drink later he was back with the same request, with the same result. This nuisance happened another 10 minutes later, and another, and another, and one by one we decamped to the session in the garden of the village hall nearby (a magnificent centuries-old stone barn) where they were doing set-dancing. So no more trouble from the gentleman in the Talbot.

Some of us were discussing how to deal with this sort of situation, perhaps in a normal pub session where there’s nowhere else to escape to. Make clear from the outset that we’re not playing for singers? Challenge the singer to sing on his or her own without benefit of accompaniment (could cause even worse problems)? Any other ideas to defuse such a situation politely?

Trevor

A group of fiddlers to my aquaintance feel similarly about piano accordians. Their strategy at festivals is a mass lowering of the tuning by a quarter tone, and then watching the the ensuing confusion. But i can’t see how to apply this to singers, except maybe by everyone playing in a different key for the requested song. Being a banjo player, I never had much concern about piano accordians. There’s no sustain on my instrument to lose in the din of an accordian anyway. In the hands of someone like Traycho Sinapov, I can fall in love with the sound. I draw the line at singers though. Was it Frank Sinatra who claimed all musicians are frustrated vocalists?

A couple of years ago, during the summer drinking season, we had a couple of very drunk fellows show up at our session. The expensively dressed yet sallow guy in the cowbow outfit slid over to the bar and stayed there, but his Ghanian friend took one look at us and thought, “Great! Musicians. Just to say that sometimes it’s worth sitting through a few old chestnuts to hear something really great. No bones players, please.

The reason I said ‘shoot them’ was because I’d just come back from a local beer and music festival, and
a) I was somewhat taken with the drink
and
b) a friend and I had studied the festival programme to find a time when there wasn’t an advertised session (we knew that all advertised sessions would be packed with local songsters and morris-minded melodian murderers), so we could go and play some tunes and maybe attract a few like minded souls to do the same. We went to a pub and managed to get through half a dozen sets before one of the people who had drifted in asked if they could sing a song. We said “No,” but they seemed to think we were joking and just started anyway. At this all the seemingly innocent people who had wandered in revealed themselves to be singers of long and tedious songs, and our little session ground to a halt.
So I went off and got drunk.

Re: Unwanted singers in sessions

I don’t see that harm in letting them sing one; like baorbrat says, it’s a session, not a performance. I, for one, always assumed that sessions were open to the occasional floor singer, as they are to the occasional soloist. But I suppose part of the trouble is that that they usually want you to accompany them in a song you’ve had no part in choosing, and there are some pretty ropey songs about. The pub-goers like to hear a song, though, and I think they all feel more included if one of their number has got up. If s/he’s a good singer, everyone will want to hear more, if their egos can handle it. And if bad (and you’d have to be really bad, surely, to cause any real offence), playing out of tune can work. Or letting them sing unaccompanied. If the entire audience decides to dispense with your services and it becomes a singalong free-for-all, why in heaven’s name would you want to stay? Run like the wind!
