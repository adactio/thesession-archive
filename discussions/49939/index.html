Re: Musical Notation Must Die!

Here’s a hill I’ll die on (that has nothing to do with whether people should / shouldn’t read it, or any judgement on traditional music and notation):

The musical notation system we use is a crap designed system and could (should) be made way better.

I don’t think it does a very good job conveying some of the most important information, obfuscates important musical concepts, and adds too high of a learning tax.

I think a well designed notation system would line up with how humans experience music (relatively). Thus:

-A well designed notation system would convey intervals easily and with very little mental tax
-A well designed system would allow very fluid transposing between keys without any effort, in fact, one could argue that a well design notation system would be largely independent

I understand that the musical notation we have is what everyone knows, and that it has all that information if you learn it, but that’s not signs of a great system.

I come from casual video game development (Bejeweled, Plants vs. Zombies) and one thing I learned is that everything should lower the unnecessary cognitive load. So in Bejeweled (for those familiar with it) we made all the gems different shapes, colors, and had simple art to easily differentiate quickly, because the fun was finding and making matches, not trying to differentiate small details.

For music the important thing is conveying the tune and the key musical elements, and I just feel like notation makes everything that ought to be easy harder than necessary.

Rant over. I’m curious to hear other thoughts. As a reminder, this isn’t me saying reading notation is bad, just that the currently design system is mediocre.

↳ Posted by Bregolas on January 31st, 2025.

Re: Musical Notation Must Die!

A lot of ground has been covered in the comments above already.
A lot of pertinent stuff.
And perhaps that’s the word we need to keep in mind:
Pertinence to specific needs -including how much free time a user has at their disposal, how foreign to the task they are as beginners, etc.
No system is perfect but a system which does not take into account the needs of specific users is not fit for purpose (think braille staff notation?!).
For comparison, written English works if you’re a native speaker but it is useless at conveying the stress pattern, pitch and speed changes or phonetics (including specific accents) to someone trying to learn it from scratch. The phonetic alphabet is a better tool but if the student relies solely on it, they will fail at deciphering the written word world which is a massive part of English culture.
Likewise, ITM was born out of a hybrid universe where notation had its place:
not all composers and servants of the art were blind harpers or illiterate. Many great tunes were born of paper and ink.
ITM is a robust oral tradition because the structure of the tunes respect what our brains require in order to reproduce it entirely orally; but to believe the entire bulk of the ITM repertoire owes its existence to an oral-only tradition is a myth unsupported by historical evidence.
Having said that, ITM now survives and thrives, in its region of origin, without much help from notation.
Today we are at a technological juncture where sound can be made visible -in so many ways!- at the click of a mouse and written traces can be turned into sound at the touch of finger. So the IT developpers’ role at this point is to create interfaces in which, regardless of the type of music (or language) learned or created, various parameters/options can be switched on or off at will for an enhanced reading experienced.
These are generalities worth bearing in mind I think.
Personnaly, I do recommand teaching (and teaching yourself) with no interference from screens or folders. The whole point of ITM is that it’s portable: the teacher must be able to “demonstrate” (by her / his sheer “presentation” style) that this is the case.
Traditional musicians all over the world have something in commom: there is POWER in what they do, they put POWER and SKILL in what they do, and they COMMUNICATE VITALITY and create SOCIAL BOUND. Letting screens and scores take too much space, even in the class room, is an attack on these precious things; think of it like a covid mask…
There are only a handful of musical traditions in the world where the page hasn’t been banished: Sacred Harp singing and Bavarian fanfarre. I have no time for the latter if only because it is so stilted and conservative, it is a very poor outlet for vitality. The former is redeemed because the book is only there to create a bound between complete strangers and time spent practitionners hardly look at the page anyway: It is very vibrant because the text is not strictly adhered to.
Notation came about because it was important for the Christians to keep a check on unorthodoxies which might develop if orality was the only medium. A valid point.
It was only in the 19th century that classical musicians, who developped the system over 900 years, lost the plot and started to act up the centrality and sacrosanctity of the score.
Opera singers are the exception. What actor read their lines on stage?
About 10 years ago I went to a solo piano concert at the NCHD and the Classical pianist played the whole gig without anyscore. Classical music never appeared so vibrant and miraculous to me; it was as if the pianist was engaged in composing, improvising every note right in front of our eyes. How amazing.
Amazing because you don’t often get that impression of total communication from classical musicians: the commitment is there but the score comes between you and them.
That’s a long text! Enough will be said if I add that I came to ITM from classical music years ago specifically to escape the written strictures (the fetters/diktat it imposes on your soul, and the shortcoming in expression and communication it imposes) …and ever looked back.
I still appreciate all types of music but I cringe a lot when I see scores where they are not necessary: when they come beteeen performer and audience, teacher and student, and, above all, between the student and her/himself.

I’ll add one more remark from observations i’ve made while teaching writing/reading;
