Re: Guitar Sounds!

Don’t panic, for an intro guitar, Yamahas are pretty good. I would take it to a local instrument store with a repair department and see if they have either a guitar tech or someone who knows the instrument well. They often do, usually aspiring musicians take a job in a music store, and often have a good idea as to what will make it better.

have them recommend a good string, and check the playability for you, and set it up to make it play easily. Also any new instrument will have a break in period. If you got it used, was it played just a little prior? If so, give it a few months of good play, it should “wake up” the top and get a better tone.

If you are still not real happy with it, save up for something really special. Get the option, in writing, that you have a week to return it. You will often play it in one place with great acoustics, then bring it home and it’s deader. A good test would be to take your Yamaha for comparison.

It’s good to have a secondary instrument, I find ( don’t get like me and end up with about 15 guitars!) to bring out to either a not so great area, or a crowded pub where it would get dinged up. Or if the other needs repair, you’ll always have something else to play.

You did right to get a Yamaha at this point. Don’t worry. I hear they record really well too.

Re: Guitar Sounds!

Firstly, your guitar has to mature;
play every avaiable note up the fingerboard, on every string, 6 times a a day.
Always play with clean fingers, and wipe the strings down after palying with a clean cloth; some people swear by Fastfret as well - I use it but I can’t swear how much it does.
Try experimenting with; different materials of string; silk‘n’steel, steel; I used to use John Alvey Turners monel-covered for a while; bronze; phosphur-bronze; THEN try different string guages of your preferred material.
And keep doing the 6-times a day exercise. Always play the instrument with some vigour, as well as softly, or it won’t develop.
And I find that a thinner pick gives a more satisfying sound, unless you need to make the maximum volume. And the closer to the fingerboard you play, the more mellow the sound.
I bet you’ll go back to your classical instrument in three months and think it’s strung with rubber bands !

Re: Guitar Sounds!

“Most people think new strings sound tinny because they’re used to playing/hearing guitars with dud strings which give that ‘mellow’ (but usually out of tune) sound.”

I’m guessing that by “tinny” you mean “excessively bright”. Every long-time player I know recognizes that new strings will sound extra bright for a few days, but that they’ll “settle in” and be relatively stable for some weeks or months.

Here’s a factor that complicates the discussion. The better the guitar, the longer the strings will sound good. A low-end guitar often needs bright, new strings to sound decent and starts to sound dead once the strings settle in. A really good, sensitive guitar will sound pretty good with old strings, as long as they still play in tune. How long they stay in tune depends on the type of string and how you play.

Years ago, when I used ordinary uncoated, untreated strings, I had to change every week or two. I typically get at least three months out of modern mirco-coated strings (Martin SP, et al) before they lose intonation. Well worth a few extra bucks.

I used to use Elixirs on a guitar that was slightly dull and needed brightening up. When I tried them on my best instrument, they pushed the tonal palette too far toward the bright end. When I went to the SP’s, the guitar sounded warmer and had more useable tonal range.

Re: Guitar Sounds!

When I just had one main playing guitar I changed the strings every week or two. They still sounded OK, but a used up string will lose some flexibility and therefore the intonation can go slightly off, which is annoying. Now I rotate between a bunch of different guitars and each is not played so often, but I seem to be changing strings on something every 3rd week or so.

I love those cheap martin phosphors though, even brand new they never sound tinny, they sound pretty nice. After a few hours they are a little better. The thing with them, you can buy in bulk at various places online for as low as $3.50 a set. So I buy a dozen at a time and have them a good while. I think they give the best sound and feel in spite of the price…. just one opinion here.

Guitars, and instruments in general are living things. They are happy one day, grumpy the next, they don’t like the weather or they do, they sound better one day than another. Think of them as a plant, they came from a plant. Moisture goes in and out of them. Keep a humidifier in them in the winter. Leave them in the case when not played, it’s great protection. If you want somthing out for decoration get a broken guitar and hang it up.

One thing I absolutley hate about guitars especially these days, though I know it protects them, is the thick glossy finishes. I will absolutely ONLY French polish my guitars. It’s about as much finish as on a fine old fiddle. Yes it can get dinged up more easily, but it is easily repaired, easier than the gloop they spray on them. The main thing though is it lets the wood resonate if not trapped in the heavy finish. When I was at school, some people opted to leave their unfinished wood guitar behind for a fellow who used to to finishing at Martin. He would do the “proper” finish for them. I called it killing the sound and paying $400 for the privilege. I live near enough to my teacher to go back, and did for comparison. I had heard these guitars before and after, a big difference. They lost a lot. Still after playing them a year some of the sound will come back, but never like before with no finish on them. The French polish is next to no finish. You can get a matte finish that looks like it but it’s still not the same thing. The FP is just shellac cut with alcohol and a drop of olive (or other) oil added and hand rubbed on, it is time consuming, but very simple to do. I won’t even fill the pores, they do that with pumice or latex on the “proper” finishes to get a mirror finish. The FP fills them enough and I like to see the pores, not a mirror shine.

I don’t advise stripping the top of the Yamaha down yourself, but if someone ever refinished it, you may consider asking them to FP just the top. You’d likely be very surprised at the change. Sometimes too, a luthier can shave the braces down a little from inside if they look to heavy, it’s not an expensive job, should be $50-80.00 or so at most. That will let the top sing better too. You can always run it by a repair person and see what can be done. I did this to a cheap guitar and it sounds really good now.

All this…thought for the future, just get playing it for now and enjoy it!

Yup, I like the long curved chisel as well - much easier than using straight chisels.
My adaptable mold thingy - I modelled it on the ones we used on my course. Basically it’s a plywood base with lots of channels cut into it. In those channels I fit dowelling rods and fix them in position with wingnuts. Think of all those spool clamps actually being wooden rods and you’ll get the idea.
The bit I find the most satisfying about building is the carving of the neck and the bridge (I was dreading doing those jobs). The bit I like least is thicknessing rosewood backs and sides with a No.5 plane (even if it is a Clifton). Oh, for the day when I can afford a thicknessing sander.
The neck set is a head numbing task. It needs plenty of time and care. You have a little leeway if you leave the final shaping of the bridge until the fingerboard is on. That way you can adjust the height accordingly. One thing that perturbs me about the stewmac kit is that the bottom of the bridge is flat. I was taught to shape the bottom of the bridge so that it matched the curvature of the top. I’m not sure if that applies in the case of the kit.
You’re right about the cardboard inner for the mold. I should have taken more care of that instead of letting my young “apprentice” make a bit of a mess of it (he has yet to learn that patience is a virtue). But hey, who’s going to notice if the waist is 1/4" over size.
