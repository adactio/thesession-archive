Getting to Session Speed on Fiddle

Will speed come over time by playing well, but slowly or do you think it requires the work of trying to play fast: Even if playing faster than your ability results in, at least for some time, the deterioration of other parameters like tone, articulation, even tempo and so on. For those experienced session fiddlers already playing at speed: When you’ve reached the ability to play well at session speed, then, when learning a brand new tune, is it “natural” and effortless to learn play the that new tune at speed?

Re: Getting to Session Speed on Fiddle

A jazz musician (I wish I could remember who) said something to the effect: “If you can’t play it well slow, you sure as hell can’t play it well fast.” And playing well has to be the prime objective.

If you’re playing a fiddle too fast your muscles are tense, you’re forcing your fingers, your touch is too heavy, your bowing is uncontrolled and your intonation is, at best, approximate. The worst thing about all this is that your body is learning it all and will continue to repeat that behaviour every time you pick up your fiddle. I know this because I went through just that; I kept trying to keep up with speed merchants and I practised tunes as fast as I could. It set me back more years than I am prepared to admit. Eventually I forced myself to slow down and, lo and behold, my playing began to improve. And, interestingly, I found I didn’t actually know the tunes I thought I knew; because there’s more to knowing a tune than merely being able to whistle the notes. When I listened to what I was playing I realised the intonation was often way out; and my fingers were dropping onto exactly the same false notes every time. They had learned the tune that way and, until I consciously redirected them, that’s what they were going to continue with. Slowing down the bowing, too, gave me the chance to develop expression and phrasing and the sound improved.

I realise now that I’d been fighting the instrument rather than cooperating with it. What a waste of time that was. You have to treat a fiddle as a partner not as an enemy; but, like any partnership, there has to be respect and understanding. Trying to force matters gets you nowhere.

Re: Getting to Session Speed on Fiddle

Playing slowly needs a different movement pattern than playing fast. It’s old school to learn a tune in slow motion and then try to speed it up gradually.

It’s true: Practicing slowly helps to get difficult parts clear in your mind. It builds a strong foundation for playing, as AlBrown sais. But it is not enough to have it clear in mind, because when you play fast, you don’t control your playing with the mind anymore. It comes out of an automatism. But how build this automatism?

Automatisms occur, as soon as you speed up. Then, the mind isn’t able to take control anymore. So the best way to school motoric learning is to speed up.

As soon as you are an intermediate player, it’s necessary to learn a kind of practicing, where you also learn tunes in original tempo, from the very beginning, and then practice the difficult parts slowly, afterwards. But how learning tunes in original tempo?

It’s easy. Say goodbye to playing through! Cut a tune into tiny pieces and practice each one “a tempo” until it works. Even if it’s only 4 notes, cut it down. Don’t try a following bit before the preceding is safe.

This helps you listening to the tune in original tempo, and that means, you keep the right idea from the tune from the very beginning up to the end, while you’re learning it. Thus, you avoid the danger of fumbling around with ornaments, that only work in slow motion but spoil your timing completely when tried to play fast.

So the categoric imperative of “practice slowly!” is only true for beginners. As you get more experience, you should practice tempo orientated: A mixture of slow and fast tempos.

When learning tunes I strip the tune out to the basics but play it at speed.
Sometimes that’s just one beat every bar if I’m unfamiliar with the tune.
Then I’ll repeat adding the next level of notes, again centred firmly on the beats.
So I go until i’m able to add in the grace notes and touches that complete the tune.
The only need I find for slow prctice are those finer details which are isolated for practice,
as are any issues of tone or instrument skill.
The idea of trying to improve something like tone or technique at the same time as learning to get a tune up to speed seems unwise to me.
The ability of a player to produce a good sound or apply techniques doesn’t change with each new tune. That’s a different practice session from taking tunes in and playing them up to speed. You’ll use what you learned in your specific practice there if you do practice those aspects, many trad players don’t & didn’t bother too much with tone perfection & the like. You can buy records of their playing and it’s actually not a target faster playing in trad.
I think learning tunes slow then trying to speed them up would leave me sounding like the ‘eternal student’ as the push and feel of the tune would not be there until the speed was brought in, so my practice up to then would just be partial and would have hidden any snags that occour at speed.
I prefer to keep the rhythm and pace fixed and just stake out the spaces into which the rest must fit, then drop those in onthe next pass.

Re: Getting to Session Speed on Fiddle

One of the methods some find useful is setting a metronome or rhythm backing track to a tempo (eg 120 bpm) and play to that.

If there’s any difficulty, then knock the setting back 4bpm at a time, and try again until you’re comfortable with it. Then that’s the point where you try to understand why it’s difficult to play any faster. Is your fingering economical? Are your using too much bow for the notes? Could you ‘tighten’ things up a bit? Can you play the ornaments with ease, as your speed increases?

Maybe some very basic isolation exercises are needed for any of the above.

Those are just a few things to think about.

Also, what is “session speed” It varies quite a lot!

We all know that playing fast is nothing new - listening back to old recordings of Michael Coleman, Neilidh Boyle or Johnny Doran is enough to tell us that. Perhaps this is a result of the fact that many people’s main point of contact with traditional music is bands like The Bothy Band, Altan, Dervish (and other bands so famous that I’ve never heard of them), and have simply not discovered that a reel can have all the drive and lift you want without being played at 140bpm. Re: Getting to Session Speed on Fiddle

I guess my question is; why can’t you play the tune up to your target speed? Do your fingers fail to hit the correct spots or does your brain do the *mumble, mumble,mumble, b,a* and on you go? The answer to that question will provide the solution. If you have to think about putting your fingers down then the bit isn’t in kinesthetic memory (muscle memory) and it needs practice until it is. If you can’t pull it out of your brain fast enough then you don’t really know the tune.

So many pieces of tunes are already under our fingers that it’s easy to assume that we know them when we meet them again. Really, if you’re a proficient player you need to woodshed the bits that are new and then the connections until you can play them without thought. Or, if you hate practice, and why are you playing an instrument, then play the bits you can at tempo and, depending on the session, muddle through the rest. If you’re playing with friends then you’ll slowly hit those sections 1/3 times, then 2/3 then most of the time.
I think fast versus slow sessions are an altogether different question. This applies to playing faster than I can now which can be the transition from beginner to intermediate or an intermediate player sitting down with a new group of players and going gee, thought I had that…

Re: Getting to Session Speed on Fiddle

Most problems with speed are due to tension in the wrong places. Speed is a function of relaxation . Trying to play fast when nervous and tense is like driving with the handbrake on.
I found when learning that I would close up whereas opening up is the way forwards . So play standing , barefoot, shoulders back feel your toes . This is where movement starts , from the toes up the torso through the arm and out the bow .
Relax your face and shoulders l loosen up, warm up with a jog and a few stretches, fiddlers prayer , whatever it takes to get limber and relaxed.
It can help to stand on something high , to get above the tune , not below it. Not crouched and bent over but free and released .
IMO there are 2 speeds, dance speed , and the rest. So learn to play at dance speed and the rest will come.imo it’s pointless playing with all the ornaments but be unable to achieve dance speed, so get the primary aim: playing for dancers, then learn and begin to ornament that. The other way round may cause problems…..
The ornaments come after the function of the music has been achieved…..Better to play with no ornaments and be useful and functional .
What really impresses me is when a fiddler can play blindingly fast and ornament freely and as though they were playing slowly.
Remember the idols you aim to emulate could have started as children , they didn’t start fully formed but built up their skills as a matter of progression. They started simple. And simple is strong , keep it simple.
That’s one of the reasons I’m always saying learn to play without ornaments , but with verve, lift, excitement , drive, and interesting phrasing. Then is the time to add in the masters touches. IMO

Sometimes when people are learning to play at full speed there bow becomes out of time with there fingers .Sometimes this is just because the fiddler is trying to use to much bow for playing at that speed . Some people think that it just depends on how much bow they use controls how loud there fiddle is.IT IS NOT . It all depends on how quickly your bow is moving . If your bow is moving very slowly then your fiddle will be very quiet and if you are moving your bow very quickly you will get a louder and happier sound.If you are like this then you should try using a bit less bow and also try to loosen your bow wrist off .But more often than not it is the fiddler either isn’t good enough or they have not practiced the piece they are playing enough .When music teachers say start slowly and then get faster they don’t mean play it slowly once ,a medium speed once then full speed all time.They mean play it slowly until it feels natural to play it faster and then continue doing this until you have got the piece being played easily at full speed.It is also much much easier to play something quickly if you have NOT got any any music and you have memorized it well. If you are learning to play something fast with grace notes in then start off playing the grace notes and do not try to start playing the grace notes when you already know the tune well . it will not end well .If you can sight read well then you could start off learning the tune a little bit faster but if not then just stick to starting slow. I have a friend that can sight read really well and he can start off playing something for the first time playing something full speed with no mistakes and after he has played something once he will have it memorized and he will never forget it.

Re: Getting to Session Speed on Fiddle

Jamie, “IMO” is just internet-speak for “in my opinion . ”IMHO“ is ”in my humble opinion".

Also, you may know - volume depends on a few factors - not just bow speed. Volume is also affected by the amount of bow pressure on the string, and the position of the bow between the bridge and the end of the fingerboard.

You were implying that should not shoehorn in the grace notes after you have learned the tune, but play them as you learn the tune. Did you mean just single grace notes, or ornamentation, including cuts, rolls and trebles?

There’s quite a difference of opinion on this board on this - between ‘learn the bare bones of the tune first’ and ‘learn the tune, including all ornaments’.
