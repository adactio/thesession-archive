Re: Nerves

I too suffer from stage fright still, after 40+ years of performing. Not sure that mine will ever go away completely, but I have found that some things can ease it. Of course, first among these is experience, i.e. keep making yourself do it, even if it is uncomfortable. You can find public venues that are less critical/threatening; when I started playing my pipes in public, it was at nursing homes. The old folks were happy to have visitors and music of any kind, and smile and clap no matter if you make mistakes or not. There are other opportunities for playing that are a service to others, and always appreciated - funerals, hospitals, schools, etc.

Play with a friend or two. Gives you an excuse to practice the same repertoire, and it is less anxious when you are not the only one in the spotlight.

Only play tunes that you really love, even if that makes a short set / performance time. Get lost in the beauty of the music and letting that beauty come out, rather than stressing on a note-perfect performance. When I can do this, the audience just fades away for me and I barely know they are there.

If keeping a strict rhythm with no mistakes for dance music makes you anxious (it does me), then try some song airs or slow airs, where the rhythm is less structured and more open to interpretation.

These are some of the things that work for me. In the end, I play to please myself, and if any others happen to enjoy it too, then that is icing on the cake. Good luck on your musical journey in any event.

Re: Nerves

I think I’ll always have nerves, in a sense. They creep up on me at unexpected times - sometimes I’ll find I’ll get crippling nerves when it’s my turn to sing at an informal singaround, but I might happily have no problem playing a solo gig on the (rare) occasions I’ve played to a sizeable crowd. No rhyme or reason to it. I think relaxing has a lot to do with it and knowing what you’re doing.

Kevin Burke said an interesting thing about nerves. He said that there is actually an element of egotism in nerves. He didn’t mean that people who get stagefright are egotistical or arrogant – what he meant was that you should be thinking about the music, and thinking about how to express it to the listeners (not thinking about yourself). So before playing, think about what it is in the music that makes you want to play it to people. That’s the important stuff.

If you get the mood right, get the rhythm right, it doesn’t matter if you make a mistake. In a sense, if you get all that right, you literally cannot make a mistake. I’ve seen super-talented professional musicians fluff notes but the audience has either not noticed or not cared, because people aren’t there to hear a robotic reproduction of some dots on a page; they’re there to hear a human being play some music that does something to them.

My advice would be to make a list of the simplest, easiest tunes you know - and use them as the basis for your next performance (or whatever form your public playing takes). Example: facing the same issue (nerves), awhile back I started a performance with Harvest Home, went into My Love is But a Lassie Yet, then Soldier’s Joy, then maybe St. Anne’s Reel, then maybe Whiskey Before Breakfast (don’t remember how far I carried the joke, exactly). From a musician’s point of view, not a very interesting, exciting, or otherwise impressive medley, but I was able to get through it without crashing and burning, the audience - regular folks - liked it, and I felt a little more confident by the end than I had at the beginning. Then I played another set of tunes of the same ilk. Etc. Eventually got to a little more challenging stuff, which I probably would have muffed it I’d started off with it. So, I make a point now of swallowing my pride and starting off with the easy, tired old chestnuts, whether in performance or just playing with friends.

Practicing in public helped me get over my performance anxiety. Some people who hear you may be rude and say some not so nice things, but that’s kind of the point of it. Shutting down that part of the brain that cares what others think. But you have to be confident in your own abilities, given, you’re only practicing, and the people who are judging you for not sounding so great don’t even know what you truly sound like. I also 2nd the advice to busk, because, well… Now you’re not practicing. You’re performing, in an unassuming, non-pressurized setting. On the stage, you have all those eyes of judgment staring back at you. But busking, nobody is really paying attention. You’re kind of just there. And if people like what they hear, they’ll just sit and listen, and most of the time do not even bother you. I’ve had people sit literally just 10 ft away and listen, and not say a word. It’s usually the kids who sit that close, but sometimes adults will “sit in my space” too. If they like what they’re hearing they may need a little musical therapy. You never know what the music is doing for them. And knowing that people enjoy your music enough to sit closely and listen is an invaluable experience to boost your confidence and make the anxiety go away.

I had performance anxiety my entire youth, and it didn’t truly start to go away until around 6 years ago when I did start busking. But I practiced in public first before busking.

Re: Nerves

My nerves show up occasionally, and it’s really annoying. But there are a few things that have helped me. First off, see if you can identify specifically what triggers them. For me, it’s not performing in public, it’s performing in front of other musicians that I respect. So it bites me sometimes in Ireland, when I’m sitting in a session with people I don’t know. Another time is when I’m performing at camps in front of nothing but musicians, or sitting on stage with a bunch of musicians that I consider to be better than me, and having to try to play solo.

My nerves have also been known to get in the way for me is when I’m recording. (The dreaded “red light syndrome”). It used to be any time anybody was recording - the player sitting next to me pulling out a recorder to catch a tune in a session used to do it. But over the years, I got to the point where I can whip out a recorder, record a tune for someone in one take and send it on its way… But then I went to record an album - and that added a new layer of anxiety. The only way that I found to really deal with that was to take it head on and just do it.

In both cases, part of my ability to overcome is to identify exactly what it is that gets me tied in knots. There have been a few conversations that I have had with well-respected musicians that really help me too. First off, I had a conversation with a very well known player at a camp once, and I was surprised to learn that he had big jitters every time he stepped on stage. And his advice to me was to simplify what I was playing to get into the flow. For instance, if your ornaments are failing, and screwing up your rhythm, take them out until you feel yourself relaxing and getting into the flow. Another conversation that I had with a great player was centered around identifying the fact that I cared more about the music I was playing when I was in front of other musicians, and her advice was to work on caring that much about the music every time I play, whether it be practicing, sessioning, or performing. Once you get used to putting that level of concentration into the music, it won’t be as apt to derail you.

And finally, a thing that I read just in the last couple of days made a huge amount of sense to me. This comes from the “Laws of Brainjo” site, which was written by a neurologist who also plays (5 string) banjo. It is a fascinating series about how our brains work in relation to learning, remembering, and performing music. In one of his posts dealing with conquering stage fright, he points out that a lot of what makes us able to play music is that we’ve developed specific neural pathways in our brains to handle a lot of the mechanics and mental aspects of playing music. This is related to the idea of “kinesthetic memory” (muscle memory). A lot of the things that we do in the course of our day is relegated to these neural pathways. You don’t have to think about how you brush your teeth, for instance, because you learned how to do it a long time ago, and you’ve put it on “automatic”. So you don’t have any problem, say, brushing your teeth, and humming a tune at the same time. Or thinking about what you want to say in your morning meeting with your boss while you’re eating your cereal… A lot of what “learning to play music” is doing is building the neural pathways necessary to put a lot of the mechanics of playing music on autopilot. You don’t have to think where you need to put your finger on the fret, and how hard you need to push. Or which direction your bellows need to be going for that C natural. Or how to form the embouchure to get a tone out of your flute. You’ve learned how to do those things, and the neural pathways that have been developed for those things have long been used to do it automatically.

But he also points out that when you care more about it, your conscious brain wants to try to help and make sure that everything goes smoothly, which will invariably have the opposite effect. So in this case, he suggests trying to calm yourself by focusing on the flow of the music, and NOT on the fact that you’re the one playing it.

If you want to read that specific article, it’s here: https://clawhammerbanjo.net/the-immutable-laws-of-brainjo-the-art-and-science-of-effective-practice-episode-16/

Re: Nerves

Matt, I run a weekly tune learning session. So for every tune I teach, there’s a moment where every person there pulls out a recorder and plops it on the table so that I can record the tune for them. So I really had to find a way past the “red light syndrome”. There are a couple of things that helped me do that.

First off, I used to record the tune myself, and send it to them, instead of letting them record me. I would still get the playing issues when I was recording myself, but I would take solace in the fact that if I stumbled in part of the tune, I could play that part again and edit the file before sending it. Just the very fact of not caring as much if I messed up made it so that more often than not, I would make it through just fine, because I knew that I had a “safety net”.

From there, I got comfortable enough knowing that I could do it, that I would let people record me - and since it was an informal situation, I could make them all stop and delete the recording if things went horribly wrong. I think I maybe only did that once. But just the act of recording a bunch made it so that I don’t even think about it anymore in that situation. It certainly helps that in that particular situation, I am playing fairly slow, steady, and somewhat basic settings of the tunes.

One thing you have to do in general when playing music is to let go of the past, and stay focused on the present and the future. If you make a mistake in a session, there are basically no repercussions. Maybe nobody noticed, but even if they did, the music keeps going because it really doesn’t matter. You need to take on that same kind of attitude when you’re recording. But our brains are telling us that what we play is more important when we’re being recorded, because there’s a more permanent record of it.

The first time I ever hit the really big nerves was a gig I did many years ago, where my hands felt like they just weren’t functioning. It was by far the biggest gig of my “career” at that point, and I couldn’t get my pick motion smooth, and it felt like I was going to drop my pick, so I was gripping it harder. Something was blocking the simplest of motions. And it was all in my head, of course. The idea put forth in that article about it being our conscious mind trying to lend a hand to make sure everything goes to plan really resonated with me. Reading that was a bit like the proverbial light bulb going off over my head. It totally makes sense. And even though I hadn’t read that article until this week, I realized that the author’s suggestion of focusing on the music, instead of focusing on myself is exactly how I have learned to deal with it. I sometimes close my eyes, which allows me to focus on the music. And I try to let go of the past. If I missed a triplet, instead of spending time worrying about that, I try to stay focused on what comes next. Otherwise, it can be a pretty serious downward spiral.

There are some good tips in this discussion. Relaxation, breathing, focusing on the music and not the situation, and letting go of the past are all things that can really help!

One more thing. With instrumental music, I find it is even more important to properly warm up than with singing. It’s tough, and it’s one of those cases where no advice is probably much good… I started playing in public at the age of 42, with my two teenage, nerveless kids. Grr. One duff note played on a Friday night kept me mortified until at least next Tuesday. It took me years to realise several things. First, open your eyes and look at the listeners. Second, understand that everyone bar none, in the band or in the crowd, is willing you on. Third, no-one gives a flying f*rt about your mistakes. Fourth, most people listening don’t even REALISE that you’ve just made a mistake! Fifth (ok, bit of ego…), most people listening can’t even get near to your skill level on your axe, no matter how flawed. Sixth, if you’re not out there to enjoy yourself, well what the hell! Seventh, if you’re enjoying yourself you are also increasing the enjoyment of everyone else there. Yep, know your tunes in order to decrease the chance of screwing up. That helps. But screwing up can also be the best laugh that you, your mates and the audience have had all week if you play it right!

Nerves are a necessary part of performance for most people. The main thing is to accept their existence and try to build confidence during a performance by maintaining a flow and rhythm appropriate to what you are playing. In so many cases your mistakes won’t get picked up to anything like the extent that you think.

One tactic that I will shamelessly admit to (all in the past, obviously!!!) - as a guitar accompanist, if I made a mistake on the first round of a tune, I would repeat the mistake or a version of it, to make it look like it was deliberate. The sad thing is that a huge percentage of the average audience won’t notice AT ALL;m and those that do may well think that you are thinking “outside the box”…

Having said that, as more of a back-line musician I rarely have the upfront situation of melody players for whom mistakes can be a lot more obvious! The answer in so many cases is PRACTICE. Sorry!
