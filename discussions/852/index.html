Re: Flute question - help!

About humidity: in Chicago we get everything from 20% to 80%+ humidity. My unscientific experience is that my wooden flutes are “happier” when they’re properly hydrated. So, in the dry wheather, i keep a small piece of damp cloth in the case with my wood head (without it touching the wood). I keep my piccolo’s case inside an airtight “ziplock” bag, to preserve the humidity that’s generated from playing. When the humidity of the air is at least 50%, i don’t need to do anything special to keep them happy.

Some say that oiling the flute every other month also helps preserve proper hydration. Others say oiling does nothing at all. Others say oiling is bad for the flute… I oil when the wood stops being shiny; expect for my piccolo head, which the maker (Eldred Spell) asked me not to oil, so i don’t.

I have a cheap hygrometer from Radio Shack, to monitor all this.

I don’t think this is your problem, or it would get better with playing, not worse.

I would suspect, Joyce, that if you are playing every day or almost every day, then your flute should be adequately hydrated. So that is probably not the issue. I would say that you are simply in the midst of building your chops. What you should also note is that, by the time you get to the end of a set, not only is your embrochure failing, but your entire demeanor and posture is probably compromised as well. Even the most hardy embrochure won’t work if you’re smashing the flute to your face and on the verge hyperventaliting.

If I start to feel strained and exhausted in the middle of a set, which happens quite a bit of the time for me still, I simply pause for a few bars to collect myself and relax, then i jump back with a whoppinly refreshed tone. You will find, after a while, the pauses will be less and less. It is not good to reinforce bad habits by forcing yourself to perform when the umph isn’t there.

As one final bit of advice: if you smoke, quit. Those things will kill ya. ;-}

Joyce - humidity does have an effect on the instrument and a high relative humidity will make the flut sound better. This is probably because any varitaions on the smoothness of the bore and the degree of porosity of the wood is affected.

The comment you make about kung power is really not an issue - Matt Molloy only has one lung having had the other removed as a result of TB and Desi Wilkinson suffers seriously from Asthma (as do I). Breath control is all about the diaphragm. In a way it’s a bit like T’ai Chi breathing!

I would guess that the reason you are losing out on tone has a lot to do with your lips and facial muscle control - practice, practice, practice and it should improve.

On the question of oiling the flute? I used to oil it about once a month with almond oil and that certainly had a short term improvement in tone quality. More recently, however, a lot of flute players over here don’t bother with that - just a clean out with a cloth at the end of a session to remove the gunge. Seems to work as my Monzani is currently playing the best it’s ever done for me.

Good luck with it all anyway!

Almond and other oils

Breandan, the problem with almond oil is that it doesn’t dry, so it doesn’t stay in the flute. So it does help, but you have to use it more often than once a month. When i used almond oil, i did a major oiling every week, and swabbed the headjoint with an oily cloth every day. Using a drying oil (like linseed, or bore oil), you can do it every other month or so. I’ve been using http://www.doctorsprod.com (also a drying oil).

I think when you start playing a wooden flute, it takes about 20-30 min for the wood to get impregnated with moisture, and it plays nicer after that. When it’s oiled, it takes less time to reach this stage. Other than that, the oil doens’t seem to affect the sound much.

As to whether it protects the flute from cracking or not, there are strong opinions all along the continuum; take your pick… another hot topic is whether petrol-derivates (e.g. clarinet bore oil) are good or bad for the wood.

↳ Posted by breandan on September 4th, 2002.

Linseed

Yikes! I’ve never used linseed because it has a reputation for being hard to use. I’d recommend almond oil if you want a non-drying oil, or the one i’ve been using (see URL above) if you want a drying oil. Another option for drying oil is clarinet bore oil, as long as you don’t hold the option that petrolleum derivates “rot” the wood. So choose your opinions first, then choose your oil. :-)

The advantage of non-drying oil (e.g.: almond) is that it’s very forgiving. It won’t stain, and it won’t stay there forever (you’ll gradually take it off when you swab the flute!). Other people have used successfully extra-virgin olive oil and grapeseed oil.

The advantage of drying oil is that you don’t have to use it so often.

If you’re using a drying oil, it’s a good idea to take the headjoint apart (take the cork off) when you oil, so you can reach the whole area.

There seems to be little or no advantage on oiling the flute’s body (as opposed to the headjoint), as far as the sound is concerned. But if you believe the oil helps prevent cracking, then this may be a reason to oil the body. One of the people you should ask is the flute maker (if alive!).

Whenever i see “extra virgin” olive oil, it makes me think of people who get “slightly pregnant”. :-)

Re: Flute question - help!

This is a reply to Joyce’s original question about losing tone after a few minutes. Having experienced this off and on over the years, I thought I’d take a stab at it.

In my experience this problem is not with the flute, and no matter how many times you oil, check for leaks, change the orientation of embouchure and finger holes or swab, it doesn’t really get any better, unless there really was a leak.

There are several factors at work here, but the main one is embouchure and stamina. You start off blowing really well, and it sounds great, but your lips don’t have the muscles to sustain the effort for very long. Or, you might be straining to get that great tone and putting your lips into an unnatural (for you) embouchure. The solution is practice -- blowing long tones, whistle tones, octave jumping using just the embouchure, and lots of tunes. All of these things will promote stamina, and more importantly, lead to a more efficient and stable embouchure.

Two other things that affect your ability to sustain good tone for long periods are volume and nerves. Playing in a noisy session, or a room with lots of dampening materials (curtains, carpets) can lead you to believe that you’re not playing loud enough. You compensate by trying to play louder, and end up wearing yourself out quite soon. I’ve heard several flute teachers over the years say, “blow for tone, not volume”. This is easier said than done, but it’s great advice. Try to learn to get a sense of how much effort you’re putting into blowing, and pace yourself a bit.

Finally, nervousness accentuates any embouchure difficulties you might have, and causes you to try to play too loud and too fast, which is usually a recipe for disaster. Try to relax ;-) . Know your tunes, your flute and your session mates, and try to just enjoy playing. You might want to just accept that nervousness is going to affect the way you play, and that it will become less of a factor over time.

Hope this helps!

Greg

Re: Flute question - help!

On the sublect of losing tone after a couple of tunes. I suffered the frustration of this for a long time and explored most of the suggestions made in this discussion already. They all had some impact for me and I learned a lot from all the messing around but my fix (and the problem is completely gone!) was to widen the gap between my lips. I was happy with the tone I had when I started playing so I had thought that I must be basically OK on embouchure. I think now that the airstream was too tightly focussed and too difficult to maintain. The change I made feels like moving my upper lip slightly outward. The shape of the gap between the lips is still pretty narrow but it feels more o-shaped. After adapting to this (not much involved in that) I have consistent and much-improved (I think!) tone overall, a more integrated sound over the range.

The other point that I think is important is to be sure that flute is properly lined up with the gap between your lips. This is often not central and changing the flute position to properly align with the lips is very important. Check with a mirror if you haven’t already.

I hope this helps because I know the frustration!
