Re: Slip jigs

Slip jigs go over well around here, but there’s not nearly the common repertoire of them that there is of reels or jigs.
Kid on the Mountain is popular, of course, and the Butterfly (which I could do without, but it’s fun to have the whole gang bashing away at it). The main thing for me is that I don’t like them to be rushed. I don’t like any tunes to be rushed, but it seems to be especially important for slipjigs. There’s a tendency to just hit the one-two-three and not really get into the actual tune. Try to keep hold of the beat, so you don’t get dragged into the downhill motor race.

I’ve been pushing a few slip jigs lately, but I only know the names of two of them, Hardiman the Fiddler and Dever the Dancer. The other two are called something like Humors of Whiskey or Drops of Brandy or Tankards of Tequila or something like that, and there are so many tunes by those names that I just assume they’re one step from “gan ainm”.

Anyway, if there’s a tune you like and you can carry it, play it. If someone doesn’t know it, maybe they’ll learn it. It’s good to bring in new material, as long as you’re also playing stuff other people know as well. Maybe you should just build up a repertoire of slip jigs and play a new one every once in a while - eventually, people will learn them.

Re: Slip jigs

If you start a tune at a session and nobody joins in, you’re pretty much dead in the water.

What do you do? Do you bravely carry on,solo-ing it through the standard three repetitions, or do you terminate it after one time through, mumbling some words of apology?

Either way, it’s a little embarassing.

So here’s my tip. Persuade a couple of other musicians who normally atttend your session to learn your desired tune. Make it easy for them by sending them a good recording of it it - maybe supported with a score or abcs.

Then later when you start that tune at your session, you’ll have two other people joining in. The rest of them will be fooled into to thinking that it’s a newly fashionable tune, and that they need to learn it. Pride is everything!

Repeat this over the course of the next few sessions, and they will. Your tune will then have achieved the status of being a “standard” at your local session!

First off, a jig / reel / slip jig is still a jig / reel / slip jig to a bodhran player. A session where a good bodhran player, who is there regularly, does not join in, is one I would rather avoid, even if they are leaving you on your own to get to know your style, it’s not the Spanish inquisition and it’s not a performance, most of us playing for any amount of time could fairly handy organise a solo performance somewhere or attend classes where a teacher makes you play the tune and inspects your technique. even those of us who record our progress on youtube. The point is, unless you know ahead of time that nobody, including a bodhran player or a guitarist who either can hear what key you’re in or is prompted at the beginning, is going to leave you high and dry for the first tune you play that night, it’s going to un nerve and unsettle even the best musician in my opinion.

That said, if a gathering of friends who play together regularly and are all melody instrument players are trying to keep the session fresh and one suggests a tune the others don’t know, they say go ahead and do you mind if I record you playing it, that creates a great sense of everyone wanting to be able to play that tune. Some may even try and play it the 3rd time through if they’re confident that it won’t interfere with the tune.

So like a lot of other things, it depends on how you feel at the time if you are starting a tune and nobody is joining in, you can always tell with a fair degree of certainty whether you are being judged on your ability to play a tune that at least one other player knows how to play or whether you have actually struck up a tune that nobody else wants to hear.

"1) What would everybody else like to play?
2) What would everybody else like to learn to play?
3) What would I like to play?"

This are good rules. And if, having considered these rules, you end up playing something on your own, it’s probably because you think it’s something others would want to learn, or because it’s something you’d like to play. And if you get along well with the people who you play with, well, even the latter is likely to be a tune they’ll want to hear.
On the other hand, if you’re at a session where the set of material defined by “what I want to play” and the set defined by “what everyone else wants to play, learn, or at least hear” do not have a pretty large intersection, then maybe you’re at the wong session.

So if you find that you’re feeling embarrassed by what you choose to play, it might indicate that you’re at the wrong session, and you need to find or start a different one.
But if you’re at the right session for you, you should go ahead and play the tune that seems best at the moment, and trust that it’ll fall into one of those three, and that your tune picker will weight its selections correctly, to favor criteria 1, 2, and 3 in that order.

Re: Slip jigs

“If you end up playing a solo without anyone else even attempting to join in you have (intentionally or unintentionally) gone against the inclusive nature of the session.”

Mix, I think your notion of inclusion is a bit skewed. Inclusion isn’t about every person playing on every single tune.

I bend over backwards to play tunes everyone else knows at our local session (and I *do* know what’s in each person’s repertoire). But that doesn’t stop me from playing a tune that only Bob knows, or another one that only Sally can join in on. And I occasionally play tunes that I know I will play by myself because they’re such good tunes I want to introduce them to the session.

Being inclusive also means that I play tunes with visitors when they sit in, even when they play tunes that no one else in our circle knows. Being inclusive also means listening when someone brings an unfamiliar tune to our session and plays it 3 or 4 times through.

Besides, not all sessions are that inclusive. It would be rude as a visitor to assume that every session should be as welcoming as the last. Some sessions will ask a newcomer to start a set. But some sessions won’t, and that’s their prerogative. Some sessions will choose tunes and tempos to suit the lowest common denominator, and some sessions won’t.

To add to Will’s response, I would say if you’ve actually thought about those things and you’re playing with people who like the same sorts of tunes you do, you’re likely to play a tune and have nobody pick up on it from time to time, and that’s fine.

If you’re in the right place, it will work.
you can help it work by following an unknown tune with a few well-known ones, and by making sure that you play the unknown tune as well as it can be played, to give people the tune at its best, and by never ever feeling embarrassed about or apologizing for playing something that you honestly like, and think that everyone else will like and eventually want to play.

I suspect that it’s got a lot to do with your motivation. If your motivation is to share something that’s really neat, that will come through, and people will enjoy it, and they will enjoy playing it with you when they learn it. If your motivation is to get a chance to “play a solo” (an interesting choice of words, and an ambiguous one) and show off your own personal skills and the depth of your collection of tunes, then that will come through as well, and you’ll be rightly considered a jackass.

And, yeah, mostly you should play tunes that everyone knows, or at least tunes that someone in particular knows (Will’s “Bob” and “Sally”)

We’re not necessarily talking about a lowest common denominator situation and I do realise that “inclusiveness” isn’t compulsory(as Will has said). However, it does make for a much more enjoyable experience if there is some common ground rather than having one or two people at a time playing on their own while everyone else “sits on their hands”.

A couple of years ago, I visited a regular session(in Perthshire…sadly now defunct) with a very good reputation which was being led that night by highly experienced fiddler from one of Scotland’s top bands. Although I know he has a huge repertoire, he chose many tunes with which he knew or guessed that most of us there would have been familiar.

There was no “dumbing down”, however, either in terms of the choice of tunes, i.e. no ‘cliches’, overplayed etc or the speed in which they were executed… all at brisk pace when and where necessary. Also, as far as the punters were concerned, it was just a great night of good (mostly) Scottish music. So, everyone was happy.

Re: Slip jigs

That “Kitty come down from Limerick” example is interesting because it shows Barney on the banjo doing something that was common more than 200 years ago. A lot of 18th century copies of 9/8 jigs from Scotland and England are in a simple variation form that looks like somebody simply wrote down what the piper or fiddler made up on the spot (maybe polished a bit on repeated tries). They tend to have a fixed format of 4-bar phrases where bars 2 and 4 never change, but each new part introduces a new tweak on bars 1 and 3, without changing the rhythm or introducing anything that might mean a change of pace. And like Barney’s set they are unusually long, much longer than the typical multi-part tune.

That makes a lot of sense for a “longways for as many as will” dance with one player doing the music, but fits a session about as well as an interlude of rattlesnake handling. Which is sad because it’s one of the few places in the traditional music of the British Isles where substantial improvisation was the norm.

@ Jon Kiparksky - When I said “solo-ing” a tune, I was just using the word objectively - just shorthand for the situation of starting a tune only to find that nobody else joins in because they don’t know it. I would agree though that it all rather depends on the type of session concerned. In a small “kitchen-type” session there’s plenty of scope for discussion and trying out new tunes - possibly with just one person playing.

They feature quite a lot in the sessions I go to in Durham (England). In one of the pubs the centre of gravity is Irish music, in the other it’s Northumbrian and the old Border tradition, and slip-jigs happen to be plentiful in both.

Irish slip-jigs that turn up often enough are The Foxhunters’, The Kid On The Mountain, An Phis Phliuch and others I’m sure are Irish but can’t put names to. Farewell To Whalley Range gets in - nice tune, I’ve recently learned it. (Well, sort of…)

Northumbrian / Border ones include The Peacock Followed The Hen, Four Bare Legs Together, Kiss Her Under The Coverlet, A Mile To Ride and Drops Of Brandy (both of which seem to have originated this side of the water), and My Laddie Sits Ower Late Up, which local piper Paul Martin turns from an (IMO) merely rather quaint basic tune into a rousing multi-part one by the addition of parts culled from one or other of the tomes of c18-19 music that have surfaced in this part of the world; unless he made them up himself, that is.

I get the impression these pre-Victorian Northumbrians were a fairly basic lot.
