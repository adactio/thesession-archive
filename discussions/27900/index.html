Re: Learnability?

Some tunes consist largely of a phrase or two repeated using different intervals. Once you have learned and memorised the phrase, it is relatively easy to adapt it to the basic ‘chord changes’ (for want of a better explanation) and the whole tune is easy to internalise. Tunes like The Boys of Bluehill (come to think of it, lots of hornpipes are like this) and The Kid on the Mountain. Other tunes, eg Berehaven, have hardly any repeated phrases, and are much more difficult to memorise; although I find that the latter sort are much more interesting to play once you get the hang of them.
And of course some tunes are easy to memorise but have tricky fingering (depending on the instrument) in them which makes them hard to play, and other tunes that are hard to memorise are quite easy to play.

Re: Learnability?

“My whole music life has been about becoming a more efficient learner. The faster i can learn the more music i can enjoy in my lifetime”

There’s a problem here with regards to this music and I see it a lot. I usually refer to it as the philatelist approach. It’s about having a collector’s mindset rather than one who can simply enjoy the moment. You see it a lot also in birdwatchers. It’s about lists. Twitchers, as they are called, will travel miles at great expense to see a species of bird they have never seen, but is very similar to a species of bird they may see every day in their own back garden. They make lists of species and tick them off.

And a great many diddley players do the same with tunes. Their goal is in the acquisition and not the simple enjoyment of the moment.

The other day I watched a carrion crow (a very common and unremarkable looking bird round these parts) in a town park do an extraordinary thing. It had found a discarded kebab still wrapped in its newspaper parcel. The bird shook the parcel until the pita fell out. Then it shook the pita until its contents fell out - meat, onions, salad, chili sauce. Next, very carefully and precisely, using its feet and beak, it opened out the empty pita and laid it flat on the grass. Then, with remarkable deliberacy, it picked out all the pieces of meat, shaking the salad etc from them and placing them in the middle of the pita. It then folded the pita into a parcel and flew off with it.

Now, a twitcher would never be able to experience such a wonder because they are simply not interested in the carrion crows on their doorstep. They are more interested in catching a glimpse of a much rarer crow just sat in a tree doing nothing.

And the philatelists of the diddley world never get to be really good players because they never give enough time to really dig into tunes. They frown on familiarity where familiarity should be their goal.

“The faster i can learn, the more music i can enjoy in my lifetime”

The trouble with this is that you are missing the central part of what this music is, namely its ability to constantly give surprise. And the more familiar you are with a tune the more you are able to recognise, appreciate, and express this surprise.

Re: Learnability?

I’ve been trying to explain to a pupil who is enthusiastically attempting to increase her repertoire that there is actually no such thing as having ‘learned’ a tune.

She’s been depending on what I prefer to consider as the lepidopterist’s approach, convinced that ‘memorising’ a series of notes, intervals, and phrases, means that the tune has been ‘learned’. It has its place on the board, with a neat label, but the big pin straight through its heart means it will never fly again.

The problem with collecting tunes as if they’re butterflies is that once they’re pinned down to the board, there’s no room for another butterfly of the same species with slightly different markings, a wider wingspan, a more impressive body. It just sits in the ‘collection’, decomposing, while the collector is running around the fields with a net looking for another specimen that would fit nicely next to it.

Try to take photographs of the butterflies in their natural habitat, rather than hunting them down, and killing and preserving their cold, dead bodies. It’s better to have a dozen beautiful little common butterflies flying around your garden than a hundred of their more magnificent dead cousins rotting in a collection in your study.

Re: Learnability?

Going back to learnability, certainly some tunes are harder to learn than others. Some tunes have note sequences that are physically awkward to finger or bow, so might take longer to learn, but that is about learning technique , not learning tunes. But some tune are hard to learn as tunes. For me it’s all about patterns. There are certain common note sequences and phrases that turn up a lot. When a tune uses a lot of those standard phrases it’s usually easy to learn. But when a tune starts into a standard phrase, then does something unexpected half way through, those are the tunes that are always hard to learn for me. It takes time to train my brain and fingers not to automatically complete the standard phrase.

As far as stopping lessons goes, the sooner you stop taking lessons, the sooner you’ll develop an individual style. A few lessons at the start are pretty much essential so that you don’t start off with a bow grip that will hold you back later on, or other serious bad habits. But as soon as you’ve got that sorted then you need to start developing in your own way. Fiddling isn’t like classical violin, where you have to learn to do everyting in exactly the same way as all the other violins in the orchestra; with the fiddle individuality is crucial. So as long as you keep listening to top players, so that you always have a clear idea of what sort of sound you are trying to achieve, then you will find your own techniques for creating those sounds.

“Aye prof …. and they’ll all be coming here … to ”learn“ tunes. It’s depressing.”

What’s depressing about it? Things have changed considerably since you sat down copying your favourite Bothy Band albums, Mr Gill.

Sometime towards the end of the 90s, I decided to take a wee cycling jaunt, it being a glorious sunny day. I also noted that Rothesay folk festival was on, so I thought I’d encompass that on the trip.
Up the Clyde coast to Gourock, across to Dunoon - met a cycling buddy coming the other way - up by Clacaig, Glenlean, and that steep ascent after coming round the head of Loch Striven. Down to Colintraive, over the wee ferry to Bute, great weather, great ride. I arrive in Rothesay, and find a bench by the pier. Two blokes meet up in front of me. One, with a heavy Fife accent, a large beer gut, and smelling of the stuff that caused that gut, say “which ones have you done so far this year?”. “Oh this is only my third”, says the other. “Ah weel”, says beer gut, “I’ve done quite a few, I didn’t see you at ‘Muchty, this year eh”. By this time, it dawned on me that these guys were the ’twitcher/trainspotters’ of the folkie world. It also dawned on me that these guys were missing such a glorious day by bevvying their way through another festival to add to their list. So I got straight on the ferry to Wemyss Bay, somewhat disillusioned by the whole festival scene.

Anyway, unlike a board of pinned butterflies, the tunes on this site can be put to more use than an addition to a collection. They can fly again. Ah, those dreaded dots and ABCs, you might say.

Long ago in the midsts of time, we were having a session after a Boys of the Lough gig. Most of the band were at the session, and we were playing a cheesy Finnish waltz that I’d learned from some friends in Nordby, on the island of Fanø, in Denmark. It was played quite differently than the way it’s usually played in Finland. Cathal McConnell took a shine to it - even though we were playing it as a deliberately cheesy waltz. “Have you got the tadpoles for that tune?”, he asks. “No, but I can get them for you”, says I. seemingly, Cathal doesn’t read music, but the plan was to get Dave Richardson to play the tune, and he’d learn it from that. So next evening, I caught up with Cathal and gave him a copy of Nils Thorlund’s manuscript. The tune appeared on the band’s next album - and a version was also recorded by Kevin Burke - quite likely from the same source, as it was the same way they play it on Fanø. Much later, I was walking along a beach on Iona with an Australian friend, and she started diddling that same tune. “Where did you get that?” I asked. “Oh, I got it from some friends in Tasmania, and they got it from Cathal McConnell”.
So that tune had been to the other side of the world and back, yet the seed had been planted by those tadpoles.
Cathal very much stamps his identity on the tune, but for him, it came from our session and the scrap of paper with the dots on it. the tune flew again from those dots - unlike a gassed and pinned Camberwell Beauty, and on the clip I linked to earlier, was a tune that was learned from this site - flying again.

Don’t knock it. Those old Bothy Band albums are not the only way to learn tunes - and if some people never really learn them but collect the dots, well that’s up to them. It doesn’t stop others from making good use of them and helping them fly again. It’s no more depressing than watching a corbie pull a pitta bread out of a poke (which must have been quite entertaining).

Re: Learnability?

if you are talking about starting in adulthood, you CAN develop your ability to hear and learn music much, much farther than you or stupid, unimaginative people who started training (often classically) in childhood and thus have extremely uninformed prejudices about this issue, might think possible.

the question has not been properly tested because the great majority of adult starters do not end up sticking with it long enough, or putting in enough practicing/listening/exposure-to-master-players time, to properly measure what can be done. this is often due to time commitments, but also often due to discomfort and impatience with being a beginner and being lame, which you are for a long time.

for this discussion, we’ll stipulate, dance-based world folk music---unless you are one of the teensy fraction of people with literally NO feeling for music (and there is a teensy fraction), you can do it. you don’t have to be genius or extremely gifted. you have to have SOME feeling for music, and the vast majority of people who love a musical genre enough to feel compelled to learn it do have this.

given that measure of feeling for music, you have to do your best to reproduce how kids get it, and how they get it, is, informed technique teaching, plus proper practice approaches, plus, constant exposure through seeing it played by master players, and hearing it played through records. gobs and gobs and gobs of that. if you don’t live in the milieu where it’s in the air you breathe, you have to reproduce it the best you can by making recordings part of the very air you breathe. the youtube era presents you with an unbelievable chance to get that crucial visual component of the exposure issue.

at some point if you are doing all that stuff, something happens, and it happens at different rates for different brains. your brain starts to identify structural patterns in the music in your chosen genre. first the less notey stuff becomes easier to pick up. then the notier stuff starts to become, if not a snap, less of an impenetrable thicket. if you can stay with it long enough for the technique part to be less of a hurdle, and on top of that if you have love, imagination, and empathy, the feeling part of it will also start to come. i guess some people might thing you’ve “arrived” when you start to get the technique down, but actually that is the real beginning of the journey. the previous hurdles were just the introductory chapters…..
