Re: fiddle bowing?

Royston this is a big subject. Fiddlers use bowing to give shape to the tunes. Although lots of different bowings are used, players tend to emphasise the strong beats (1st and to a lesser extent 4th notes of six) to give a pulse to the music Sometimes regional or national styles can be distinguised by the more frequent use of detatched or slurred playing e.g. Donegal v Clare, or Cape Breton v Sligo, but individual players within these traditions also break the “rules” when it suits, and there are plenty of atypical players in each tradition. And then there is slurring across bars !

As we always say on the mustard board listen carefully to recordings, watch youtube videos of good players, get a good teacher, sit next to the best players you can and watch, listen and learn.

Personally I find the totally hacked bowing you describe wearing on the ear, I think if you do as I have suggested above it will help you to develop your own personal style within the tradition.

Re: fiddle bowing?

Quite often, on this discussion forum, questions are asked that are really non-questions. A non-question is a peculiar thing. It’s about asking something which on its face value, makes reasonable sense, except that when you look into it, one requires a little knowledge of something more fundamental about the topic.

To show what I mean, here’s an example of what would constitute a “non-question”:

Q. “What happened before the big bang?”
It’s a non-question because time started at the big bang and the word “before” is about time, and since time started at the big bang, the concept of “before” is non existent.

This is a good analogy that helps:
Q. “What is north of north?”
Where ever you are on the planet, one can travel north and everyone doing so will meet at the north pole. And there is nothing north of north. Of course, there is a vague notion that to travel north is to travel upwards, but when you get to the north pole and follow this notion, you’ll be going up into the sky. Which is really a different dimension.

So “what is the best bowing to do for jigs?” is a non-question. Get over it and think in a different dimension.

Re: fiddle bowing?

Not patterns exactly, but there are slurring techniques that can help give your playing some real pulse.

Royston, don’t know your level but in case you don’t know, slurring is when you play two or more notes with one stroke of the bow, rather than playing each note with a different bow stroke.

A general principle is to slur INTO the downbeat, which for jigs are often notes 1 and 4.

| 123 456 | 123 456 | 123 456 |

So your bowing might go like this. The slurs are in parentheses. Cap letters=downbeat. Note slurs can be either up bow or down bow.

| Du(d D)u(d | D)ud Ud(u | U)du dud |

You can slur more than two notes at a time, but three or four is about the limit to what I’ve heard in This Music. The important thing is to not slur mechanically all the time – it’s a principle, not a pattern. You have to follow the individual path of a tune which will have it’s own ‘key’ notes to emphasize that you’ll have to learn to listen for. What makes it even harder to pin down – and more fun – is that those key notes may shift around depending on how you want to play the tune at any given time.

Reading back what I’ve written seems confusing – hope I haven’t botched it but it may serve to show you that there ARE some bowing techniques that can help. Best thing to do would be to seek out a real live person who knows what they’re doing and have them help you. It’s easier to understand one-on-one. Good luck!

Re: fiddle bowing?

The attitude about bowing for classical music is quite a bit different. The composer (or arranger) has very specific techniques in mind and the details of the bowing is indicated. It is no accident that string players bow together in an orchestra.

For ITM, I agree with the idea that the musical result is what drives the choice of bowing. Over time, a fiddler learns a variety of patterns and what they can do, and fits what works to create the sound they want at the moment. It becomes fairly instinctive, and is done without much thought. I vary bowing depending on who else is playing, the speed, the mood, and lots of other things.

Llig, I think you were confusing “non-questions” with “beginner’s questions”. Not everybody who asks “why is the sky blue” wants the explanation that gets into quantum physics equations.

When Royston asked “what’s the best bowing for jigs” he really means: “can you suggest a/some good bowing patterns for jigs?”… which is a perfectly reasonable question.

I think it’s perfectly acceptable to teach beginners specific bowing patterns for tunes. Just like kids often learn how to draw by tracing comics, giving a specific bowing pattern gets the physical mechanics of bowing under a beginner’s hand--which is no small challenge… Then they can choose to use it or not.

In fact, since I started teaching specific bowing patterns to my students a few years ago, I’ve noticed a generally noticeable uptick in the quality of their bowing.
