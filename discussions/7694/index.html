Re: How far should the boundaries of Irish music be pushed?

“non-trad instrument”

Ah yes but ‘traditional’ to whom? I’d contend that the guitar is a VERY traditional instrument and so is the zouk.

Once upon a time the citern was known as the ‘English Guitar’ because it was once so common in these islands (I don’t know if ‘English’ here is a non-british-person’s generalisation for Britain or specifically means England). Now that begs a question… with the convergent evolution of the modern Mandola, zouk and the citern (bearing in mind the former two can come in 5-course varieties and the latter occassionally comes in a four-course variety) when is a zouk not a citern, or when is a Mandola not a zouk?

Also, the violin from the 14th century is a very differnt beast from the violin of today and the flute has gone through many revolutions in its time, the current most popular version being comparatively modern.

What I’m saying is that even so-called ‘traditional’ instruments themselves evolve and sound different over time and so does the music (as can be seen from the number of variations on tunes).

Flook can do what they want, when and where they want--I bought one of their albums after hearing one of their more traditional-sounding tunes on the radio, and myself will not buy any more, although I respect their energy and musical ability. ↳ Posted by tulloch on September 9th, 2005.

Re: How far should the boundaries of Irish music be pushed?

John J,
Good point about “arrangements” entering sessions. While a lot of the groups I listen to swap things around and make it interesting (such as zouk and fiddles playing the A, while accordion and guitar play the B, and then they all pile on the next time around--adding harmonies and guitar interludes, etc), if too much of that “arranging” enters the session, it can tend to erode the communal nature of the event (for example: “hey, didn’t you know the flute was supposed to sit out the second time through the tune?”).
Sessions should, in general, strive to remain open and inclusive--and sticking to everyone playing the tunes in unison is an important part of that. Sure an occasional harmony or trick will crop up and even be fun, but too much of that is not necessarily a good thing.

It depends on who is doing the pushing. Somebody like Donal Lunny can produce a Coolfin album with lots of non-trad touches, and it’s a good thing, because he really knows Irish music.
