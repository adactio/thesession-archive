It’s true, my survey is quite short. One reason is that SurveyMonkey charges a fee to be able to include more than 10 questions on a survey, and I can’t afford to pay.

For this particular class, the fieldwork project is one of several projects we have been assigned. Because of time constraints, it is meant to be a taste of the process and problems of doing fieldwork. I chose a web-based community exactly because “fieldwork” isn’t necessarily done in a physical field anymore. Rene T.A. Lysloff did (much better than I) research on an online community of musicians. Check out his article “Musical Community on the Internet: An On-Line Ethnography.”

I very much appreciate the response to my survey. I was worried about how many people would take the time to fill it out, and I was pleasantly surprised at how quickly I began getting completed surveys.

Your criticisms are very much appreciated. This is a learning process, and the feedback I’ve received from all of you will be a tremendous help in writing about my fieldwork experience and in all of my future projects.

One criticism I’d specifically like to address is “Do you know a fecking thing about Irish music?”

Not nearly as much as any of you. My main research area is Hindustani (North Indian) classical music. This project was a chance to step outside my comfort zone and learn about a type of music that I know very little about, but am genuinely interested in learning more about.

I’ve wondered about some of the things ceolachan described, if the internet has changed anything. I’m a member of a couple other online forums and I often see people posting links to surveys they’re doing for some class project or dissertation. Usually such surveys aren’t interested in online communities themselves as pertaining to whatever they’re researching; they are just using the online community as an easy (lazy?) way of exposing the survey to lots of people. Most of the surveys look like they were scrapped together in haste (which happens. I had 1000+ footnotes that were scrapped together in haste, but nevermind).

When I took this undergrad psych course in quantitative research methods, we could not even send our surveys out into the world without them being reviewed and commented on by the prof. There was also an ethics review for our entire study, including whatever surveys it may require, before we could subject live research participants to anything. And as I said, this was all at the undergraduate level. Regardless of the degree, however, the ethics review was a legal requirement before any study using live participants could be carried out. Our profs would not let us use a survey with rubbish or overly vague questions. After all, the point of the class was in part teaching us how to formulate and analyse survey questions.

Have the intervening six or seven years and lots more internet changed all that? Can you throw together a halfassed survey in five minutes and just post it on the web? Does the fact that your participants are (often) anonymous posters on a website who potentially live on the other side of the planet make a difference? If so, what? There must be a meta-study in here somewhere.

Absolutely, TSS!

I taught survey construction at Higher National level as part of various courses over several years. Obviously, these weren’t of the same depth as those forming part of degree or postgraduate level qualifications, but rigour was still a major component.
