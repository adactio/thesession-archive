Can high self-esteem damage traditions? ~ delusion & expectations of income

I love watching things like “Pop-Idol” and “X-Factor” in their early stages, all those wanna-bes, people who are clueless to how bad they are and who think they deserve to be the next big thing in that area of music, the watch-mes, the spoiled precosious brats grown up ~ no, defintely not grown up, lacking any real signs of ‘maturity’ in the area of reason… They remind me of a number of Irish musicians it has been my discomfort to either bump into, doorstep us, or find ourselves in the position of working with… As is usual, mostly our experiences are good, but the bad ones stick out like a rat snake thrown in front of a table of girls in a high school cafeteria… ;-)

The worse experiences I can think of at the moment have to do with trying to pull off some local dancing with live music, trying to pull a band together. You can imagine, no shortage of session musicians, but who have little or no idea of what ‘dance’ music is really about, so, inexperienced on that point, and sometimes on all points. In other words, they more often than not have a lot to learn. What gets me riled is that they often come with the idea of making money rather than learning anything, with the notion that there isn’t anything to it and they have nothing to learn. We’ve had some really fat egos drop by. They not only want money, they want lots of it. They want big returns for little or no effort, the quick buck. My answer is usually that it might come ~ eventually, if as a group we can get tight and earn the return. They often aren’t patient enough for that, for waiting, or, lacking any idea they have anything to learn ~ are not willing to commit time and effort, not willing to put in the practice, the time to get good at something before expecting others to worship them or pay them. Like the before mentioned yahoos featured on the tele programs, they want it NOW!!! I’ve even known a few inept bands going that expect high dollar for their ineptitude, but since there’s little competition, they actually do get the occassional gig. Oh yeah, and you know the really crazy people on those tele programs, yes, I’ve ran into a few of them too, or they have into me, including at least one harp strumming druid and a flaky lady on Appalachian dulcimer singing KumbayYah… :-/

So, how is this delusion damaging? Well, my ideal is ‘dance music’ ~ live music for live dance, something community oriented. Part of that ideal is that you get your experience and accumulate that knowledge and skill by playing for local events, maybe even FREE!, heaven forbid. Then, when you start to take off and people start really enjoying the level of skill you develop, with effort and practice and experience, then the jobs are likely to come along, or you can then promote yourself as a dance musician or dance band, with a CV that has content, history…

In an atmosphere of little competition some folks start to get full of themselves and think they can play, or that what they play is already ‘dance’ music, even though they’ve never or rarely played for dancers. Of course, tone deaf parents and friends that have always told them how wonderful they are carry a good part of the credit for creating such monsters. I have come across these twits more than once, as I have also come across communities that could benefit greatly from nurturing a link between musicians and dancers but haven’t ~ because the chemistry of egos is not conducive to such generosity of spirit, sharing and developing together.

Hey, any good stories are also welcome, as I’ve not added those here in this opening, and we’ve had those too…

Re: Can high self-esteem damage traditions? ~ delusion & expectations of income

The thing about playing music for dancing as in the days of the Ceili Bands was discipline. There was normally a dress code for a start, Usually a dress suit white shirt, dickey bow, that sort of thing. The idea of turning up on stage in a sweat shirt, a pair of jeans and trainers just didn’t exist, and would not be allowed no matter how good a musician you were. Each band had a leader who said what was to be played and when to play it. There was a laid down set of tunes for each particular dance and you knew how to play them and many times each tune was to be played. Sometimes a particular dance might have to be extended and in that case the band leader gave the nod to the rest of the band and the set usually ended when you heard the drummer give ‘two taps’ on the wood block (clog box in my day)..
You didn’t usually drink on stage, not alcohol anyway’ but you had a break halfway through the dance, Many musicians took their break in a nearby boozer and that sometimes led to friction with bandleader. How do I know all this? Because I played with a few ceili bands when they were in their heyday.

Bands are definitely not the same as sessions, and not always fun. I see lots of people that just scratch something session-y together at the last minute, but those things often don’t work well or create a good impression. I have seen the imbalanced egos at work, and they can truly be destructive. My wife and I are in a band, and there is a lot of work involved in getting a tight group together that all shows up with the proper equipment at the proper place and time, with sets worked out in advance so there are not large gaps between the musical numbers, and the stuff all practiced so it comes out nice and tight and polished. Not to mention, getting gigs at a pace that makes everyone happy (too many or too little can create stress), playing numbers everyone likes to play, giving everyone their moment to shine. And you play what makes the person who hired you happy, not what makes you happy. Performing can be rewarding, and often when everything comes together right, you can enjoy it, but it is work.
That is why I appreciate sessions, you can just play what you enjoy because you love to do it.

Hello all,

How many do this professionally, and I mean full-time?

This thread intrests me greatly, as I must admit to deriving the grand majority of my income from playing music. It just so happens that my repertoire is primarily based in ITM, but I also play Scottish, Acadian and Quebecois tunes. I tell those who call me for gigs that I play Celtic / North Atlantic traditional dance music.

Earning a living by playing music has been described as the second to oldest trade.

Considering the public I get in these parts, which considers our music to be rather foreign and exotic, I could continue to play my gigs until the end of my days without the slightest intention of bettering myself. This is, of course, unthinkable.

Few people have the opportunity in their lifetime to do something they love mightily. I consider myself one of the very lucky ones, and the price of this is imposing an unyielding expectation, taking into account our respective limitations, of excellence upon my colleagues and myself.

Then there is the question of self-esteem. What conditions are needed for someone to consider him/herself “good enough” for a public?

Too few people play this sort of music of ours in this part of the world, yet it benefits from considerable popularity. There are all sorts of events which are organised around the theme of somesuch “celticness” or other which requires the services of such bands as my own.

Yet there are very few sessions here (I went from six sessions per week where I came from to five per month and only in winter over here…).

Just so as were’re clear on the matter, I never charge much more than the road expenses and the minimum legal (yes, there is here a “legal”) fee for musicians.

Self appraisal and honesty are as objective as the individual in question. I’ve come across some egos that had their own gravitational fields, and avoided them. I’ve also run into people who were so insecure that they undermined themselves. I even worked with one character who had a huge ego one day and had none the following (it’s called a bipolar disorder I know).

The rewards of music are not financial. That is rhetorical to anyone that’s ever actually made a living out of it.

At any rate, Frisbee, I gladly fall into the category of the (a) stupid. Terminally stupid if I may add. The reason being that I understand entirely the fact that there is no financial reward, yet continue to consider it my trade regardless of my talent (or lack thereof, I’ll let you judge if you’re interested).

People still call me in to do concerts (we tell them to dance however they can here). I don’t have much time for anything else, and if I did, I would probably do something else, like flipping burgers.

I’d rather keep playing music.

When you are playing every weekend, you need decent dosh or you undervalue your time. I would rather catch up with video than do a gig for £20 a piece.
We do treat playing for dances as an ITM session as we are only loosely following the set, we do improvise, and will change the set on a whim - but we can do it as we play together regularly.
Most importantly, I do have to be in charge of the temo (and enforce it), so we have a “band speed” that is the same, year in, year out. I am also strict about repertoire - I allow the band members to have a tune in between sets, but had to insist one fiddler didn’t play slow airs or his own compositions in between - they were not in keeping with the rest of the repertoire (and sent the audience to sleep).
The band are all very aware it is a “tradition” we are keeping going and try not to introduce anything to “spoil” it, but are open to things evolving slowly if it works.

More important than anyting else, is the forward planning that goes into gigging - getting different musicians and callers to the right place at the right time, with the right pa, can be a nightmare if you are not highly organised. And when things go wrong with the gig, that really sorts out who is the true pro.

Re: Can high self-esteem damage traditions? ~ delusion & expectations of income

I play the fiddle and my spouse plays guitar, and we put together a band that’s been playing together for about 8 years. We practice (almost) every week, and we play some weddings, festivals, farmer’s markets.. that kind of thing. We’ve developed the “F System” to decide whether to play a gig. In order for us to say yes, at least one of the “F Factors” must be high enough to make it worthwhile, or the overall “score” needs to be sufficiently high.

* Fortune (does it pay enough?)
* Fame (will there be LOTS of people or publicity?)
* Fun (will they provide the sound system? Will there be firedancing or some other excitement?)
* Food (will there be lots of good food and drink?)
* Friends (we’ll play free gigs for friends that we would otherwise turn down)

Our bass player insists that there is yet another “F factor”, but he refuses to elaborate. ; )
