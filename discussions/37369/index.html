Taking Music to the next level? Advice.

Hello everyone!

I am a singer and musician who has sold CDs locally and at events in Ireland, the UK and Czech Republic and am interested in taking my music to the next level.

Because of the content of my existing CDs, I have marketed myself as a blues singer (www.patrickwallblues.com) although I also sing gospel, oldtime US country, rock ‘n’ roll, bluegrass, Western swing and, of course, Irish songs and rebel ballads. I want to record future albums that feature Irish music as well as blues, etc. and other folk. I plan to do a collection of classic rebel ballads.

However, there is not enough support for different types of music I feel. Every radio station seems to focus on Country and Irish music and do not have much of an opening for blues, oldtime country (i.e. Moon Mullican, Hank Williams, Bob Wills, etc.), bluegrass or indeed traditional Irish song. On top of this, I notice that rebel songs are often totally banned from our airwaves. But classic songs like “Foggy Dew” and “Banna Strand” are very much part of our heritage.

Likewise, promoters are few and far between. There are plenty Country and Irish ones who do not want to get in touch with anyone it seems and they are content with what they have in what is an oversaturated scene. No promoters of traditional Irish song, blues, or blues based country exists as far as I know.

A third issue is that the venue and dancing seem to have taken over too. It is all about live gigs and less about the CD or even the download sales. Gigs are great and it is great to hear live music BUT what many do not realise is that it is frightfully expensive and very prohibitive for most. Buying gig equipment is expensive as is travelling perhaps from Waterford to Leitrim, Cavan or Longford to get an appropriate gig (or indeed to the Czech Republic or Austria). Often, one comes out at a loss. As well as this, band members need to be paid.

A new music model that promotes alternative types of Irish music is needed, be it trad Irish, Irish blues or alternative forms of Irish country. Artists in all types of Irish music other than pop and Country and Irish do not seem to get the same opportunities and this is unfair.

Thanks for any help and advice.
Patrick.

Re: Taking Music to the next level? Advice.

Nothing glib about it. It is the way the world works. Promoters promote the music that audiences want to hear. If a musician is struggling to cover costs it isn’t because the world is unfair, or because promoters are narrow minded, it is because they aren’t producing music that audiences will to pay to hear. Music of any genre is a cut-throat business, and to stand any chance of success you have to be brutally honest with yourself, not look for other people to blame.

It’s no use thinking that the system is wrong, the world isn’t going to change just to suit you. You have to adapt to suit the world. If you really believe that your music has the potential, then the solution isn’t to sit and whine about how narrow minded the professional promoters are, it is to get out and promote it yourself - scatter press releases and free demos like confetti; put your money where your mouth is - hire a hall and print some posters; do whatever it takes. If you prove yourself right, the moment you show that there is a quid to be made from your music the professional promoters (and all the other industry sharks) will be queuing at your door.

Patrick: “you could have very healthy album sales but a set of concerts will not cover the costs of equipment, touring, marketing it, etc. I have a financial qualification and know that gigging really works best for venues and equipment hirers/sellers much moreso than artists. Gigging has become ultra-expensive at least in Ireland.”

That is pretty much the point I was trying to put across to you. If concerts won’t cover the costs for you, they won’t cover the costs for a promoter either. It’s not a question of having no audience, it is a question of not having a big enough audience to pay the costs, that is why promoters concentrate on the mainstream genres, where they know they can fill the hall.

Times have changed. I live in a rural part of Scotland that probably equates to the situation in Ireland. 10 years ago we were able to put on major names on a regular basis. We can’t do it today, because the biggest hall in the area holds 250 people, and even if we cut corners and call in favours wherever possible 250 tickets won’t cover costs. If you try to put on lesser known acts it gets even worse - the artistes fee might be less, but all the overheads stay the same, and you have to cover them from much lower ticket sales.

If I were in your shoes I’d forget about concerts and promoters completely, and target your self-promotion campaign exclusively on festival organizers. Festivals are the one remaining area where there is money to be made in niche genres - not only do they have more money available from larger audiences and often some form of backing or sponsorship, but they also usually like to book a few unusual and off-genre acts to add variety to the programme.

Re: Taking Music to the next level? Advice.

One other bit of advice: remove the word ‘luck’ from your vocabulary. Luck doesn’t come into it - success in music (or just about any other field other than horse racing) comes down to hard work and determination. ‘Luck’ is an excuse used by losers, because it’s easier to say ‘the other guy got lucky’ than ‘I don’t have what it takes’.

Sam Philips didn’t get lucky - he noticed that there was already a huge audience for a type of music that wasn’t being recorded. He saw a business opportunity and then he worked his butt off to exploit it. The Beatles didn’t get lucky, they spend years playing pubs, seeing what the punters liked and honing their act accordingly before they got famous. If they had just sat back after their first recording and said “Chuck Berry covers are what we do, someone should get us an audience” they wouldn’t even have made it to one-hit wonders.

But good luck to you, I hope you do find a way to make things work.

I don’t go with that - all the big names started out as struggling entertainers, but they didn’t stay struggling. Why not? because they worked damned hard to produce music that the masses wanted to hear. Local popularity became regional popularity, became national fame. Ok, if you play a niche genre you probably are going to continue struggling no matter how good you are, because there isn’t much demand for your music. If you want to make a living you are probably going to have to ‘sell out’ and play stuff that people like.

I also don’t buy this stuff about C&I suppressing other genres. For a blues artist to complain that the he can’t get work because there is too much C&I is like someone who makes tomato flavour ice cream complaining that Ben and Jerry are putting him out of business because they make vanilla ice cream. Ben and Jerry make vanilla because that is what people like, and if you want to sell ice cream you have to do the same. If there was a market for tomato ice cream, you can bet your bottom dollar Ben and Jerry would be making it. You can’t change popular taste to suit your product, you have to change your product to suit popular taste.

Here in SW Scotland we have exactly the same problem - the only gigs that ever turn a profit are C&I and Americana, so that is all the commercial promoters put on. I wouldn’t expect anything else. But there is a small core of us who are enthusiastic enough to put on folk and trad gigs on a voluntary ‘not for profit’ basis (sometimes underwriting losses out of our own pockets). Our gigs are just as well promoted and advertised as the commercial ones, the acts we bring in are usually ‘bigger names’ than the Belfast pub singers that the C&I brigade flock to, and yet people stay away in droves. Would they come to our gigs if there were no C&I gigs? No, of course not. The reason they stay away is because they just aren’t interested in our sort of music. If you really want to move up to the next level you have to stop trying to pass the buck and blaming other genres and promoters, take responsibility for your own success, and concentrate all your efforts on improving the popularity of your own music.

Re: Taking Music to the next level? Advice.

Free Reed, AB Steen: I covered these issues before. Making the ‘perfect’ record is very very expensive nowadays and one has to go with the budget they have. Doing a solo CD means that it is the one person and no one musician is 100% flawless. I have done other CDs with bands and varied instruments but as said, these 4 CDs were very expensive to do and were recorded before the worst of the recession struck.

Timing is always a subjective issue. People said the same about Bob Dylan, Woody Guthrie and Robert Johnson, artists I admire a lot. I guess part of the appeal of stripped down folk, blues or trad is that it is not meant to be 100% technically perfect.

If I had the budget and support, I could do a lot more, use a lot more musicians and only do the singing. But when one self produces music, it is more difficult.

Various: I did not say I hated Country & Irish (C&I) but what I did say is that it is the only type of country music promoted. To me, country music has always been a blues based music. Jimmie Rodgers, Moon Mullican, Bob Wills, Bill Monroe and Hank Williams for example are among the early innovators and very blues focused.

C&I seems to be the only thing they understood. On my first album, I did some songs that resembled the earlier 1960s style of C&I (which I like more than the newer version) with accordion, etc. But I have to stay true to the bluesier country music I was inspired by.

There is a lot of organisational politics in the Irish music world for certain. It is all about promoting some styles and the songwriting efforts of certain people. I know people who went into studios, were told and advised to change their songs, timing, backing, and even style and were transformed into C&I outfits because of efforts to pitch a song. One of these is now a very famous C&I singer.

The Wise Troll: thanks for this advice. Again, I am well aware of the fact that the market for it is probably outside of Ireland. But again it is a matter of being here and not there! I have other work here but would certainly visit there some stage. Ireland’s music promotion scene at present is poor and discourages one from making music outside of the pop/C&I idioms. I know many trad players who go over and back to the USA all the time as they find there’s more of an audience for Irish trad over there!

Michael: I agree 100% about the preferential treatment given to C&I. It is a genuine obstacle as it is all is promoted and has replaced the emphasis once given to Irish folk music. The example of Ben and Jerry v the tomato icecream would play out like this: imagine if ONLY B&J were allowed sell icecream and everyone else was not promoted. That’s how C&I works. My experience shows that trad, folk, blues and oldtime country programmes are played at the graveyard shift if at all on radio and rebel songs are often banned. No audience can be found if music is not played. C&I’s fans will stick with this or similar but there are fans of other forms out there and those who are trying to find something different to listen to. But the same thing is played year in year out on local radio because it is being pushed.

As said, I have nothing against C&I in moderation and like all forms, there’s some of it better than more of it. But it is a shame other forms are not being promoted. There are people out there who will never hear the music of De Dannan (one member of this group was from my home village) or Tony McMahon, or even the Chieftains. The fusing of Irish music with other influences such as what Planxty, Moving Hearts, Christy Moore, Horslips and Stockton’s Wing did would not be possible today. Projects like AfroCelt should be much more promoted today too but there is not movement in place to support music and mould and nurture raw talent and take away the rough edges and bring into into a mainstream form that does not compromise.

Anyway, thanks for all the help and advise everyone. Take care, Patrick
