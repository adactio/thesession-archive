If you could study fiddle from anyone anywhere….

Greetings,

I am a beginner fiddler (~1 year) that the more I get into it the harder it is to put the darn thing down. I am learning by ear, and have no formal music background or training (ie, illiterate when it comes to reading music). I am most intrigued and inspired by Irish, Scottish, American Old Time, and Cajun styles. I may have an opportunity to do some extended (3-6 month) international travelling soon, and the thought occurred to me perhaps to try to combine the two, such as an extended foray that might also facilitate soaking up as much as I can from ITM sessions and instructors abroad. England and/or Ireland are the places that come to mind, however I’m sure there’s probably some great hubs in the US as well….

So, my pie in the sky question is, if you could take 3-6 months and go anywhere to learn from any particular instructing fiddler today, who/where would you try to go? Any recommendations for a particular geographic region or community where there is a particularly active “slow session” scene (thus conducive to progressing beginners)? Or, any particular regions in England or Ireland? I realize I’m casting a ridiculously wide net here! I’ve found numerous mentions of various week long fiddle camps etc all the world over on the discussion forums here, but I am looking for a more extended experience since I will likely be travelling for awhile. For example, I ran across mention of Pete Cooper’s Fiddle School in London, that provides weekly group lessons for 12 week sessions. Any other things of this like that anyone is aware of or could recommend?

Thanks!

Re: If you could study fiddle from anyone anywhere….

The weekend of workshops I took with Matt Cranitch was invaluable to me. Not just with Irish fiddle actually, I was able to apply a lot of the lessons to the English folk fiddle I play. I bought his books afterwards, but I think I got extra value from them having already had an in-the-flesh introduction to/demonstration of his bowings and accents and the overall scrupulousness of his teaching: that weekend was worth every penny, and I would be half the player I am now if I hadn’t gone – I had a very limited understanding of Irish fiddle playing before then. (Not that I’m an amazing player now or anything!) A couple of others at that weekend found his teaching a little intimidating, I think, but I like his no-nonsense style. It’s a world away from the “it’s folk music, you can play it however you like!” approach that you sometimes encounter.

Were you to travel to Ireland, I’d go look him up.

Re: If you could study fiddle from anyone anywhere….

“I neglected to mention I do have an instructor, focusing on Irish and Old Time tunes.”

VERY different bowing for ITM vs. OT. Indeed, what works for one will not serve you well for the other. Is your present instructor totally conversant with both styles? Are you confident you are able to keep the styles, especially bowing, entirely separate?

I’m with the others who have suggested that “focusing” on ITM and OT may not really be focusing. You don’t want your ITM to sound at all OT, and you don’t want your OT to sound at all Irish. I would think about choosing a preference for now, and diving into the deep end with just that preference. Just one style alone can take a lifetime to plumb the depths.

Re: If you could study fiddle from anyone anywhere….

OP, you’ve been playing fiddle for less than a year and you’ve tried to pick it up on your own. I’ll bet that you have serious flaws in holding the bow, bowing, holding the fiddle, and how your fiddle fits your body (chin, neck) yet alone your intonation and playing. You may want to study with Kevin Burke, take lessons from Matt Cranitch, and have Mairéad Ní Mhaonaigh as your personal tutor, but you really need to be of a certain standard in your own playing for it to make sense before you seek out lessons with the best proponents of the music. The reason why I mentioned the Center for Irish Music in St. Paul (or something similar near you) is that they have numerous fiddle teachers who are excellent teachers of kids and adults and highly accomplished on their instrument (Dáithí Sproule of Altan teaches guitar). Second, you were looking for a good “learners session”. Well we (Minneapolis/St. Paul) have an excellent one every Sunday at 4pm just before the advanced session at 6pm. We also have 5 sessions a week of various levels some of which compare favorably with the best sessions anywhere. There aren’t too many places where you have a session of 4 button accordions, 2-3 concertinas, 4-10 fiddles, 5 or 6 flutes, a couple of banjo’s, one guitar, a harp player, and 1-3 pipers. In addition, we frequently have traveling artists give lessons, a couple of festivals each year with workshops, and a reasonable drive to great Irish festivals.

I enjoyed Willie Clancy week, relished playing with Sharon Shannon in Doolin (30 years ago), spent way too many nights with my friends in Westport, Co. Mayo, and had great times during Irish Week at Augusta Heritage Center, but the best sessions week in and week out are right here in Minneapolis/St. Paul. Spending 3-4 months here, taking lessons once or twice a week, playing 3-4 sessions a week, and then taking in a workshop or two over the summer would be a great way to improve your playing so that you could actually get something out of Willie Clancy or a visit to Matt’s

I mostly play Scottish music and have most of my life but the difference between what I, as an enthusiastic outsider, and what a native plays is often massive. I’m not saying I can’t hold my own but there are differences I hear time and time again when I play with or listen to some of the true proponents of the art. I’ve said it time and time again on these boards, and nothing will convince me otherwise, that most of us are merely genre musicians not traditional musicians or members of a tradition in music. It’s a hard thing to swallow but unless you go for an immersive experience with a proper background in your instrument ( complete with all the countless hours of technique and musical learning ) the sound you make will always be an ersatz approximation of the real thing. And the real thing as all those pure drop threads testify is something that just is rather than something easily defined or characterized.

Re: If you could study fiddle from anyone anywhere….

“most of us are merely genre musicians not traditional musicians or members of a tradition in music.”

Yes I’ve always been acutely aware of the distinction.

When I teach I don’t put myself forward as a source, but as a medium. I’ll say things like “you’ll hear them do this…” and “I’ve heard them do it this way…”

The “they” and “them” are traditional Irish players.

Now, I am a member of a tradition of sorts, the tradition of West Coast USA players of ITM. But it would be ridiculous to put that forward as a source for repertoire and style.

I’m actually surprised by the answers people have put forward. What I expected, when I first saw the title, was people putting forward the names of various “big name” players past and present. I was prepared to point out that great players often do not make good teachers. But no, the answers have been focused on good instruction as they should be.

It’s tempting to say, when you hear a great player, “I’d love to take lessons from him/her!”

Would you now? The person might be a horrible teacher. They might not have the slightest clue what they themselves are doing. They might be an unpleasant person, or a drunk, or an addict, or be irresponsible and forget about your lessons etc.

It happens all the time in the Pipe Band world, people hearing a great band and saying “I would love to play in that band!”

Would you now? Have you attended their practices? Have you seen how the band is managed? The Pipe Major might spend practices yelling at everybody, demeaning and insulting them. The whole atmosphere might be poison and tense. The band might be run in a chaotic and slapdash manner. There might be strongly opposed cliques operating to tear the band apart. Half the people might have various addiction issues.
