Re: “the old ‘press and draw’ manner”

The ‘press and draw’ style is mostly derived from the melodeon, where you have a single row of notes, in a single key, and you play tunes in those keys by pressing as drawing you bellows as much as necessary (which is very often!).
With an accordion you can choose to play notes in one of the 2 (sometimes 3!) rows: this often lets you to pick buttons that allow you not to switch the bellows direction. More legato, if you wish so…
This of course depends on the note and on the key of the accordion…
C#/D box are usually associated with the press&draw style because a lot of tunes are in D, and so you get all notes in the D row. When you play a tune in the same key as one of your box, you can play it like a melodeon, on a single row.
Of course you can play a press&draw-ish style of B/C boxes also, it’s a matter of personal preference.
Let’s just say that players associated with the press&draw style are not shy about changing the bellow direction as often as necessary, while other players favor legato phrases.
Not a box player here, I am sure someone can provide a better answer :)

Re: “the old ‘press and draw’ manner”

There is overlap between the two rows of a two-row box that can make a big difference to how you play the note. Any duplicated notes are press on one row and draw on the other. Here is a first-octave D scale on a B/C, C#/D, and D melodeon:

B/C: Draw/Either/Press/Press/Draw/Either/Press/Draw

C#/D: Press/Draw/Either/Draw/Press/Draw/Either/Press

D: Press/Draw/Press/Draw/Press/Draw/Draw/Press

As you can see, to play a tune on the D melodeon you are changing the bellows direction quite a bit as you go note-to-note. This gives a punchy sound, more accented as the jerk of changing direction creates a bit of a staccato. As one-row boxes were common well before the two-row, this is the “old-fashioned” way of playing.

On the two-row box, you have more options. In general, B/C players tend to use those options a lot to try to minimize bellows changes, which creates a more flowing style. It helps that the two “either” notes are E and B, which are used a lot in tunes in D, Em, G, and Am.

A C#/D box is basically a one-row melodeon with another row tacked on. Many players play it that way, only venturing out into the C# row when they need to. Also, the two duplicated notes are F# and C#, a bit less useful overall than E and B (especially since C# isn’t used at all in many tunes). So, a player who plays in the “press and draw” style is actively moving the bellows a lot more, and getting a punchier, “older” sound.

Re: “the old ‘press and draw’ manner”

I’d say that it’s an expression that has lost its relevance in recent decades with the prominence of top-notch C#/D players, Jackie probably being the first of them. The “old press and draw style” was meant in contrast to the “new” B/C style, said to be played “on the draw” because of the predominance of pull notes in the key of D, or the handy run of 5 notes that can be played on the pull (ABC#DE). But once you get away from the key of D, things even out pretty quickly in the press-and-draw department. B/C players sometimes switch to press-and-draw style, anyway, e.g. Joe Burke playing Coleman’s setting of Bonnie Kate in C, clearly because it would be too awkward in D on the B/C, Martin Mulhaire composing https://thesession.org/tunes/2716 in C almost certainly for the same reason.

@Tony O’R: About Paddy and Joe Fitz and the press-and-draw style: there was a truly fascinating interview with Paddy (RIP) on the Blarney Pilgrims podcast series (and later another equally wonderful one with Joe).

Paddy made it clear that, if the brothers started on a one-row melodeon, they quickly progressed to C#/D, and that was the system they were both playing on their arrival in Australia. The way Paddy explains it, Joe came back from a trip home to Ireland (in the late 60s?) and told his brother that everyone at home was playing B/C and that this was the new thing and they had better get on the bandwagon. Joe might have embraced the system more enthusiastically but I got the impression that Paddy did so somewhat reluctantly, and found the transition a considerable effort. He made several comments to the effect that after decades of playing B/C, tunes still came more naturally to him on C#/D, and when the interviewer asked what the advantages of the B/C system were, IIRC he didn’t have a very convincing answer!

The reason tunes in C would sound “sweeter” in the church might have had something to do with the fact that press-and-draw was their original style, but I would think the lower pitch would be an important factor.

Re: “the old ‘press and draw’ manner”

Interesting discussion.

As others have said, press and draw seems to be more about a signature sound than purely the number of bellows reversals though they are closely linked.

It can be used to describe accordion playing (almost always c#/d system playing or melodeon) and a more old fashioned concertina style (I think the middle row of a concertina maps to the D row of a c#d layout accordion or the D row of a melodeon).

Joe Burke is seen as the antithesis of ‘press and draw’. His playing seems to me to float above the rhythm and the use of certain accents, very ‘smoothed out’ with elaborate triplets and rolls.

I think in the early days, he could play the more intricate and elaborate tunes that were being composed better than others.

His playing divides opinion as a search of the session will quickly reveal!

In what I take to be ‘press and draw’, the playing ‘is’ the rhythm with a heavier accent on certain notes. Listen to Jackie Daly straight after Joe Burke…it’s clear.

Important point….above is a widely accepted perspective on the playing of Joe Burke. He is a master technician and stylist but divides opinion like few others.

Far be it from me to say anything at all about Noel Hill’s style even though he employs a more elaborate cross row technique!

There is a similar discourse in piping - open versus closed with e.g. Paddy Keenan seen as the legato type player. Maybe other instruments have it too.

That’s press and draw for me…

On a related matter, one thing I would say on that aspect of the discussion regarding methods is this.

In other fields, there is a tendency to look at what the best player does technically and decide that this is ‘the technique’ that everyone must learn.

Certain things will be framed as flaws e.g. ‘chopping’ above.

I think certainty about ‘the best’ technique is worth looking at closely. Do some top concertina players ‘chop’? I’d love to know.

All that matters is the sound and in many cases players will be better of figuring it out for themselves with some very basic guidance - rather than seeking to adopt an elaborate system from the get go.

That gives the player a better chance of achieving a technique that matches their mind and physiology.

Obvious technical limitations are to be avoided e.g. only playing on the downstroke or upside down hands.

There are many fantastic players with highly idiosyncratic techniques and mental models who figured it out for themselves.

As ever, all of the above is there to be shot at :-)

Re: “the old ‘press and draw’ manner”

For the benefit of those outside the free-reed world, chopping refers to using the same finger for two consecutive notes.

In concertina-land, chopping is generally avoided, with a some caveats:
- if you play chords or drones on the left side in some extreme cases you need to chop (as the “non-chopping” button is not in the bellow direction you’re using)
- with small finger notes sometimes you need to chop as the finger takes care of two buttons. The same probably applies to the index finger in concertinas with more than 30 buttons
- I recently saw a video on OAIM where Liam O’Brien displays a passage where he chops because, he says, he is used to it. In that passage there is no reason for chopping. But remember we’re talking about a player with super fast fingers so I guess no one could have noticed the chop in a real, fast-pace playing context.
Myself I think non-chopping is much easier than chopping once you’re used to it, so I see no reason to avoid chopping if you can.

About press-and draw with concertina, unless you use G/D concertina (which are comparatively quite rare), you can press and draw only on C and G tunes, the native keys of the rows. There’s no way to avoid cross row playing in the key od D with a C/G instrument.
However it is true older players used press-and-draw style, but (this is crucial!) they played D tunes in C.
