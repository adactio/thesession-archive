Ultra-Instinct - “The Secret of The Body Moving On It’s Own”

Is it possible to act without thinking? When I say “act without thinking”, I mean “to cause yourself to do something without having consciously thought of doing it”? As someone who has seen many musicians, live and onscreen, perform astonishing improvisational work on their instruments, I’m left to wonder. Is this what it is; Ultra-Instinct?

During the anime series Dragon Ball Super, there is a key moment during one of the episodes where the Angel, Whis, an otherworldly martial arts trainer, explains to the main characters, Goku and Vegeta, that they are not at the appropriate level to spar with him.

“You two are still thinking before you move rather than just moving. This overthinking is limiting your [fighting] speed. Messages can only travel through your nervous system so fast. When you rely on thoughts for physical action, you lose precious [fighting] time. (…) Your end goal should be to master the ability to have each part of your body move independently of the other parts. But I admit this is exceedingly difficult…”

Later in the series, there is a martial arts tournament where you learn the significance of this moment. But this moment, along with all of the relevant events throughout the rest of the series, has forced me to give this idea some serious thought. This “Ultra-Instinct” as it has come to be called.

In the context of playing music, especially in heavily improvisatory styles of music like Irish Traditional Music, would you say that it is a realistic idea? So much so that it is* what is happening with these excellent musicians? That they are so well-practiced and studied that they barely have to think while they play? Or that they can clear their minds completely and just play? All of the music just comes out? I was listening to Edel Fox’s “Chords and Beryls” the other day, and it was refreshed for me just how amazing some of these musicians are. I guess that’s one reason why I like solo albums like this one so much. You hear all the simple and subtle things that happen, and it blows your mind.

But even with younger musicians, they do some pretty remarkable things too. I mean, is it instinctual? And instinctual makes sense? Is this “Ultra-Instinct” just a glorified way of saying “2nd Nature”? Is 2nd Nature instinct? Is it logically possible to even *react without thinking? Or is this just another fantastical idea from an imaginary cosmos?

I play another heavily improvisatory style of music on piano, but I have never been able to understand, or explain, what is happening while I’m playing. I guess I can sincerely say it feels like a series of reactions. And I guess rather than thinking of each individual note I want to play, I feel around for the overall sound that I want, and allow that to follow. But am I “not thinking”, or am I rather just thinking at the speed of sound? Have you been in the various meditative states while practicing, playing, or performing? How does it feel to you, or how would you explain the process of improvising?

Peter, one thing that has fascinated me about improvising, is that it seems to be it’s own skill, independent of ear ability or sight reading ability. I’m currently of the mind to believe that ear training makes understanding improvising more accessible than sight reading, because with ear training you have to stop and pay attention to what you are hearing; In turn, allowing you to notice how the different sounds relate to each other. I feel that with sight reading, the opportunity is still there, but much less apparent because you don’t necessarily “have” to listen to what you are doing, you can watch what you’re doing instead. What I mean is, as someone who’s first learning style is by ear, I can’t tell if I’m playing a wrong note/chord by looking at what I’m playing. I can only tell by what I’m hearing. So my ear training is still a very important part of sight reading.

But more on the note of improvising being independent of ear or sight ability; One of my close friends said that as a sight reader, he doesn’t know how to improvise because he doesn’t understand what to listen for, as someone who plays by ear does. And I’ve met plenty of musicians who can’t even play their instrument without their sheet music. But even then, you have ear musicians who can only play music from their repertoire, but can’t compose or improvise. So, these are all just crazy ideas I like to reexamine when I come across a new idea, like the main idea of this post. Playing without thinking.

Re: Ultra-Instinct - “The Secret of The Body Moving On It’s Own”

Well, yes. I remember one of my professors in an undergrad psychology course explaining how physical processes you learn eventually become automatic, unconscious, so you don’t have to think about how you do whatever those things are. Your body eventually just does them. That frees up your mind and attention to do other things. She used driving a car and playing a musical instrument as examples.

With driving, your attention obviously needs to be on the other morons on the road, but the point is that you can do that because controlling your own car has become an automatic process. You don’t have to think about how much pressure to apply to the brake or the gas, how to steer, or if you’re not like me and can drive a manual car, how to operate a clutch.

When you start learning how to play an instrument, you have to think about every single movement, how to get a sound out of the instrument, how to get from one note to the next, how each tune goes. Each of that takes conscious brain power, lots of it. That’s all you can do. Forget improvisation; that would overload the system. As you get better, increasingly more of it becomes unconscious and in the case of Irish music, the tunes eventually become free from the early shackles of rote memorization and that’s when you can play variations, sling tunes together in ad hoc sets, or play some tune you have no memory of ever learning.

Re: Ultra-Instinct - “The Secret of The Body Moving On It’s Own”

David L, I think there are several things to consider.

First, I didn’t say that Irish Traditional music was the most improvisatory style, I said that it was heavily improvisatory. Those mean different things.

Second, I say Irish Traditional music is heavily improvisatory because there is a strong consensus that we are never supposed to play a tune the same way twice. You use melodic variations. You use different kinds of ornaments in different places. You use different ornaments in the same places. And at times, you even have quite a number of settings of one tune because so many people have found different ways to play it. That’s why I consider it heavily improvisatory.

Third, I believe that you left out a notch on the continuum; “Playing the melody with variations”; Which would be different from “Playing the outline of the melody with variations”.

Fourth, for backing instruments like guitar and piano, Irish music is* one of the most* improvisatory styles. The logical and musical chords that fit the melodic progressions are so many, and the structure of their progressions is practically alive. Backing for Irish music is “complete free improvisation”. I say this because when a guitarist asks which chords to play, or a pianist asks which notes are the bassline, you can sincerely answer “all of them”.

So to wrap it up; It may not be the most improvisatory style, but it certainly gets a high score on the chart. Because whether or not we consider it improvisatory, the idea remains that we aren’t supposed to play the tunes the same way every time, meaning that we have to use the same parts of our brain that we use when improvising.
