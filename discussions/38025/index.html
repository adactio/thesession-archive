Knowing a tune vs. Memorizing a tune

This could be just a nonsense discussion, but I am curious about how you feel about this idea. Often on the Session we hear from someone who talks about getting one tune or another “memorized”. I’ll just state my experience because I’m not really sure how to ask the question. If I “memorize” a tune I do it in it’s entirety, notes, fingering, ornaments, rhythm…everything, and play it on one instrument only, exactly the same way every time. Even worse if I forget even the smallest element the whole tune falls apart and I will eventually forget the whole thing! To the contrary, if I “know” a tune, through listening to it played with different “lift”, on various instruments ( and it takes a lot if listening) I don’t forget it easily, can play it on more than one instrument, can use a variety of ornamentations, and am not thrown off track if another player doesn’t play it note for note with me. I discovered this while looking back over my posted Tunebook. I doubt that I could start half of them in a session, might be able to play some of the rest if somebody else starts them, and would struggle to even follow the rest. A few I don’t even recognize. Those that I know…well, I KNOW.

The best example of what I mean is the simplest tune of all…Happy Birthday. I doubt that any of us have it memorized, but we all know it.

So what do you think? Is your experience the same or different. Do you find it easier if you learn by ear or score? (I’ll say here that, for me, it doesn’t matter, just different paths to the same place…again, for me.) Is it easier to learn a tune when you hear it on the instrument you play or can you pick it up even if it’s played on a different instrument? Do you think it’s just a matter of semantics? I’m just looking for a discussion among friends here about your personal experiences, but I’m really curious to know what you feel about it. Thanks.

Re: Knowing a tune vs. Memorizing a tune

My two cents, for what they’re worth…

If there’s a particular tune I want to learn, it’s usually because I heard it either at a session or on a recording and something in the tune grabbed my attention. I’ll try to get my hands on a few different recordings of it and listen to them until the tune in its entirety sinks in. I might possibly favour recordings of whistle/flute/pipes, but I seem to have picked up a disproportionate amount of tunes from box and concertina players.

I listen to whatever recordings of the tune I can get my hands on, and often browse through the settings posted on here to decipher any phrases that were too quick or that I couldn’t grasp with ears alone. I definitely don’t come up with a ‘note for note’ setting of a tune that I play the same every time. I have a sort of bare-bones setting of the tune that I ‘know’, and while playing it I’ll use various ornaments/variations from my arsenal, including any interesting variations that I might have heard on a recording.

I find it extremely difficult to memorise an unfamiliar tune from a transcription alone, unless it’s a very simple one, although I think a former member here used to ask something like “If you have a transcription of the tune, then what do you need your ears for?”, right? ;-)

Re: Knowing a tune vs. Memorizing a tune

I have three “stages” to learning a tune. First is short term memory, then long term memory, then the tune is well committed to muscle memory. At the first stage, if the tune is simple enough I can learn the whole thing in less than an hour and even perform and record it. But it’s not in long term memory and the next day I can’t play a lick. I takes me several days to get it into long term memory, then longer for muscle memory. If the tune is long or complicated enough, I might have two or three stages going at the same time with the piece. It seems like I always have the most troubles learning the B part of any tune.

I hate mental blocks. I’ve always been plagued by them. When in my 20’s I gave a solo piano recital at our church. On the program was a certain Bach Prelude and Fugue that I had been playing for months. When I practiced it the morning of the recital, I got a mental block half way through the fugue. I tried about a dozen times to get it back but couldn’t. I had no choice but to announce to the audience that I would be playing the prelude but not the fugue from that selection.

Re: Knowing a tune vs. Memorizing a tune

I hesitate to make generalisations since it depends on the tune. I think of memorisation as being different from learning (though each can be used together). From memory alone I’m able to play one setting of a tune, with repeats, from beginning to end. To retain it for any length of time I’ll put markers in my memory (first few notes, 1st & 2nd repeats, the turn).

At that point I could say I *know* the tune though it doesn’t mean I know it inside out. In other words I have not learnt the tune such that while playing I can easily think of bits before I play them, choose variations, listen to other players’ variations and/or be distracted but still be able to join back into playing without missing a beat.

Basically the better I know a tune the more I’ve learnt & the more there is to learn.
Memory is self limiting in certain ways.

Personally it works for me to learn by ear. Listening, lilting, getting the tune into my head; all before playing the tune on my flute.

Re: Knowing a tune vs. Memorizing a tune

Yep, Cognitive research does indeed show it is all good for the brain - forming all these new billions of neural connections and constantly challenging short and long term memory. Way better than TV and a six pack.

Phillip Glass, composer, child prodigy and genius, in his new autobiography, stated that a composer composing a piece of music or a musician playing a piece of music from memory is unconsciously thinking about the note just ahead ahead: Something like what sight readers do reading ahead to the next note/bar to be played. Or maybe it is not “subconscious” but happens in such rapid real time we can not perceive the passage of a few milliseconds.

I’ve recently made the jump from learning tunes from the sheet music to playing along with a recording slowed down very much at first and without any notation aids. I put Glass’s observation to my own test and it does seem to help to focus - to keep the level of concentration required to play and to play along without too much effort to recall “how the tune goes”. How the brain learns music is a fascinating and largely unexplored area. Although there is always a great range of opinions here about the topic of learning the instruments and learning the music, I think there may be better and speedier pathways that do have universal applications to all musicians.

Re: Knowing a tune vs. Memorizing a tune

I believe memory works like this: Short term memory may learn a tune very quickly but it’s gone within a few hours. To store something in longer term memory takes several days, or weeks, of practice. Then there is what we call muscle memory where I can play the tune easily without thinking about it. It is at this point where I probably know the tune best and can play about with it. Fiddler13 describes this perfectly well without me embellishing it further. I have also found muscle memory can become confused once my thinking concious brain tries to get involved or in stressful situations such as playing solo. How many times have you walked into a room and forgotten why you went there? Usually this happens at times of stress and you may discover your feet have walked to to exactly the part of the room you needed to be in. Your body has remembered even though your concious mind has become confused. Likewise when you watch Who Wants to be a Millionaire you can see how even the most intellectual contestant forgets the answers as the stakes are raised. Afterwards they will say “I don’t believe I didn’t get that!” That is stress. You can overcome this by giving your brain something else to think about. I find humming the tune in my head while I play is enough to keep me on track and keeps the stressful thoughts from entering. I can then, while I am playing, imagine how I may play it differently, muck about by playing it jazz style, giving it some swing or maybe even reggae, but never in a session. I find by breaking all the rules I can then come back to the rules the tunes really needs without being straitjacketed by what I have heard from other recordings.

Then, when I could play it fairly well from the sheet there would come a phase of deliberately trying to memorise it so that I could play it without the music (and that was always the hard part). The other way is to know the tune before you start to learn it - i.e. listen to it enough that you can hum it, sing it, or just play it through in your head before you attempt to play it on an instrument. The disadvantage of learning a tune from music then memorising it is that what you memorise is mostly a sequence of finger positions rather than the sound of the tune - it makes it difficult to transpose into another key, and if you make a mistake you usually get de-railed into a total train wreck. Whereas if you know the tune before you start learning to play it, then the tune is running in your head and you just play along with it. That makes it almost as easy to play in another key as the original and, most importantly, if you make a mistake with your fingers the tune keeps running in your head so it is easy to turn the mistake into a variation and get back on track.

When I was learning the cello as a boy and working on things like the Bach Cello Suites and Beethoven Cello Sonatas my teacher told me, as a student, never to listen to the same recording of a piece more than once if I could help it, and to go to as many live performances whenever possible (which in practice usually meant listening to the radio). He said that concentrating on one recording would stifle my understanding of the music and hinder me in developing my own style. He went on to say that he didn’t want any of his pupils ending up as not-very-good junior imitators of Pablo Casals or whoever!

Re: Knowing a tune vs. Memorizing a tune

I may ‘memorize’ a tune when I first learn it. However over time as I play it in various contexts, and with various different groups of individuals, I come to understand the tune in a completely different fashion. I come to ‘grok’ it‘, get into its inner workings. At that point I find I am introducing small variations and trying different ’settings’.
