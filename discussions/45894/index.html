Cut and paste from my comment in a somehow related discussion (https://thesession.org/discussions/45190):

"Addressing sean-nós singing, Jim Cowdery in his book The melodic tradition of Ireland speaks of «easthetic of understatement» and quotes Tomás Ó Canainn, Traditional music in Ireland:

«Those who expect the traditional Irish dancer to throw his arms about and shout in the style of another country just do not understand that the normal Irish artistic restraint combined with a minute attention to intricate patterns is exhibited in the dance just as surely as in the sean-nós style of singing. It is not difficult to see an analogy with traditional visual art in Ireland: the same attention to minute detail is evident, combined with a determination to let the art speak for itself unaided by any form of gimmickry».

I’m a newcomer here playing the flute for less than one year, but it seems to me that this applies to instrumental music as well - certainly whenever I watch a great flute player weaving subtleties with an almost still posture."

I think still vs. not so still is mainly down to personality, although a bit of animation can be useful to help connect with the audience in a performance band. I’m reminded of a quote I read somewhere about the Lúnasa band, saying that the initial group all played staring at the floor until Kevin Crawford joined the band and livened up the performances with his personality and ability to connect with the audience and other band members. I’ve seen videos of Martin Hayes playing with others in an informal pub session, and while he moves around a little, it’s nothing like he’s doing in that performance video above. Anyway, I don’t think musicians who remain fairly still even while performing like Kevin Burke or Frankie Gavin, are trying to signal that “it’s easy” when it really isn’t. It’s just their personality.

Re: Trying hard to make it look easy?

I think it’s just a matter of different musical traditions having different habits regarding how much, and in what ways, the musician moves body parts that aren’t involved in creating the music.

I’ve heard the comment many times over the years, from people seeing Irish music for the first time, about how still the musicians are.

More than once here I’ve heard expressions like “heads down, lads!”

Highland piping is the same- whether in competition or playing in a pipe band serious pipers just stand there and play.

In both the trad Irish world and in the Highland piping world it stands out when someone is animated, either in facial expressions or moving their body about.

In the “classical” flute world there’s a thing of raising one’s eyebrows while playing, and a thing where the end of the flute describes a circle in the air. I think if a trad Irish fluteplayer did that it would seem odd to other trad players.

Both in the Irish trad world and in the “classical” world it stands out to me as looking mannered, even phony, when musicians are very animated. I suppose to a professional musician performing onstage it’s stagecraft, meant to make the music more interesting to the audience.

Re: Trying hard to make it look easy?

A distinction might be able to be made between the words “easy” and “effortless”. I tend to think of it in terms of “effortless” more than “easy”.

I think there are a few things at play here… First off, to play well fast, you generally have to be relaxed. Our natural inclination is to play *harder* to play faster (in the same way that if you want to run faster, you run harder…). But in the world of the relatively small motions required to play most instruments, if you’re playing hard, you end up fighting your own musculature, and the music doesn’t flow.

Another reason for limited movement might be more attributed to session playing. In sessions, there is often a lot of non-verbal communication happening. The slight nod, or repositioning of your instrument that signals a pending tune change from the person who started the set. The eye contact that is accompanied by a slight shrug and a quick nod in another person’s direction meaning “I can’t think of what to play next, what have you got?”. The glare with a glance down at someone’s instrument that means “you’re out of tune”, etc. It can be somewhat disconcerting to have someone who is smiling and trying to make eye contact with everyone when they’re NOT trying to convey something specific! (I heard Randal Bays once say that when he was first introduced to this music, his reaction was “A bunch of old guys sitting around, staring at the floor? I could do that!”)

For me, the real nirvana of playing this music comes in those somewhat rare moments when everyone that is playing is relaxed, comfortable, and “in the groove”. That’s the moment when the tunes start to soar, bringing your emotions with it, and the music feels truly “effortless” as if you’re not even really trying, but everything is perfect…

Re: Trying hard to make it look easy?

We may be missing something when comparing these video clips of static vs. animated performers. In the early days of the “Folk revival,” musicians on stage used external microphones for amplification, where it was a requirement to stay fairly still or you would get wide swings in volume level.

I remember seeing Kevin Burke in his later years, at a concert with Cal Scott on guitar maybe 8 or 9 years ago, where he played seated and had a condenser mic on a boom stand. I swear that mic was never more than an inch away from his fiddle. You can see that approach here in his younger days in the clip below. Look how he never moves around, never gets the fiddle very far from the microphone:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PMNzoTmUoEI

Now look again at the clip of Martin Hayes and Steve Cooney near the top of this thread. He’s using a pickup on his fiddle, you can see the cord. No need to to keep the fiddle steady relative to an external microphone. Other performers use wireless amplification for even more freedom of movement. I’ve been to a few Natalie McMaster concerts and she couldn’t do what she does -- actually standing up and step-dancing while playing the fiddle -- without that wireless rig.

So the improvements in technology may be a factor in what we see in “animated” stage performance in recent years.

When Natalie MacMaster plays back in Cape Breton, she generally just sits and plays, in the traditional manner - the playing-and-step-dancing and the rest of the show is for the urban rubes. The tradition in Cape Breton and Prince Edward Island - and probably the rest of rural Canada - is for the fiddler to just play - any of the modern showbiz touches would have been regarded as pretentious affectation until fairly recently. More latitude for expressions of joie de vivre among Acadians. Nothing to do with amplification, one way or the other.

Re: Trying hard to make it look easy?

AB said:
“Highland pipes don’t require amplification.”

One wouldn’t think so, but they do.

Here, there’s The Wicked Tinkers which (until COVID) were making their fulltime living performing onstage at festivals all over the region.

The Highland piper in the band has a brilliant wireless setup for his Highland pipes, with a mic for the chanter and mics at the ends of the drones all feeding into a mini-mixer and wireless broadcaster hidden inside his bag-cover.

Thus he can jump down off the stage and march around through the audience etc without having the band’s overall sound levels and mix affected.

A band playing on a big stage at a big festival through a big amplification system would have a barely audible piper otherwise.

Though the Highland pipes seem loud, and for sure when I do church gigs and outdoor funerals etc I certainly don’t need amplification, I have had times like playing in an extremely crowded noisy pub on St Patrick’s Day when I could barely hear my own pipes.
