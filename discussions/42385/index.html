Posting self-written tunes

I posted the note below on the Tunes page in response to the submission of a self-penned tune, but it’s actually meant as a point for discussion and not as a comment on that particular entry:~

I was refreshing my memory about Jeremy’s guidelines for the site and was reminded about this one:

"Can I submit my own compositions?
Well… The Session isn’t really intended for that. If you do post one of your own compositions, then you must do your “penance”, so to speak, by balancing each original composition with about five trad tune settings".

Is there a case for adopting a convention to the effect that posters of own tunes accompany their entry with a note giving the names or Session ref. numbers of about five genuinely traditional* tune settings that they have offered by way of balance/penance in the spirit of Jeremy’s stipulation? Just a thought.

*Please don’t get in a huff about what constitutes ‘genuinely traditional’. I’m sure you know what I mean.

Re: Posting self-written tunes

This recommendation does not strike me as necessary.

If one clicks on the user-name of any tune poster here, it is straightforward to find all that poster’s previous tune-submissions via that poster’s profile page.

Very generally (though not always) there is enough information accompanying a tune entry to indicate if the tune is traditional, or at any rate pre-existing. This can be supplied by the person entering the tune, and/or by other posters. A variety of versions, and/or its existence on recordings, are other data that would indicate at least a strong likelihood that a given tune was around before its submission date.

Re: Posting self-written tunes

Jeremy or someone else may correct me but I believe that the original ethos behind the tune database was that we should submit these tunes which we are likely to play at our local sessions and as played there.

The database is huge now and even with the endless possibilities of local and regional variations and taste, it’s fairly obvious that the majority of us haven’t really been following these guidelines as strictly as we could.

In general, the tunes I’ve submitted have been played at places I go whether they be sessions, workshops, or for fiddle concerts. There are one or two which I just like and haven’t really “cropped up” in some of the places I’ve visited but they are good enough tunes which will get played somewhere I’m sure. In fact, one of the best feelings is when you visit a different session or meet a new player and get a chance to play good tunes you’ve learned which don’t usually feature too regularly.

Anyway, I daresay if your own original composition or that of one of your “mates”(A Llig term) gets played at your local session, then I don’t see an issue with it being posted here. However, just submitting newly composed tunes for the sake of it is a different matter.

As regards variations, I agree this is getting a little out of hand. I’m not sure what the value of this is unless the notes are significantly different. Of course, this does comply with the “as played at your local session” guideline but it’s a tricky one. Who is to say what is the “best” version of a tune? Even if it’s by a known composer, we don’t always know if it is accurate or the preferred version or a tune as he/she may not even play it the same way each time either. Also, it is not the done thing to transcribe a published setting directly from a book although we might have learned it from that source originally(In the mists of time) which is a different matter and, arguably, OK if posted then.

Re the last paragraph, sometimes I’ve searched several versions of a tune for the one I play but all in vain. Quite often, there was a bit of it in each. :-). However, I’ve never thought it particularly helpful to submit a slightly different setting of a tune which would not be, in my opinion, significantly better or worse than the one already there.

Mmm.

I don’t believe that tunes should be built up or constructed as if you were using Lego bricks. If your version of a tune is a little different, that’s OK. Unless you’ve really made a hash of learning it, the version or setting will be quite valid.

However, I think there’s a difference between learning a different setting of a tune or even a “learned version” evolving over a period of time naturally than deliberately adding, removing, or swapping parts or sections to suit.
Of course, adding ornaments according to your own taste or style of playing would be OK too but that’s slightly different, IMO.

Re: Posting self-written tunes

I think variations make for better music. It’s a lot more fun to play with somebody who plays a different version of the same tune.
There are some obvious rules for playing a variation:
#1: The chords have to be the same, or else it’s a different tune. It also has to be the same length.
#2: It has to be similar enough for other musicians to play along. If the musicians get confused, you need to stop and play a different version. This rule can be bended. There is a variation of Skye Boat song (I think Kevin Burke plays it, but I could be wrong) that has a completely different A part, and it’s really fun to play along with, as long as all the musicians are skilled enough to not be derailed by doing so.

ABC notation from arbitrary measure of hypothetical jig:

Re: Posting self-written tunes

I write tunes, though have not posted any here, partly due to my inefficiencies in ABC. I would say that I write in traditional style, and there are plenty of examples of tunes (and songs) out there that sound traditional but are in fact, not! And there are other regular subscribers here who do the same. So, was that tune composed in 2018 or 1758?
My first instrument is piano, so that is where most of my composition is done, and I would write in my preferred chords to fit my tune: probably for a major key tune, this would use a simple I IV V system first time round but add in relative minors for second time and beyond. But I then might have to take a backward step when I consider whether I could actually play my preferred chords on my button box - back to the relative majors! (So IMHO, tunes aren’t just about the melody, but also what you put with them.)
Yes, it is a great compliment if people ask you, “What was that tune” and ask for copies of it, and want to go on playing it. For me, it has happened rarely, but very rewarding when it happens.
And as an aside to this: I do love writing other parts and arrangements for existing tunes, and some of these are used by my musician friends on a regular basis.

Re: Posting self-written tunes

Interesting how the subject matter of a thread can evolve during the course of an exchange of views. I raised the topic of posting self-written tunes to test the water, so to speak, about whether people felt inclined to follow Jeremy’s guidance: that is, it’s not really what the site is for, but if you do it, then you should also make a reasonable contribution to the stock of traditional tunes. Personally, I’m inclined to go with Jerry O’Donnell’s view (above) but clearly other folks have other ideas.

It all became a discussion of the merits or otherwise of posting additional settings - sometimes with only minor variations - of tunes already on the site. At first, I wasn’t so exercised by this subject, but today I see some new contributions (q.v.) about ‘The Boy in the Gap’ - perhaps the first reel I ever learned, from the playing of Micho Russell whose recording features two distinct versions of the tune. I’m persuaded that there are tunes, like this one, where it really is worth taking a close look at different ways of phrasing it. The examples posted show how a really interesting melody line can be re-shaped to bring out a different character.

I guess over the years the site has become a repository of as extensive a corpus of material as is likely to exist anywhere in other traditional music collections, and it’s an increasing rarity to see first-time contributions of old tunes (because most of them are already here). So a lot of new postings are of new(ish) compositions transcribed from recordings of muzos working within the tradition: McCusker, McGoldrick, Hayes, Finnegan, Gavin . . . Perhaps it’s a fine line between adding a tune written, recorded, and published by someone in a currently-active Irish-genre band and a new tune written by yer average amateur sessioneer (like me). However like Kilcash (above) I’m cautious about the vanity-driven possibilities of the latter.
