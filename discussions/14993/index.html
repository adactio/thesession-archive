Re: The bass in ITM

The bass is not new in traditional music. I’m sure that the old Kilfenora céilí band had one and Angela Merry (now in her nineties) used to play bass with Clare’s Laichtín Naofa back in the 1950s. My own view is that it’s a lot more at home in a céilí band than a saxaphone (and I’m not biased as I used to play both). The approach I used was to nail the first beat mainly using the root note and the fifth. Some tunes would have interesting minor parts (like the turn in Sackow’s) and you could also fit in the odd run here and there (the turn in the Trumpet hornpipe was a great example of this). Follow the advice already given by listening to bands such as Lunasa or any recordings you find with Paul O’Driscoll on accompaniment as he’s a first rate trad bass player.

Re: The bass in ITM

My reservation about having bass in a session is much the same as that about having multiple backers of any kind. A single guitarist, pianist or bouzouki player in a session has has the freedom to experiment with voicings, substitutions, perhaps even unorthodox chord choices - all of which *can*, if done well, add to the overall energy of the session. The moment another backing instrument comes in, some of that creative freedom is taken away and the harmony begins to become prescriptive, as there has to be some form of agreement between the backers as to what chords they play. Bringing in a bass in addition to, say, a guitar, means that the backing cover a very wide frequency spectrum, thus becoming all the more dominant in the overall sound. There is then a tendency for the music to be driven less by the tunes themselves and more by the chords - which seems to me to undermine the essence of the music.

Standing back from myself for a moment, I can see that I might appear to be anti-progress. I think that there are bands who do great things with harmonic accompaniment and I certainly appreciate the degree of musical vision and innovation that went into the arrangements of The Bothy Band, De Danann and the many bands who have followed their precedent and developed it further. But, playing in a session, I do not want to be constrained by rigid harmonic progressions.

I have no doubt that it is *possible* for bass to be used effectively in a session. An accomplished jazz bassist is well used to interacting with several other musicians at once and, in theory, there is no reason why this cannot also be applied to traditional music. But accomplished jazz musicians who also have a thorough grasp of traditional music are few and far between and a half-ar$3d wannabe-trad-bass-player has great potential for destroying a session.

Re: The bass in ITM

I was in a session recently that had an accomplished bass player. The player actually played tunes on the thing. While being technically impressive, it was also quite annoying, because as soon as that started, I couldn’t hear a single one of the other 8 instruments playing, including my own. So my first suggestion would be to be aware of the fact that you can be extremely loud, and that’s not a good thing. (Being a banjo player, this is also something that I try to be aware of at all times…)

One of the common things that makes the general public NOT like Irish Traditional Music is the lack of bass. When you grow up on Rock and Roll or other popular forms of music, you get used to hearing the full dynamic range, and ITM often sounds “tinny” to people. So a bass in a session can be useful for making the music more accessible to the public. I think that’s one of the reasons that Lúnasa is successful in a wider market - they have almost Rock-like production, and the fact that they have a bass, and their set arrangements contribute to their accessibility.

I don’t play bass, so I can’t help you specifically, but I do play some counter-melody on bouzouki from time to time. An overly simplistic view of it would be all I could offer at the moment. I find that it is very important to know the tunes, and then I do things fairly consciously, like echoing runs, mirroring runs, and highlighting key notes with a harmonic or octave of the note. Now, with a bouzouki, I have a lot of other bits going on, like picking patterns and droning strings too, so I don’t know how helpful this would be.

Good luck with it!

Re: The bass in ITM

MacCruiskeen, I brought that point up for a couple of reasons. It was something that *I* thought when I was first introduced to this music. And it is also something that has been asked of me twice in the last month… “So… Is there any bass in this music? It’s all kind of tinny sounding” is a direct quote from a week ago.

When you’re raised on Rock, Country, Jazz, Hip Hop, or any number of other forms of music, you’re used to the music being built from the ground up - the “rhythm section” is always there. Irish traditional music is built from the top down. The melody is what matters, and any accompaniment is there to add to it, but it’s not the foundation. So people that aren’t familiar with the tradition often come to it missing what they would think of as being an integral part.

Re: The bass in ITM

There’s no need to take the approach that a bass doesn’t belong in a session. Sessions aren’t for creating a Pure-Drop (god, I hate that expression, but I really don’t know why) sound - and anyway there’s a good precedent of double basses and cello being used in trad before the X-brace acoustic guitar was invented, so really that argument’s not so good. On the other hand (as you’ll already know), it frequently sounds louder depending on whereabouts you are in location to the instrument - so be assertive about checking with people in different positions if it’s too loud. If anyone says yes, you might have to use a mute. This will also depend on whether you’ve got pizzacato, arco or hybrid strings on the bass.

In terms of what to play, following the guitarist’s bass lines and and playing fifths and thirds around that is a good place to start. Also, simply mirroring the bass of the guitarist’s changes but linking the bass notes together through scalar tunes can also work. Having a rough idea of the changes, and simply linking the arpeggios together can work - although the chromatic approach notes should probably be used sparingly! Removing every other note from the tune usually leads to a surprisingly effective bass line, which shouldn’t be too tricky to play - if they’re going at breakneck pace, taking the notes of beats 1 and 3 is also a reasonable way to get something out that fits the tune. Cycle of fifths/fourths is also useful for resolving at the end of a part.

A good way I found to get into making interesting bass lines (for guitar, not bass - although the bass line from a guitar part I wrote for a tune worked well when I used it was a bass part) is to use notation software like Sibelius to write bass parts. If you’ve got half-decent music theory, you’ll pick up fairly quickly what ideas do and don’t work in various melodic situations.

Re: The bass in ITM

“Nail the first beat of the bar” .

I fear there is a lot more to making the bass work in Irish Music
(especially in a Session) than a command like this yapped from the opposite side of a pint laden table.( No offence.)

For all we know,the poster, Mr Smith, is probably an accomplished bassist in whatever field, Irish or otherwise.

I’ve played double bass in sessions and used fretless
and fretted basses in bands playing Irish music. I’ve recorded with these instruments as well.
Here are some of my findings.
They are just ideas, not rules.
Things Ive found work.

(1) Quarter note syncopation in reels adds lift, bounce and doesn’t bog a light airy reel down .

(2) Melodic or harmony parts played high on the bass work well
punctuated by big open strings left to ring over a bar or two.

(3)Tonic to Fifth country two to the bar destroys Irish Music in my opinion.

(4) Walking fours can sound great on single jigs ( Treat 2 x 6/8
bars as 1x 4/4 bar).

(5) Walking fours can sound great on hornpipes.

(6) If you want to give the music somewhere to go don’t necessarily automatically go to beats one and three.

(7) Bouzouki’s often use off beat bass punctuation to great effect. Why shouldn’t the bass.

(8) I agree with listening to some of the piano backing. Brian Mcgrath would spring to mind. He might play a two to the bar but it will be a very harmonically mobile Walking two to the bar.

(9) 1 to the bar works great on reels and jigs. Even longer if you like. T.H. does spring to mind.

(10) Most bass players have got great ears anyway.

(11) Listen to the bass player from the Buena Vista Social Club. I don’t think any one plays on the beat for that whole work,
and that is dance music. The principal to me is the same for
jigs and reels etc. The melody instruments provide the beat themselves, and indeed most of these tunes sit great unaccompanied. The other instruments role is to lift them even higher, not pin them down.

(12) Start learning the tunes on the bass. Its fun, and you can punctuate with melodic stuff at the end of sections etc.

These are just my humble findings, I’m still working on all this stuff myself on guitar and basses.

Some great suggestions so far. I definitely agree - don’t try and play 1+5 2 beat riffs ad naseum like a country/western bassist (if you get anything out of this thread, take that home with you). I like the top down/bottom up analogy. Growing up as an American - the thing that stands out most to me in ITM is the unison playing - similar to everyone in pub singing a song with no instruments and no harmony parts. The melodies are perfect as they are - everyone seems to understand that they don’t “need” anything more, as though those tunes belong to the community, not the individual. You just don’t get that over here. (I know, a bunch of cowboys…) Not to say that everything in ITM has to be played this way, just that this is a very identifying element of the style. I can certainly understand why people resist tampering with strumming guitars and other new elements that basically can’t participate in the unmodified melody unless the player is very experienced.

Introducing new elements into a traditional style - broad acceptance typically requires that you first really understand the style inside/out, so you know what you are manipulating in the first place. Figure out what the elements of ITM are that has made it so widely appealing. I suggest one approach for anyone that wants to add bass to this music is to put your time in solidly on a fiddle first. If you understand the style well enough to make it work well from one of its core instruments, you are more apt to be in a position to start modifying it in a manner that is palatable to the majority of seasoned players.

Probably the longest way to get where your headed, but shortcuts rarely work in anything complex enough to hold your interest…
