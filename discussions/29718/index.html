Composing.

I’m curious about how other musicians compose. None of my music teachers or other mentors were composers, so I never received guidance or counsel on it. How I compose is, I check my inspiration, how I feel about it, then go to my instrument and play random patterns and phrases and see what pops up that I can work with. This works really well, but it’s not good for all cases, especially this one.

The thing is, I want to compose a reel. I want it to sound Irish. Not sure if it is necessary that it sounds Irish, but i’d like for it to. The problem is, I don’t have that deep, rooted influence. I also lack experience on fiddle. The method I use for composing is very dependent on influence, and my skill on piano. My idea is that the influence will work out the music in my head, and my skill will pull it out and put it into the instrument.

Because of this, i figure that I may have to dig a little deeper. Maybe go on an “Irish Only” music break. Or maybe go out and surround myself with the inspiration.(The inspiration is “Sunsets”, by the way.) Or maybe sit at the piano and work out a note by note melody, something I’ve never done before cause i’m afraid it may turn out “mechanical”.

So composers, how do you do it?

I compose melodies in my head all the time but never write
them down. Thinking of a nice melody and turning it into a
memorable Trad dance tune are two different things. At
least you should have a few ideas popping when you’re walking
the dog or whatever.

Then start thinking about structure and hooks - like top 40 pop
tunes do so well. Think about Cliffs of Moher for example - it
has an unforgettable hook at the beginning and a contrasting
B part - a comfortable structure.

I figure there’s enough great tunes out there that I don’t need
to add to the pile.

Re: Composing.

Actually I think u need to join that site to access the blog so here it is:

For those of you that don’t know me, I’m Enda Seery a traditional Irish musician and composer from Co. Westmeath, Ireland. I’m currently on the Masters in Irish Traditional Music Performance course at the Irish World Academy, University of Limerick. I have been on an extended Xmas holiday for the past while and I have been composing alot. I thought I would give some views on composing traditional Irish music.

For me, anyone can compose music, but to be a composer you must immerse yourself in your chosen genre of music. Of course for me, thats traditional Irish. Even the oldest of tunes have to have had a composer. When I compose I always keep the tune very much in the traditional idiom. That’s what I was brought up on listening and learning from great musicians like John Joe Gannon and Davy Joe Fallon here in Co. Westmeath. People have said that my tunes don’t sound like ‘new tunes’ but that this is a good thing. I’m flattered by these comments but that’s what I want to achieve when composing: keep the tune traditional whilst having a nice flow and rhythm to the tune.

I would like to say that I have no problem with some of the more modern, contemporary compositions but are they traditional? I don’t think so. But’s its a sort of a grey area for another day’s discussion. All I will say is for me, our traditional Irish music is a dance music, meant to be danced to and listened to with our ears and feet.

Composers I like are people like Paddy O’Brien(Tipperary), Sean Ryan(Fiddle) Charlie Lennon, Vincent Broderick, Richard Dwyer etc… composers and musicians who concentrate(d) on the traditional flow and melody of the tune.

How, when, where do I compose? Well it can happen at anytime, anyplace, anywhere. Sometimes some notes or a particular melody will just come into my head and I will hum the melody and record it onto my phone or dictaphone. I never write down the notes first. I will compose the tune and later write it down for my own records and teaching purposes. Tunes have come into my head whilst driving, in bed, teaching a class, even at mass! The key is try and keep the melody and record it as quickly as possible. I compose alot at home where my instruments are close at hand. I also love walking around the beautiful coutryside in Co. Westmeath where a particular melody of a jig, reel, hornpipe, slow air, polka etc… might come to me.

I agree with the late Ed Reavy who said he needed to be in a particular good mood to compose. Ultimately though, one needs to be listening to and playing music all the time. My first album ‘The Winding Clock’ featured seven of own compositions, 5 jigs, 1 reel and 1 slow air but I can tell you that my next album release will have many more, around 9-10 compositions. Albums cost money so bear with me! Also I am currently compiling my compositions for a future publication and collection, entitled ‘Tunes of the Streams’: Irish Traditional Music Compositions by Enda Seery. Streams is a reference to my home village of Streamstown.

Feel free to check out my website http://www.endaseery.com for info on my music. Let’s keep the tradition going strong!

Re: Composing.

If you can improvise then you are composing.

Random snippets are the bread and butter of composing.

A very good knowledge of music theory is essential because you have to know the rules to break them convincingly. And study all the instrument groups, their ranges, the effects they can do, how they sound in various combinations et cetera.

Don’t ‘try’ to sound ‘Irish’ or anything else, just be yourself. Since your influences will affect everything you come up anyway, if you are playing or thinking about ‘Irish’ music it is likely to pop up in the pieces you attempt.

If you are very good at transcribing music, fine, but try using software such as Sibelius. This will get your ideas down and proof read them for you. I have often found what I’ve got on the page sounds OK to me, but when someone else plays it, its ‘wrong’ (especially rhythm notation).

Good luck!

Re: Composing.

I think you’ve answered your own question. Immersion in, or familiarity with, the music is needed in order to recognise whether it ‘sounds Irish’.
Likewise, familiarity with your instrument is needed so that you aren’t side-tracked by the mechanics of playing, but can concentrate purely on the sound.
Your deep understanding of music in general will be invaluable in avoiding what I might call the ‘audio no-no’, but only you will know whether your composition is satisfactory. Like most of us, you will no doubt over the years accumulate discarded, abandoned, or ‘filed’ pieces of music that never got finished. Good luck, and give it your best, is all I can add.

Re: Composing.

I’ve written tunes with an instrument at hand, but they’ve all been derivative rubbish. I think the problem is in them falling under your fingers. Good tunes are in the mind, not the fingers. The best tunes are always “thought”.

I heard it described once that a good tune goes somewhere where you least expect it to go, but once it’s been there, you get the feeling that it could not have possibly gone anywhere else. I like that.

All the best tunes I’ve written have started in my head and been fully formed and polished long before the’ve been anywhere near the mechanics of actually playing them. (I’m not saying they are good tunes, of course, just better than the ones done on an instrument.)

I think every player of this music should be encouraged to write tunes. I think the process gets you to appreciate better which are the better tunes in the repertoire. It helps you to understand better the reasons for the choices that the really great composers made/make. I think there is a tendency in this music to revere some tunes as timeless classics, but an understanding of the composing process helps to appreciate the best tunes like they are new.

Re: Composing.

Understanding that there is a structure can be an advantage if you want your tune to follow a traditional pattern - how a motif or phrase is repeated in the course of a tune, and how another motif or phrase might be a response to the original. Even the understanding of this could be useful if you want to “break the rules” (though there seems to be a lot of this in what seems to be the composer attempting to sound original but sounding naff in the process).
What you might try to avoid is that structure becoming a formula. Tweaking something that starts in the head to conform to the structure to a degree might be a more fruitful course to follow.

[*So composers, how do you do it?*]

Many ways. Listening to music in general (but not exclusively trad) puts some ideas in, and gets the roots of a tune in the head - sometimes! From the top down, if you like.

Then there’s the “from the bottom up” way … playing various chord sequences, on piano if guitar, and working in a melody into in that way.

There are so many repeating common patterns and phrases in trad (as there are in most musics), and using these is yet another way of getting tunes out.

Re: Composing.

Making up tunes can happen is several ways, but if I try to make it happen it generally doesn’t.

Once a tune starts to form it seem to happen quite quickly - as if it is being set free as much as being created. In fact I often do not believe I can have actually created the tune, I regularly suspect myself of remembering it from something heard long ago and then nearly forgotten

The initial ideas can come in several ways such as
walking or driving - which seems to set up a pulse - but often these tunes escape and are forgotten by the time I get somewehere to write them down.

A similar risk applies when messing about with an instrument - I often stumble across nice sounding phrases and patterns and think they are so good that I am bound to remember them - only to find they have gone again

Improvising around existing tunes can generate new ideas if you keep going long enough - personally I really enjoy this

But for me the best ideas are related to outside (i.e. non musical) factors such as people, landscape, nature, moods, pictures etc

I find generally I develop the idea in my head and then see if I can play it on something. I need to write it down quickly or it may escape. It seems to get slightly easer the more I do, so maybe in another 50 or 100 years I will eventauilly compose some really good tunes :)

A final point - creating a new set is also an act of composition, even if the tunes are all indiviudally well known, as is devising accompaniement, as is all the little personal variations that people come up with for well known tunes

There’s a lot more composing going on than is often recognised - even by thise doing some of it

Re: Composing.

The londonderry hornpipe is the type of tune that is easy to latch on to, for those uninitiated to Irish tunes. It’s easy for them to digest because theoretically it makes them happy to see the chords line up.

I think the tunes that are really at the heart of the tradition are more ambiguous. Just when it appears that we have marked out two notes to an arpeggio the tune makes some stepwise movement or otherwise falls out of whatever pattern was beginning to form and starts a new pattern. all the patterns together make up a macro pattern of some sort. So you see the harmonic changes are present but they change at unusual times, and this can be why we say that no single harmonic analysis can be dictated, there are many different ways to interpret what is happening and that is why it sounds exciting to the ears.

The best tunes (in my opinion)have harmonic progressions that change out of sync with the beat at times and sometimes on off beats, sometimes on the downbeat.

The only thing that is really predictable about it is that changes on the downbeat often mark the beginning or end of a phrase, or the beginning of a cadence like figure. This type of figure can create the resolution necessary to make sense the more loose harmony of the center of the phrase.

I see many symphonies as being collections smaller parts or tunes. Tunes being collections of phrases that work together to create musical question and answer scenarios. At least that’s one way to interpret it. There are of course other forms that a tune or composition can take. Some baroque music is harmonic progression that literally repeats over and over, and you can find that pattern in folk tunes as well. Pachelbel’s Canon is a tune where each pair of harmonic changes contains a reflection of the progression as a whole within it’s individual notes, if that makes any sense.

Re: Composing.

I too often hear music in my head when performing some menial non cerebral task. Often I hum when I walk and I’m not really sure what I’m humming. Sometimes I hum seemingly endless quarter notes each one leading to some unknown point and each one creating simultaneous dissonance and consonance depending on if you compare it to the note previous to it or the note after it, or perhaps two notes before, or two notes after. I think that is what composers like Bach were doing some of the time. You could take each note and try to analyze an underlying harmony, perhaps modulations or perhaps just suspensions and anticipations. The note might be the root or harmonize with the root at the beginning and/or end of the phrase but as you approach the core of the phrase the note is less often attached to the root of any chord you might ascribe to it as much as it is attached to it’s own progression through the preceding and following notes. It is dictating the harmony though not explicitly, not obviously.

Jerone, From my perspective as a sometimes writer, the way you learn how to do it is to write. Put words in a row, try to paint a picture with them, try to illustrate a point (or in this case, put notes in a row). Learning to compose is just like learning to play a tune, the more you do it the better you get. You will produce a lot of junk along the way, but that is not a bad thing. Just resist the urge to share everything you write with the world. Writers talk about having a ‘trunk’ of their early stuff, and the worst thing that can happen is after a great author dies, they open that trunk and start releasing stuff that was better left alone.

Good thing I didn’t present it to my friends as my own work, that would have gotten a chuckle!

Re: Composing.

Writing tunes isn’t hard. Writing original tunes that sound traditional without sounding like every other tune is hard. So is coming up with something along the lines of Llig’s “unexpected and inevitable” that holds together well and is fun to play. You’ll know you’re on the right track when a tune just sticks, takes no real effort to remember how it goes.

Of course it helps to have a lot of trad tunes already in your head, to know the idioms and the vernacular inside and out. You’ll likely go through a phase of inventing tunes that are either bland, outlandish, or very similar to already-existent tunes. No worries. The good stuff will come out if you keep at it.

Re: Composing.

Several sessioners I know compose tunes. I may add, they are all seasoned players who know and have listened to a great deal of music - though not necessarily to the extent of the legendary walking encyclopedias of this music.

Their tunes tend to provoke one of four responses in me:

(1) “This is an okay tune. However, there’s an awkward join in the sixth bar of the second part, and isn’t the first part basically the third part of The Frolicking Cabbage Pickers, or something off ‘The Branch Line’?”

(2) “This is an OK tune.”

(3) “This is another OK tune.”

(4) “WOW !!”

The last one is my reaction to a tune that (at least to me) is really something else, and they do come along, but seem to do so rather less frequently.

Perhaps one road to composing good ones is composing quite prolifically, with a lot of okay ones or also-rans being as it were the base of one’s mountain of compositions. I haven’t really gone in for this. But in art, I know that the more one sketches, the more proficient one will be at doing more substantial works - not necessarily because one’s sketches will feed directly into them, but because the exercise of varied sketching will have increased one’s overall knowledge of the seen world and extended one’s powers of thinking laterally, and whatever. So, doing plentiful trial compositions *could* be helpful in a similar way.

I find it reasonably easy to rip tunes off.
Usually, it starts with a request or reason/occasion to compose. That usually dictates what time-signature/type of tune is required.
You should either develop your own style, or imitate someone elses style, and put your own stamp on it. Stick to your own style.
From then, the ideas generally come from picking a similar tune to what is required, then do something similar, but different. Take a phrase, invert it and see what happens.
I often try 3 or 4 different phrases until somethings takes shape.
