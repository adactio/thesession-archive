Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

I would recommend taking the fretting hand out of the situation and work on the pattern (DUD DUD …) on an open string, probably A or D would be most comfortable. Start slowly and only increase the tempo when you can play the pattern successfully at your chosen tempo. If possible, rather than playing equal quavers (eighths), try to make the first (D) a bit longer and the second (U) a bit shorter.

Another way to think of the pattern is to start playing, say A2A A2A (crotchet-quaver/quarter-eighth), all downstrokes. Once you have mastered that, put a wee upstroke between each of the two downstrokes.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

Practicing a DUD DUD picking pattern is a good way to get the jig rhythm instilled in your brain if you’re not already familiar with it. I think that’s the main benefit of pattern picking, just burning that jig feel into muscle memory (I know that isn’t a thing) when you’re first starting out.

However, once you have a good DUD DUD feel for the rhythm, you’ll probably find that you have to get comfortable breaking out of that pattern, even reversing it or using alternating picking while maintaining the same beat emphasis. This is because ornaments like hammer-on, pull-off, and “trebles” can break the pattern. String crossings and emphasizing the start of a new phrase or section in the tune with a downstroke will also mess with the pattern.

So you have to be flexible. In this respect there is no right or wrong way to pick the strings. Maintaining the rhythm pulse is what’s important.

For what it’s worth, I started by learning the DUD DUD pattern but I play mandolin now mostly with alternate picking, throwing in ornaments where it feels right and just maintaining the jig pulse throughout. I do stick with a fairly consistent DUD DUD pattern when playing rhythm guitar backup, because I’m not dealing with ornaments or string crossings.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

I’ll second Donald’s suggestion of starting with only the right hand, open strings. All of your focus will be on that hand and you can truly internalize the pattern.

After focusing on one string at a time, try incorporating multi-string patterns in jig time:
|:G,DD DG,G,:| |:DAA ADD:| |:Aee eAA:|
|:G,DG DG,D:| |:DAD ADA:| |:AeA eAe:|
|:G,DA ADG:| |:DAe eAD:| |:G,DA eAD:|

From there, scales in jig time are one option, or better yet, actual phrases in tunes.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

Good advice above! The reason that DUD DUD picking is really good for getting a jig rhythm going is that the notes in a jig (or any dance tune, for that matter) aren’t equal in length. In a jig, the 1 is a long note, the 2 is a really short note, and the 3 is a longer note, maybe halfway between the lengths of the 1 and 2. (This is a generalization. The 3 is sometimes emphasized, and can be up to as long as the 1).

Doing a DUD DUD picking pattern naturally leads you to that rhythm. Down strokes are naturally stronger due to gravity and our wrist mechanics, and the 1 & 3 are the notes that need to be stronger. The 3 also gets naturally shortened a bit because you need time to move your hand back up for the following down stroke. So just by using that picking pattern, you are giving yourself a good shot at playing jigs with a nice rhythm. Another benefit of picking with a strict pattern is that once you’ve internalized the pattern and never have to think of it again, there is never any ambiguity about which direction you’re going to pick any note, no matter what ornamentation or variation you might play.

However, triplets become a problem in DUD DUD picking scenario. There are 2 places you would put a triplet in a jig, either on the 1&2 or on the 2&3. And either way, if you want to keep your picking pattern going (and you want to start a triplet on a down stroke), then you’re going to end up with three down strokes in a row.

DUD (dud)D DUD (note the end of the triplet, followed by the 3, followed by the 1. All down strokes).
or
DUD D(dud) DUD (note the 3, followed by the 1, followed by the start of the triplet. All down strokes).

There are a couple of ways to deal with that. Some people do up-triplets

DUD D(udu) DUD.
But I can’t bring myself to do the other one with an upstroke:
DUD (udu)D DUD

because I have it so ingrained that I play a down stroke on the 1 that trying to start a triplet on the upstroke on the 1 doesn’t work for me.

For my own playing, I tend to just do the 3 down strokes in a row, because at speed at least, a triplet is a ‘gesture’ that I make that feels like one movement, not three (I tighten my wrist slightly as if I’m trying to stop the pick in the middle of the string, and it bounces back and forth). But I am more of a banjo player than a mandolin player, and it’s a bit more difficult on mandolin. The other thing I do, especially in jigs, is a bunch of ornaments that are done with my left hand, and my right hand just keeps the steady picking pattern.

Or finally, you can go the route of Conical bore and start adjusting your picking pattern in anticipation of your ornaments, or switch to alternating DUD UDU picking.

The bottom line is that it really doesn’t matter how you pick your jigs as long as they sound right! DUD DUD picking will likely give you the best chance at that, but once you have really internalized the rhythms, however you want to pick that works for you is OK.

this is one more vi-vs-emacs holy war. mandolin standard technique is “alternate picking”. it works for reels, jigs, polkas, waltzes, etc. proponents of “irish mandolin” insist that jigs be played dud-dud but offer no evidence (i.e. blind test results) that it works better by any definition of better.

proponents of dud-dud say words about this pattern accidentally creating correct note length for an irish jig to sound irish. I think one should not rely on such accidents and instead have full control over note length and placement when using any picking pattern.

dud-dud (3+3) is not the only jig pattern. there is also the 1+4 pattern (good example was just posted by TheBlindBard). there is longer patterns that cover 2 bars (learn from Natalie Haas). there is English and Sweedish jigs. there “just a tune in 6/8”. dud-dud is insufficient to cover all of them. to play Greek music patterns 3+2+2, etc one will have to learn dud-dud-dud-dud sooner or later).

then there is beginners who struggle mastering basic alternate picking and become lost and confused when told “but jigs have to play like this”. beginners should learn solid basic technique before going into special techniques.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

“pick direction is more important in guitar as a downstroke and upstroke sound very different on a chord but not so on a mandolin, where both up and down sound exactly the same.”

Obviously, when playing chords, pick direction is especially important. But as for up- and downstrokes sounding exactly the same on mandolin* (or flatpicked guitar), that depends on picking technique. Without working on this specific aspect of technique, the pick will not necessarily attack the string at the same angle in both directions, so the note will not necessarily sound the same in volume or tone; also, a downstroke is assisted by the weight of the player’s arm (whislt an upstroke is hindered by that weight) so it will naturally tend to be stronger. This is partly how different picking patterns ‘colour’ the rhythm in certain ways. You can *learn* to make up- and downstrokes sound identical by default, and adjust individual strokes as the rhythm demands, but you can also capitalise on the difference between them.

*This is assuming you are playing melody, not chords.

I was thinking about the whole “pattern” thing. One aspect which I don’t think gets mentioned enough is when you’re strumming DUD DUD (or DUDU or whatever you’re playing), that isn’t really an instruction to hit every single eighth note. Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

Joseph, lots of “unnatural” skills seem awkward at first. Try pedal or figure-8 bowing on fiddle—when most people first try it, they can feel their neural circuits melting.

The point is to do the skill until it *does* feel natural. This applies whether you want to use alternating picking to play jigs (and you have to work at putting the emphasis in the right place whether on a down or up pick) or the DUD DUD pattern. I have yet to meet anyone who could do either of these things the first time they held a plectrum. Neither of them is “natural” or innate.

A broader point: we improve as musicians only by acquiring skills and modes of expression beyond our comfort zone. As we progress, our comfort zone expands, but there are always new ideas that beg us to reach and stretch.

As for a specific jig, Jimmy Ward’s Favourite works well on plectrum instruments such as mandolin, banjo, or zouk. It all fits on the D and A strings without too many string crossings, and you can play three quavers (eighth notes) for any dotted crotchet (dotted quarter) notes—just keep the DUD DUD pattern going. In the beginning, don’t try to put in any triplets or other ornaments, just focus on playing the basic tune with jig timing and pulse.

If that feel too awkward, start by simply playing the DUD DUD pattern on a single open string and focus on creating a smooth jiggy timing and pulse.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

“But following the picking pattern (which he denotes with up and down arrow) it still doesn’t come natural to me.”

The nice thing about the DUD DUD hand rhythm is that once you have it down, it will feel like it comes naturally to you, and you’ll never have to think about it again! (Same as DuD UdU jig hand rhythm would, but that one doesn’t automatically give you a nice jig feel, and you have to work on getting the emphases right, which is why I used upper and lower case letters there…)

I would suggest that when you’re first working on that picking pattern, you DON’T try it on a jig that you already know, because you’ll be fighting your muscle memory. As gimpy mentions, doing it on a single string to get the feel of it is a good way to start. The next step might be to cross strings in a couple ways (i.e. DAD DAD, and then ADA ADA). And then maybe work on playing scales with it. Start that process by playing the same note for all three (i.e. GGG AAA BBB etc.), then you can push that further by moving in the scale faster (i.e. GAA ABB BCC) and then further with GAB BCD DEF, etc. You can make up your own little drills to do all this if you like.

Once you have a little feel for the hand rhythm, then try learning a new jig (gimpy’s suggestion of Jimmy Ward’s is a good one if you don’t already play it. But pretty much any jig will do…) and try to learn it with the new picking pattern. Go as slowly as you need to get it right. Then once you have the tune down, try speeding it up a bit without compromising the picking pattern.

This is a painful process. I remember that it felt like it ruined my jig picking for months on end. But at some point, it will click, and you’ll feel your hand dancing to the tune. From then on, it will feel automatic and you’ll never have to think of it when learning or playing jigs, and any time you break the pattern, that’s what will feel awkward!

"As a banjo player, one of the most common rhythms you’ll come across is the down up, down up pattern. This is a great rhythm for many songs, but when it comes to playing Irish jigs, I prefer to use the down up down, down up down pattern.

The reason for this is that the down up down, down up down pattern better captures the bouncy, lively feel of an Irish jig. The extra downbeat in the middle of the measure gives the music a bit more swing and energy, which is perfect for the fast-paced nature of a jig.

Of course, there’s no right or wrong way to play an Irish jig on the banjo. Different players will have their own preferences, and there’s no one-size-fits-all approach. But for me, the down up down, down up down pattern just feels more natural and fitting for this genre of music. It allows me to capture the lively spirit of an Irish jig and add my own spin and flair to the music.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

It is interesting that nobody has yet mentioned DDU picking – it has been discussed at some length at Mandolin Cafe. There are a few players that advocate it as the go-to pattern for jigs (Dan Beimborn is one); Donal Lunny uses it, although he is better known as an accompanist than a tune player.

I have tried it, largely unsuccessfully. Although, technically, it is merely a different phase (‘mode’) of DUD, it somehow does not embody what I find to be the natural rhythm of a jig and therefore, feels like a major struggle for my co-ordination. Perhaps, if one puts in the effort to overcome its counter-intuitiveness, there are benefits to be reaped (the different possibilties for pick ornamentaion is one I have seen mentioned).

Whilst I may not be a fan of DDU as a ‘staple’, it certainly has a place as part of a system of ‘economy’ picking. The Kid on the Mountain is a good example of how this can come into play. In bar 6 (i.e. bar 2 of the B-part), the phrase

GAB dge

the pick crosses to a higher (in pitch) string on the 2nd note of each group of three. So it is perfectly natural, and ergonomic, to use two successive downstrokes. (I believe this is how a classical mandolinist would typically approach a phrase like this.) I would not necessarily pick it that way, as I am quite comfortable with strict DUD most of the time. But if I were having to keep up with a very fast session, I might; it allows the hand a bit of a rest and lessens the risk of tensing up.

It could be useful to practise DDU picking as an exercise, so that you have it available when you need it.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

this thread came full circle, let’s see if we can get a few more rounds out of this topic.

DUD-DUD will give you a 3+3 groove, that’s it. if that’s what the tune wants and what other band members play, great.

alternate picking is basic mandolin technique that can be used to produce any groove, for example, 3+3, 1+4, 4+1, etc irish jig grooves.

so, the usual question of general vs special technique.

for slip-jigs there is a special kink. with alternate picking, you will be playing “upside down” half of the time, a yummy flavour change . compare to DUD-DUD-DUD-… machine-gun groove.

btw, there is a connected misconception that “D” strokes are inherently stronger than “U” strokes. of course, it is basic picking technique to make “D” and “U” strokes sound the same (tremolo is an obvious test/application of this). next basic picking technique is to make either D or U sound stronger or weaker as needed, under full control.

btw2, playing solo, you can use any groove, you can even play jigs as reels and reels as jigs, using any picking pattern you want. playing with other musicians, grooves have to be compatible. this requires some flexibility. a player who can only do dud-dud 3+3 jig grooves may find themselves boxed in.

Re: Learning Jig Picking (mandolin)

You certainly have the physical ability to be able to emphasize any picking stroke, but the very existence of gravity will make a down stroke naturally stronger, combined with the fact that our musculature has evolved to push more than pull (think pounding a nail with a hammer or casting a fishing line), but I’m not trying to say that we can’t overcome that. Certainly, there are great players who play with completely different picking strategies. I just tend to recommend DUD DUD to people that are struggling with playing a nice jig rhythm. (This tends to be people who haven’t been surrounded by the music their whole lives -- like myself). Learning to play jigs with that pattern definitely helped me develop a nice jig rhythm, which I was struggling with before (some 20+ years ago).

With regards to DDU DDU picking, many people that I have talked to people about that are actually talking about the same DUD DUD pattern, but they think of it in their heads as DDU DDU because what really makes a jig swing is the swell from the 3 to the 1 (DD). I have seen a few people teach the picking pattern by starting people just picking the down strokes (DD2 DD2), and then adding the up stroke instead of playing the quarter note. Which gives you DDU DDU. But in reality with where it is in the jig, it’s D|DU D|DU D|… (Dan Beimborn is an exception to that, he actually picks DDU DDU, but I’m not sure I’ve ever seen anyone else do that on a regular basis…)

The bottom line is that any way you pick jigs is going to have advantages and drawbacks, and it’s really up to the player to find what both works for them and sounds good. For me, it was ingraining DUD DUD that ultimately helped my jigs sound “right”.
