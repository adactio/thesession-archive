Re: Two questions

Unfortunately “Concert Flute” has multiple meanings, but they stem from the same root:

A concert flute is what the classical soloist plays.

So there is a particular wooden, simple system flute that is refered to as a “Concert Flute.” This is the flute from which the modern Irish Flute is derived, but the modern Irish flute is not a Concert Flute. The Concert Flute is always keyed and tuned in C. The Irish Flute may or may not have keys and is tuned in D.

But in modern classical jargon the instrument that has taken the place of the above flute is also refered to as a concert flute (note the lack of capitalization). It is the Boehm system flute, but only the Boehm system flute tuned in C.

What the soloist plays.

Re: Two questions

“I would say G. Even though it’s not really a G on that A440 scale.”

And you would be incorrect, because G is not defined by a dot on a page, but rather by it’s relationship to your tuning standard. It’s a frequency, not a dot. The dot is not the note. The tone is the note. What your electronic tuner would tell you the note is the note.

Within a certain frame of reference you might make yourself understood, but outside that frame of reference you would be speaking nonsense, because that frame of reference is one that is used a shorthand, but does not actually represent “The Truth.”

When you say “G” what you really mean to convey is a certain fingering and you are using “G” as a shorthand way of saying “put your fingers like this,” but “G” is an actual note, not a fingering, and if you say “G” to the marimba player he’s going to play a G as the tuner would show it. Confusion all around because you are using the shorthand outside the frame of reference where it is valid.

In point of fact, what you really mean to convey is play “Fa.” If you specify a root note (Bb session) and a scale system (diatonic) only the root note and intervals have any “reality.” It’s all Do Re Mi. Standard notation, which is, at heart, really piano notation, obscures that.

The issue between Q and myself is because he is similarly using a shorthand way of speaking that is understood within certain, limited frames of reference, but outside of where that the frame of reference is valid. So he calls an instrument which cannot even play the note C, nevermind a whole C scale, a C instrument.

And it all derives from thinking of the dots on the paper as having some “reality” to them. They are a pure abstract idea. A note is physical. It’s “real.”

“Is that why you’re saying we can use duckies and cows instead of A, B, and C? Because deciding the tone at 440 is called ”A“ is arbitrary and relative?”

No, no, no. I’m saying that every *symbol* we use to denote a particular tone is arbitrary. Since the symbols are arbitrary we can chose to use whatever symbol we want for any particular tone, and if we assign some sort of glyph to each tone Q’s way of looking at things vanishes instantly.

If the D produced by a trumpet is represented by a picture of ducky, and only that tone is so represented, he cannot confuse it with the symbol he interprets as C, which would be a picture of a cow.

Simple diatonic instruments such as whistle really should be notated with just symbols for Do Re Mi that are unrelated to standard notation. It would avoid a lot of the pain and anguish that whistle players indure trying to make sense of a notation that was not intended for such simple instruments.

I can easlly convert any non C instrument to a C instrument by Q’s defintion; and vice versa, with one simple tool, without touching the instrument.

The tool is a pencil. I would use it to change a single symbol on the score.

The clef.

Defining A as some particular frequency is just a means of insuring that mulitple instruments can play in unison. That is what is meant by “concert pitch,” and it’s needless for solos.

“How can ‘Doh’ not be the lowest note on a whistle?”

Take a tube. Tune it to a fundamental tone of C#. Now, drill holes in it that produce a diatonic D scale. Ta da! You now have a “vented” D whistle. Put your pinky down on the “vent” and you play low, low Re. The D scale actually begins with one hole open. Look at a “vented” flute.

I started this with a seemingly innocent question (now I realise there aren’t any!) - why is a boehm flute/ descant recorder considered ‘in’ C whilst the simple system flute / whistle most commonly played is ‘in’ D. Note that the two ‘C’ instruments mentioned are considered chromatic, although maybe someone will shout ‘foul’ because of the cross fingerings required, and with some justification - it’s tough to play in Ab on the recorder! Then you have other fully chromatic instruments like the clarinet that are still labelled as being ‘in’ Bb. So I think we must differentiate between transposing instruments (Clarinets, Treble recorders etc etc) and diatonic instruments available for convenience in different keys (whsitles, some button boxes).

The comparison made between the soprano/descant recorder and a vented whistle actually muddies the water again - precisely because the recorder whilst chromatic through various compromises is tuned to ‘C’ - as KFG points out it would need modification or cross fingering to get the D scale. So the comparison doesn’t help with the core problem of labelling the instruments C or D. What makes it even more confusing and highlights the lack of consistency in this key labelling scheme is the keyed simple system flute in D with a C foot - same length tube as the boehm, siimilar range (maybe not the top half an octave) and both chromatic. One is labelled as being in D, the other in C. If there’s any sense to be made of it, it can only be that the simplest scale (lifting one finger at a time) generates notes in the D scale on one and C scale on the other. But something about even this definition doesn’t quite feel right.

My original question was about the labelling, not the physics or even the music theory, both of which I feel fairly happy with.

As for what’s in what key, we have two different systems going on here:

Official music: the D whistle or flute is a C instrument, because the note C that you play on the whistle is the note C as written on the page.

The system ITM always used: the lowest (6 finger) note defines the key of the instrument. You only read the music reluctantly anyway.
