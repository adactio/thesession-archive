Re: Why is it considered blasphemy to learn tunes only from the notes?

I think it depends on what you mean by “only” from the notes. Everybody’s met that (usually classically-trained, but could be rock/pop/folk too) musician that thinks that reading the dots on the page will tell them everything they need to know about the music and there’s no need to spend time listening to, say, Tommy Peoples or Matt Molloy to actually get the feel of the music. That position is pretty hard to argue in favor of. But, if you’re talking about learning a tune from the dots once you’ve learned the feel of the music (which does take a substantial amount of listening), you’ll have plenty of people who wouldn’t have any problem with that. But, I do think if you find a recording or two to listen to, even just on youtube, I think it will enhance your learning process.

Personally, when I learn a tune, I find I learn it easiest, quickest, and most permanently by 1) listening first, 2) then looking at dots while I put my fingers where they need to go, 3) taking the music away as soon as possible, and 4) doing it again the next day, preferably without music at all, though often I have to check once. I learn best by feel and touch (“kinetic learner” for you educational types). Different learning styles may have different learning processes.

Re: Why is it considered blasphemy to learn tunes only from the notes?

There is a popular myth that ITM is somehow different from other forms of music. The popular argument is that you cannot learn the essence of Irish music from the notation, as if you can with any other form of music (which, of course, you can’t). The willful ignorance of that argument ignores the fact that one who learns a tune with notation is not, in fact, attempting to learn the art of playing ITM. To willfully confuse the two goals is to elongate a senseless argument for which many threads can be found on this site. Simply stated, the process of learning how to effectively play ITM is a subject for another thread. If your goal is to learn a new tune, use any method that allows you to get the tune down. If you have also learned how ITM should be played, learning a new tune using written notation cannot possibly slow you down or somehow diminish your traditional chops.

The two kinds of people most likely to learn tunes only from the dots are exactly the people who shouldn’t be:
- classically trained musicians (may have hubris of thinking that sheetmusic tells them most of what they need to know, may lack ability to discern the magnitude of difference between their playing of a tune and someone elses, may mistakenly believe that they are incapable of learning by ear)

Re: Why is it considered blasphemy to learn tunes only from the notes?

My own experience is that in learning a tune by ear I’ve explored all sorts of possible, mostly ‘wrong’, notes and in doing so found a number of different ‘right’ ones - looking at dots provides only a single ‘right’ note in each case. Sure you can find dots which include variations, as an experienced player you can develop variations from a single line of dots, but that understanding of the tune as a fluid open set of sounds is what you get from ear learning.

A separate though related point often made by several departed members was that well developed ear skills are essential to playing the music with other people at a high level; you have to be able to hear exactly how others are playing the tune and adjust your own playing as necessary. That’s what makes a session really sing, If learning a tune by ear seems like hard work compared to looking up the dots then you’re not even approaching the level of ear skills necessary.

This is a point of view that upsets many people. It upset me when I came across it first, because I didn’t care to admit that I simply haven’t worked hard enough at my own ear training, but that’s the truth. So no, it’s not blasphemy, just a poor representation of the deity. I can confess my sins of sloth in resorting to dots, gluttony for wanting more tunes than I work out by ear in the time I commit, envy of those who really have done the hard work.

Mea culpa. But I’m OK with what I do, I know what I’d have to do to make the difference, unlikely to have the time and energy these days. I’ve learnt enough to know when to shut up and listen.

@tdrury

I agree that there are some aspects in which the “sheetmusic is to music as writing is to language” analogy works, in that there are similarities, but I feel that, particularly for this discussion, the differences outweigh the similarities.

First, music is not a language. It has no semantics, it has very little grammar. Yes, it’s like a language and there are many useful parallels that can be drawn, but ultimately, it is very specifically *not* a language.

Because of this, where the main concern of sheetmusic is to convey as precisely as possible how music should sound (with the caveat that with infinite precision comes infinite verbosity), the main concern of most written language is to convey the morphemes that make up what is said - i.e. the elements that create meaning, as opposed to the elements that create sound - although many languages do have a writing system that more or less approximately convey sound.

Featural scripts, such as hangul (where each character is constructed in ways that give clues as to how it’s pronounced) and the rare languages that have a good sound-to-writing mapping do convey some part of the sound in the writing and, as you highlight in your analogy, then you have to know *how* they sound to be able to pronounce them correctly (“if you speak the language”) - i.e. there is a mapping between the writing and the phonemes (or “sounds that make up the language”), not between the writing and the specific phones (or “sounds in the abstract such that anyone can pronounce them”).

In that sense, sheetmusic is a bit more like the international phonetic alphabet. But even that is often written in “shorthand”, underdescribing sounds because we know “how they are pronounced in the language we are transcribing”.

Re: Why is it considered blasphemy to learn tunes only from the notes?

The biggest mistake I’ve seen many beginner musicians making (an alarming number really) is that they believe someone who tells them that they should not learn from sheet music but, should instead sit in on sessions and try figure the tunes out for themselves. They’re often told “even if you play just one or too notes on each play through thats fine”. It’s not.

Inevitably the sessions are pacing along at a rapid pace and many tunes might not recur for 3-4 weeks.

Its all well and good encouraging beginners to get into the music, and immerse themselves in it so as to understand it and feel it, and learn how to listen to it, so that they can then play it. However, I’ve come across beginners who have been playing for 2-3 years and still don’t have any grasp on the basic physical techniques required to play properly, because they still know very little tunes, and are still hopfully waiting for the “breakthrough” they believe will come when one day they will turn up at a session and play every tune like they composed it themselves.

The only way to learn muscle memory and technique quickly is to play constantly every day. If you don’t play a tune properly, you won’t develop the necessary technique to play the tune correct, nor will you develop the technique to enable you pick up other tunes quicker.

I believe, the best way to learn Traditional Music is to intensely learn the correct notes of as many tunes as you can remember using sheet music (to simply memorise the phrases) and then constantly develop those tunes by referencing other players via recordings or sessions, and also by arranging them in sets.

Personally I think the idea of telling an outright beginner that they need to learn from ear, and should not use sheet music is nonsense and very inhibitive.

Its a different story entirely when one has developed a good grasp on the basic techniques required to play the music and has a good selection of tunes in their repertoire that will provide them with a reasonable range of aural reference and plenty of technical know-how to quickly and smoothly “fall in” with familiar phrases.

Re: Why is it considered blasphemy to learn tunes only from the notes?

It might be a good practice to get one of those book/CD sets that contains both the audio and an “exact” transcription of the music. Then - without looking at the sheet music first - listen to the tune and try and give yourself 30 minutes to transcribe it. Pretend, if you like, that you’re a music transcriber who has been hired to transcribe this tune for someone, and you only have a half hour to do it. Then compare your transcription to the actual notation and grade yourself on how far off you were, making note of places you were way off and understanding why you heard it differently. Were you cutting corners by adding a standard “lick” in a place where this tune had a unique phrase? Spend 30 minutes a day doing this 4 or 5 nights a week and your ear will have no choice but to improve. Soon you’ll be transcribing more accurately in less time.

Another way of thinking about it is to act as if you were writing the tune - like it were an original tune you are writing for yourself. Take an existing tune that you want to learn and listen to it enough so that you can hear it in your head. Then go on a walk outside and whistle the tune to yourself, imagining that it is a brand new melody you are hearing in your head for the first time that you’ve gotta get down on paper or record before you forget it. (I’ve seen tune composers describe this experience). If you act as if you are writing an “original” tune that sounds like this, then it doesn’t matter if you get a note off here or there. Over time you can refine that or just have your own version/interpretation of the tune, right or wrong.

This is folk music so you don’t have to worry too much about getting all the notes exactly right, as long as the feeling and lilt are there. If you view the tunes as incarnations rather than carved in stone written pieces, then whatever you manage to conjure up in the moment will suffice, depending on what your intent or ultimate goal is. In other words, don’t let a fear of messing up prevent you from playing by ear. What’s worse - playing a rote tune from sheet music note for note, or playing a similar sounding melody that is pouring through you, where you and your instrument are just the hose spraying out musical water?
