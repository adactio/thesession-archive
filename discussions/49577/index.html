Tips for learning new tunes

Hello, I’m trying to expand my repertoire and therefore learn new tunes by heart.
I find that I take too much time to memorize a tune, I often miss passages here and there while I can play it quite easily by reading the tab.
I play every day but I probably don’t have the right method.
Normally my brain should work properly as I’m 36 years old :)
Do you have any advice or can you share your way of learning new pieces?
I play tenor banjo,I only play traditionnal music without dispersing myself towards other styles of music since 2 years. And I listen most of the time traditionnal music.
Thank you!

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

The best is always playing in a group, live - no notes/tabs. Find a recording or a YouTube video of the tune and play along - no music/tab.

It’s a common challenge for people learning tunes - being able to read and play but it’s just “not there” when the score is taken away. You need to train your ears and your tune memory. Just takes time. And it does get easier.

Can you whistle, hum or lilt/sing the tune? I know I “have” it when I can do this.

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

What these discussions over the years have shown is that different people have different learning modalities.

Myself, I memorise a tune much much more quickly if I never see any written version, but just listen and play along.

I go to the tune here, find the version that’s closest to the way I want to play it, and hit Play Audio and simply play along over and over (not looking at the music on the screen). I’ll have the tune down pretty quickly. The fact that I’m forced to figure everything out using only my ear and memory is what makes it go into longterm memory straightaway.

I used to make my living reading sheet music, but when I sightread it goes straight from my eyes to my fingers and bypasses my longterm memory.

But there are people who are the opposite! People with photographic memory, so they look at the sheet music once, and ever after play the tune by sightreading the sheet music they see in their mind. They don’t “learn” the tune at all, they don’t have to.

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

Don’t try to learn the whole tune in one lump, break it down into bite size chunks of one or two bars. Play the first phrase repeatedly, then close your eyes and check that you’ve got it. Move on to the second phrase check that you’ve got that, then join the two together and play them with your eyes closed. Keep adding a couple of bars at a time like this until you are playing the whole first half of the tune. Then move on the second part.

To expand on gbyrne’s comment: try learning the tune by heart _before_ you attempt to play it. In other words, don’t try a tune on your instrument until you have listened to it enough that you can sing it or lilt it, or at least know in your head exactly what note or phrase is coming next. Liberate the tune from your banjo - it’ll thank you :-)

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

I try to think of learning tunes as “internalizing” them, instead of “memorizing” them. To me, memorization is a process we use to cram often uninteresting information into our brains for later recall. If you think of learning capital cities, multiplication tables, or the history of leaders of your country in chronological order, that’s what is brought to mind for me when I think of memorization. If you think of music or songs from your childhood that you can still remember (“Happy Birthday” is often used as an example), that’s what I think of as internalization. You generally learned that from repetition and context. It’s pretty likely that you didn’t pull out written notation to learn Happy Birthday, or any of the Beatles songs, or whatever…

For me, learning tunes generally starts with repetition. I have a new recording that I like, or all of a sudden, people are playing a tune in sessions a lot. I hear it repeatedly. You say that you primarily listen to Irish trad, so my first tip is to turn your listening into a more active process. Instead of just listening, try listening in a more deliberate way. Listen to the overall context of the tune. Like where does it go up and where does it go down? Where does it do something catchy or unexpected? What makes the parts of the tune fit together, do the parts resolve the same, or echo the other parts in some way? Then take whatever information that you have gathered and make it an interactive process. The easy thing to do is to try to lilt or sing along with the tune as you’re listening to it. Even when you’ve sung a part wrong, you’re learning more about the tune. (BTW, a good singing voice is not required for this!)

That same kind of process can work with an instrument in your hand. The more experience you have with your instrument, the better, but this can be a useful process for even a complete beginner. So it will help you at any stage of your playing. Like others have mentioned, it can be useful to break tunes down into smaller, digestible pieces, but you want to make sure that you’re not losing the context of the melody. So if you need to work on a phrase that you struggle with on your instrument, it can help to keep it in context by playing the bits before and after the part that you’re working on.

My next tip would be to try to get yourself to a place where you use written notation of a tune (dots, tab, or whatever) only as a reference, and not as your source. I find with a lot of people, especially early on in their journey, using written notation when they’re learning makes it difficult to play when they’re not looking at the notation. But ultimately, our goal is to be able to play a tune without notation in front of us. The best way to get there is to learn the same way you want to play. These days, with ubiquitous recording and playback devices that can slow down music, we have the world of trad music at our fingertips, and lots of resources for audio sources of tunes. So I would suggest using those for learning, and only using notation as a reference if you’re struggling to figure out what a particular phrase is actually doing, or can’t remember how a tune starts, etc.

One more tip I will offer is to learn the tunes that really speak to you. If there’s a tune you love, you’re already part of the way to learning it, and learning it will feel less like work and more like fun!

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

The short answer: Wholeheartedly Yes to singing the tune first (singing the tune and not just the notes of the tune, there is often a world of difference). Don’t memorize the tune. Learn the tune, make it yours. Study and practice right and left hand mechanical skills. Combine that with intimate knowledge of your instrument, and that is wholly different from learning where to put your fingers for “that” string of notes. When you can hear a figure and make it happen without thinking … you got it. It takes time and thoughtful, disciplined practice. Full disclosure here, I’m not all that good, but I’m a lot better than I was before I started to practice what I preach.

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

Anyone who actually knows a tune can hum, whistle or lilt it. Maybe not perfectly, but recgniseably. We need to be able to hear the tune in our heads in order to play it.

The sine qua non of tune acquisition is listening. If you hear it enough-- and really listen to it, like close your eyes and focus on just listening, and not thinking about modes/finger moves/scales bla bla-- eventually you will have mental representation of the tune. You will be able to “hear” it in your head. At this point, learning the finger and/or mouth moves is much easier than if you start with dots, or by playing something once and then trying to imitate it right away. Listening is a kind of practice.

We also know from cognitive psych that practicing something at intervals (rather than for a long period of time at once) is best. So….make a playlits of say 4 tunes. Listen every day once or twice. Once you can hum/whislte/lilt them, start with your instrument.

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

Learning tunes by ear is great, but if you’re not there yet, don’t worry. You’ll get there, honest - and it’ll surprise you when it happens. It’s not something that happens by trying harder, you’ll just realize one day that you know a bit of some tune that you’ve never learned. What’s going on there is your brain recognizing patterns that you’ve internalized from learning, and more precisely from playing other tunes. Until you’ve played lots of tunes, it can be difficult to connect the sounds coming in with the sounds you already know how to make.

In the meantime, you can speed up the process of internalizing tunes by writing them out in a notebook in ABC notation, and when you find yourself with a bit of down time (waiting for a bus, say, or if you’re early for an appointment - any time you have a few minutes unspoken for) you can “play them back” in your head off the page. You can visualize playing the tune at the same time (playing the tune on your mental banjo) or you can “visualize” just the sound of the tune - whichever works better for you. Over time, this will get the tune into your brain, where it belongs, and ultimately it will come out of your fingers.

I do not recommend trying to memorize tunes by playing them directly from the sheet music - this is great practice in the art of sight reading, but not very helpful for playing the tune in a session context.

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

When I want to learn a new tune, I do it strictly by ear if I have an easy-to-follow demo recording. Otherwise I use a process that engages both parts of my musical brain: I listen to a number of different recordings, pick out the one I like best, then find sheet music that is close to the recording. Then I listen to the recording again several times while following along on the sheet music. Then I play along with the recording while reading from the sheet music. I usually have the tune internalized in short order after that.

The fastest I ever learned a completely unfamiliar tune was by transcribing it as I listened.

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

Drouch, lots of good advice above. Yes, focus on learning by ear, and on internalising the melody, not just memorizing it.

Listen for the call-and-answer structure of many tunes. Learn to recognize that many tunes do something like this:
Phrase 1|Phrase 2|Phrase 1|Not-really-an-ending|
Phrase 3|Phrase 2|Phrase 3|Actual ending||

To develop those skills, start with short, simple, repetitive tunes. I’m not saying you shouldn’t learn some convoluted five-part reel if that’s what calls to you. But it’s far easier to train your ear, your grasp of phrasing, your understanding of intervals and melodic contours, etc., on short, simple tunes. It will make it easier to then move on to more complicated tunes. Focus on the basic melody line. Focus on one tune until you can consistently play it through 3 or 4 times in a row without stopping. When you’re learning a tune, play it for 20 or 30 minutes every day. Play it before you go to bed at night and again first thing in the morning. Walk before you try to run.

Of course, it helps to learn the tunes that get played at your local sessions. Chat up the players there and make a list of what they play. Then start with the shorter, simpler tunes.

If you don’t already know them, here’s a short list of good, simple reels and jigs, common to sessions worldwide, for growing your tune-learning skills. There are hundreds more, so if you find short, simple tunes you want to learn, add them to the list. Learn them all.

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

My process is basically two steps:

1) Be able to sight read it.
2) Record a section (usually an 8 bar part) and put it on loop playback and play along till I get it right. Repeat daily until bored.

Step (1) for me is generally immediate but you’ve got to get it to the point where it’s fluent and correct before you try to memorise it, or you’re just remembering stuff wrong.

Step (2) is really simple, and the only thing I’d say is that you have to keep going back to it, first daily and then building in gaps (google the concept of the forgetting curve).

Re: Tips for learning new tunes

drop the transcribing. when you transcribe, you concentrate on the minute details of one player’s playing and you loose the forest for the trees.

instead, do the opposite, listen to every player on youtube playing this tune on every instrument, concentrate on what’s the common element between all the players. *that*’s the tune, not the dots, not the transcriptions.

find the especially slow and clear recordings, download them on your computer (yt-dlp), put them on repeat and try to play along, find as many notes as you can *by ear* and *without* writing them down. eventually, you will find *and remember* all the important notes.

make sure to use high quality head phones, so you can hear yourself, you can hear the recording, but the sound of loud motorcycles and ambulances is greatly attenuated.

or you can memorize the dots (remember: other people’s dots are always better! most tunes have already been transcribed and posted here and elsewhere).
