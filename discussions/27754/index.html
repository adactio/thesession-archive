I don’t understand this notion that there is something called “correct intonation”.

Supposing you want to play the sequence of notes: CEGC

You could play the C, go up a major third, up another perfect Major third, then a perfect fourth.

The first third raises you 384 cents above C, the second takes that to 768, and the fourth takes you to 1272 - a full semitone sharp of the octave, despite you having played all the intervals with “correct intonation”.

Or from the C you could choose to play the E as a major third (384) then play the G a perfect fifth above the C(696). But that leave the third from E to G at 312 - a minor third not a major one.

So there is no such thing as “correct intonation” it is impossible to achieve on any instrument.

On the piano and other mechanical contrivances the ‘correction’ is applied equally over the whole scale. But the beauty of the fiddle is that you can choose where you make the ‘correction’. - You can choose to play perfect intervals between notes, or perfect intervals from the root, or a combination and mixture of both. In that CEGc run you can play perfect intervals and let the top C go # if it is not an important note. But in the same tune the same sequence might come up with the top C as an important note, in which case you would adjust the other intervals to give a perfect octave.

A good fiddler will be changing where he makes the adjustments to suit the tune, so he won’t necessarily put the notes in the same place every time, where he put them will depand on the phrasing, whether he is ascending or decending, and what sort of feeling he wants to give to the music. This has always been a feature of fiddle music, and it is a very large part of why the fiddle is so much more expressive than the piano or guitar, where the player is shackled to equal temperament.

If you have ever tuned a violin from a piano you will know that when you pluck all 4 strings at once the chord sounds dull and lifeless. “Classical string players invariably play in equal temperament, because they play with other, fixed pitch instruments.”

This is not the case. For a start the violin is tuned in perfect 5ths (not tempered), the fourth note of the scale from the open string is also a pure 4th. Any E, A, D or G is tuned to the open string, and so on, not even close to equal temperament.
Prof. Simon Fischer has an article on this “Tuning each note of the scale” in The Strad, August 2009, pages 66-67, where he explains all this, and he does insist that there is a difference between E-flat & D-sharp, B-flat & A-sharp etc. (so not ET) or have a look around http://www.violinist.com to find out that violinists don’t (or at least shouldn’t) play in equal temperament.
As for playing with ET instruments, Gary Karr writes “Playing [violin] with a piano is a matter of timing, not pitch” The Strad, July 2010, page 50.

This is not only the case for those violinists who are play in historically accurate Baroque music etc. Even for 20th century music violinists do not follow equal temperament. Some early 20th century violinists did play in ET, Jascha Heifetz for one, but it simply doesn’t sound good, it’s lifeless, so now it’s not done.

(As for auto-tuning, I never suggested it ought to be in equal temperament, or in pure just. But do you not notice that as the tunes progress its becomes more and more internally out of tune? Do you really not hear that? If not, does anything ever sound out of tune to you?)
