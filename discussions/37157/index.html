Re: Jig bowing patterns?

[*Are you saying that in general there is less slurring in jigs than reels? *]

Yes, but only because of the different structures between the tune types. Reels have notes in groups of 4, and it’s common to play note 1 on a down-bow and notes 2,3,4 slurred on an up-bow. With jigs it’s groups of three, so the slurring could occur on notes 2 and 3. So, less slurring in jigs for that reason.

In the Tara Breen vid, she’s playing mostly D-U-D with some slurring on two consecutive notes. I can tell that right away just from a first watch / listen, but there’s a lot of left-hand / bow hand ornamentation going on throughout, and I don’t have the time to slow the video down and analyse to bow-direction level.

To be honest, even if I did, there’s a good chance that you wouldn’t sound the way you wanted to. There is so much individuality between fiddlers’ styles, in that a pattern that works for one player may not work at all for another.

It’s really best if you try to get it sounding ‘your’ way - so that means playing it simply, no ornamentation, single D-U-D pattern to begin with, then try some slurring. Every tune is different, so no single pattern will work best for all of the tunes.

Why not have a go at a jig you like, apply your own bowing and accents, and see what comes out?

Re: Jig bowing patterns?

Patterns are lazy crutches -- and ultimately reduce flexibility and limit your playing. Common examples are those players who can only play bowed triplets starting with a down bow. They picked up a pattern, and it limits their playing. General rules like: emphasize the first beat with a down bow, are basically useless. A good ITM player must be able to emphasize beats with either up or down bow -- on any beat. If you must learn a pattern for some ill-advised pedagogical reason, at least also learn the opposite pattern and alternative patterns equally well, so the damage of creating the bad habit is a bit lessened. And maybe someday you will be able to lose the crutch. In my view, it is much better to simply never pick up the bad habit.

Certainly there are other fiddle styles -- like bluegrass, or old timey, where bowing patterns could be useful -- or even essential. I won’t comment on those, other than to say I often hear of players of such styles talk about their patterns. But, in ITM, it is best not to get into patterns. The music should not be rigid and the player should never feel limited in how they express the music. I think that tension between the inexorable pulse of the music and the flow of the phrasing, with variations, is one of the wonderful things about ITM.

Re: Jig bowing patterns?

Whenever you put bow to string you are using bowing patterns of one sort or another.

The bowing patterns for a jig are: DUD, UDU, DUU, UDD, DDU, UUD, DDD, UUU.

They need to repeated individually continuously until you get the pulse right. Once you can do that, put them together into all their various combinations and repeat those. Once you can do all that slurring the same-direction notes, do it all again using Louré (separate strokes in the same direction).

Slurring across strings? If the tune needs a slur at a point where it happens to cross strings in the key you play it in, then you need to slur across strings. Slurring across strings is no different to slurring on one string, it is just slightly harder to do. Which is why some teachers tell you not to try it in the early stages.

But Jim, people do that sort of thing (DUD DUD) all the time! You just stick a gracenote in between the downs to separate them a bit if you’re worried about that. You can’t keep doing it forever of course because you run out of bow. (And anyway it’d be boring.)

Arthur, I’d say if in doubt use single bows, (DUD UDU etc…). There’s no harm in them and you won’t come unstuck. You’ll want to mess with it occasionally so it isn’t too repetitive though.

Try to slur into the one or the three from time to time, (DUD UDU UDU DUD etc… or DUD DUD UDU UDU). I don’t tend to play things like DDD UUU very often if I can avoid it, but any time you play a roll, you’re probably slurring through three beats, and I do them all the time on jigs. (Thinking about it, I very often slur into a roll so it’s U UUU, or D DDD.) Sometimes I do things like DDU UDU UDD DUU etc… Keep track of your bow and if you’re running out, tie a few notes together in the other direction or, ideally set yourself up to be going back towards the middle of the bow on the next note you want to emphasise and give it a bit of welly. (Extra points for slurring into that note, but even that could be a bad habit if you did it absolutely all the time.)

Almost anything goes so long as you don’t lose track of the beat, wreck the tune with dodgy phrasing, or play in a way that’s too repetitive and predictable. Don’t stick to one simple pattern all the way through a tune whatever you do, especially if the tune itself won’t cooperate.

Really, the bowing should be at the service of the phrasing, it’ll vary a bit because there’s always a few different ways to bow something. It’ll vary a bit more on top of that because the phrase itself changes a bit when you go through it again, or the ornaments do.

Careful listening is never the problem.

Experienced players know what to listen for. And what to listen for is player dependent. Over time, you learn what various components of the music sound like, and you don’t need to spend time appreciating the nuances to get to the aspects you need to learn in order to play a tune. Hence experienced players pick up tunes more quickly. For a newcomer, there is no substitute for putting in the time and listening carefully to what the music is supposed to sound like. Shortcuts give bad results -- and if a student just wants to play something NOW, without putting in the work to understand what it is supposed to sound like, then you should not expect good results. Talking about music has its place in the pedagogy, but far more important is listening to properly played music. Listen carefully, and try to make what you play sound like what you are listening to. It isn’t magic. It isn’t complicated. But it is hard work -- and a rewarding journey if you keep at it. A teacher can certainly help. A good teacher doesn’t send a student down irrelevant paths that instill habits the student will need to get rid of someday.

No advice is usually better than bad advice.

Re: Jig bowing patterns?

Not hard, but not automatically applicable to any random tune. I’m not against patterns per se, but I stay away from using the same pattern throughout a whole tune - whichever tune it may be. Some phrases suggest just one pattern, e.g. two consecutive long notes/rolls (like the opening of Morrison’s jig: ~E3 ~B3). Correspondingly, there’s usually one preferred way of “getting out of” a bowed triplet (especially a unison triplet/treble).

If you play, say, the Paddy Fahy jig which Tara Been starts with in the video, you can probably do separate bow strokes for nearly the whole tune as long as there are six notes in a measure, and you will still start every measure on a down-bow (if that’s something you find important). When other phrases occur, you solve the problem by doing something else.

When I was a fairly new fiddler, Connaughtman’s Rambles (Matt Cranitch’s version from the book) showed me some really nice options, even U-D-(U U)(D D) (if I remember correctly).

Arthur, I’ve been playing fiddle for about 4 years and this is how I figure things out: I go in a room and shut the door. I bring a recording device. I pick a tune I know well and take just a part of it, like 1 phrase, or even one measure. I play it one way and record it. I play it another way and record that. I play it in as many ways that I can think of (which may not be very many). I listen to how they sound, and how they are different. Then I go and find as many recordings I can of different people playing the tune (not just fiddle players) and make note of the thousand ways I missed. I think of this as building my tool box. I try everything, and never throw something out. Just because it doesn’t work in one instance doesn’t mean it wouldn’t be useful someday, somewhere. I don’t have a teacher, so my best method is to explore and figure it out myself. I don’t do this all the time, just occasionally when I’m feeling stale or uninspired. This is the SLOW way of doing it, if you want fast progress a teacher is your best bet (I can’t afford one right now). I also like playing along with recordings, it’s like having a private session in my own home.

That’s actually one part of a valid bowing strategy. (The other part is to be musical.)

Keeping your bow hand relaxed and supple is vital to getting a good consistent tone, and positioning the bow has a lot to do with that. Re: Jig bowing patterns?

Well, on that sheet there is persistent slurring of every group of three notes, for three consecutive bars, so I think that would make the tune lose a bit of punch. Maybe just slur on a maximum of three notes and then some single bows to balance it up. Jigs are meant to exude a bit of bounce, not constant legatotude (if such a word existed). The other slurring method would be across three strings, which would sound a bit different and more punchy.
