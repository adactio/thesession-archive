Re: New flute - can get a sound out of it - what next?

Congrat’s on your new flute and finding the first octave in the first week. I remember the first time I tried to play flute, 26 years ago, and I recall dizziness, a very small note range, and no ability to play for any length of time…so what you are experiencing isn’t unusual at all.

Right now, I’d focus on playing the notes you can for as long as you can. It’s basic long tone practice. This will help you improve your embouchure (how tight your lips are) which is the key for playing flute. With a good embouchure, you’ll play longer and that second octave will be a breeze.

However, I wonder, which octave are you really playing? If you play A, is it in the same octave as the low A on a tin whistle? If it is, you may be playing the second octave and need to try blowing softer to get the first (this isn’t uncommon in new players who blow too hard).

Your embouchure shape shouldn’t be round - it should be more of a flat slit if you can achieve that. Try playing in front of a mirror and see what you look like.

Good luck,

Eric

Re: New flute - can get a sound out of it - what next?

Usually, the challenge to a new flute player is controlling which octave you are in. It’s somewhat like when a boy’s voice changes to a man’s - for a while it kind of cracks between the deeper voice of a man, and the higher voice of a boy. Your lips can be a bit more taut when in the second octave. Blow cold air as you would to blow out a candle. For the lower octave, relax the lips a bit, and exhale warm air, rather than blowing cool air. The embouchure and endurance will come from practice. Play a lot in the bathroom in front of a mirror. The acoustics of the bathroom will reinforce your evaluation of your tone until you find the “sweet spot” by touch. Watching in the mirror will help to establish good posture and positioning of the flute. Always play standing up and keep the body open and loose, but keep your positioning in control. Good tone is your best friend, so strive for that above all else. Play as often as you can, but don’t obsess about for how long. Five minutes is good. If you try to block out a half hour or an hour, you may never be consistent. Develop the habit of taking in more air before you actually need it, and you won’t crap out on the sustained tones. Start your playing by noodling around your low G. It is the easiest range in the flute to play, and encourages good tone, which will set the bar for the other notes you eventually get to as you play.

Welcome to flute playing. It’s the greatest!

Re: New flute - can get a sound out of it - what next?

All the above advice looks good to me. When you are ready for it, the exercise referred to by Brian really works for a lot of people.
It is surprising that it’s the lower octave you are getting - the bottom of the lower octave is one of the hardest parts to get a good sound. But reading what you say about the fact that you do get to the upper e, it does seem to be true in your case. If you are getting a note (even if it’s not a fabulous one) down at the very bottom, you are doing ok at this stage.
Do make sure that the advice you get is based on the wooden flute and “Irish” style. The metal flutes used in orchestras today respond quite differently, added to which their players are aiming at a different sound. It’s almost a different instrument altogether, and I can only repeat: classical embouchure will just not work properly for our flutes.
For our flute style, pushing your lips forward is, as you suspect, quite wrong. If you do manage to get a sound that way, it will quite probably be woolly, quiet and out of tune.
The dizziness just goes - you are still using far too much air (like every beginner). You will automatically use less air as your embouchure gets better.
My hunch (it’s only that) would be that if you focus on getting a good *sound* in the lower octave, the upper one will become quite easy as that sound improves.
Then again, remember that the journey to a really *good* sound is a long one - you should not be surprised if it takes some years. But there is not much connection between the success or lack of it you get in the first hours of playing and the tone you achieve after a lot of experience. Someone who happens to find it very easy to get an initial sound may not achieve a good one for a very long time, if at all, and vice versa.
Finally - congratulations. The flute is indisputably :) the best of all possible instruments. You have started what promises to be a wonderful journey.

Don’t get too hung up about the difference between “Classical” and “traditional” embouchure

As the link below shows, there are a lot of possible ways of geting a good result. (Piccies)

http://www.larrykrantz.com/embpic.htm

I think it is a bad idea to pay too much attention to the difference between classical and traditional approaches to embouchure. It may be a good aproach for someone coming from classical flute (as I did) who first needs to understand that things mught work a bit differently on the wooden flute, but much of the good advice available for classical playing is still relevant for traditional playing. And do not forget, these flutes were the classical flutes of their time!

The approach that works for me is to think about the air stream I generate. How fast is it? How wide is it (how much air is involved)? What direction is it going in? Where and in what orientation is it meeting the embouchure hole (should be roughle evenly split by the far edge of the hole)? What obstructions is it meeting on the way (for me it is always my throat which is too constricted unless I concentrate on it.)? Do not forget that the air stream starts at your diaphgram and ends at the far end of your flute.

I you are aware of all these parameters and just play about with them (over years!…) and pay attention to the resuls (listen directly to yourself - always! and also try recording yourself from time to time too.) then you will find what works best for you and your flute. Just try to remember what you were doing when it sounded best and reproduce it… (Easy to say…) And remember breath support and embouchure are different for every note on the flute, so don’t assume that what gets one note out will get its’ neighbours singing too - more true of the wooden flute than the classical due to the variation in the hole sizes.

Also , for the high notes, try to avoid pinching off the embouchure in the search for a faster air stream - the higher pressure needs to come from you diaphragm. And lower pressure for the low notes comes from there too.

and remember:

http://www.larrykrantz.com/yoda.htm

↳ Posted by Rhod on January 12th, 2007.

Off-flute exercises.

Think of it as training. You are building muscle strength and coordination the more you use them. The muscles around mouth, lips and throat are not that micro either.

There certainly are exercises without the flute. Starting with the pure breathing exercises, but these may be a bad idea while you’re driving yourself… There are lots of sources out there on the web for breathing for singers/wind instrument players. There’s nothing particularly trad-specific about it.

Another possibility is to blow out imaginary candles. They should be from 1 to 2 metres away… You can also vary the direction you’re blowing in without moving yourhead (building conscious control of where your air is going…) You can even practice with real candles occasionally. After that you’ve got the “hold a piece of paper against the wall by blowing at it” exercise - get further away from the wall the better you get.

Basically anything involving a narrow, high speed, unimpeded and controlled stream of air is of benefit.

And don’t forget the beer bottle top at every possible opportunity. Trying to get the second harmonic out of one is not easy. (It’s more than an octave jump since the bottle has one closed end.)

Chris.
