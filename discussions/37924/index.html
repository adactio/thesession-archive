Re: Dorian Mode, Aeolian Mode, Minor Key… What’s the Difference?

Key = Mode + Tonic

Dorian and aeolian are minor modes; minor and aeolian are often used synonymously, though. The classical (as opposed to traditional) understanding of minor includes the scales of natural, melodic and harmonic minor, hence the leading note to a given tonic. But this has nothing to do with the distinction of mode and key per se.

Minor is a mode, A minor (say) is a key, harmonic minor (-> leading note to the tonic) is a scale. Maybe the author is confused because this scale normally doesn’t stand for itself, but only appears in certain melodic contexts (particularly when resolving to the tonic, which a mode itself doesn’t have).

Re: Dorian Mode, Aeolian Mode, Minor Key… What’s the Difference?

That article is terrible. Completely over complicates what modes and keys are, goodness they’re already complicated enough.

I like to describe keys and modes as colors. The way I see it, the different keys are different colors, and the different modes are different shades of the same color. Let’s look at CMajor, amin and Amaj for example.

CMaj is NOT related to Amaj. CMaj has no sharps or flats and AMaj has 3 sharps. AMaj also only utilizes 3 of the keys that CMaj does(D, E, and A). On instruments, you can’t play in CMaj like you play in AMaj cause the notes and chords are different. On paper, they’re notated differently. So it’s a completely different color. You can tell the difference by literally looking at them. Let’s say CMaj is Yellow and AMaj is Blue.

amin on the other hand has no sharps or flats, like CMaj. It uses all the same notes and chords as CMaj. And you play it the same way you play CMaj, the Tonic is just simply in a different place. It is also notated on paper the same as CMaj. You can hardly tell a difference by looking at them. So it is a mode of Yellow, a shade of Yellow, or a mode of C, a shade of C. That’s why it’s fittingly called CMajor’s *Relative Minor*.

But why do I say the article is terrible? Because the writer distinguishes the Aeolian mode from the Relative Minor key when they’re EXACTLY THE SAME THING. Just as the Ionian mode is exactly the same as Major. And then the writer says that “Music evolved”? No, it didn’t evolve, we just learned that we like the Ionian and Aeolian scales the most because they made the most sense to us.

What’s even worse? The Aeolian Mode, the Dorian Mode and “Minor Key” all end on an unresolved tonic! That “leading tone” he’s talking about is absent in ALL OF THEM, including the "Minor Key! Gah! There is no E Major chord in the key of amin, because the G is natural, not sharp. This entire article needs to be edited or deleted altogether.

Re: Dorian Mode, Aeolian Mode, Minor Key… What’s the Difference?

This is timely: Only last night there was a discussion in our session about whether Banish Misfortunes was in A dorian or D mixolydian which have the same key signature. (it’s definitely in D mix, but what about the Yellow Wattle?).

Here’s the way I like to think of all this: Given a scale, modes are just the other scales which can be made by starting on each note of the original scale. If you start with the major scale, you get dorian, mixolydian and friends, but you don’t have to start with the major scale. For example, Freygish is the mode of the natural minor scale starting on the fifth note.

There’s two ways you can think about this though - you can keep the scale constant, e.g. taking G major and then getting A dorian, D mixolydian etc. from it, or keeping the root note constant and working out modes from that e.g. G major, G Dorian, G mixolydian etc. In the first case you’re changing the root note but keeping the key signature the same and in the second you’re keeping the same root note but changing the scale and key signature. I often hear that only the first of these ways is correct and you should burn any literature which talks about the other, but they’re both useful. I find knowing how the scales change to be the most useful as it lets you work them out on the fly: I can say in my head: “dorian has sharp 6th, flat 7th, mix has flat 7th etc.”), count on my fingers a bit, and I’m ready to go just about the time the tune has come to an end.

Can I take a moment to complain about people who either tell you that a tune is in two sharps (not distinguishing between D and B minor) or is in A (not distinguishing between A major and A dorian)? I just find these intensely annoying because they’re actively misleading to the guitarist who asked the question “which key?”. Of course, nobody should be asking that question anyway, but we live in an imperfect world.

Re: Dorian Mode, Aeolian Mode, Minor Key… What’s the Difference?

Modality and tonality are complex subjects, and without much black-and-white in the real world of music. They are worth understanding, but it takes study. As has been noted above, it’s certainly possible to enjoy a pretty tune without study. But, as with any subject, the more you learn, the more you discover is still to be learned.

The use of the word “minor” implies tonality - that is to say, “tonal music”. It does indeed rely extensively on the presence of the “leading tone” - that is, the note one half-step lower than the “tonic” or main note - which is present in the major scale and in the harmonic and the melodic minor scales, but not the natural minor. Of course, music isn’t written in “harmonic minor” or “natural minor”, just “minor”, and the different minor scales are taught to music students to facilitate understanding of how minor actually works in the tonal system. (The basis of the tonal system is the I-V-I or i-V-i progression, but an in-depth explanation of this is beyond the scope of this comment. Suffice to say that the V chord will always be major. This is why “tonal” music in A minor will contain many E major chords, for example.)

Modality is different. It may help to point out here that everything in music theory is descriptive - by this, I mean that the point is to describe how music works, not to discern a list of rules that composers/tunewriters follow. (There’s no such list.) In Irish music, people talk about modes to explain the difference between, say, Maid Behind the Bar (D major), Drowsy Maggie (E dorian), and Sligo Maid (A mixolydian). All three tunes use the same seven notes (D, E, F#, G, A, B, C#), but each is centered around a different note - the one the mode is named after. Much jazz works this way, too - Miles Davis’s ‘So What’ is a particularly well-known example. In Irish music, the only commonly used mode that contains the “leading tone” is the major or Ionian. Tunes in this mode tend to resemble tonal music much more closely than tunes in other modes, which is maybe why people tend to just say “major” and not “Ionian”.

And, last, you can ignore everything I’ve written and still thoroughly enjoy music.

Ionian mode and major are not the same, and Aeolian mode and minor are also not the same. Major and minor refer to a specific system that involves harmonies moving in certain specific ways; modal theory does not. Re: Dorian Mode, Aeolian Mode, Minor Key… What’s the Difference?

sebastian the metafrog, and anybody else interested: when music theory types say “tonal music” they mean music that follows a specific system, involving one and five (I, V) chords and generally holding to certain conventions. (As Jerome says, it’s not hard science, just descriptors.) “Atonal” music means the stuff guys like Schoenberg and Messiaen came up with, that basically broke that system down. Unfortunately, those two terms are not understood to mean “everything is one or the other”. Irish music would not be described as “tonal” OR “atonal”, but “modal”. Much jazz and popular music is modal, too. As far as I understand the music of India, it basically works that way, too.

This is how “major” and “Ionian” don’t mean the same thing, by the way. Yes, a major scale and the Ionian mode contain the same notes, but “major” refers to music that follows a specific harmonic system, where “Ionian” would only refer to music that sticks to a certain set of notes, with a specified ‘central’ note. Maybe it would be helpful to think of “major” as a harmonic idea, where Ionian is a melodic idea.

Yes, modes are about melody, not harmony. Harmony simply means more than one voice (musical line) going at the same time. It doesn’t have to involve chords. “Tonal” harmony, that I’ve referenced above, refers to a specific system of harmony that does involve major and minor triads and seventh chords that move together in specific patterns. But music that gets described as “modal” doesn’t depend on these chords and patterns. That doesn’t mean chords won’t be used, but the music isn’t defined by them.

“Modes are about melody not harmony? I don’t understand what you mean. You stack your notes in thirds to make up the triads from these modes/scales. That’s where the harmony comes from these modes/scales. Where else could they come from?”

Melody is probably the single most important concept in music. Interesting thing though, early on melody was not played with equal intervals; musicians used varying combinations of tones & semitones. If it weren’t you wouldn’t need a modal system, or a circle of fifths, or tritones, or major & minor chords.

So, yes, harmony has always been a part of music; just not in the way it’s understood by the more recent tonal system. A good way to understand the tonal system and harmony is horizontally; i.e. ~ more than one voice (pitch) at the same time. With modes harmony tends to be vertical. In other words the tonal system has relatively fixed chord progressions but with modes the melody might *suggest* a larger variety of possible harmonies. They’re not as easily defined, which leaves them open to interpretation.

Re: Dorian Mode, Aeolian Mode, Minor Key… What’s the Difference?

AB Steen - How about “music was played using the diatonic scale in different ways such that melodies could be classified according to the note, relative to the root of the scale, on which they could be brought to an end in a way that was satisfying to listeners”.

The diatonic scale is not a result of combining tones and semitones, it is a psycho-acoustic phenomenon. Intervals made up of multiple semitones are a way of describing the scale not defining it. Modes are ways of describing tunes, not defining them. As the discussion of the Sligo Maid shows this can have its problems.
