Why do you play ( Or what is your goal when you play)?

A couple of weeks ago, one of the members here informed me during a discussion that he was a “Master of his chosen instrument, the flute” after having obviously looked at my profile where I described myself as a “Jack of many instruments”. This member may or not have been joking but I wonder if being a “master of your chosen instrument” , in as much as you can define such a concept, should be your goal. Obviously, you should strive to be as good as you can be. I’m not a “Jack” through choice and the instruments I play are all related to some extent. It’s just that I am realistic about my achievements so far and probable limitations for the future. However, I feel my desire to play is more out of a “love for the music”, discovering new (and old) tunes to play, and for my own enjoyment rather than “mastering an instrument” or trying to be “the best musician”.

Of course, the better I (or you) become, the more we are likely to get out of the music i.e playing skills, ability to learn/play more difficult tunes, opportunity to be more creative, more satisfaction etc. This is reason why I feel that we should all try to improve and be as good as we can be-- not to be a “master” or “super technician” for the sake of it. For instance, I might try to emulate a certain player’s technique but only because I appreciate that certain aspect of his/her playing and feel that it might work for me rather than wanting to be a “Frankie Gavin”, “Kevin Burke” or whoever myself.

For some people, the “love of their chosen instrument” is more important than the music. Yet, I still think it should be possible for them to play these for their own sake rather than just out of a desire to be “masters”.

I realise that there is not a straight forward dichotomy here. Some of us may have started out wishing to be “masters” but now just enjoy playing music. Others may have just intended to enjoy the music but ended up as great musicians or technicians. Many of the rest of us may just enjoy playing the music but want to get better. How about you?

Re: Why do you play ( Or what is your goal when you play)?

I was conversing on something along very similar lines with a couple of people, one of whom is a well-respected fiddler/contra dance musician from New England.
The fiddle player talked about her training as a classical violinist, and working with and auditioning for a teacher, an acclaimed musician in his own right. It was, she said, both an exhilarating and intimidating experience, and as much as she learned she would feel very self-conscious about her ability relative to his.
Then, she finally realized that she didn’t _have_ to be “like” her teacher-mentor. She knew she needed to achieve a certain level of competence and command of the violin, but she was pretty much there, anyway. The task for her was to have her playing be as true as an expression of herself as possible, that would show her love and enjoyment of the music.
That may sound a little touchy-feely, but I did understand what she was talking about, and it’s something I try to keep in mind -- and to be honest, the fact that it came from someone of her stature made it all the more compelling.
There are easily a dozen or so musicians off the top of my mind I would love to emulate and be “as good as.” But unless I quit my day job, leave the wife and kids and immerse myself in practice and instruction for, oh, I don’t know, a year, that’s probably not going to happen -- hell, even if I did make such draconian changes, that’s still no guarantee.
So I’ll continue to learn tunes, work on my rhythm and chordwork, learn tunes, improve my sight-reading, learn tunes, practice my singing, learn tunes -- and if I can achieve a certain level of competence while making the result sound like me instead of some half-realized ideal, then I figure I’m doing OK.

I like the creation and perhaps ironically the end of the tune. When a piece clicks, the musicians have crafted a moment in space-time that’s special. The musicians feel it, the listeners feel it, and all, together, feel privileged to have participated in that special moment. Then the tune ends, the moment’s gone and no one else can experience it again. Now, all have a common experience of a privileged moment. To me it’s satisfying to get up at the end of an evening and be able to say to a friend that “we made some music tonight.”

Re: Why do you play ( Or what is your goal when you play)?

Will and Emily, regarding your problems dealing with the flute in hostile climates, here’s the perspective of someone who’s been there, done that and managed to overcome that hump (or fight it to a standoff, anyway). Granted, my climate here in DC may not be as hostile towards wooden flutes as Arizona or Montana, but for half the year it’s pretty damn dry around here. And as we all know, flutes don’t like dry.

Back when I got my first flute in 1988 or so (a Casey Burns that was okay but not great), I was heavily into set dancing, to the point of going to ceilis every weekend and classes two or three nights a week. One year I actually counted the number of days on which I danced, and it was close to 200. Given that I have a day job, most of those dancing days I wasn’t able to pick up the flute at all. Then around the time I got my Olwell in 1992, I fell into a long distance romance and ended up being on the road pretty much every other weekend for about a year, and when at home I was running up the long distance phone bill. I always took the flute with me on my trips, but (for some reason I can’t recall now :->) I seemed to have trouble finding time to play it. Like you guys, during this whole time I experienced the cycle of brief epiphanies in my playing followed by long bouts of frustration. I was making progress, but it was at the rate of 1000 steps forward, 999 steps back.

Then, when my romance bit the dust I decided to take a different tack. I was still a busy guy, but I swore to myself that I would not let a day go by without playing the flute, even if only for 15 minutes. And you know what? That made all the difference! The amount of time I had to spend re-introducing myself to my flute each time I picked it up went to near zero very quickly. If I had the time to really play, I could actually use it to make progress instead of having to repeat the frustrating warmup sequence every time. I actually started making progress! Now I find myself neglecting other activities just so I can play the flute more.

Based upon this experience, I have come to the conclusion that one’s relationship with the flute is like one’s relationship to a spouse or SO. (Of course, unless you’re in Massachusetts or San Francisco, that flute…er spouse better be of the opposite sex. :->) This is probably true for all instruments, but I’d say it’s much more true with the flute. After all, what other instruments besides the flute do you actually have to kiss in order to play? (But remember, this is Irish music we’re talking about, so no tongue…) So think about it. When you come waltzing up to your woman lookin‘ for some lovin’ after being nowhere to be seen for a couple of days, are you gonna get any? Hell no, not without a lot of warming up first, and probably some roses or such too if you’ve been gone longer than a few days. (Sorry to be sexist there, but I had to use “woman” rather than “man” in that example. Because if I used “man” the answer would have to be “hell yes!” But you get my drift.)

So my point is, if you want to move your relationship with your flute to the next level, and then the level after that, and then the one after that, etc, etc, you need to pay attention to it just as you’d pay attention to that spouse or SO, or be prepared to pay the same consequences. Tupperware is good, but 15 minutes (or more) each and every day is absolutely essential!

One other interesting thing I’ve found, now that after years of absolute fidelity to my flute I have been cheating on it with a concertina for the last year or so: The concertina doesn’t seem to mind if I skip a few days here or there. But even after such a long and successful relationship, the flute still reacts if I’m away for more than 24 hours. Yet another reason why I think the flute is a whole different animal than other instruments…

Re: Why do you play ( Or what is your goal when you play)?

I played piano and guitar in my earlier life and then went through a stretch of several years when I wasn’t singing or playing anything. Not surprisingly I wasn’t that happy. 18 months ago I decided to pick up a whistle and see if I could play. Took me 6 months to get back into music - many hours sitting in the car, occasionally at red lights before I felt the sound I was making wasn’t too offensive. In the last 6 months I’ve found a session of beginners who made me very welcome and tought me about 50 tunes so far. Now I just can’t get enough. Again, not surprisingly, I’m a much happier person even when I’m not playing. It’s way better than drugs and is more effective as well.

As a consequence I bought a concertina at christmas and I’m making pretty good progress with it. As far as becoming a Master…. I found that if I can play a few tunes well and reasonably fast then when I join in behind the more experienced players I can feel part of the buzz. It just makes me want to go and wear my fingers out some more.

As for goals when I’m picking tunes to play I’m purely self indulgent and go for the ones that grab me. I usually have a much harder time with tunes that people say “you should learn this one it’s a really good tune”. I usually find that it just doesn’t mesh with what I’m looking for. When I first went to a few sessions it became apparent that most of the tunes I was playing had an animal in the title. I still haven’t found a better way to try out new tunes than look for those animals (found “bang your frog on the sofa” a couple of days ago) Currently finding lilting strains of the banks of the Seine playing the Weasel Waltz on concertina.

Wish I could stay and type longer but I’m just having too much fun with the music!

Robin

Re: Why do you play ( Or what is your goal when you play)?

Well, I play because I love it! I want to devote the rest of my life to the playing and study of this great music. Irish and Scottish music, especially, bring me back to my roots. I feel a deep tug at my heart and soul that pulls me to my feet or brings tears streaming down my face when I hear a tune or set played exceptionally well (with soul, lift, groove…). (If another person’s playing can move me to those extremes, then surely if I work hard enough and put the soul and technique into it, I can achieve the same result for myself and possibly for others.) I get tremendous joy and satisfaction from teaching, sharing, and playing what I know about the music. I am exhilarated every time I learn something new and even more elevated when it starts to show in my playing. The time I spend ‘mucking around’ in the music often leads my down unexpected, new (for me) paths within the music and myself (on a deeper level). I am really in my element when immersed in the music.

Goal? One current goal is to reach the point where the instrument (fiddle) becomes an extension of myself. To find the point where the music comes alive, where it just resonates and flows.
