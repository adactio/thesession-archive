Re: Button Accordions

Laura:

Best sources I know for button accordions are three:

The Button Box (Amherst, MA; www.buttonbox.com): very knowledgeable folks, good prices, pretty wide range of options in used and “as-is” instruments

The Erin Breeze (W Coast; www.erinbreeze.com; ask for Chris Moran): good prices on new instruments; very interesting and informative website with lots of useful free info.

The Box Office (E Coast; http://members.aol.com/jimattheboxofc/): good prices on a fairly small line of affordable new instruments. Jim Coogan is very knowledgeable and the father of Cherish the Lady’s Mary Coogan.

I’ve done the most business with the Button Box (my old stomping grounds) but had good and informative conversations with the other two shops.

Suggestion:
Consider *urging* your friend--in the strongest possible terms--to consider purchasing a 2-row button accordion pitched either B/C or C#/D. These are the common key arrangements for Irish-style players and are by far the most widely-used. C#/D is close to melodian/single-row style (e.g., play D, Em, A and some G tunes on a single row) and should be a fairly comfortable adaptation. A 3-row, particularly with the rows a fourth apart, would be very awkward for Irish music, though very suitable for other things including English/Morris.

Further inquiries offlist OK.

Re: Button Accordions

Greenwiggle said:

“Use your sense of adventure and spur your fiddle playing friends to learn some new keys and positions.”

Adventurousness is all well and good, but if a box player waltzed into a session and started playing tunes a third away from where they’re usually played, most all of the other participants would pack up and leave. If someone is going to go to the trouble of learning a bunch of Irish tunes, mightn’t it be considerate to suggest that person learn them in keys that will let her play readily with others?

Paul_draper said:

“A 3 row pitched in ADG wouldn’t be at all awkward for Irish music. The ornamentation would be a little different and, unless you modify the instrument, some useful low notes such as F natural and C natural would be missing. But it’s quite easy to play in G, Am, D, Em, A, Bm and E. . . . In the hands of a practiced player you would hardly haer the difference in style and would have the advantage of the basses being a bit more versatile than on a BC.”

Nope, I’d still disagree. Two reasons:

(a) Bellows direction. Phrasing for Irish box is largely shaped by shifts of bellows direction and use of the air button. This is why the B/C and C#/D have different sounds and why a knowledgeable listener can tell by hearing which type is being played. Touchstones for B/C include Joe Burke and Paddy O Brien of Tipperary; for the C#/D, Joe Cooley (actually D/D#, but with the same fingerings), Tony Mac Mahon, Jackie Daly.

(b) A three-row box is going to be markedly heavier than a two-row and its action will be different as a result.

Yes, of course one can play Irish-style on a 4th-tuned 3-row, just as one can play Irish-style on an English concertina. However, for someone just making the transition as was described, why encourage that person to swim against the tide? Why not assume that the vast majority of B/C or C#/D players know what they’re doing?

Re: Button Accordions

Greenwiggle, a few responses:

“No problem- my understanding with the accordion is that if you learn the fingering as natural for that box when you pick up a box in ”normal“ tuning it should ring true.”

I don’t quite understand this. Yes, it is possible to play just “on one row” on the box: D on a D melodeon, C on a C melodeon, or G and D on a two-row G/D accordion. But that is not how Irish-style accordion is conventionally played. Instead, one accommodates the necessity for altered scale degrees by playing “across the rows.” That’s why Irish-style players tend to play either B/C or C#/D two-row boxes: not so they can play in B and C, or C# and D, but so that they have closely-adjacent access to accidentals that let them play in a variety of keys on one box.

“The delicate matter of tunings has been rehashed on this site so many times so I won’t try it again here. Equal Temperment and all that.”

Sorry, that’s not what I’m talking about. Temperament is not the same thing as either key area or diatonicism in instruments.

“But I do think that all of ”us“ (trad musicians) need to realise that the music was not always played in the keys that it is now played in.”

No, but it *was* played in a fairly small group of keys and in a quite-specific range of modes based on those keys. These groups are not arbitrary or “conventional”: instead, they reflect the particular keys that are readily-available and stylistically-appropriate on the core instruments of flute (pitched in D), fiddle (chromatic, but most resonant and responsive in "keys of a few sharps: C, G, D, A--just ask Vivaldi), and pipes (pitched in D). As a result of these instruments’ physical mechanics, the keys of C, G, D, A and the modes based on them have become the stylistic norm.

“Perhaps the outsider criticism that ”it all sounds the same“ is partially due to the reluctance and indifference of players to change up or move around from the ”safe“ first position or ”popular session keys“.”

I would completely disagree. This is not a question of “safe”-ness or the “reluctance and indifference of players”. Hardcore traditional players in every generation have experimented with shifting the key areas of the tunes: pipers on low-pitch sets, Clare and E Galway fiddlers (who often took tunes down 1 whole step in order to have different scale degrees as fingered notes which could be inflected microtonally--see Paddy Canny/Martin Hayes/Martin Rochford), etc. Northern fiddlers have experimented with shifting positions under the influence of the Scott Skinner Scottish style (see Sean Maguire). But changing to a remote key, or shifting to higher positions, changes the music (especially in terms of playing) and, for a learning player, it seems that such changes--like using a button box which does not employ the most common Irish-style tuning system--complicates things at a bad time in the player’s development.

"When I listen to music I personally like to be challenged by the
players intelligence and sense of wonder, rather than comforted by a misleading sense of a continuance of tradition."

There’s a lot of “intelligence and sense of wonder” that can be discovered through an “enlightened” sense of continuing tradition. If one can’t find such within the tradition, why play in that style? Just so you can change it?

“Buying a box in relative keys may be good for session playing but in the past it was not always affordable nor possible.”

Maybe not, but it *was* precisely the question with which mandolinchick began the thread.

“I for one would not pack up and leave just because someone was playing in a different key.”

Maybe not, but if playing in a session is about playing together, and if mandolinchick’s friend is, as she said, just beginning to get into the music, don’t you think she’d be well-advised to learn the tunes in keys that *other*, more experienced players know them in? A session is *not* simply a public performance of material that players have previously worked out in advance (in remote keys, for example). It is a participatory event in which participants are *together* engaging with the tradition as it’s been handed down--not trotting out pre-arranged material.

“Isn’t the idea of playing in a session about learning from one another-not just majority rules?”

Yes--but it’s much *more* about *learning from the tradition.* And that includes grasping, in an enlightened way, *why* the music (including preferred tunes, preferred instruments, and preferred keys) has evolved the way it has.

chris smith
