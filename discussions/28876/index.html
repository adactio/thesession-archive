A pure mish~mash!

I thought I might rattle the chains of any closet purists out there! ;-)

Martin Carthy once said: “It is utterly ridiculous to try to keep folk music pure because it has never been pure; it is a mish-mash”

Well, we all know that it’s not what life hands you, but what you do with it, just as it doesn’t really matter where you were born, but what is important is what you do with your time, wherever you are.

Likewise, we know that most, if not all, of the dance rhythms in Irish Music started out elsewhere, as did most of the instruments, yet the Irish have made a wonderful job of making them their own.

Which does make you wonder just how boring Irish Music would be today, if the purists had had more influence & control over the music in Ireland & folks hadn’t been quite so open to so many musical influences from elsewhere, including other dance rhythms, instruments, tunes etc. etc.

Which also makes me wonder just how far you can actually take the purist approach, before you start interfering too much in it’s natural evolution & instead, start damaging the culture you are trying to preserve.

So, can anyone tell me, do purists really exist out there, in Irish Music today, or are they just a figment of our imagination?

If so, do they wear this title like a badge of honour, or do they skulk around in the shadows?

Cheers,
Dick

Martin Carthy had this to say about the purity of folk music:
“The period with Steeleye was tremendous fun and was a great eye opener. By the time I left at the end of 1971, I had come to understand that folk music could withstand anything that I, or anyone else equally well-intentioned could do to it. The sort of mix-up that occurred when the folk and rock worlds met is the sort of mix-up that has always gone on in folk culture. It is utterly ridiculous to try to keep folk music pure because it has never been pure; it is a mish-mash. The idea of their being a folk pedigree is a joke, (neither is it true that the best necessarily survives). Above all, the thing to remember about folk music is that its sturdiness comes from the very diversity of its roots.”

Re: A pure mish~mash!

In 35 years of playing folk music (in addition to playing Irish traditional), I’ve never met anyone who wanted to keep “folk” music pure. And I’ve met very few purists around Irish traditional music.

I have met musical conservatives who prefer to play the music as close to the way it was handed to them. But that isn’t the same as being a purist. The conservative’s focus is less on purity and more on the pace of change, two different things.

Compare Irish traditional music to, say, classical, bluegrass, or rock. My hunch is that Irish trad is slower to change. For all of its borrowing from other genres, the core of it has been a fairly conservative tradition.

I think music in the end inhabits and makes its own laws, so that the music that was good when it was created will come to be appreciated as such in due time even if it wasn’t at first, and the music that was delinquent in the respects that matter will come to be regarded as a waste of space further down the line even if it came into the world as a big hit or a supposedly important avant-garde piece.

There’s an implication in what I’ve written that music that “inhabits its own laws” is the music that is also going to inhabit our ears and minds the most happily. Created for one another? I myself believe so. My ideas of what is good music should be normative, natch, but the question is one I won’t go into because it would involve exhausting masses of argument and probably get me scragged.

Same thing about inner laws applies IMO to painting and poetry. Mind, you could spend a few lifetimes wittering on about how to begin to approach the matter of what these actually *are*…but anyone who pursues any of these things for any appreciable time becomes (in general) better able to see the virtue or mojo in a piece if it’s there, and sometimes (not always) to describe it, whether poetically or technically.
