“This music is so easy that you don’t have to spend time practising it!”

Added by 🎻 Melody Player 🎻 on March 29th, 2005

“This music is so easy that you don’t have to spend time practising it!”

You will probably have realised who said that! However, while I can understand the argument and agree that there’s an element of truth to this statement, I’m sure that this isn’t everyone’s experience here. It’s certainly not mine.

If you’ve been playing the music for as long as you remember, e.g from a child, or perhaps having done it once for a living and taking things easy, even playing in sessions for years and years then, of course, you’ll find it easy. I also agree that the music has a simple structure and the tunes mainly follow a similar pattern. So do most forms of traditional music, rock and roll, blues etc for that matter. However, if you’ve started later in life (or even just starting), you still have to learn and practise. I’d suggest that those who say it is so easy have been playing the music so long that they’ve forgotten that they had to start somewhere.

Of course, the more you play, the easier it gets. I can pick up many tunes in a few minutes whereas it used to take me days. Some still do, though. I can work on some for weeks before they’re half presentable. Also, I don’t need to do things like scales etc as I’m practising this while playing the tune. In fact, just playing the music accounts for much of my practice these days. So, I agree with the originator of the statement in this respect.
Also, I don’t believe that the music needs to take up all your life. It does actually take up a lot of mine but I also spend a lot of time listening to other people, e.g concerts, CDs, sessions, and other genres. I also make a conscious effort to learn new tunes at home as opposed to just “picking them up” in sessions. Yet, I’ve plenty of time to do other things in life too. Mainly because I’m too lazy to work :-) (enjoying retirement).

Anyway, that’s my take on this theory. What’s yours?

Re: “This music is so easy that you don’t have to spend time practising it!”

I agree with JohnJ. Sessions are in general not the places to practice tunes you don’t know. In those cases you should be listening and then going home and doing the necessary woodshedding. That is not to say, though, that if you have a tune you only half-know you can’t play along very quietly (instrument permitting, of course) in those tricky bits so as to work out what the notes are, without disturbing other people.
If technique isn’t up to scratch then it needs to be practiced at home, and certainly not in a session. If you’re playing the scale and arpeggio bits easily in sessions then you’re obviously not practicing them because they are techniques you already know well.
Warming up before the start of a session on bits and peices of scales, arpeggios, and tunes isn’t practicing either. It is just warming up - reminding your fingers and bow of what they’re supposed to be doing and working stiffness out of joints and muscles.

Re: “This music is so easy that you don’t have to spend time practising it!”

We are in violent agreement here. I never thought of how I defined practicing, but when I thought about it, it is what I do at home to get tunes to the point where they are presentable in a public forum. I suppose in its broadest definition, you could call all playing “practice” because the more you do, the better you get. Some practice does creep into a session, as someone runs over a phrase or two after the tune stops to make sure they were getting it right, or someone shows another how to play a tricky passage. And I always find that I am calibrating my playing the first few times I play a new tune in public, as when playing it alone, playing from memory, or reading it from sheet music, some differences creep in that must be corrected in order to match yourself to the group’s unison playing. And people do sometimes work to pick up a tune as it is played in the session (although one must be as unobtrusive as possible when engaging in this behavior or they will be “noodling,” to raise the ugly head of a past discussion point). Too much practicing in public, however, is not fun for the audience nor the session participants. I would not call this music easy, if for nothing else, because of the speed at which we play.

Re: “This music is so easy that you don’t have to spend time practising it!”

A couple of weeks ago I was invited to play in a band for set dancers at their ceili on March 17, in a village about 25 miles from Bristol.
On arrival I was miked up and then the dancing started. I very quickly discovered that I knew hardly any of the tunes played in that part of the county, but the interesting thing was that the dancing was so fast, faster than almost anything played in sessions, that I didn’t have time to think and found myself absorbing and playing these new tunes on the hoof, as it were. A sort of aural equivalent of sight-reading under the intense pressure of playing for that very fast set dancing is the nearest I can describe it. A very odd sensation indeed, but it worked, and I played all the tunes. But whether I could play any of those tunes on my own the next day or since is a very different matter!
Has anyone else experienced this sort of thing? Is there a psychological explanation? Is the phenomenon related to the (alleged) “easiness” of the music? - I certainly didn’t have any opportunity for practising beforehand.

I’d like to put forward a suggestion that practice is more important for “performance”. If you are playing in a concert or with a band, you’ll maybe want to try out different things. Some of these may be more complicated, subtle or whatever. So, perhaps, a bit of effort is more important here.
However, in playing for sessions, you have to “go with the flow” and it’s probably better just to relax and enjoy the music. It’s not really the place for trying out all the clever(I’ll use the term loosely) stuff you’ve been working on elsewhere. Of course, I’m not saying that you shouldn’t learn to play the music in the first place or become sloppy but, as Michael suggests, there’s a limit to the benefit of extra practice in these situations.
