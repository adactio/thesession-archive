Re: About tuning down for fiddle…

Some fiddles respond better one way or the other. I particularly enjoy playing mine with the D tuned down to C or Bb - it gets real growly on the low end. It makes it tougher to play cleaner, and triplets get harder to pull off, but the rolls are big and fat and juicy, and the instrument rumbles nicely into my chest. I’ve started using higher tension strings on the G & D because I play more frequently than not these days tuned down to C, and that seems to help.

Whatever you decide to do - just be easy on your instrument when tuning up or down - not just the obvious stress on your strings, but tuning up to Eb puts alot of stress on your instrument as well, and similarly you should be wary about your sound post when tuning down. This is not to say that your instrument is going to snap in half when tuning up or that your post is sure to fall when tuning down. It’s just that the fluctuations in tension stress the instrument - more of a life-of-the-instrument concern I suppose. I know some fiddlers who have the luxury of keeping one of their fiddles tuned ‘flat’ and another one kept in concert pitch.

I tend to rough in the tuning when going up or down - that is, don’t fine tune each string until you’ve stepped all four down. It seems scary when you first start doing it, but you get used to it. Kind of like getting used to wearing contact lenses.

Now go get yourself a piper with a flat set and have some fun.

I know this has all been hashed over before, but tuning down affords you the opportunity to get some cheap thrills from playing even the most tired tunes in your repetoire. For many of the tunes, it seems like their personality changes altogether. This affords the opportunity for much delight and giggling within a session. :-)

And frisbee’s right about the droning…there’s a great deal more vibration occuring and tones being produced - I find it possible to add lovely droning in parts of tunes where it just doesn’t come off as nicely when playing in concert pitch. Just another way to explore your tunes in more detail.

Re: About tuning down for fiddle…

I was just about to give out to people for not realising it is not uncommon for Irish music to be tuned differently, when I realised my own first exposure to it a few years ago. I was fortunate to hear someone say it was an Eb seisiún before I joined. So when I joined I confidently said ‘This is an Eb seisiún isn’t it?’. I got immediate kudos because I was a guitarist who could actually tell it was not standard tuning, good little bluffing trick I have to say, Now I can actually really tell immediately if it is tuned differently, but most friendly seisiúns will tell you when you’ve joined.

So I’m not going to give anyone any crap for not knowing Irish music is quite commonly in different tunings. Instead I’ll just give some advice.

Guitarists/other accompanists. if you join a seisiún and you realise immediately that tunes you know sound different ask straight away, ‘Is this an Eb Seisiún’ you’ll either be told ‘Yes, would you like to join us’ or ‘no it is a C seisiún’ or ‘no it is a regular seisiún you must have your ears painted on’. If the true answer is the latter, you’ve a lot of catching up to do, otherwise just tune your guitar up a semitone for Eb or down a tone for C, presuming you play either drop D, Open G or DADGAD. There are some pipes tuned to B so you might have to go down that far so just use medium/heavy gauge strings and you’ll be grand, or simply learn how to back the tunes in other tunings with the use of a capo.

Similar things apply to tune players. Fiddle, mandolin, banjo and other similarly tuned players need to tune all strings up a semi-tone for an Eb seisiún. All strings down a full tone for a C seisiún and another half-step for a B seisiún, etc.

Wind players need to carry other instruments in the other keys on the off chance they’ll arrive at a seisiún which is in a key they can’t play in with their regular instrument. I learnt this from the seisiún I speak of above.

A flautist turned up after travelling many miles to reach the seisiún then upon arrival he heard that it was an Eb seisiún. He came up to me and cursed his luck saying ‘If only I’d have known, I left my Eb Flute in the Hotel’. The hotel was a long way away!

I also know this from the really good pipers and flute/whistle players I know who always carry about 100 extra flutes/whistles with them no matter what!

So overall for those of you who didn’t know about different tunings, you need to be aware that they exist and that you need to be prepared for them because no matter how good you are in regular tuning, if you start playing in an irregularly tuned seisiún without realising it is one, you won’t just have egg on your face, you’ll have a whole stinking omelet!!!
