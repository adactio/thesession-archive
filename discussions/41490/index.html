The History

I’ve sat here many a time typing out questions about the tradition, and instruments, but there has been one question that has always intrigued me in a way none other has been able to. I originally thought of this question probably last year, but up to this point, I have always forgotten to actually ask it. Where did it all come from? Where did this tradition of learning tunes by ear, and adding them to a massive repertoire start? Where did the Session come from? Where did it start? When did it start? How old are these tunes? I’m genuinely curious. In my opinion, this is one of the most important questions one can ask because were it not for the history, this site wouldn’t exist. Let’s take a moment to recap all of the events that led up to the creation of Irish traditional music as not only a form of music, but a tradition for all to enjoy. Let us take a moment to appreciate how far this tradition has come.

Re: The History

Irish music, in its modern form, is generally considered to have formed in the 18th century with the advent of the uilleann pipes. Not much is known about what came before. Sure, the harping stuff, but that was more of a nobility thing. Trad music is really at its core a rural music, and, as you can imagine, very few farmers or fishermen kept good historical records. Literacy wasn’t really a thing 300 years ago. Anyway, lots of people surmise that trad at that time was similar or shared influences with the harping tradition, but was separate. Nobody even knows what instruments might have been used. There are hints at flutes or whistles of some type as early as the 13th century, but nothing definite besides some mouth blown bagpipes prior to uilleann pipes.

As is the case with most musics, it is most certainly based on singing at some point, if you go back far enough. Church modes, folk songs, etc have had an effect on the development of today’s music.

And the big plot twist is the pipes. Contrary to what you might think, a set of pipes cost about the same as a house. Poor rural farmers didn’t own them. So richer people or rich sponsors no doubt had an influence on the music. The dance forms associated with the music would also have an effect on the formation of the music. Figure out when these forms started, and I think you’ll get your answer.

Some say that most of the music was actually Scottish, and that it was brought home by itinerant workers and got it’s own flavor.

Now, about that whole irish-music-having-thousands-of-tunes thing…..thats a relatively modern phenomena. Each community had their own repertoire of a few tunes. Traveling musicians and interaction with neighboring communities resulted in spreading tunes around, but some places might only know a handf of tunes. There was no such thing as a unified Irish music tradition or huge music library until modern times. Commonalities and similarities, yes, but not a universal style. When you hear people talk about regional styles, that’s a real thing. More watered down today, of course, but heavily pronounced in the days before cars and phones and record players.

Re: The History

We don’t know what the tunes were like, but the style seems to go a long way back. Giraldus Cambrensis (Gerald of Wales) visited Ireland in about 1204 and wrote “the only thing to which I find that this people apply a commendable industry is playing upon musical instruments; in which they are incomparably more skilful than any other nation I have ever seen. For their modulation on these instruments, unlike that of the Britons to which I am accustomed, is not slow and harsh, but lively and rapid, while the harmony is both sweet and gay. It is astonishing that in so complex and rapid a movement of the fingers, the musical proportions can be preserved…….. it must be remarked however, that both Scotland and Wales strive to rival Ireland in the art of music….”

“Also the Pub session in particular, is it true they only arose after WWII in the Irish diaspora in England?”

As I and others have advised countless times on this site: read Reg Hall’s book on the subject.

The notion that has become ‘intuitive’, that musicians just naturally play together when-and-where-ever they meet, does not hold up, it seems to me. In my younger days, it was common for fiddlers - who were the music tradition-bearers in my circles - to listen to each other play solo; for them to play together, if it happened at all, was usually a kind of novelty. Having a guitar or piano accompanist was usual - but ensemble-playing was rare. Proviso: that’s not getting into (semi)professional dance bands.

Re: The History

“Where did this tradition of learning tunes by ear, and adding them to a massive repertoire start?”

Learning tunes by ear goes back as far as you care to look - it is not something specific to Irish Traditional music or traditional dance music. It just fell out of practice in Western Art Music because it was not practical.

As for massive repertoires, they have probably become much larger since the arrival of the wireless (i.e. radio), gramophone and motorised transport. Before that time, most musicians in Ireland would have stayed within a much smaller area, meeting fewer musicians and had no means, other than direct contact with other musicians, of learning new tunes. There would have been some musicians around Ireland that could read music and had access to whatever printed collections existed at the time but, by and large, repertoires would have been smaller and more localised.

“Where did the Session come from?”

Allegedly North London in the early 1950s ;-) . I find it hard to believe that dance musicians in Ireland did not sometimes find themselves playing tunes together just for the craic - and perhaps it happened in pubs now and again. But it was apparently in London that the pub session became a ‘thing’ - fellow Irish immigrants got talking in the pub, each discovered that the other had played music back home and they agreed to meet there with their instruments on a subsequent occasion. Publicans soon realised that having the music was a draw to their Irish clientele and encouraged the musicians to play regularly, rewarding them with free-flowing drink or sometimes cash. It seems likely that the same thing might have been happening simultaneously in the US and perhaps parts of Ireland (although, in general, Traditional Music was highly unfashionable there at the time), but London always seems to take the credit for the development of the ‘session’ as we know it.

Re: The History

About the history of the various musical instruments, I’ve done a huge amount of reading about one- bagpipes- and the bottom line is that there just isn’t much information to go on.

Sadly, the less hard facts there are about something the wider the door opens for guesswork, speculation, and sheer fantasy.

With bagpipe history the same myths have been repeated so often that they have gained the illusion of fact, at least for those who don’t take the trouble to look for the evidence and sources behind the assertions. Those who do look, will find that they don’t exist.

I can only assume that the same is true of all musical instruments. With most instruments there are no surviving instruments from early times, and all there is to go on are vague verbal descriptions and artistic depictions of varying quality here and there.

A rare exception is the ancient Celtic war-trumpet, the carynx, of which there are surviving examples in playable condition. Wood instruments nearly always rot away.
