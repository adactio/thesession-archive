Session seat grabbers….

Just back from Ennis Trad where I noticed quite a strange and frankly annoying phenomenon.

During Ennis Trad there are a lot of scheduled sessions. The programme tells everyone the sessions start at a certain time, yet some sessions get too crowded even before the booked musicians arrive or any music starts,

So a session might be down to start at 9.30 but by 9 all the seats except a few for the booked musicians are taken up by over-enthusiastic or perhaps just selfish people who just have to get a good spot in the session.

Now I wouldn’t mind so much if these over-eager people were great players or even young kids but I’m sad to say more often than not they are mediocre foreign players generally of the same particular age group. I won’t say exactly which these are because I know I’ll get given out to by people of these nationalities and ages, but for the good of session culture can we drop the political correctness on this one?!!

I know that foreign players don’t get much chance to come to Ireland so the enthusiam for getting a place in a session is understandable. However it’s highly likely a bit more humility and a bit less punctuality from such players will result in them experiencing a much more satisfying level of session.

I know sessions are primarily social occasions but please remember they are also musical occasions and they are normally best when there is a good proportion of quality players to drive them along, but if you take up all the seats before the session even starts you aren’t going to get the right proportion of quality players. I saw it countless times over Ennis Trad where a good player would walk in say half an hour or an hour into the session but because it was so crowded they’d either just sit and listen or go somewhere else.

So can I suggest in future to those over-eager session goers to just chill out. In Ireland we tend to be a bit more relaxed about punctuality, if a session is down to start at 9.30 that doesn’t mean you have to get there at 9 and rush for a seat. In fact a session down to start at 9.30 is more likely to start at 10.

If you want to insist on turning up half an hour before the session starts then by all means take a seat in the bar and save it in case you want to join in but give the booked musicians a chance to get settled and maybe play a few tunes on their own before asking to join them. Don’t impose yourself on the main area of session unless you are either asked or are sure you can make a strong contribution. Besides you can learn a lot from just listening you know.

This isn’t intended to be a wind-up or antagonising I’m just simply trying to point out something I see as a problem,

Are you sure you’re not labeling what they’re doing as a “gig” because it doesn’t meet your particular preconception of what a “session” is? The organizers might have a broader and more accurate understanding of what a session is and how it relates to the people they hire and the setting it’s in. The misunderstanding might well be with the people visiting who expect the sessions to be modeled after their own particular preconception.

I really don’t think it’s necessary for the organizers to attempt to explain how their concept of a “session” might be different from the preconceptions of the visitors, and I don’t think the hired musicians need to bother with it either. The responsibility falls on the visitor to use common sense to suss out what’s going on before becoming involved.

When I was at that Festival I felt as welcome as the advertisement promised, and I didn’t see anyone being excluded. There might have been some people who were disappointed because the sessions didn’t meet their expectations, but I didn’t meet any of them or have any conversations about it.

I think the important point that Frisbee seems to be suggesting is that we all take a little responsibility as visitors to make sure we aren’t infringing on the event with our eagerness to participate. Frisbee doesn’t seem to suggest anyone is doing it maliciously, but rather unwittingly. I agree that we need to take some responsibility to understand and cooperate with the modus operandi of the event to prevent this sort of thing from being repeated.
