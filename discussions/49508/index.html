Re: How do you write a tune?

You dick around and find phrases. Make them rhyme with or mirror others. Tunes have to be 93% balanced. You put a bunch of stuff together, play it, leave it, come back to it, embarass yourself at how derivative/brilliant it was, toss/modify etc. Like any art form, what you throw away is as important as what you keep.

Or you make like Paddy Fahy and spend your days with sheep and tractors and the tunes emerge at the pub, perfect.

I think there will be a variety of different ways.

When I was much younger, I composed a few songs and worked out the melodies in my head. I then worked out the chords on the guitar.
I’ve not really tried composing many new tunes although I’ve had ideas, parts of melody lines come into my head and so on.
However, I’m sure if I really focused I’d be able to do it but, as you say, there are so many good tunes out there already and I enjoy gathering them.
Of course, creativity is good thing but it’s not entirely wasted if you allow it to influence and improve your style of playing. It’s not necessary to be a composer.

I think a lot depends on your musical background too. Many great players and musicians have composed lots of tunes but never actually “written down” any notes. Some will have “noodled about on their instruments” as DSS suggests or just worked out melodies in their head.
Some players with a classical background may do it all “on paper” before even touching an instrument.

Re: How do you write a tune?

“How do you write a good tune?”

That’s a very good question, DrSS, and I don’t suppose anyone really has an answer to that because the composer, for various reasons, is often oblivious to the “goodness” that other people hear.

If the question were merely, “How do you go about writing a tune”, then that would be a lot easier to answer, although still quite involved.
For me it often depends on the context. If it’s for a commission then I’ll often start by picking a tune type (air, waltz, jig, 4/4 march, etc) that seems appropriate to the subject and try to find as phrase that can be developed into a tune. At other times often tunes appear by accident while noodling away in an idle moment and grow wings, as it were.

I suppose the best advice I could give is to write lots of tunes and then play them to your friends and they will tell you which, if any, are the good ones. There are probably only a handful of cases where I composed what I thought was a good tune and then have that opinion confirmed by others.
We all have different likes and dislikes. I haven’t yet been able to identify what qualities in a tune make it more likely to be thought of as “good” by lots of people.

Re: How do you write a tune?

Not all of my compositions were things that I sat down specifically to compose, but when I am consciously working to compose, I do usually start by noodling until I find a phrase that is catchy, and then I generally try to guide where it goes. I will try different things until I have a general contour of a part. Then I will work on a second part and try to make it feel like a continuation. Sometimes that means echoing bits of what appeared in the first part, but sometimes that ends up sounding trite to me. And sometimes, I end up with multiple things that sound good, and that has occasionally turned into a 3 or more part tune.

Once I have an overall general shape of a tune, I will record it for reference later if I think it has real potential. But generally, if it isn’t stuck in my head and completely disappears from my memory, I won’t go back to it. If I can pick up and instrument hours or days later, and remember most of it, that is a sign of potential and I will then work to refine it further.

Sometimes I will compose a tune with a specific thing in mind, like composing a tune that doesn’t actually resolve, or “chases its own tail”. Or I’ll work on a tune that echoes something from earlier in a completely different way (different mode, different octave, different part of a phrase, etc.) A couple of my better tunes have actually flowed out almost fully formed, and those ones generally seem to feel less contrived to me.

A few of my tunes get played around. Some of the ones that I have recorded on my albums got picked up by other players, and one of my more recent tunes gets played by a lot of folks around the local scene, but I don’t know if it has spread much further… However, I don’t compose tunes to become the next great composer. I do it because composing helps me learn more about the music, and makes me think more deeply about what I like in certain tunes, etc.

Re: How do you write a tune?

These things, in any order:
1 - want to
2 - explore
3 - indulge in the experience completely
4 - creativity

Those aren’t in order. #4 is often closely tied to #2. Often, #1 comes last AFTER you discover something by using #2, 3, and 4 that you arrive at a new experience, a new realization, an inkling about something that is NOT a repeat of what you’ve experienced before. An analytical mind will destroy exploration and creativity faster and more completely than anything other than death or having your ukulele run over by a steam roller (which in fact can lead to much new creativity in using that ukulele in comedy skits). Never wait for #1. Don’t hope for it. Go directly to….., well, a good recommendation would be do them in this order:
3, 2, 4, then 1. When you get to #1, “want to”, you’ll then need to leap back into one or more of #2, 3 and 4 in order to build/work your song/creative project to completion, if it isn’t already complete.

When I think of completing music or lyrics, it’s always with a sense of the original feel or experience that drove its invention in the first place, but with a sense of now needing to assure it’s identity, integrity, character, content, style, whatever, some of those things being internal organic parts of it and some being a little more external considerations, or “after the fact”, to say later, “It’s DONE!”. It’s a communication back to the universe, so you want it to be clear. And of course anything being ultimately “done” is not how it goes. No music or lyrics are ever terminally DONE, they just reach a point where they can be considered excellent, or above a standard of acceptance and you hit RECORD on the computer and start playing it into the microphone, hoping you can hit the definitive current version within 15 takes. I like doing lots of takes, there’s often a little to learn or a little kind of magic in most of them.

Also, carry with you 24/7 a way to capture your ideas: pen and notebooks, voice recorder, etc. Those “notions” you have from time to time are usually more integrated, complex and meaningful than left-brain categorized ways of thinking “oh, I should write a song about THAT topic”. Why use 5% of your creative capacity when you can use 100% and increase it? Stay fluid between the ears. You can from time to time suddenly have your subconscious come up with a great alteration or addition to a lyric or music, and you NEED to capture that idea, or believe me, you’ll forget most of them, even though came come from your natural genius having spent time on it. The noise of urban life is distracting. PROTECT CREATIVITY!

Re: How do you write a tune?

There’s a famous quote by Ira Glass which is worth reading in full:

“Nobody tells this to people who are beginners, I wish someone told me. All of us who do creative work, we get into it because we have good taste. But there is this gap. For the first couple years you make stuff, it’s just not that good. It’s trying to be good, it has potential, but it’s not. But your taste, the thing that got you into the game, is still killer. And your taste is why your work disappoints you. A lot of people never get past this phase, they quit. Most people I know who do interesting, creative work went through years of this. We know our work doesn’t have this special thing that we want it to have. We all go through this. And if you are just starting out or you are still in this phase, you gotta know its normal and the most important thing you can do is do a lot of work. Put yourself on a deadline so that every week you will finish one story. It is only by going through a volume of work that you will close that gap, and your work will be as good as your ambitions. And I took longer to figure out how to do this than anyone I’ve ever met. It’s gonna take awhile. It’s normal to take awhile. You’ve just gotta fight your way through.”

And this I think is the key to it. There’s no secret to writing a banger; you just write a few thousand tunes that get filed mostly in the waste paper basket and once in a while, one comes along that plays effortlessly and feels ageless. There are lots of tricks - the Phil Cunningham trick of taking that bar of a tune and making it the first bar of a new tune, that kind of thing. I don’t write much, but usually I simply set out to a tune like X for a particular purpose. I work out the ending, turnaround bars, an opening, and once I’ve got a basic structure you’re just pasting in details. Of course this can end up sounding pretty derivative, but that can also simply be your own perception.

I don’t.

However, sometimes I feel inspired and I have a sense that an interesting “seed” or phrase is emerging, and it starts boggling in my mind. If I have the fiddle handy, I’ll record it as a voice memo on my phone, or just lilt it into the phone (trying not to catch attention if I’m outdoors or in public…:-). And even the tunes that have reached the final stage I have only played or recorded- never written down…

Re: How do you write a tune?

A little music analysis can prevent much wasted effort.
I know folks who say they like a certain tune, but can’t say WHAT they like about it.
Music preference is personal - so, take a few moments to identify what YOU like about favorite tunes, in terms of: rhythm, scale or mode, melodic movement, syncopation, chord progression, etc… the more specific, the better. I start with elements I already know I like, create a riff - then experiment from there.
