Flute Embouchure Question: James Galway vs Charles Nicholson

I’ve been playing the fiddle for a long time but took a notion to learn the flute. I bought a cheap nach Meyer on eBay and fixed it up with new corks and pads and some extensive crack repair. I’m actually quite pleased with it.

Anyway, I was perusing Terry McGee’s website and read this article by C. Nicholson on tone which Terry surmises as:

http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Nicholson_on_Tone.htm

"
So what did we learn? Primarily that the tone Nicholson was after, and for which he was so very famous and influential, was produced by a high-pressure, short-jet, well-covered embouchure approach, entirely at odds with today’s art-music’s relaxed, low pressure, long-jet, open embouchure approach….
"

(thanks Terry!)

Anyway I’m not too familiar with the idiosyncrasies of the flute but I think I get what Nicholson is trying to say when he says:

"
The tone ought to be as reedy as possible, as much like that of the hautboy [oboe] as you can get, it, but embodying the round mellowness of the clarionet.
"

I also found this old Masterclass from James Galway

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQg0vScnQ8E

My question is, are they roughly trying to do similar things?

They are both:
-covering half or more of the flute hole with the lower lip
-saying they blow into the flute as opposed to across it
-not rolling out

Re: Flute Embouchure Question: James Galway vs Charles Nicholson

I get the impression that in this video Sir James makes more use of lifting his jet for the octave than I think Nicholson or modern Irish players would. But then he’s going to be expecting a full range on the flute of three octaves or more, which is more than we generally aspire to. And he has a bit more time than we have - few classical pieces are taken at the speeds we regard as normal!

Now, just for fun, I took the head off my flute and tried the same octave jump experiment he does with just the head alone. I’m now higher pitch than him as my flute head (without barrel) is shorter than his Boehm head. I habitually have the head turned towards me, the embouchure hole well covered and I’m blowing down towards the centre of the flute, not in any way across it. I found I had to redirect the jet a lot upwards for the octave. A surprising amount upwards.

Add the barrel, I’m now at slightly lower pitch, but still need to redirect it upwards considerably for the octave.

But add the rest of the flute and finger G = xxx ooo, and I can now seamlessly slide from G4 to G5 doing hardly more than thinking about it. I’m presumably making minor alteration to jet direction, jet tightness or jet pressure, or more probably more than one of these, but the changes are in the realm of almost sub-conscious change rather than the dramatic changes needed with head alone. And entirely achievable at speed.

So Galway’s head-only experiment is perhaps most useful for exploring and demonstrating jet physics. But completing the journey I’ve just taken - head only, head plus barrel, head plus rest of flute - reminds us that the flute is a complex inter-reactive system involving:
- the generator (the jet),
- the resonator (the length of tube selected by the fingering), and
- the resonator feedback that enables and controls the jet-switching action. This is the secret ingredient to flute efficiency, and our job is to encourage it to reach its best.

Getting the best out of the flute is the subject of this article:
https://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Getting_the_hard_dark_tone.htm

Re: Flute Embouchure Question: James Galway vs Charles Nicholson

The lessons involved are as applicable to a beer bottle as to a flute embouchure. It really isn’t the instrument.

A good classical player nowadays is supposed to be able to generate a wide range of tone qualities, of which our favoured dark tone would count as one extreme end of the N-Dimensional spectrum. James Galway has a very strong and very widely appreciated tone, but he comes from an era where the flexibility was not held up as quite so high an ideal.
This is not to say that he can’t probably still get any sound out of a flute that I could and better. And also not to say that most people playing a Boehm flute for classical music today can do it.

I find the question of tone an aesthetic one rather than a technical one. If you can hear yourself and listen to yourself then you can keep trying things out until you like the sound of your own playing. For this, stand in front of a wall or record yourself.

Re: Flute Embouchure Question: James Galway vs Charles Nicholson

Some great observations by C. Nicholson in that article, I’m going to re-read it. Lots of info.
I would point out to flute players that the embouchure is a muscular apparatus and needs to be developed. When I play regularly, fine. If I take time off, such as month or so, it takes me a solid two weeks of daily playing for at least an hour of heavy embouchure effort, to get the full control back. What I’m after is ability to feel the air stream as as small an air jet stream as possible, for control and efficiency in converting air to quality tone. You don’t want air spreading out and creating wind tone or even worse, not even knowing how inefficient you’re being and what you COULD be getting out of the flute if you worked out the embouchure as hard as working out biceps or legs in a gym with heavy weights. Go Arnold on the embouchure; you really need to, to find your full control and best air jet possibilities.

The other point by Nicholson I like is how he mentions, a little vaguely though, of how the direction/aim of the air jet should be adjusted between playing in the lower octave vs playing in the second or third octave. That means directing the air stream more downward into the tube for first octave, and more higher at the far edge of the top of the tone hole, for second and third octave. That redirection is greatly helped by having the air jet a small diameter stream, which is a dimension minimized by having practiced for that, by practicing your embouchure ability to create the tiniest yet fully flowing air stream diameter as possible.

Work the embouchure with great effort. Get those muscles burned and exhausted, at some point in practice sessions. You can take the head joint off a metal flute and practice this for hours, or even just put a finger under your lower lip any time to get the same effect as the mouth placed on the flute, where the lower lip is braced against a surface, and the upper lip then has ability to press down on the lower lip on the sides, and the air stream hole minimized in size. When this embouchure control gets toned up, you’ll feel it as a tiny air stream you can control. If your embouchure is out of condition, it’s just a vague feel, and much less efficient when playing the instrument.

Eventually, after practicing this with intense effort, after perhaps a couple weeks solid, you should experience profound improvement in ability to control octaves and produce a much cleaner, more efficient tone.

Aim high. Aim low. Aim mid-blade. This takes practice, as each flute design has slight variations that have to be adjusted to for how it responds for you, so angles of air flow are not 100% consistent between metal flutes, and that much more variable between wood flutes with different shaped tone holes, bore sizes, materials, wall thicknesses, etc.

Another good point for flute players is that flute designs vary in how they respond to the PRECISE DIRECTION the air stream is applied, relative to the length of the flute body. You might start off presuming that a perfectly 90-degree approach to the length of the flute body is what’s required, but not quite in some cases. Start by playing the flute with the flute held at 90-degrees to the right of the direction of your air stream. Then, try playing by SLIGHTLY adjusting the air stream to be aimed a little towards the right, towards going down the length of the flute. This adjustment will be tiny, just a few degrees, between 3 and 10 degrees, just a slight variation. You may find, depending on the model of the flute, that it responds better in one or all octaves if the air stream is aimed slightly to the right, like that. I have played about 7 different silver flute models and some would really respond better with this approach. Some not so much. It takes experimenting to find the ideal. You could do this by slightly pushing the flute body forward, more in front of you, just a little.

On the circular chart link included, there is a 0 degree mark at the far right, where let’s say the flute body is aimed, so your air stream is going straight across the flute body, towards the 90-degree mark. Then while playing constantly, push the flute body slowly forwards on the right, in front of you towards the 5-degree or 10-degree angle, while keeping the air stream still going in the same direction as before, and see how the flute responds to this variation in air stream angling. Any difference in any of the octaves? Any better option? Each flute model will have it’s own answers.

It’s interesting to look at flutes that are claimed to have been Nicholson’s to see how he set them up to play:

https://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Nicholson%27s%20Flute.htm

And I’ve always loved this quote from him. The flute he’s talking about was the Astor small-holed flute of which his father (also Charles) enlarged the finger holes. We can expect that that dramatically improved (and sharpened) the body notes, but would have made the foot notes very flat. We see that in early “Nicholson’s Improved” flutes.

“With this flute, I came to London, and although my public performances met with a gratifying reception, yet my flute was not approved of, inasmuch as it required a total alteration in the system of fingering; and it was generally asserted, that I was the only person who could play in tune on a flute with large holes.”

@usurpator - with the whistle the “embouchure” is 100% inbuilt in the head. There is very little by way of adjustment which the player can effect.

Get somebody to try out your delrin flute (which I’m assuming is keyless) to make sure it is in playable order. At least then you can eliminate any “is it me or the flute” questions.

It is normal and natural to be able to play the middle octave G-G‘ comfortably before being able to sustain the lower notes well. And for the higher notes A’..D‘’ to be playable but with tuning challenges and shrill. Brendan Mulholland focuses on practicing the low D/F#/G notes up/down and lo/hi/lo octave when building tone and embouchure. On the basis that once you crack these notes everything else is easy. The omission of E is conscious - it is intrinsically a weaker note due to the size of the 6th hole. And he emphasises strength and balance of the D/F#/G combinations.

Indian flute players name the key of their flute by the SA note which is the XXXOOO i.e. G. They would refer to that as the key of the flute and not the XXXXXX ‘D’ mode. The G-scale is the sweet spot for early practice.

There is an element to the part of the discussion that dealt with playing the higher notes that is missing, and it is generally missing in the teaching of Irish flute playing. The only exception I know of is Majella Bartley’s lesson series on OAIM where she gives what I think she calls ‘diaphragm punch’ exercises. This missing element is abdominal pressure and it is beautifully taught in this youtube video by Emily Beynon:
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1xu9EXC1MRI
(Note however that Emily Beynon’s discussion fails to note that an important way to increase airspeed is by moving the lips closer to where the airstream is directed inside the flute. This movement is taught clearly in the OAIM flute series by Steph Geremia.)
I was taught to play the higher notes many years ago (in the late 1960s and early 70s), when learning the silver flute, using primarily abdominal pressure, with the lips being used for fine adjustments of tuning. My teacher, the Swiss flutist Ed Raas, was a pupil of the also Swiss flutist Aurèle Nicolet, who was a pupil of Marcel Moyse. Chet
