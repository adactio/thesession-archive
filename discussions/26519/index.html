Re: Recorder vs Tin Whistle techniques

Here are my attempts at answering your questions:

1. The amount of ornamentation used varies from one player to another. Some use a massive amount of ornamentation, some use virtually none. If you are approaching the tradition from ‘the outside’, the biggest concern is probably making sure your rhythm and phrasing are good. Once you have good rhythm and phrasing, you can always continue to work on your ornamentation. Personally, I think any whistle player *should* learn how to play rolls/cuts/taps/cranns - It can definitely add to your playing.

2. I’m not sure about ornamentation that doesn’t fit into a category… are you talking about ‘classical’ techniques as opposed to the rolls/cuts/taps typical of irish whistle/flute playing?

3. Tonguing also varies from one whistler to the next. Sean Ryan takes it to an extreme, some players don’t tongue at all, but most whistle players use it to some extent.

Here is a site with some great clips of whistle playing and an explanation of the ornamentation and phrasing used:
http://www.rogermillington.com/tunetoc/

Grey Larsen’s book really goes in depth about whistle and flute ornamentation, as well as tonguing and phrasing - I highly recommend it.

Also, as suggested above, listen to some whistle players (and flute players). Donnchadh O Briain and Mary Bergin spring to mind immediately, but there are lots of others out there.

Re: Recorder vs Tin Whistle techniques

Brueggen, the one thing it’s really important to understand is that a lot of the cuts, taps, rolls etc. are not used as “ornamentation” in the classical sense but to add articulation and phrasing to the tune. They are almost always used in service to the rhythm.

If you get the rhythm right, what you do will sound right. That goes for whether you are cutting and tapping all over the place, or tonguing nearly every note. I personally use very little ornamentation compared to some.

Hup’s comment above is rather unfortunate, but I would say that you can’t learn about whistle ornamentation just from watching Youtube videos, in fact it can lead you down the garden path because there are people on there who appear to be technically proficient but whose playing really lacks the proper feeling and lift.

It’s definitely worth practicing the techniques of cuts, rolls, taps, crans etc; if you put the time in, you’ll find that these are great, effective weapons in your musical arsenal even if you only use them sparsely.

Re: Recorder vs Tin Whistle techniques

1. Ornamentation is fundamental to Irish wind playing because it’s how we articulate. Small finger movements mostly replace tonguing and serve as articulation. Some players also bend into and out of notes and use finger vibrato. Your music won’t sound Irish without some of this. The degree to which you use finger articulations, which ones, and where you put them is largely a style choice.

2. Short trills instead of rolls - no, no, no, never. STOP. A roll is a very well defined, precise, and rhythmic set of finger articulations. Learn how to do them right. Running up from one pitch to another is done in airs but not dance tunes. The most you might find are rhythmic “triplets” (which are not triplets) connecting notes a third apart.

3. See number 1. If you are mostly tonguing, you’re doing it wrong.

Playing proper ornamentation is actually pretty important to playing Irish music. You don’t need to play too much of it to get by, but it does have to be something you do pretty intentionally.

Re: Recorder vs Tin Whistle techniques

Brueggen… I have a distinction for you which is maybe a little too picky.

Don’t view the question as one of being “authentic” - playing the way one is supposed to play so as to get “cred” and “props” as someone who is “true to the tradition” (or some similar crap).

View the question as one of “doing something that works”. The reason that people don’t go around playing music on recorder with random ornamentation (trills, etc) thrown in is not because it’s “not authentic”, but because it’s an endeavour which has repeatedly shown to yield pretty much nothing of value (except maybe to the player who just sees Irish as a repertoire of melodies which they plan to treat in exactly the same way as they treat any other repertoire).

The problem is that “doing something that works” implies a kind of “aboutness” which is hard to determine - in a way, the music is “about” combining melody and articulation to generate a dance rhythm - but there are probably plenty of people who would not put it that way…

Re: Recorder vs Tin Whistle techniques

1 . Is it ok to play a tune with minimum ornamentation, or to be considered a “real” whistle player does this need to be developed to a great degree?

Depends on the tune - sometimes less is more, otherwise it adds lift and bounce and on many tunes, if you don’t use the standard ornaments in the standard places, they don’t sound right.

2. Is it ok to play ornamentation that doesn’t fit into a definite category?

Yes - but only if you use the “correct” ornaments as a basis for it. You should never be afraid to try things out but only keep the stuff that fits !!

3. As a recorder player I also tend to tongue quite a bit. Do I need to learn to slur most of a tune?

Get the skills to play either leggato or stuccato as suits the tune. 99% of the time each tune will sound better with a mixture of both.

Re: Recorder vs Tin Whistle techniques

Red Menace said what I was going to say: cuts, pats, and the rolls that are created from cuts and pats aren’t “ornaments” per se but rather articulations. I use the high-sounding term “digital articulation” but I don’t know of a better thing to call it.

Think of the child’s song “Mary Had a Little Lamb”.

On the whistle it could start B A G A B B B.

Now imagine that you can’t tongue the last BBB but have to use your fingers instead: B (C#) B (A) B.

The C# and A in parentheses are played VERY quickly so that they aren’t heard as note, but only as articulations on the B melody notes.

That’s a “roll” on B.

Rolls aren’t “ornaments”. An “ornament”, by definition, is something superfluous to the melody which is added for decorative effect. Rolls aren’t like that, but are the articulations which also have a rhythmic effect which drive jigs and reels along.

Anyhow you can always spot the recorder player, even if he’s now playing a whistle, by little clues like:

1) lifting the upper thumb off the back of the whistle when playing middle D. There’s no hole there on the whistle but oftentimes recorder players lift their thumb out of habit.

2) tonguing in the wrong places, or tonguing too much.

3) playing trills and half-trills (praltrillers or mordents) all over the place.

4) using a diaphragm vibrato at inappropriate times.

5) moving the whistle about, moving the head about, or raising the eyebrows while playing.

6) playing with the tips of the fingers and the fingers strongly arched.

7) a lack of cuts, pats, and rolls in the playing.

8) playing Irish jigs and reels the way they’re written in a book somewhere, not the way they actually sound.

There are a lot of woodwind players in all genres who seem to imagine there’s a feather duster on the end of their instrument and the floor needs a sweep. Irish whistle players are just as likely to do it as classical recorder players. I think it looks silly but then I’m one of the sit-there-like-a-stone-buddha types and some folks probably think that makes me a po-faced git. Here’s somebody at the opposite extreme from Bruggen, Racheal Cogan playing with Ross Daly’s band::

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7zDOn9o8_Rk

One thing I will add to the constructive points made here: one thing you can do more easily on a recorder than a whistle is a semitonal strike (for some notes, anyway). =c{B}=c by using the T-23 ---- fingering for B is one example. This is something you can do easily on a melodeon or keyed flute, but not so cleanly on a whistle. Where it’s appropriate to the tune, it’ll sound idiomatic despite being outside the standard whistle bag of tricks. You also have slides between some notes that work more like the fiddle than the whistle. Most sessions have more than one kind of instrument and there’s no obligation to always pretend to be a whistle or flute, you might blend in more with somebody else in the texture for a moment.
