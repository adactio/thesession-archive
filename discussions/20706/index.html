Re: chromatic box thoughts

What do you mean by the best of both worlds? I don’t understand how a C system chromatic could embody the best of diatonic and PA styles.

You do have a functionally chromatic box and that’s nice for additional keys; but diatonic phrasing would seem impossible to replicate or at least so difficult to that it wouldn’t be worth the effort. That’s what I’m getting at, I wouldn’t want to have to redevelop a style just to replicate what I already know. If I were to come into a chromatic, I’d want to learn how to play it properly instead of forcing it to sound like a diatonic.

One of the charms I see in the diatonic is its utterly limiting keyboard layout. Having operated within those confines for so long, I don’t think I’d know what to do with a unisonoric chromatic box. I’d have to retrain, and I’d lose all the tricks and shortcuts I picked up on the diatonic. I’d be like a single middle aged man who just hit the lottery. I’d go nuts with all the newly found freedom. As it is, I like the sport provided by playing around the limitations of the diatonic box.

what the op asked was whether anyone had tried a saltarelle chaville. i would like to know this myself. i already play b/c accordion and anglo concertina and am currently teaching myself cba. i spent a couple of hours today transferring “plains of boyle” and “porthole of the kelp” to cba and both of them sounded ravishing. did they sound like jimmy kimmel? hell no, but i don’t give two sh****** about sounding like a melodeon. they sounded gorgeous on their own terms. if you keep your ornamention plain and use a tad of musette to relieve that ugly dry PA sound, and don’t shotgun it too fast and/or staccato like certain irish pa stars who shall remain nameless……it can sound pretty darn ravishing….i was initially using itm tunes just like hanon exercises to learn cba for musette & piazolla, but the fact is that itm sounds beautiful in its own right on this instrument…

anyway, the chaville is interesting because it gives you 36 (or 37?) notes and 60 basses at an unbelievably compact weight of 12.5 pounds. most compact cbas are around 30 notes, or even 34, which is not enough for itm or anything else worth investing that kind of dough in. for example, the castagnari compact cba has only 34 notes and is even more horrifyingly expensive than the chaville.

which brings us to this point----the chaville is cheaper than the castagnari compact, but is still demmed expensive, at about 4 grand---double the price of a bouebe. some pretty gnarly questions present themselves---are these things as stiff as saltarelle irish diatonics? do they have the same-grade reeds? bouebes and nouages have factory reeds. allegedly better-grade factory reeds, but factory reeds. at four grand, one would hope the chaville reeds were premium and that the box was more responsive and less stiff than the bouebe & the nuage. you can’t spend four grand without knowing all this stuff and i think it’s great that you’re asking.

all i know at this point in my own research is that the chaville’s PA cousin---same deal, plenty of notes in a compact package with 60 basses----is being played right now by jimmy keane of bohola and if it’s good enough for him, it must be pretty good. also, here is a wonderful youtube clip of itm being played credibly and gorgeously (though not melodeonly) on a salt PA in donegal. too dry, but still beautiful:

Been ponderin’ my Omnigraffle ciphers of different layouts and may be coming back full-circle (well, maybe semi-circle…) to some sort of diatonic box with added accidentals.

There is much info on that topic here in this forum as well as others.

For “expanded” diatonic, it seems to come down to:

1a) 3-row B/C/C# boxes with Stradella bass (so-called ‘British’ or ‘Scottish’ style.) Big advantage in that I can transfer the B/C knowledge -- already chromatic melodically -- and get a few breaks on certain passages as far as having to change the bellows direction, Also, layout of three rows is somewhat isomorphic -- less ‘odds & sods’ (love that expression…) note positionings.

1b) Have seen mention of B/C/F boxes, too. I guess the advantage here is a sort of ‘dual purpose’ box that can play Irish as well as other music suited to the two-row 4th layout.

2) B/C 2.5 row -- like Connemara (John Williams), a little like (1) but push/pull note reversals only in the middle octaves of the range. This of course will keep the weight down and makes for a, dunno how to say it, simpler looking box. 12-bass traditional melodeon (i.e., not Stradella), but usually the basses have been customized. I don’t know that care about having the Stradella-style bass accompaniment.

I’ll mention that one of the things I like about the diatonic boxes is the ability to treat it as a solo wind instrument, like a sax or trumpet (or, of course, a harmonica…) Just like there are screwy fingerings and layouts on brass and woodwind instruments, here you are as well on the diatonic. Muscle-memory becomes the critical factor.

As was pointed out earlier in the thread, it is those limitations which cause one to get inventive about rendering tunes, songs and other musical arrangements. And then you have your own musical style!

I started with a Honer Nova 48 bass to learn CBA and it’s kind of worn now after five years of practice. So I recently purchased the Roland FR-1 . I am pretty happy with the Roland now. My reasons for picking it were the low cost, light weight and expected freedom from repairs compared to traditional accordions.

Some things I like: the action is much faster on the buttons; many more choices of sounds for treble and bass; headphones for practice. The Roland is a 72 bass which should help my accompaniment to be less boring. Also I can switch to three rows of bass notes instead of two which would help if I ever figure out how to do bass runs.

The down side is now I need an amp for others to hear though.

So overall a good purchase which allowed me to continue with my accordion hobby since there would be no way for me to afford a new accordion costing excess of five thousand dollars now.
