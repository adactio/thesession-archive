Re: Ivory and Insanity: If your instruments have ivory in them, leave them home…

I might be going to Ireland next year, so I have done some research and have contacted the appropriate government agencies. This is what I have learned:

1. In the case of the pipers, they admit their documentation was not current. You must have the correct documentation. The fact that an instrument is an antique does not alone get you off the hook. See #2.

2. In my case, none of the flutes I own are of protected material, but an agent could make a mistake and I would be out of luck. To avoid problems, I need to authenticate the material used in making the flute. They won’t exempt an instrument for being an antique until they know what material they are exempting. I went over this point at length, so read carefully: Being an antique does not make the point of whether now-protected species were used moot. If it is a protected species, you must authenticate that it predates the protection, but if you are afraid an unprotected species will be mistaken for a protected one, authenticate what it really is.

3. If you have faux ivory (as I do) there are two tests that the agents know. If it is bone (like mine), carry a magnifier. Under magnification, bone reveals pores that ivory does not have. If it is plastic, carry a pin. When heated, the pin will make a dent in the plastic, whereas ivory is too hard.

4. I am also in the process of getting documentation from the maker to authenticate what materials were used to make the flute and when it was made.

I hope this helps to dispel the mystery on this subject.

Re: Ivory and Insanity: If your instruments have ivory in them, leave them home…

The new law in the USA, an Executive Order by the President (not a law passed by Congress), bans the ownership and sale of ivory, not just transportation across borders.

Anyone’s instrument containing ivory is subject to seizure at any time, out playing at a pub or what have you.

You have to protect yourself by getting the proper certificate. To get it you must be able to establish that 1) the instrument was brought into the USA prior to 1976 (or that it was brought in, with certification, prior to 1990) AND that you bought the instrument prior to February 2014. No sales or possession of uncertified ivory are allowed after Feb 2014, period.

Congress might pass a law modifying or overturning the Executive Order but for now that’s the way it is. Many Highland pipers, with 100 year old pipes covered in ivory, are scrambling to either get certified or to send their pipes to a local maker to have the ivory removed.

Re: Ivory and Insanity: If your instruments have ivory in them, leave them home…

“The new law in the USA, an Executive Order by the President (not a law passed by Congress), bans the ownership and sale of ivory, not just transportation across borders.”

Thanks to Gary’s link, this can be clarified. The statement I quoted is in no way accurate. There is no new law and neither sale nor ownership of ivory has been banned. What the President did was to sign an Executive Order to, in cooperation with other nations, direct the Fish and Wildlife agency, among others, to take all appropriate action to combat poaching of endangered species. So, as I thought, it is up to the agencies to establish the rules and means of enforcement. The part I got wrong is that there is, in fact, an Executive Order, but its scope is as a directive, not a specific set of rules.

Something that needs to be appreciated about that Executive Order is that it requires the agencies to do whatever possible to reduce the market for ivory. This is why it is so difficult to deal with even antique ivory. If ivory continues to be sought after and have increased value due to the ban, poachers will be encouraged to continue their efforts to black-market ivory. The strategy is to make the import, export and transport of ivory tough enough so the market will shrink. The hassle for musicians is collateral damage that will probably shake out over time, but in the meantime, there are ways to get appropriate documentation to transport your instrument, if you can brook the hassle and cost.

Re: Ivory and Insanity: If your instruments have ivory in them, leave them home…

As someone who has actually used CITES certificates to travel with an historic instrument, I can say it’s not that complicated.

I have an 19th century set of uilleann pipes that has loads of ivory on it. I have travelled with the set internationally on a couple of occasions and applied for the appropriate import and export certificates each time. No, it is not expensive, contrary to some of the suggestions I have read. Nor does it take a particularly long time to process applications. You won’t get a one week turn around (probably not even a one month turn around), but so what? For myself, I plan my year around the international music festivals I want to attend, so am rarely if ever in a ‘last minute’ situation regarding travel. CITES is just one more thing to do ahead of time, along with taking time off work, and booking flights and accommodations.

It also my experience that CITES certificates do not particularly complicate things at customs. I have found I end up knowing a lot more about the process than the customs agents, and have to explain to them what they need to do. I actually have enjoyed showing the set to the customs agents, who in the times I have travelled have always been very interested in the old instrument.

At the end of the day, I probably could have travelled quite easily without CITES. Which I’m sure is what these poor fellows were hoping. But for those of us who play historic instruments that contain ivory, it is a calculated risk to travel without the appropriate documents. I for one certainly wouldn’t take the 1 in 100 risk that my pipes would get taken off me because I don’t have the right paperwork.

Re: Ivory and Insanity: If your instruments have ivory in them, leave them home…

Hi callsion.

You won’t find a universal CITES application on the web, as the treaty is administered domestically by every government. So each has its own form. My experience is that is it done via the Department of the Environment, or something similar, as CITES is an international environmental law treaty. So google ‘CITES UK’ or ‘CITES Brazil’ as a start.

I would suggest avoiding the web as a single resource and call the relevant government departments directly. I found that incredibly easy. There’s no long waits on hold to talk about CITES. I called people on both ends, in my case UK and Canada. Maybe I’m unique but the people I talked to were really helpful. It also allowed me to ask questions that were unclear from the web pages. So my advice would be that it is actually important to talk on the phone to the relevant people. To them, moving antique ivory across borders is what they do. Very different than what you might get walking cold into a customs agent who has never dealth with it, and therefore might presume it illegal. I also got names and direct phone numbers in case there was an issue the border when I arrived.

I made sure to take through all the steps I needed to do, which to be honest weren’t clear from the websites. This was also helpful as the customs officers in general didn’t know how to deal with the documents. So when the time came it was all pretty routine. Though it would have been less so if I hadn’t done my homework.

As to provenance, that’s a tough one. I had my set authenticated in writing by an antique woodwind dealer in London, which was sufficient for CITES. But then again my pipes are 150+ years old. Dating mid 20th century ivory may indeed be more difficult.

CITES paperwork doesn’t need to be validated at the border, as the permit is issued by the government (be that be US, UK, Canada, etc). It is a government permit. And keep in mind you need one from the country you are leaving and one for the country you are entering. The validation of the instrument/ivory happens at the time the paperwork is issued, not when it is reviewed by the border officer. All the customs agent does is stamp the official paperwork that is already issued by the government to attest to the fact it is crossing a border.

Re: Ivory and Insanity: If your instruments have ivory in them, leave them home…

Hi Ailin,

Yes you absolutely need certificates for both countries. So for example, when I travel from the UK to Canada with my pipes, I need to stop at UK customs on the way out to show them both certificates, and have them stamp the UK issued one to show that the instrument has been legally exported. Then when arriving in Canada I need to show customs the stamped legal export cert from the UK, and have them stamp the Canadian issued one to prove legal import. When I return I have to do the whole thing again in reverse.

CITES is an international treaty but the administrative workings are the responsibility of each country to implement. There is no such thing as a universal passport in the system (although that would certainly make things much easier for musicians).
