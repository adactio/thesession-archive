Re: Sessions - good/bad/ world experience

A little over a year ago, I was invited to an almost weekly session in a private house. It was a great opportunity to play *with* people, as opposed to play *along to* people - and there was always good soup provided. I did feel, however, that these players regarded themselves more as *learners* than *players* - not that they weren’t good, just that they lacked the self-confidence to play in public, and in the absence of a ‘leader’ (which I am not), there was no-one to ‘drive’ the music. Consequently, the session always lacked a certain edge that one can find in a good pub session, regardless of how ‘good’ the players are.

Of course, this is more the fault of the people than the place. I have encountered the same problem in a few pub sessions - and I have been lucky enough to have some great private sessions as well (Usually, the reason for these taking place in private spaces is that all the pubs are closed, or that nobody can be bothered to go out to the pub - or everyone is just so eager to get the tunes out that they have to play there and then).

As I said in the previous thread on this subject, in my experience, a regular session, frequented by the same people each week, rarely has the magic of an impromptu session. How can turning up at the same pub (church hall, kitchen, wherever) each week with the intention of playing tunes - perhaps the same tunes - with the same people, compete with playing tunes together with people because you just happen to be in the same place at the same time with the same tunes and the same motivation? Having said that, I’m an old man of 32 and I crave regularity and familiarity like a man twice my age, so I understand the concept of a weekly session. But I’m also a rebellious teenager and sometimes need to do things that have never been done before.

Re: Sessions - good/bad/ world experience

I suppose a comparison of the local session I’m often referring to and the ones I have enjoyed visiting in Ireland might provide the answer you’re looking for, Larsheen. When I said “I only wish I had a pub with sessions like that in the city where I live” I’m including a lot about the sessions at the Cobblestone that would be impossible to duplicate here in SF, i.e. the way the Guinness tastes, the atmosphere, the frequency of amazing sessions, and the abundance of incredible players.

Here in SF we do the best with what we have. It’s an Irish pub, but it’s not in Ireland, (one give-away is the after taste in the Guinness,) and it’s populated by the local players who are very good but hard to compare to the numbers of great players I found on hand at the Cobblestone. But instead of complaining, I do enjoy our sessions for what they do have: nice folks, good musicians, and good friends. Also, (very important) a man who genuinely loves traditional music and owns the pub, and a fair amount of punters coming in to enjoy the music.

Currently we rotate who gets paid for being the evening’s host, and that has worked well to keep the sessions from becoming stagnated. For 10 years I was one of two or three regular hosts there every week, but now I enjoy having the option of going at all, and I enjoy being along for the ride at other people’s sessions when I do go… which is most of the time. I think the other musicians have enjoyed taking turns being the host and presenting their favorite tunes into the over-all mix. Also, everyone has developed their own style and it keeps it interesting. But as hosts no one is telling anyone what to play and how to play it; they just pick most of the tunes and invite others to choose tunes and such.

For the most part we all get along and there are many levels of musicians in attendance and feeling welcome. People who are learning are included and encouraged, but the session doesn’t cater to them exclusively. The session is for enjoying the music and the experience of getting together for a few tunes and a little fun.

Just a word now about the concept of paying for hosts:

I have seen volunteer sessions start up and be great for a while, but then fizzle out after the enthusiasm drops off. These sessions are usually initiated by a core group of charismatic folks who usually function as un-paid hosts. As soon as that core begins to break up or move on the life of the volunteer session is up in the air unless dedicated people make the effort to keep it going. The session in the pub I play at started out in the mid 70s as a volunteer session I believe. It was the likes of Joe Cooley and Kevin Keegan that kept the ball rolling then. After those two gentlemen passed, their powerful musical spirit and legacy carried the session for many years. By the time I moved to town the session was in its death throws and another group of local charismatic musicians had started a session just down the road. The owner of the pub was disappointed when he realized the session was expiring and decided to take action. He mistook my cohorts and myself for a group of charismatic local musicians and asked us to host a session in the pub. He offered to compensate us with a bit of money to encourage us to do that instead of going down the street to the really cool session. We obliged and began gathering for tunes in his pub. At first it would just be the three of us, but eventually people began to trickle in. When the really cool session down the street fizzled out, (it was also a paid-host session BTW) people came flooding in because we were the only session in the city at that stage. My point is that the owner, who as I mentioned, loves the music, wanted to be sure there would be a session in his pub… and paying the hosts seemed to do the trick.

Currently, he has asked me to help organize the shared rotating host thing, (since it was my idea in the first place,) and I can tell you now that if I had to ask people to promise to show up on a given night without any compensation to inspire them we’d have a good few nights without sessions. If people drove in from the places they come from only to find they were the only musician, or sitting with a one or two shy intermediate players, they might not bother to make the trip any other Sunday or Tuesday evening again.

So in other words; paying musicians to host sessions doesn’t mean it’s a “gig” and they become “leaders” that “tell you what to play and how to play it.” All it means is that a session with capable musicians is guaranteed. What could be wrong with that? I’m sure there are people who will find something to complain about, especially if they’re frustrated for one reason or another, but that’s just the way it works. If you don’t like it you can always just stay home and bounce the tunes off your kitchen wall instead I suppose, but I prefer to enjoy it for what it is -- getting together to share a few tunes and a few laughs at the pub.

Re: Sessions - good/bad/ world experience

Sessions become “private” or “closed” when, even though held in public, newcomers are shunned and ignored--in other words, when the players are unfriendly, and the events are not truly “open to people who want to join in.” This is folk music, and it would be nice if we set out to include folks in the playing of the music.
When I am a newcomer to a session, I don’t need to lead off a tune or set to be happy, but it would be nice to be able to play along with at least a set or two during an evening. That can be solved by someone who turns to the quiet newcomer, and says something as simple as “Is there a set you would like to play with us?”
On the other hand, I don’t expect everyone to drop everything, and cater to an outsider all night long.
I have seen extreme examples of both of these cases--the “session” that ignores folks that walk in with an instrument, even after they ask if they can join in, and the poor unfortunate outsider that wants you to drop everything to play “his” tunes, and expects you to play all the old music hall songs he got from a piano musicbook in the key of C, because he thinks that is what Irish music is, and without ever apparently having listened to a session, doesn’t understand why it doesn’t keep going in his direction after the first tune or two.
There has to be some compromising and accomodating on both sides of the issue, from both old-timers and newcomers. It can’t be reduced to a formal set of “rules” or “commandmants.” Like Mr. what?!!? says, it simply comes down to manners, common sense, etc.

Re: Sessions - good/bad/ world experience

A session becomes private when it actively excludes well-mannered, competent visiting musicians from sitting in.

Of course, that’s anyone’s perogative. But it rankles some folks for whom the term “session” connotes a more open, welcoming social event.

I’ve seen the term “session” applied to everything from genre-less free-for-alls to closed, paid band gigs (with a wide array of formats in between).

The key here, I think, is to recognize each “session” for what it is, compare it to what you hoped for when you walked in the door, and act accordingly. If it’s a closed, paid gig, there’s no point in getting in a snit over it. Likewise, if it’s a by-invite-only session, find out what it takes to get an invitation or go to the bar and enjoy being a punter. But don’t bother holding a grudge about it.

If the A-tier players in your town don’t welcome you into the fold, they must have their reasons. Could be they’ve already got what they need and can’t be arsed with distractions. Could be after 30 years they’ve exhausted their patience.

Sessions are often more selective with their own local pool of musicians than they are of people passing through. It’s easier to put up with someone who isn’t a “good fit” (musically or otherwise) when you know it’s a one-off--they’re leaving town and won’t be back anytime soon. A bad fit become a bigger concern, however, when the culprit lives nearby and will gamely show up every week.
