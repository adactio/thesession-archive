How to avoid getting bewildered?

I have been a Mandolin player for the past decade or so.

During this time, I have incorporated a number of tunes in tunes into my repertoire.

These have served me fairly well over the past up until now, but presently is the time for me to exapnd a little.

I have also added playing a little violin into the mix, and this combination of things, along with the desire to learn more tunes, is the current state of play.

However, when I set about to start learning something new, for the mandolin primarily, I get somewhat a feeling of dread(!) at the vast range of potential tunes out there.

I appreciate that this diversity of tunes is one of the fantastic things about the Irish tradition, but, nevertheless, I feel it in the pit of my stomach, and it can be a little bewildering to me.

Does anyone have any advice as to how to deal with these feelings, and to thereby maximise one’s learning experience on this fantastic site?

Thank you.

Re: How to avoid getting bewildered?

My advice(I am not a professional musician and “distinctly average” as the saying goes) is just not to worry about it too much.

You’ll never learn all the tunes or even all of those you would like to learn in a life time. Even the best players don’t know them all although the better ones can pick up a new/different tune very quickly and, sometimes, even after one or two hearings.

Just concentrate on learning those tunes you enjoy or think you might need for a particular situation, session, or concert/gig etc.

Unfortunately, one downside of expanding your repertoire too much is that many old favourites end up on the back burner or you get a bit rusty with them but they will come back.

I think everyone has a limit as to how many tunes they can play perfectly without reference or practice at any given time. I’m sure there are ways to expand this memory facility(You don’t really forget them as such) but this would be another discussion.

I’ve actually known composers of tunes who forget how they go at times. I remember one very known player who couldn’t remember his own tune during a concert. I “hummed” it to him during the interval in the bar and he played it perfectly in the second half. :-)

Ah! I see heallan! Thank you so much for that detailed and thorough response.

The best advice I can give you in that regard is to always, *always, try first. I’ve been playing music my entire life, and I can tell you from personal experience: For some of us there is always some discomfort or apprehension about learning something new. There are a lot of psychological things happening that I think may be too much to get into here in this thread. Though, that “anxiety” is a very real thing, and you’re definitely not alone in experiencing it. I still deal with it all the time, and I have to remind myself of my philosophies to push through that anxiety.

One of the fascinating and intriguing things about music is the very fact that there is always something new, which means there is always something to learn, which further means there is always something to make us feel uncomfortable or apprenhensive. So when I say “always try first”, what I mean by that is: When you find a tune that you like, ーbefore convincing yourself that it sounds too hard to learn, or before talking youself out of learning it for whatever reasonー try it first. Give yourself a chance. You would be surprised at what you’re capable of, or far along you’ve come, especially if you haven’t picked up anything new in a while.

Sometimes a tune just may be above your level, but all that means is you’ve discovered new skills to unlock and develop. You’re always the better for it after having tried first. Usually though, you’ll find it to be just challenging enough to show you a good time without putting you completely off of it. I hope this is more in line with what you’re looking for. Good luck in your learning adventures!

Re: How to avoid getting bewildered?

I think Jerone got at this a little bit, I have three questions: how much time do you spend listening to this music and how much time do you spend playing, and do you actually enjoy Irish music?

The latter is going to seem like a snarky question, but it’s not meant that way. If you’re learning these tunes because you like listening to and playing this music, then there’s some advice we can give that won’t make sense if you’re learning these tunes because you can get decent money playing them at bar gigs and weddings but you don’t particularly care for them. And either way is fine, honestly. There’s certainly no shame in trying to learn new things, whatever your motivation!

So, if you enjoy Irish music enough to listen to it as you go about your day, then the simplest thing I can suggest is to take note of what you’re listening to, and when you hear a tune that sticks with you, that is, a tune that you can play back in your head or sing bits of it, learn that tune. You don’t have to learn it by ear, but it’s a lot easier if at least some of the bits are stuck in your head, and having bits of it stuck in your head are good evidence that there’s at least something about the tune that grabs you. The other thing I’d suggest is that you spend time playing tunes that you know well enough to play off without visual assistance, and in particular that you spend time playing those tunes slowly and simply and trying to hear what’s in between the beats of them. Making a tune sound like music at a slow tempo is a surprisingly deep exercise, and I think it’s the key to playing tunes at tempo without feeling or sounding like you’re in a rush. But more than just making some of your tunes sound a bit better, what I’m hoping you’ll get from this is permission to take time with the tunes and see what they’ve got in them, and that this in turn will help with the anxiety you’ve talked about.

Might or might not work for you, but it’s worth trying.

Re: How to avoid getting bewildered?

My strategy for learning tunes depends on my objectives.

Most important to me is learning tunes just because I like them. If I find myself repeatedly remembering or humming a tune, I do what I can to learn it on my instrument(s), either aurally or by tracking it down on this site. I’ll occasionally introduce some of those to our local sessions.

When I’ve wanted to learn tunes that are played at a new-to-me session, I’ve noted down the tune names, or sometimes (with permission) recorded them, then tracked them down later. At some sessions, one or more members has even given me a list of tunes commonly played there.

To increase my repertoire of tunes that may be useful at lots of other sessions, I’ve tracked down lists of “most popular” tunes and made sure I knew the ones near the top. Here’s an example: https://thesession.org/tunes/popular Sure, sessions vary; but there’s a lot of commonality in the tunes. That strategy has paid off time and again for me.

Re: How to avoid getting bewildered?

Anxiety is part of being human, and playing music seems to have some unique triggers for people. At the very least, every Irish musician I know, including players you’ve heard of, has some underlying anxiety about the tunes that they don’t know. In fact, the more experienced I have become, that anxiety increases, because I start to feel like I should know EVERY tune, which isn’t possible.

Music is also fairly entwined with our sense of self, and our ego. It’s really common for someone learning to play music to feel anxiety when playing in public. We feel like we’re opening ourselves up to being judged by other people, and that can be a basis for performance anxiety. It’s pretty common for someone to give a constructive tip to a newer player, and have that interaction feel like a deep personal insult to the player. So music is more entrenched in our psyche that we might imagine.

In your case, it sounds like much of this struggle is within yourself. So I think it’s important for you to try to identify the specific triggers you’re experiencing and what emotions they are triggering. And then you can try to come up with tactics for dealing with them head on. I think this thread is a step in the right direction, for what it’s worth!

You have mentioned a few things specifically:

Dread at the vast range and diversity of tunes - Again, everybody feels that to one extent or another. Some strategies for dealing that might include understanding that you’re not alone, finding the joy in the tunes that you do know, and realizing that there is no destination in this journey. The journey IS the point. You won’t finally get to a point where you’ve conquered all of Irish music, and you can dust yourself off and say “that was fun… what’s next?”. Rather, the journey is a lifelong sojourn into self-discovery, sharing music with others, keeping your brain active, and making new friends. Those things are as true when you know 10 tunes as they are when you know 1000 tunes.

Discomfort in learning and practicing new tunes - It’s important to see if you can hone in on exactly what feelings are being triggered, and then maybe see if you can figure out why. Is it that you feel like a failure because you didn’t learn the tune well enough or fast enough? Is it that you are comparing your playing to recordings of professionals, and you feel inadequate? Some things that help in this regard might include realizing that tune learning (especially when learning by ear, which can be daunting in its own right if you’re not used to doing it) becomes exponentially easier the more that you do it, but the only way to get there is by doing it! You can also maybe focus on the joy you feel playing the tunes that you do know, so that you’re looking forward to the joy of playing the tune you’re currently learning, rather than focusing on the discomfort of the learning itself. And you should revel in any feeling of accomplishment, however small, you get from successfully learning a new tune!

Homesickness when traveling away from known things - This is maybe centered in self doubt. We all experience self doubt. Heck, I have recorded multiple albums, which is a really humbling experience. When I am working on mixing something I have recorded, it involves pretty intense scrutiny on my own playing, which is uncomfortable to say the least! By the time I’m done with an album, I find myself filled with self doubt, and generally believe I am not worthy of holding an instrument. Those feelings can be mitigated by the joy of the music. This brings me back to the journey of self discovery. For me, personally, I feel best when I feel like I am growing and learning, and any little accomplishment from learning a new tune to recording an album feels like progress in a positive direction.

So realizing that you’re not alone might help you in your journey, and you should lean into that. Irish music is inherently a social medium. Bring other people along on your journey! Find people to both learn and play tunes with, and you’ll likely make new lifelong friends! At the very least, you will have a support group (besides the one you’ve found on this website)!
