How do you influence the musical growth of a new session?

How does one influence the growth of a new session into more of a pure-drop direction? The host and the other strong players have very much an americanized view of sessions, and, frankly, it’s not my cup of tea. But like it or not, it’s the only session I can really attend. If I were a better player, I could influence things more, but I’m not. I do understand that I’m not the host, but I also think that most of our players are open to adapting a bit and finding common ground.

Also, I don’t want to PO the host or our other players, so please suggest tactful approaches/ideas/experiences.

So far, I have a few ideas:

practice to become a stronger player (obviously)
learn a louder instrument like banjo so others are forced to play to my style(only half joking here)
‘starter’ tune list with only/mainly Irish tunes
Facebook/website page
list of suggested listening material

↳ Posted by David Levine on July 8th, 2017.

Re: How do you influence the musical growth of a new session?

I’m familiar with the situation, especially if this is a mixed OldTime/Folk Song/Irish jam. Maybe the OP’s situation isn’t quite that drastic, but I’ve only seen the problem “solved” in the following ways:

1. Invite people interested in a more pure drop approach to a private house session, which either continues on a regular basis, or moves to a public venue.

2. Skip that first step if you have enough interested people, and start a new session at a public venue.

3. The venue owner decides they want a pure Irish session, and the focus of the session suddenly shifts to avoid losing the venue.

I’ve seen and/or participated in all of the above. I have never seen an existing session shifted in the way the OP is suggesting, in the absence of an outside force (like #3 above), and as long as most people who show up are content with the status quo.

Re: How do you influence the musical growth of a new session?

Carl, the short answer is that pure drop is traditional music as something organic, with deep roots, but open to interpretation. Not to hot or too cold. Music which is shared in communities but not confined to a single locality or region… Sorry, this is not a good short answer. Basically, in one context ‘pure drop’ is used in a way that it’s meaning is understood. And it this context the term does not have negative connotations.

On the other hand it has also been used to imply rather strong connotations (passion, direct connections with the past, ideal concepts of purity…). The connotations can be difficult to avoid. But they obviously come up. One which comes up was when Tony McMahon spoke at a Crossroads conference where he spoke about innovation in Irish traditional music. I think he mentioned pure drop (didn’t he host the TV show for awhile?). Anyway he spoke out about how passionate trad players are (fair play) but he also wrote off the Chieftains notable collaborations with musicians from outside traditions of Irish music.

Honestly, the only thing I can see working is that you play lots of Irish tunes when called on. youre not really in a position to impose your tastes on the other people, which I think you realise. If you have a few other people who want the session to be very Irish, you could enlist them to play mainly Irish stuff too. Maybe compliment people more when they play Irish stuff and less when they don’t, but even there you would need some subtlety to not come off badly.
In the long run, the only way to have a session that conforms to your tastes is find some people who have similar tastes and play with them.
In the meantime, play Irish tunes and see who else joins in or really likes them at this session. With a bit of luck you’ll find that lots of people are into or want to get into Irish tunes. With a bit less luck you’ll find one or two who may eventually constitute the core of a new session. At the worst you’ll realise that he people at this session just aren’t interested and you’ll be free to look for something else.

Re: How do you influence the musical growth of a new session?

Why don’t you just learn new tunes with the host? What if the host just doesn’t know that one of the players(you) has more of an Irish repertoire? I mean, have you all talked about the direction you all want the session to go? Maybe suggest having a formal meeting with the most committed players about what the session WILL and WON’T be? Since it is a new session and you all don’t have a consensus, why not make the move to have a consensus decided on? But, if you go this route, realize that if the other players don’t want pure drop, they’ll decide on something else.

When I go to a new session, I conform to the session. Over the first few months I go, I play my absolute favorite tunes to see who else knows them, and I also write down the tunes other people play that I happen to know. Never play the same tune twice until everyone has heard every single tune you know. That way, not only does the session learn who you are and your tastes, but you’ve also done your part by making the session more inclusive by understanding who knows what.

When I was first going to the session I go to now, I was disheartened that I didn’t know a lot of the tunes the host played and was worried that nobody knew “my tunes”(cause the tunes I learn are so personal given I play mostly private). Turns out, the host knows almost every single tune in my repertoire. I just don’t know all of his! Lol, so that means I have work to do learning new tunes, but I can still play my own tunes because everyone else knows them. And also, I’ve noticed that some of my peers have picked up on some of my favorites that they didn’t know before.

This probably isn’t stuff you want to hear, but you don’t have a lot of options if you’re not willing or capable or starting your own session. My final word: Talk to the host.

Re: How do you influence the musical growth of a new session?

* If there’s an ‘interval’ in your session, chat to someone you think is likely to be friendly/and/or interested in Irish tunes and see if you can find some tunes in common, which you can then strike up together.

* Or get there early & when the next person turns up, see if you can ‘practise tunes together’ (quietly) before the session really starts. That might spark off a response as more participants arrive. Don’t do that if you think it will annoy the host, though.

* Find out which Irish tunes are also played (maybe slightly differently) in American tradition & (having learned them) suggest them by name during that silence when everyone waits to see who’ll start the next tune.

* Have a word with the host and ask if s/he’ll give you the opportunity ‘next time’ to do a set of specifically Irish tunes.

* I think the advice given about writing down the tunes played and chasing them up afterwards is very good. My husband always writes down the tunes, and when he asks the name of the ones that have just finished, finds he’s doing a useful job for others. I learned how to play a lot of tunes that really aren’t my cup of tea, but I did it so that I could join in and eventually, I hope, earn the right to introduce tunes of my own choice.

* Good luck. I go to two pub sessions and each one has its own ‘social issues’ which seem to loom as large as the musical ones. Quite often I go home feeling ‘less than exhilarated’. As an older woman and a newcomer to ‘the scene’, I don’t really fit in - but hey, I just thole it! :)

Best wishes,
Mollie x
