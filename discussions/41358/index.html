Challenge Values 2: The Significance of Time.

I enjoyed the last “Challenge Values” discussion so much, I’ve been contemplating which concept I would like to go over next because challenge value isn’t a one-dimensional idea. The other night I was deciding which non-musical skill I would like to work on going into next year, as is a personal tradition. I decided on expanding my yoga abilities since yoga has been tremendously helpful since I began playing piano for a living, and sitting in a chair for 20+ hours a week. I wouldn’t be able to busk without it.

There is a website I discovered that offered some excellent looking online classes, and me being a millennial, you know I love my online classes. Signed up for a class called “Flystrong” that teaches Handstand Mastery. Being a full-blooded artist, I’ve always had a special appreciation and fascination for athletics, because goodness they just don’t click for me. I see the handstand as the pinnacle of upper body strength, control, and balance, so this is a bucket-list milestone to achieve. So, I’m watching the preview, and the coach mentions that “you can’t really learn a handstand in 20 mins a day”, which is about how long each class is. He goes on to recommend practicing for at least an hour a day. So I’m sitting here thinking, “An hour a day… that’s 30 hours a month… 30 hours… 30 hours… That’s… That’s a day! A day and a quarter!”, and then it hit me. Time. I want to talk about Time!

Think about any musical skill, method, repertoire, etc… that you’re dealing with. Could you imagine giving it a day and a quarter of your time? But not even a day and a quarter, what about 2 hours? 10 minutes a day, for 6 days a week, for 2 weeks. You’re investing minutes, minutes! -Into something that has a lifelong return. If I commit to this class and give the recommended due diligence of an hour a day, I’m literally only investing one day of my life into a skill I will enjoy for a lifetime. Isn’t that ridiculous? It’s crazy! But it’s great!

Take a simple skill, like triplets for example. You practice them, on every single finger, or in a set of your favorite tunes. And you just practice those triplets, for 10 minutes a day before starting your routine practice. A week later, you’re knocking them out. So you take it up a notch. Triplets over passing phrases. 10min a day, for another week. Boom. You got it. The time span may have been 2 weeks, but your time investment was just over 2 hours. And now, they aren’t even hard. And you’re wondering why you ever let them bother you. 2 hours. Triplets for the rest of your life. Or what about your repertoire? You want to learn more slip-jigs, so you pick 7, and you commit to them for a week. An hour a day. It would be an intense practice, but by the time that week is over, what you got?

This may all be obvious and apparent to all of you, but for someone like me… You don’t understand the depth of my former apathy. Life was like a dream and it didn’t matter which way the wind blew. If it wasn’t too hot, I didn’t care. I didn’t value time. I thought I did, but I didn’t. And I’m still working on that part of me. But it freaks me out to think that such a small investment has such a huge return. And I’ve known this. I think it’s one of those things we eventually come to realize. But I haven’t taken it seriously. I’m glad I’m realizing it now.

I feel that making that initial time, and keeping that initial consistency, is especially important when learning a new skill, or working on a weakness. Because you’re never the same afterward. That accumulative “day” changes you. I mean, it’s been a month, what else would you expect but to have made some progress? Or if it’s a simple, repetitive skill, that hour. Yea, it’s been 6 days, but by the time that hour is over, you’ve done it 100 - 1,000 times, hopefully perfectly. And a part of you will always have that. The question is, will you challenge yourself, and invest a week… 2 weeks, a month? Finish that method book you picked up? Learn your favorite tunes from that awesome album you’ve been listening to for months? Level-Up your sight reading/ear training? Don’t forget to enjoy yourself ;-)

Steve T

“Does it really? How? Seems like a case of magical thinking to me that, or perhaps just a badly expressed nonsense.”

I’m glad you asked. This is one of the greater points a “Challenge Values”. Getting a greater return than the initial investment. One example I used up top was triplets. I’ll expand on that example.

Learning how to do triplets isn’t the easiest thing to do, but once you sit down and learn them, you have them. It may take quite a while to master them in tune use, but learning the movement, in general, is what’s important, as it’s the movement you need to even be able to practice them in tunes. It’s a simple movement, that happens in less than a second, which means that at a tempo of 60bpm, you could practice 60 triplets a minute. If you play fiddle, and you do an exercise that utilizes the triplet on all fingers and all strings, you can spread those triplets over all fingers and all strings. And then just practice the movement. At a tempo of 60bpm, you could practice 3,600 triplets in one hour. As you increase the bpm, you can do even more. But who is going to sit there and do triplet exercises for an hour when you could be practicing tunes? And we all know it’s more beneficial to rest after doing hard exercises, instead of stacking exercise on top of exercise. So why not do them for 10min a day, for a week, before your routine practice, at least to learn the initial movement?

Now, of course, this doesn’t apply to everything. Like my handstand pushup, some things require more than an accumulative hour of due diligence. Some things require an accumulative day of due diligence. I’m of the mind to believe a few tunes would fall in that category. But a lot of tunes are going to only need a few minutes, or an hour, to get them under your fingers, which is the initial investment. If you only learn your favorite tunes or the tunes that are quite close, a few minutes of work learning them does indeed outweigh the years of enjoyment you’ll get out of playing them in sessions(especially if they are home session tunes). Call it nonsense if you like. I’ll call it magic ;-)

For me finding value in the time I spend learning new skills and honing what I know is about consistency, sustaining or maintaining my focus, interest, drive, comfort and pacing myself. The last bit (pacing myself) is especially important for me. Basically it involves not pushing too hard or practicing too long on the new stuff and building up my stamina over time; not trying to do it all at once. I think I do this to give myself a musical or conceptual grounding (or foundation). If that makes sense.

Practice is important for me. But the time spent in practice isn’t the sum of my learning anything new or making improvement in my playing. It’s possible to overpractice. It’s easy to push so hard that I don’t retain what I’m trying to learn. My mind and body need time for rest, nourishment, sleep, changes of scenery, etc.
All of that seems separate from time spent practicing though I find one needs the other.

At the risk of being completely embarrassed, if i’m wrong, I’m going to tell you about my experience of how I developed style as a piano player.

I didn’t always love piano, or fiddle, or music at all. I was fascinated that I could learn music by ear, and that fascination drove me to cultivate the skill of learning by ear. Because I didn’t have a particular musical interest, I learned whatever I liked, which, at the time turned out to be a lot of Hip-Hop/R&B/Pop etc. Throughout the 11 years that I took private lessons, my piano teachers exposed me to different styles of music. If my piano teachers didn’t do anything, they sure as hell made sure I heard every single piece of music they thought I would find interest in. It wasn’t until I moved away from home, and was separated from piano, that I began to realize that I really did enjoy music.

This time was probably the most influential 6 months of my life. During this time, one of my school teachers would play an album during class assignments and test. On that album, is where I found the person that I wanted to sound like on the piano. My first influence. I also made one of the biggest musical mistakes I have ever made. Not citing the source. I never asked my teacher what that music was she played on her computer, before moving back home. For the next 3 years, I would be plagued by memories of voices, melodies, and harmonies that I couldn’t find. My first lost music. But I was also a pretty adept ear-learner by this time and fortunately, after being fed-up with my plight, I was able to sit down and pick out some chords and melodies I remembered from the album.

I asked everyone in my musical life if they recognized the music I was playing. Friends in middle-school choir, friends at piano lessons, and eventually my piano teacher. My piano teacher did not recognize the music, but she thought it sounded familiar and shared some music with me she thought might fit the bill. The artist’s name was “Enya”, and I loved her music. And, a gift from the intuition, I began studying her music, specifically, because my intuition understood that that is what I wanted. This is the long version of how I realized what I wanted out of music. Since we’re here, i’ll go ahead and give you the extended version…

…All of this happened in the early 2000’s. Before iTunes. Before Youtube. Before 90% of the world’s music was uploaded to online servers and being sold in online stores. When we bought CD’s from record stores and music shops. I looked in every local music shop in town to see if I could find more of Enya’s music. (Judging from her iTunes discography, she had 5 albums out at the time.). There was one music shop that sold music books, if i’m not mistaken it was called “SamGoody”, and they had her “A Day Without Rain” music book. But I was a teenager and didn’t have any money, so I couldn’t buy it. It took quite a while, but eventually, I got to listen to some of the music from the album by means of a peer-2-peer network. Low and behold, it was the album my 6th-grade teacher played in history class. I think I cried for like a week, that entire time is a blur for me. I also think I ended up receiving the music book as a Christmas gift. Over 10 years and plenty of lost books later, I still have it.

As much as I adore Enya, and her piano playing, eventually I wanted more. When I was young and wet behind the ears, that style, called “New Age”, was the epitome of what I wanted. But after I had learned all there was to learn, I began seeking a 2nd influence. But this time around, iTunes and YouTube were both becoming very popular. Because I didn’t want to have another “lost music” experience, I began digitally collecting all the music I had heard from movies, video games, television shows, and the like. But it was at piano practice that I discovered my next piano influence.

At the end of one of my lessons, another student was playing one of the most emotionally evocative piano pieces I had ever heard in my life. I was not going to make the same mistake I made last time, and two weeks later when I saw her again, I asked her what she was playing at the other lesson. This is how I discovered Yiruma. He was much different from Enya. He was a Korean solo pianist. I knew no modern solo-pianists, besides Enya, and you wouldn’t really consider her a “solo-pianist”. Even though I cultivated influence from only less than 40% of her work, that <%40 made up over 50% of my desired style as a pianist. From Yiruma, I would cultivate much more influence. I ended up cultivating influence from over 60% of Yiruma’s work, and am still working on his repertoire to this day. For me, his music expanded on the influence I got from Enya’s piano work.

From this point, I was on a roll. Around this time, I learned about “Intervals”, one of two of the most influential music lessons of my life. As my piano teacher said, “It’s not accurate to think of intervals as the distance between two notes. It would be more accurate to say that intervals are the ”relationship“ between two notes.”. I also took a music theory class in high school where the band director gave an excellent lesson on intervals. From there, I learned about the very nature of music itself. The building and the releasing of tension, which was an equally influential lesson. And now we are near the end of my long tale, and the greater point of all of this obsessive writing…

I wanted to play music that told a story without words. I wanted to play music that set a mood. I wanted to play music that had an apparent and clear intent.

And then it was clear. All of my favorite music, across all genres, was Ballad. Narrative. Story. And that’s what I wanted. But I didn’t want to just tell a story. I wanted to be able to tell any story. “I’m sad, and this is why…”, “I’m angry, and this is why…”, “This is what it was like to watch the Sunrise today…”, “I met a woman, and she was beautiful…”, “I’m going back home…”, “I’m dying inside…”, “I’m afraid of what could happen in a few moments…”, “It’s peaceful at night…”, “A storm is coming from the north…”, “We were best friends…”, “We have to win this battle…”, “We’re getting married…”, “Dancing is fun…”, “I got a raise at work today…” etc. etc. etc.

So for the last 8 years, I’ve been working on learning how to use every single note in every single key signature, to make a musical statement. To say something. This idea was another gift of the intuition. Expanding my aural vocabulary, so that I am not limited by lack of understanding. It’s been like learning another language. And even though all of those private lessons, the classes, the workshops, the retreats, ultimately helped me get the tools I need to move forward in that regard, I wasn’t able to truly utilize all of that knowledge until I did find my direction. Now, 8 years later, I can say that I have achieved that goal to a great extent.

Even though I still study pianists and cultivate influence from them, now, I also cultivate influence from anything that has challenge value. (Wow…. I didn’t expect to bring it back around like that…). Some readers may think this was unnecessary, and way too much information, but I wrote all of this because it took me 12 years. Twelve. For me to even develop a goal. This is why I am so passionate about this. If I can help someone else find an ultimate goal, and shorten their “grind-clock” for them then so be it. Because working without a true goal is “grinding”, and there is no challenge value at all in grinding. And even though those 12 years weren’t wasted, these last 8 years have been invaluable in my growth and development as a pianist. Because, those twelve years, I didn’t play piano. I played songs, and tunes, and pieces. Not piano. It was my high school choir director who helped me realize that, and from that point forward is when I got my direction in the next steps to take…

This has been very helpful to me. My conclusion is that when playing alone I have two separate modes of operation.

The first is the only one I started out with when I decided to “do the hours”. I picked up the instrument and just played. (I see now - but didn’t at the time - this was to get over a bereavement). The other state is that of the self-improver, the autodidact. You start the time by seeing in yourself a person who is fluffing half the notes in “The Mason’s Apron” and you split the tune up and play it and play it till at the end of the session you see yourself as a person who is only fluffing a third of the notes in “The Mason’s Apron”. That is work and it is dull, needs a lot of motivation and is only justified by the (tiny) sense of achievement at the end and the long-term (tiny) improvement to your playing. You can’t concentrate on it for long - 20 minutes maybe? - before diminishing returns set in. It is the sort of practice people seem to expect of you. If you never do it then you feel slightly guilty.

The second kind of practice interferes with the first kind because it leaves you with intrusive thoughts like: “What on earth am I doing? This is not useful. This will never make me into Aly Bain!” - and these thoughts interfere with the flow or meditative state or whatever it is - but it is the playing for the sake of it - which is the the recreation in the real sense that it creates you anew.
