I’m not sure bluegrass style is a very good choice for Irish sessions. If you plan to play chords/arpeggios as backup and can negotiate all the nesessary chords out of G tuning, maybe it will work. I’ve never heard it done but I’m sure someone’s tried it. Maybe the Scruggs-Frankie Gavin duet on Tim O’Brien’s album the Crossing is something like what you’re after. Tenor banjos are what I’ve almost always heard playing Irish or Scottish music. They’re an entirely different instrument from 5-string. Steve

Re: 5-string banjo

Insofar as I know, banjos almost never chord behind the tunes, they’re a melody instrument in ITM.

Silver Spear, Jackie Coleman’s, Sally Gardens, Maid Behind the Bar, Humors of Tulla, The Traveler -- there’s any number of tunes that are appropriate for a beginner. Look for tunes that have phrases that repeat themselves within each part, you’ll learn ’em faster.

Going down and talking to the other players first is an excellent idea, and they’ll be able to tell you which sessions standards are standard to their session. And whether the thought of a five string banjo makes them break out in hives. :)

Have fun, George!

Re: 5-string banjo

as a long time player of all kinds of banjos, i’d agree with ptarmigan - if you want to play ITM, get a tenor banjo and learn to play it. whilst it is quite possible to learn to play SOME irish tunes on the 5-string (using a 3-finger style similar to that used by the classical 5-string players), it’s real hard work and they never seem to come out sounding quite the way you would like them to - although they can be very pleasant. in my experience, it’s also much harder work figuring out the right picking patterns and finger positions for the 5-string than it would be to learn tenor banjo from scratch. the “single line melody” style favoured by most irish banjo players is quite easy to pick up - just learn your scales and the fingerboard and listen to lots of good irish banjo playing! good luck.

Re: 5-string banjo

One thing about old time 5-string banjo is that the 5th string is used. It’s a short string only going about 2/3 of the way down the neck. Sometimes it’s called the thumb string and it’s always played with the thumb. Often this string is used as a sort of drone. It’s not a drone actually because it’s not constantly being sounded but it sort of serves the same function. More modern “melodic” styles tend not to use the 5th string in this capacity as much. There are big differences in 5-string styles. There are so-called downpicking styles where the back of the fingernail of the index or middle finger is used in conjunction with the thumb. There are also various finger picking styles-classical, bluegrass (or Scruggs) etc. It’s a complicated business, really.

Re: 5-string banjo

I played 5-string banjo in bluegrass bands and gradually shifted over to Irish. Figured out a bunch of tunes in a melodic style before I’d ever heard of Bill Keith and blindly plowing a path that Tony Trishka was also working out at the time, unbeknownst to me--all way before Bela Fleck hit the scene. And after working out up tempo straight melodic versions of Flowers of Edinburgh, Drowsy Maggie, Rights of Man, Off to California, etc., I finally ditched the banjo and took up fiddle.

So my opinion? Yes, you can play ITM on 5-string, but it takes a fairly high level of expertise and even then doesn’t do the music (or the instrument) justice. Keep playing banjo if you want, but if you really want to focus on ITM, learn a more appropriate instrument (tenor banjo or whistle, say) as well. It took two decades, but now I can play ITM on fiddle and (if the spirit moves me, which it does about twice a year) bluegrass on banjo.

Look at it from the flip side--how many wooden flutes have you ever heard in a bluegrass band?

Re: 5-string banjo

to answer zina’s question - the overriding characteristic (in my view) of the 5-string banjo is that it is tuned to a chord of some sort. this means that the open strings resonate in sympathy with the strings that are actually being struck creating that lovely sound which was so aptly described by caoimghgin. whilst you can play ITM using such an instrument, the actual sound of the tunes is modified by the extra resonating strings (a bit like trying to play ITM on a hardanger fiddle). it’s nice but it isn’t really the way i would want ITM to sound in a session context.

i don’t think that the extra string length makes that much difference - as far as i recall, the fine album recorded by kevin griffin (down in doolin) was played on an old plectrum banjo ( a long neck 4 string) which was tuned an octave below a fiddle - it sounds great (though i don’t know how he managed to move his hand around such a long fingerboard that fast!)

there is also the problem of key changes - when you change key on a 5-string, you either play the notes on the tuning you have and risk the discord of open strings resonating odd notes or you re-tune the instrument to a more appropriate chord. either way, there are difficulties.

in passing, it’s my belief that the tenor banjo was deliberately invented as a melody instrument at the time of the first dance crazes (“ragtime” & “tango” c.1912 - 1914) so that out-of work violin & viola players from the redundant “palm court” trios could get a job in the “ragtime” & “tango” bands. it appears to me from the many early instruments that have passed through my workshop that lots of people went to their local banjo maker at about the same time and asked for an instrument that they could play without having to re-learn their whole musical style i.e. NOT a 5-string! it was only later that the longer necked version appeared that was the staple rhythm instrument of the 1920’s dance band - i.e. a chord playing instrument. in this way, it seems to me that ITM is probably just as valid and appropriate a use of the tenor banjo as any other (despite the views of the jazz purists!). perhaps this also expalins why most fiddle players can simply pick up a tenor banjo and play it better than i do!

Re: 5-string banjo

After reading the comments here and seeing Tom Hanways name mentioned I just had to tell him about some of your views. I live in New york and have heard Tom play. He hosts a ITM session at Baggotts on west 3rd on monday nites. He is a very fine player and has written a book called “Complete Book of Irish and Celtic 5-String Banjo” publishe by Mel Bay
I emailed Tom and this is what he had to say
"Tom Hanway here. For many years I have hosted sessions in NYC. I know
how badly the 5-string banjo can sound in Irish and Celtic music, but I
believe that it can be used in a musical manner consistent with
prevailing Irish and Celtic music standards, which are exceedingly high
and difficult to achieve -- on any instrument.

Talent is born, but it still has to be made (to practice). I think it
was Sharon Shannon who said: "Good musicians make a style of music
sound good, and bad musicians make a style of music sound bad." The
same can be said about instruments, including the 5-string (or even the
hurdy-gurdy): Decent players (in a style) make an instrument sound
decent, and rough players make an instrument sound rough -- in any and
every style. This is especially true for 5-string banjo, and just
because a person owns a 5-string and a set of fingerpicks, doesn’t mean
that he or she can play bluegrass banjo like Earl Scruggs, which is the
standard to which all serious 5-string pickers must.

The 5-string banjo is like any other instrument -- it’s just another
instrument that serious and eager musicians can get there hands on -- no
better or worse than so-called traditional or non-traditional
instruments (in any style of music). Playing tunes on the 5-string
banjo is very serious business, and it’s very hard to learn how to do
this. One first has to have a real appreciation for Irish trad just to
get into the mindset for playing tunes at a session or in concert. I am
sincere in my belief that the 5-string can be used judiciously in Irish
and Celtic styles, and a repertoire specific to 5-string banjo needs to
be explored and developed. I’ve been working on this for ten years
now, and I’m still searching for tunes - so many tunes, so little time….
