Road Maps for Progress.

I once read on an old Session thread that one needs to have played the fiddle for five years before one is any good at all. Is that true, and does it relate to other instruments too?

I have been playing for two years (since returning; I played at school), and I am certainly better than at first - more fluent bowing, can cross strings, shift positions, do ornaments. But I still can’t play fast enough for dancing. I also feel that I have hit a plateau, which I hope to improve with Matt Cranitch’s book as guide.

What is your experience of the road map of learning? When did you start to get noticeably better? Did you hit a plateau & find a way out of it to go on climbing? When could you play fast enough, or pick up tunes more efficiently? Do you have any tips to help us newbies on our way? Did you have a ‘break-through’ moment?
Thanks in advance for any replies. It is always so helpful to learn from other people’s experiences.

Re: Road Maps for Progress.

I think there are strong pros and cons to taking up an instrument later in life (although you do have the advantage of having done it before, however long ago).

The major ‘pro’ is that you will be motivated. As a kid, sometimes it’s just too boring to have to do ones practice, but the older you get the more you realise the benefits.

The major ‘con’ is that progress will be slow. Probably much slower than for kids. There’s the mental stuff for a start - we just do learn slower later in life, in my opinion. But there’s also physical stuff as well - aching joints, stiff fingers etc

I am about to be 57. I took up the flute for the first time 5 years ago. At times it’s been amazingly frustrating, and I’ve thought that I’m not making progress at all. But I *have* made progress. I think the flip side of progress being slower as we get older is that it’s steadier - it’s happening all the time, and it’s solid progress.

To me, five years of decent practice sounds about right to be able to do anything on any of the major trad instruments (maybe less for whistle for various reasons). Once you’ve got those five years of hard work under your belt, you can start to learn to play properly. :-)

(I mean the above as encouragement rather than the opposite - hope that’s clear.)

“What is your experience of the road map of learning? When did you start to get noticeably better?”

I play fiddle as a second instrument, but I’ve been a performer all of my life so I’ve always been use performing(and sometimes even practicing) in public. So for the half-part I judged my progress by the reactions I would get from peers and strangers.

Stranger reactions:

“Learning fiddle huh? That’s nice. Keep it up…”

“Not bad but I know a fiddle teacher if you need one…”

“How long have you been playing? Ah ok, I knew it couldn’t have been for long.”

“You’ve been playing for how long?Nice, Keep up the good work!”

“Wow, you sound good!”

“That was beautiful.”

Peer reactions:

“Eww! What was THAT noise?”

“Dude, don’t bring that thing!”

“You may need to stick to piano, lol, joking, I’m joking!”

“It’s easy to tell you’re a beginner, but you’re moving along ok!”

“You sound better than the last time I heard you. That was what? A couple months ago right?”

“Hey man, you’re getting pretty good, bring it over when you come visit!”

“Dude, you sound better every time I see you and I see you every week! Good job bro!”

*Peer to another peer* “Dude! Listen to this guy play! He’s amazing!”

I got these reactions over a course of three years. So that’s how I could tell I made progress with fiddle for the half-part. The other half-part, I keep a little mental journal of the things I’ve learned. I assess myself every year by going over what I learned the previous year. Something I started doing with piano as a personal and private tradition for years.

Re: Road Maps for Progress.

I think everybody gets to a stage where you feel you’re getting nowhere or worse - going backwards. One of my earliest milestones was hearing a track of someone (who shall remain nameless) playing, and thinking ‘I’ll be happy if I can play as good as that.’ Now when I hear the same recording, I think, ‘That’s terrible -- I play it better than that.’ although, strangely, I don’t feel as though I’ve improved that much.
Also, I might be playing a tune and thinking. ‘this sounds terrible,’ but recall that I used to play it five or ten years ago in front of an audience, thinking then that it sounded OK.
The plateau effect is familiar to us all. I think what happens is that as you are improving, so you are becoming much more aware, more critical, and you notice things that before would have passed you by. It is this constant refining, narrowing down the areas that need work or that you think are ‘wrong’, that makes us think we are getting nowhere, cancels out the steady increase in ability by raising our critical faculties at the same time. Like Mandelbrot sets, the more you go into it, the more there is to see.
This is why I recommend recording yourself at various stages, to give yourself milestones to mark your progress. It’s rather like taking your photograph once a year. When you look in the mirror, nothing much changes; but those school photographs…
Milestone events -- tunes (there was a thread a while ago about the same topic) or occasions, things that no longer cause problems, are one way to mark our improvement. Suddenly forgetting about the instrument and just thinking about the sound -- feeling as though the tune is playing itself (I remember one time playing in the pub, and thinking I could just slide out from under the pipes and go to the bar, and the pipes would just keep on playing -- very strange). Playing a tune that you once gave up for impossible, sliding easily over passages you once found difficult -- all these things offer encouragement when you realise they have happened.
Something to bear in mind -- people listening do not hear the mistakes. Keep going, and one day you’ll get through a tune and think, ‘hey, that was all right.’

Re: Road Maps for Progress.

Mollie, my teacher had a method for ‘clawing back time’. What he meant by that was concentrating on moving forward as quickly as possible, without wasting time on any unnecessary elements of learning the instrument.

If you say your bowing is fluent, you can cross strings, shift positions, do ornaments, but not play up to sped, then maybe (as Michele said) you need to tighten up your bowing a bit (shorter strokes as the tempo increases).

There are a few other things that would help too, and none of them involve playing tunes. I won’t bore everyone with the details here, but if you contact me I could give you a heads-up on them.

Re: Road Maps for Progress.

Mollie,

My experience with the fiddle is about 4 years and 11oo hours per year with less than 5 missed days total.

Can you define “year” in terms of playing time per day and number of days per week. And of course, everyone advances at varying speed according to quality of practice, content of practice, ability to play be ear and other factors. I think that hour(s) per day and days per week over a year is the more important variable than “years” played. I am not trying to open the 10K hours can of worms again but daily, disciplined, and quantity of play is
is a critical factor in progress. Some will say quantity of time is irrelevant and some will tell relate that Martin Hays won the national contest by playing a little before breakfast (some say a little after breakfast. Since hearing that I have personally wondered if that’s why his music sounds so much like Jazz - tonally beautiful and crystal clear but we all have our own ideas of what ITM is and isn’t. Mine is Michael Gorman and Tommy Peoples.

As far a break throughs, I learned from this site to learn the tunes/airs/songs from the written page as quickly as possible playing it slowly without mistakes (not needing the written notation at all is even better if you are a savant and you can do it all by ear). Then leave the “cheat sheets” behind and play along with a recording and a slow down program. Playing along with a good recording slowly early on (Matt Cranitch’s book with CD) once you are liberated from the written page will help insure you are getting the “swing” or perhaps the Irish accent.

As an example of much wasted effort on my part, I persisted much too long trying to playing the tune slowly and perfectly before playing at speed. I never realized that it was in my capacity to play at full speed soon after learning the tune and and then play along with the recording. The first few run through a speed may make it seem you have to go back and play it slowly. But then something else takes over as begin to listen to the tune on the recording - mistakes drop away and the swing is right and then you are just playing along without effort or thought.

That has been the closest to a break through I’ve experienced. It seems that all along I had been “thinking my way” through the tune but when playing along at full speed something else is going along and I am not thinking at all. It seems I am playing on auto-pilot. Kind of like an out of body experience watching the fingers and bow going all by themselves - the time baling by unaware to you. I think that is what Gobby means by being in the zone. I haven’t experience this playing along solo yet - without a recording but I suspect that will happen.

There is trouble and much time wasted in misdirected efforts to which I can well attest. A good and proven teacher of ITM can direct and decrease your wasted effort. I made the mistake of thinking it would be helpful to go down some pathways advocated by classical violin world. Reading these pages and an excellent teacher put me on the right track. Truth told though, there is a lot of contradicting advise here and it is tough for a beginner to know where to place trust. But a little time visiting here you should be able to tell who is close to the Truth - who knows and who still remembers what it was like to be at your point on the curve.

As far as beginning later in life, do it! Go down that path and pity anyone who tells you different.

On the issue of playing at dancing speed, my breakthrough came by focusing on playing one tune fast. In my case it was Mason’s Apron, which was played at (contra)dance speed at one of my sessions. On my banjo I felt like I was running in waist-deep water trying to keep up with runners on a race track. But I worked at getting that tune up to speed in order to play with the session. The effort did wonders for my general technique. It “balanced” my left hand, with much better position, shorter and quicker movements, etc. Since then, I can play almost any tune as fast as anyone would want to. That said, I really prefer a modest pace at sessions. It lets the music come through and one can listen to what the others are playing.

Re: Road Maps for Progress.

Mollie, think of plateaus as your friend. To my mind they are an unavoidable feature of the road or path - perhaps an essential one. You get stuck in the doldrums for a while and then something or other will happen, out of the blue, some revelation or insight, or the sudden ability to do something you couldn’t do before, and take you up to a slightly higher level. It is a very good thing if this process goes on for many years and indeed never stops, even if it will slow down.

In case it helps, I took up the fiddle in my early 20s after playing classical at school. After 5 very intense years I thought I was pretty good thank you and played in some decent bands before I realized that whatever anyone else thought I wasn’t actually very good. In the next 5 years I worked hard and did get better but I would describe them as years of constant low-level dissatisfaction with my playing. If there hadn’t been the dissatisfaction I don’t think I would have continued working. Anyway at about the 10-year mark I suddenly relaxed somewhat. I didn’t become complacent or stop working but I was happy to accept where I was and wasn’t ashamed to play in front of anybody, and I mean anybody, without worrying about what they would think. It was a satisfying moment but really it was just another plateau, albeit one with a nicer view.

Happily ever after? Not exactly. A few years later I developed focal dystonia in my bowing arm. When I realized it wasn’t going away I had to give up the fiddle (copious wailing and gnashing of teeth) and turned to other instruments (whistle and later box). Back to square one and the plateau game! ;-) So take heart and enjoy the journey, with dissatisfaction as your constant and necessary companion, and be grateful for the music you can make as long as your body lets you keep on tramping on the road.
