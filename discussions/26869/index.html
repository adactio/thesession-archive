Achille’s heel?

In all good intention, I’ve just taken delivery of Charlie Lennon’s DVD fiddle tutor, which seemed to have a decent write-up wherever I could see. I will be collecting a fiddle to try out tomorrow, and I thought that at very least I might pick up a few tips for the mandolin.

Well, I’ve watched around a third of it – and what a disappointment! I’m sure the man himself means well, but if that’s a fiddle tutor, I’m a monkey’s uncle. Maybe as a professional educator, I’m expecting too much, but I’m darned if I can see how anyone who can’t already play would be able to pick it up from this.

We start off with a corny section on holding the fiddle, where the supposedly common errors are so daft as to be clearly contrived; nothing on what I would expect to be the real likely problems (I have held and played a fiddle before, not that you would have wanted to hear it…)

We then get one quick glimpse of how to hold the bow, and then straight into treble-bowing. Not that we do this slowly so we can see how it’s done, more a case of ‘here’s what you do’, cue clip (which is going to be a pig to re-run with fiddle in hand, as the footage is not broken into easily repeatable chunks), followed by a short performance of Mr. Lennon in full flight. To be fair, there is a little explanation of *why* you need it, but I am almost none the wiser how to attempt this when I finally pick up the fiddle again. From what I can see, the rest of the DVD continues in the same vein.

O.K. so folk music is supposedly the music of the untutored classes, but if the ‘teaching’ involved basically goes ‘copy this’, with no attempt to break down the move, how is anyone supposed to learn? Is this widespread? I almost get the impression that Charlie has never really stopped to work out what he’s doing for himself in the first place. His explanation on video of how he himself learned triple-bowing amounts to “it just came to me one day”. Charlie Lennon is supposedly a master-player, but he’s certainly not a master-teacher, if this is anything to go on - limitations of the medium accepted.

My only other forray into such DVD’s was the MadforTrad bouzouki tutor, which was actually little better. Just filming someone doing something and saying ‘copy this’ is *not* teaching - for a start, you need to understand *how* and *why* to do it.

I made the comment the other day that many of the niceties regularly discussed on the Mustard Board too, are frequently mentioned but rarely explained. Well, this isn’t necessarily a learning-forum of course, but time and again I seem to get back to the problem of people, even good players, simply not being able to explain what they are doing. The only alternative is for people to work it out for themselves, which is hardly helpful. I have also come across more than one individual in my time who styled themselves a folk music teacher but whose level of musical skill/knowledge, let alone teaching skill, was doubtful to say the least.

I’m not saying that we need a full classical 25-year day-by-day programme with eight grades and a conservatoire to follow, but if this is the closest that ITM has got to teaching the world how to do it, then it certainly does the music no favours and is a major achille’s heel of the art.

Re: Achille’s heel?

Fair enough but it’s a very small part of the whole picture. Most older musicians I have ever spoken to worked things out for themselves with the odd tip here and there from an odler player.

You’re probably familiar with Junior Crehan’s description how he as a young man stood long nights behind Scully Casey playing at house dances, trying to figure out and memorise bowing patterns. And stories like that are common.

I am aware of some fiddlers who taught very strict bowing patterns, Paddy Killoran insisted on that and Patrick Kelly got his bowing patterns straight from his father.

By and large though I think I am right saying there was no tradition of formal teaching. When teaching was done, tunes were taught and technique was picked up much by imitation.

I am not aware of piping schools, outside Leo Rowsome’s long standing position at the Municipal School of Music.

Re: Achille’s heel?

ormepipes, it’s not easy, but it is simple. Even fiddle, which in my mind ranks close to pipes in terms of being a Rube Goldberg contraption for making music.

I think there are several problems with a lot of the trad teaching currently available. (Of course exceptions to the following do exist, but they’re too rare.)

First, the market favors big-name players, whether or not they have much experience or ability in teaching. It’s a shame more people aren’t willing to learn from great teachers who also happen to be able to play to a decent or even high standard.

Second, the culture of commercial recordings and concerts tends to steer instruction toward a performing-oriented approach to playing. Maybe I’m wrong, but it seems most people who want to play this music are more likely to end up playing in sessions, not in recording studios or concert halls. Session playing doesn’t have to sink to the lowest common denominator, and there’s plenty of room for personal expression. But all too often I hear instruction that emphasizes personal expression over a solid grasp of the music and the tradition first.

Third, there’s a pervasive myth about this music that the finer points cannot be explained or taught--“you just have to feel it.” I’ll agree that a player has to develop his or her own feel for the music, but I also know that a good teacher can go a long ways to explaining and demonstrating most if not all of the techniques and concepts and even the feel that go into playing this music well. With good guidance, it’s easier for a learner to find the feel.

My two cents.
