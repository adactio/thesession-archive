Re: Diatonic harmonica question

Some people really like the low D, I find the standard D is much more suitable for my tastes. It’s up to you. To my ear, the lower reeds have a “mushier” sound, and I like the snap of the standard instrument. Also, the low reeds are a lot harder to hear in a session, while a standard D sings out a lot more. I don’t use the Paddy tuning, I treat it like a whistle: it goes from D to b, and tunes that go below the staff get rejiggered if I want to play them. This is probably a limitation that I wouldn’t be so willing to accept if it were my main instrument, but I play a lot more on box and bouzouki lately than anything else, so it’s okay.
As for tunes, following are some that seem to suit the instrument as I play it. Your mileage may vary.
Donnybrook Fair, Tripping Up the Stairs, My Darling Asleep, Man of the House, Sporting Padddy, Mulqueen’s, Drunken Landlady, Red-Haired Boy, The Cliffs Hornpipe, Strike the Gay Harp (of course!)
Polkas, of course, sound great on the harmonica. Ryan’s is fun, and the Britches in Stitches as well.

There are more, but those are the ones I play most often, and I think they’re all pretty good tunes for finding your way around the harp.
The Blarney Pilgrim and Julia Delaney work nicely on the C harp.

I would suggest you get started on a standard diatonic and then figure out from there what that’s lacking and how to repair that gap. There’s an absolute ton of tunes that you can play perfectly well on the straight D - I once did some sampling and figured it’s probably about 80% of the tunes you’re likely to find, you can play. Most prominently absent from that repertoire is anything with a strong C natural, which is a big hole, but if you’re using it for the fun and for some variety you can certainly play a lot of tunes on it in a night.
When you decide to expand (not if, when) you’ll have a choice of moving to differently-tuned diatonics, chromatics, or the tremolo/echo harps that some old-school players fancy, and by that point you’ll have a pretty good idea of what you want.

Re: Diatonic harmonica question

The diatonic harmonica (assuming we’re talking about 10-hole blues harp-type ones) is a very limited instrument, so the best plan is to go for the path of least resistance. A D harp can play tunes in D major, E Dorian, B Aeolian, A Mixolydian and (if it ever comes up) G Lydian. A G harp can play in G major, A Dorian, E Aeolian, and C MIxolydian. That lot covers the vast majority of Irish tunes, as played in sessions. There are some A Major tunes, not many but a few, that are best played on an A harp. So my main harps are D, G and A. If you want to accompany someone singing you might need other keys, F (I have a low F as the standard F is ridiculously high and squeaky) and C being occasionally useful I find.

Standard D vs low D:
The word “low” is a bit misleading here, the reason being that the standard D is actually one of the highest-pitched harps. If you play tunes in the middle octave of a low D harp it puts you at the pitch of fiddles, not in Davy Jones’ locker. Yes, it’s a fairly quiet instrument, but if your sole reason for playing a high D is to be heard better, notwithstanding the squawky sound you’ll make all night that will drive your mates nuts, I think you’ve got it wrong personally. Another approach, used by Brendan Power, is to Paddify a standard D harp and play tunes on it in the bottom octave. Not for me I’m afraid. For reasons to do with physics, it’s quite hard to play those low draw notes fast and with good articulation without playing them flat. I’m not saying it can’t be done, but I really can’t be arsed to learn a whole new skill that I can get by quite well without (I’m getting too old). Path of least resistance again.

To Paddy Richter or not:
Well, if you also want to play blues on your harps, as you say, you are admittedly altering the tuning away from the usual blues tuning. Is it a hindrance? I don’t play blues, but I know of several good blues players who are happy with the Paddy tuning. If you don’t Paddify your D harp you will still be able to play the vast majority of tunes in D or the related modes, as above. There are exceptions (that hornpipe set, Home Ruler/Kitty’s Wedding, needs a Paddified D harp for both tunes, for example). On the whole, you can get away quite well with just the standard-tuned harp. But this is not the case with your G harp. You will come across very large numbers of G/related mode tunes that have that missing E in them (which is why whistles and flutes can play those tunes, as their instruments are complete right down to D. This also explains why nearly all D tunes don’t go below that bottom D - flutes and whistles wouldn’t be able to play them if they did). The missing note E is very difficult to get clean by bending when you’re playing fast tunes, so you really need your G harps Paddified. You put back the missing E by tuning up the D at 3-blow by a whole tone. You don’t lose that D altogether because it’s duplicated at 2-draw.

MInor-tuned harps, Melody Makers, etc.:
I don’t need these at all. Irish “minor” tunes are all modal, and you have all the notes on the standard harps already. Just imagine trying to play a set which has mode-changes in it, saddled with a minor-tuned harp. Forget it. Useless for Irish. Melody Maker? Hmm. Similar consideration really. I do play in sets that have mode-changes and I don’t suddenly want to finf that the second tune has a note in it that is missing on my harp…again, no thanks. There are alternative tunings invented by people like Jean Sabot and Tony Eyers that you may care to investigate, but on the whole I’m over the moon with my collection of Paddied harps.

Not loud enough:
This is an issue (jibes from other players aside). If you’re good enough you deserve to be heard, not louder than everyone else, but at least heard. In a sensitive session with a quiet zone near the session there’s no problem, but that doesn’t happen often in these parts. You can get Hohner XB40 harps in G and low D which are louder by a very considerable margin than the ordinary blues harps, and you can bend every note too, even to get that soddin‘ C natural which is the bane of D harmonica players. To me they sound a bit raw and I find I have to blow hard to get a good response, but a lot of people like ’em. In your own setup you can persuade your mates to let you play through a small battery amp. There’s no shame in it. I use a little lapel mic cupped in my hand and get the cleanest sound I can at a modest volume. My mates wouldn’t have it any other way, though of course there’s no way you could turn up to anyone else’s session with an amp. You simply wouldn’t want to, anyway.

Bending:
You can use bending judiciously for expression. Why not? Billy Pigg did it on the Northumbrian pipes and Kevin Burke does it occasionally. Good taste is what matters, not somebody else’s “rules.”

Well, Al, as I say, I’ve been doing it and little else (musically speaking - I do have a life) for quite a while. I’ve made every mistake in the book, and anyone asking for advice would do well to listen to someone who’s made the mistakes and can hold his hands up to that. I’ve wrecked harmonicas with clumsy attempts at tweaking, I’ve blown out harmonicas by the dozen by playing badly, I spent years playing with a sh*tty tone until I found out how to get a good one, I’ve train-wrecked in sessions through lack of preparedness, I’ve alienated people by being too brash, etc. etc. You name it. But I’ve also discovered how to do one or two things right, and it gets my goat just a little when I see people here of fairly limited experience declaring that it’s great to play a harp like a B/C box, it doesn’t matter what key harp you have, and so on. Start with the correct instrument and you’ll stop yourself from wasting a lot of time and dough. For a start you’ll at least be able to play along with recordings. That is a very important thing for most aspiring harmonica players of Irish tunes, believe me. And you won’t get anywhere with a chrom in sessions with harps in keys other than D and G. Not even Larry Adler could have done it because he couldn’t have got the ornamentation on his C chrom. Simple as that. I am not the world expert and I am most decidedly not a harmonica guru, but to a small extent I actually believe that I know what I’m talking about here. That’s all. Take or leave.

Re: Diatonic harmonica question

Well said, Orm. A breath of common sense! Actually, you will struggle with a good number of G/related mode tunes on a standard G harp. Not all G tunes by any means but more than enough to make it well worth having a Paddy G. In the dark ages before Brendan “invented” Paddy Richter tuning, Glenn Weiser produced his Fiddle Tunes book recommending a 12-hole G harp (Hohner 364 - a big gobful of a beast!) to play those awkward tunes. It did put you at trhe high end of the harp though. Paddy is a much better solution in my opinion. And I repeat - it isn’t a drastic mod, just a single reed retuned, and it will scarcely affect your ability to use the harp for any other kind of music.
