Re: Why do classical players still have to read from the sheet music?

Good question.

The simple answer is that they don’t have time to learn it.

If you watch concert soloists they very rarely have sheets. Because they’ve usually spent many years studying that piece and it’s part of their repertoire.

But then if you look at the orchestra they will all be reading their parts. Because they have a busy concert schedule and they might have only had one rehearsal playing this particular programme, and that rehearsal might have been just an hour or so earlier in the day or earlier in the week.

So basically, soloists usually don’t have sheets. Players in the chairs do.

↳ Posted by Donals on May 24th, 2015.

Re: Why do classical players still have to read from the sheet music?

There is also the issue that the chairs have to play tightly in unison. There is not room for interpretation, you can’t have 10 first violins each playing something slightly different. And the only practical way to achieve that is to have them all playing exactly what is written on the page. Things are different for the soloist, who has much more room for expression/improvisation/covering up mistakes, so they often work without music, in a way much more akin to how we do it.

Re: Why do classical players still have to read from the sheet music?

I’m oversimplifying, but if you know classical music, you know what I mean. A soloist will go on tour with a program that he or she will play with different orchestras for a number of dates. Each orchestra will only play the piece once or twice, but the soloist will go on doing the same piece over and over. It is not uncommon for a soloist to only play one piece out of an entire program. I do not mean to minimize the undertaking, but they simply do not go through the sheer volume of material an orchestra member goes through. An orchestra goes through probably three major pieces per night for an entire season, plus touring and recording. Add teaching and rehearsal and there isn’t much time to memorize an ever-changing number of pieces to be played. A soloist spends a lifetime learning and honing a certain amount of standard repertoire for their instrument and then will commit themselves to certain pieces that they will come back to again and again. It’s a different discipline and they do not always play from memory. Piano is the one instrument that is almost always played from memory when with an orchestra. In recital is another matter - sometimes yes, sometimes no.

Re: Why do classical players still have to read from the sheet music?

I’ll comment from the accompanist’s perspective, my area of experience. I can’t count how many times I’ve had to cover for a singer’s memory lapse (missed entrance, forgotten words, jumping a verse) and having the music in front of me is essential. In recital, accompanists almost always use music, although the great concert pianist Alicia de Larrocha did not, an amazing feat. In the realm of pit orchestra, my fellow musicians and I have had to scramble as a unit to cover an actor’s missed cue or mangled verses, with the conductor frantically stage-whispering bar numbers to keep everyone together. Can’t imagine how that would work without the music in front of us! The bottom line is, once again, the old debate about memory vs. dots and the inference that dots are lame. It seems to me there should be tolerance/acceptance of both skills - each has its place.

Re: Why do classical players still have to read from the sheet music?

Why do they do it? Because they can. They generally grew up (as musicians) literate. They’re paid to play what’s put in front of them and take directions from a guy with a stick. They can play at a high level trusting the stranger next to them to play at that level. Many can interpret the score in front of them in whatever cultural influence that moves them…some can’t or don’t. Many, many touring musicians from a whole host of genres swing into town with a couple of their own key players, pick up some hired guns, give ’em the charts and, put on a show with minimal rehearsal time. And they do a fine job of it! It’s at least as valuable a skill as the ability to “learn by ear” and play a tune with whatever one’s personal idea of “tradition” is. My hat’s off to them, wish I could do it.

I don’t recall of anyone addressing the issue of just what is this thing we call classical music. I’m pretty sure it’s not nearly as one-dimensional as we treat it. (Neither, by the way, is Irish music). To my mind the category is so broad I can’t see from one end to the other. So are most other genres…Jazz, Irish, Old Time, Scandi…all of ‘em are catchall names for subsets. Anyone want to take a shot at specifically defining “Celtic”? It covers solo guitar, chamber ensembles, full orchestras and I’m just getting’ started. So then, they read because it’s part of their profession. They can perform Russian, German, French, Star Wars, symphonies all in the same season and fine time to back a Wagner opera. I am in no way trying to put down the oral tradition. It’s a grand, warm, welcoming way of being. It’s just not the only way of being. There’s gotta be room for everybody or there isn’t room for anybody.

Re: Why do classical players still have to read from the sheet music?

29 May 2015
Should a violinist/fiddler play from memory if playing in a violin-piano duo?

Here is some relevant advice from the Juilliard School faculty teacher Laurie Smukler when she held a master class a few days ago in New York. One piece being played in the master class was a sonata by Beethoven for violin and piano, and as is common with Beethoven, the piano part is very important. Smukler took issue with the young violinist playing the sonata from memory. Her advice to the student was, when playing a sonata or other chamber music, “unless you have a partner who is playing by memory, don’t play without music. It’s a sign of respect for your partner, because it’s not your show. Even if you intend to play by memory, at least put the music on the stand.”

I have slightly adapted the above paragraph from a blog entry of 28 May 2015 on violinist.com by Laurie Niles, its editor.

On the other hand, if the piano is in an entirely subservient role, and it’s the violinist’s show, then it can be argued that it is the violinist’s choice to play from memory without music on the stand.

When rehearsing pre-19th century music (in particular), where the composers used very few dynamic markings apart from p and f , the conductor today will often amend the marks or put in extra ones to reflect his interpretation. The pros do indeed pay attention to the markings, firstly the conductor’s alterations and then the originals, and a player who messes up a performance by not doing this is taking a risk. Speaking of which, my cello teacher as a young musician before WW2 played sax in a professional dance band. The basic performance rule was, a player who made a mistake (wrong entry, duff note, etc) would get fined a substantial part of his fee; a second mistake and he’d be out on his ear. I understand it was a very good band indeed.
