Croi Ro-Naofa

Although the word “ceol” is in many languages, I was surprised to learn from a student that it means “repetition or utterance of prayer” in Arabic and Aramaic. The root word is “qaol” and the word “Quran” comes from this root. Sufi Musilims find this “ceol” the most important part of their faith and use repetitive musical tunes and utterances as a means to come into direct contact with god. They even have “Fes” or festivals of music (Feis in Irish). As we know, music is forbidden in mainstream Islam yet it was never forbidden in the Quran or the Bible. Like Irish music, the Sufi musicians were considered a threat by the religious leaders and were chased into exile and hiding.
According to some of the respected opinions in the scholarly world and here on this site, Irish music “didn’t exist” a few hundred years ago (I don’t agree with this). Sufi music although has been in written form since 600 ad? Furthermore, Sufi music sounds different in every country it comes into contact with. For example, Qawwali music of Pakistand and the Sufi music of Turkey sound completely different yet have similar cultural and thematic connections.
We were told that St. Patrick brought Monastic life to Ireland yet recent studies are pointing to the “seven Monastic Brothers” (Blanc-Ortolan) who were Coptic Christians from Egypt. Sufi Islam and Coptic Christian are both believed to be bi-products of an older mystical Egyptian religion and have unity in the same source. The Sufi mystic culture is older than Christ and Muhammad. Coptics even have “sacred harps” used for ceremonial purposes. The Sacred Heart is a commonality also between Sufi Islam, Coptic Christians and Irish Catholicism although I entirely don’t understand it.
Although I’m not Irish, I play Irish music for an odd thirty something years. I find deep peace, unity and love when I play, something that I’ve had a very hard time describing. I’m not religious, I’ve stopped going to church twenty something years ago and usually get very annoyed by religion.

Is it at all possible, that our music originally is based around a culture of mysticism?

Are you “just playing tunes?” or would you consider yourself a mystic?

In ancient Irish lore, the Three Noble Strains were three styles of playing the harp. They were drawn from the story of Boann, who gave birth to three sons. The eldest, Goltraiges, was born in great pain to her. The second, Gentraiges, was born with joy. The birth of the third, Suantres, caused her great fatigue. These boys grew up to become master harpers and their styles, or strains, were said to be derived from the manner of their birth: the goltrai (the strain of weeping), the geantrai (the strain of happiness), and the suantrai (the strain of sleep). It was these styles which harpers were expected to master and invoke in their audiences.

@gam, the Irish church and others in the literate, educated elite *were* translating classical texts into Irish while “peasants” *everywhere*, from Ireland to France to Africa and the Americas, were probably getting on with their lives. And not translating Latin or Greek to other languages.

I would also say that there is a lot of “mysticism” in Irish religious practice and culture and the manner in which the landscape is constituted in folklore, prior to the modern Catholic Church taking a very active role in how religion was done the 20th century and modernity making all those old beliefs look quaint and irrelevent. There was a lot of supernaturality infused into the landscape, with wells, lochs, hills, all having significance as sites of worship and where supernatural beings hung about, and people would come in droves to wells, say, which were said to have healing powers (see Ronan Foley, ‘Healing Waters,’ Ashgate, 2010; he was my external examiner for my PhD). Right through the 19th century, people used “fairy” or “changeling” stories, or stories about witchcraft, to explain or warn people that they ought to behave in certain ways, or to explain otherwise inexplicable behaviour (though this probably reduced after the middle of the 19th century, after there were about 22 lunatic asylums built in Ireland).

Or, if you want to go further back, to the Middle Ages, you have stuff like the Ulster Cycle or the Fenian Cycle or Baile Suibhne and Battle of Mag Rath, which are actually 7th or 8th century texts, but were first translated in the Middle Ages, where the supernatural and magic powers drive the narratives and give the heroes their powers (or in Suibhne’s case, take them away).

So gam’s Irish peasants may have been singing and dancing and digging potatoes, but also visiting holy wells and leaving offerings while avoiding hills where they might p1ss off a narky fairy.

@Gobby: I understand a mystic to be someone who believes in and/or seeks an experience of oneness, perhaps in a transcendental way.

I am always happy to have an excuse to peruse the OED; here is their definition of “mystic,” categorized under theological terms: “Originally: an exponent or advocate of mystical theology. In later use also: any person who seeks by contemplation and self-surrender to obtain union with or absorption into God, or who believes in the spiritual apprehension of truths which are beyond the intellect; a person who has or seeks mystical experiences.”

And a person who is a mystic, categorized there as a theological term: " Having a spiritual character or significance by virtue of a connection or union with God which transcends human understanding.

I can see at least two reasons for your point of view: first, that the term is sometimes used to describe anything that cannot be understood, and two, that the term itself (as above) does have a meaning that refers to something, but that that something cannot be readily explained. (The term “to mystify” might be another example that muddies the waters.) And the connection with the ineffable, which “Al Adams” mentioned above, too—incidentally, if you do not know already, there is a “famous” (I think , in music scholarship) volume on music and the ineffable by Jankélévitch. Again, the phenomenon is something that is difficult to explain and possibly to understand, but the term describing that notion or phenomenon is not really.

Similarly, within Christianity (a topic I normally avoid), there is a history of mysticism, as in the cases of the Desert Fathers and Mothers. And teresa of Avila. And more recently, individuals who come to mind are Simone Weil and Thomas Merton—just a hunch of mine, but Wikip. does list them. (And one might suspect that transubstantiation is itself a mystical belief.)

As I mentioned above, Taliesin is an interesting “Celtic” example. And so on . . .

I do think this is difficult to discuss, and perhaps something to be discussed carefully or not at all—but I would not want to confuse the possible *misuse* of the term with the actual phenomenon.

The “those who know” you mention, by the way, is one I associate with Lao Tzu (Ch. 56 of Tao the Ching). And that reminds me of Chuang Tzu, prone to mystical outbursts, as in the story about the butterfly. There is a certain amount of this in Taoism, mixed in with other strains. Buddhism is an interesting example, with a focus on oneness but a detachment (in some strains anyway) from God . . .

Anyway, I hope some of that is of interest. I’m sure there are things to be revised in what I said, but hey, we’re all one. _/|\_ (That is a bow.)
