Re: Advice on graduating from whistle for 7 year old

Here is a simple test to see if a fiddle or viola is the right size for the player. The player holds the instrument horizontally under the chin in the usual manner. If the player can then curl the left hand round the far end of the scroll while still keeping the arm slightly bent, then the instrument is the right size. Straightening the arm means that the instrument is too big.

Re: Advice on graduating from whistle for 7 year old

We have four grand children - all have the opportunity to play music. Of the older three, only one chooses to play trad on any sort of regular basis. So I don’t think parental influence matters a huge amount beyond a certain age. It probably counts in the long term - over a lifetime but invariably once a child gets to secondary school age, they rightly start to make more choices for themselves.
All families differ but generally it doesn’t work to force choices on kids - mind you, I’ve heard more than one interview with respected adult trad musicians who basically said they were glad in the long term that their mammies pulled them and made them sit down and practice for a while each day, even though they resented it at the time.
The key thing with teenagers is some sort of social outlet for playing away from parents. That’s a harder nut to crack in modern Ireland.

Re: Advice on graduating from whistle for 7 year old

Ilig, I’ll bite. I have four kids and the oldest three began exploring ITM and similar traditional music within the past year. They are 11, 10, 7, and 5, and play DADGAD guitar, whistle, and fiddle, respectively, with the 5yo still exploring but expressing an interest in button accordion. (Who am I to contradict her?)

I play in an Irish session and my oldest (11) came along just to listen for a few months before he decided he wanted it for himself. He has been playing DADGAD guitar for about six months, is highly self-motivated, and seem to love the music.

I just asked him, why do you like to play ITM? His answer: It’s fun. (With a look on his face that says, “Duh!”)

Certainly not the same thing, but we live in an area ripe with good contradance musicians along with trad players, and we often have occasion to hear them play. My 7yo fell in love with the fiddle and has been happily learning for the better part of the year. (Mostly happily - sometimes she needs a nudge to practice, but as long as I’m playing/practicing along with her, she’s fine.) She’s too young to be out at the session most weeks, but she’s been a few times and when she’s a little bigger I’ll bring her along to listen if she wants.

Not to be left out, my 10yo picked up the tin whistle pretty much on his own. His personality is different from the others and I’m well tuned in to what kind of support he needs as a learner. I’m glad I’m able to draw on my own years of diverse musical experience - voice training for breath support, for example, even though I am not a whistler. When he needs or wants outside teaching, we can pursue it, but for now it not warranted.

And for what it is worth, we don’t do weekly lessons with an outside teacher, we do lessons on an as-needed basis, and I fill in. I think the family component is really helpful.

I’m doing what I can to foster love of music and love of learning in them, help them develop common repertoire so they can play together and with others, and tend their experience carefully so they aren’t thwarted by bad or overly rigid teachers. I’m also getting them used to playing for others casually when the chance arises - at parties, etc. - even if there are no others playing music. Many good skills can be honed with that kind of experience, IMO.

I’m giving thought to keeping our life amenable to lots of casual music playing and sharing, giving the kids exposure to good local players as often as possible, and emphasizing that it’s about the love of the music first and foremost.

I do this because it’s what I know. They like it so they play it. They know they like it just as anyone knows they like something. It brings joy. I can see enjoyment and excitement on their faces. They tell me they like it. And when they are frustrated, they are willing to work through it, which tells me they can handle the long haul.

I am not forcing it on them - that is not my style in any area of parenting. But we are establishing a family culture where music is the norm (because how could I do otherwise? It is the norm for me, and they hear me playing and practicing.)

To answer your question, I do think parents‘ influence carries more weight than others’ as long as it is kindly administered.

I think it’s awfully hard for a kid to discover trad music on their own and be able to progress in any way without parental/family support. There is a sort of culture that makes it easier. I think it is not a coincidence that “culture” is also the word that describes the fertile medium found in a petri dish that enables growth of whatever is in that dish. I want my family petri dish to be conducive to music growth. If it turns out the kids aren’t into it, no harm done - and if they are, so much the better for all of us.

I do not think “responsibility” should be imposed on parents to pass along any tradition - unless they themselves want to. On the other hand, if I can foster conditions in my kids’ childhood that allow them to grow into satisfied musicians who share their music with others, it will be a boon to more than just our family, and I will feel glad about having supported the greater good.

Is that helpful? I hope so. I’m interested in your thoughts.

Re: Advice on graduating from whistle for 7 year old

I don’t know if the accordion is appreciated until one gets older.

Whistle is a nice place to start since there is transfer to other instruments that kids come across at school (recorder, flute etc), so some of the peer pressure acceptance hurdles are not as great as with the accordion. As a kid who started on PA 52 years ago…they are intense.

I personally think at 7, keep him on the whistle and get him a good piano teacher, and keep him exposed to the other instruments. I usually recommend to get a kid with interest and talent on the piano right out of diapers.

Once he’s got the piano, migtrating to other instruments is much easier since the major hurdle is getting a new instrument’s technique under control. The ear will be trained, the kid will understand how the rudiments of the Music (bass, treble, harmony etc) fit in the grand scheme of things.

I think the whistle is a superb instrument as a first instrument. The logic is linear, like the piano keyboard. More advanced play requires learing basic harmony in an inuitive way. But It is magnificent in its simplicity, and a learner gets decent results quickly to keep the interest up.

amyamand - “I think it’s awfully hard for a kid to discover trad music on their own and be able to progress in any way without parental/family support”.

so true.

In England young people are fed a constant diet of:
“football is the national game”
“manufactured boy/girl band pop is the national music”
“foul cheap lager is the national drink”
etc.

Consequently, children will never come across traditional music (or dance, or painting, or any other artistic activities) unless someone takes the trouble to introduce them to it.

My father is completely untouched by any sort of cultural pursuits or appreciation of any sort of artistic activity, but his work in the printing trade led to his involvement with the TSR recording label in the 70s. For each different record/cassette sleeve produced, the payment included a copy of the album in question, which my father would pass to me. So I ended up being introduced to a kind of music which would otherwise have remained completely outside my circle of experience.

Even with this large catalogue of music at my disposal for listening, my fathers contempt and derision for any sort of cultural pursuit rendered it impossible for me to consider playing an instrument in the family home.

Encourage your children, and any others you can.
