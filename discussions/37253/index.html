Simply say you don’t know. Most Irish music is in G, D, or their relative minors (Em and Bm). I know the key of a tune when I learn it, but generally can not toss out what key I’m in until I play the tune. I simply do not commit that information to memory. Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

The last note of a tune often gives the key away. A better way is to find out the rest note, or the note on which the melody resolves.

The mode is somewhat more difficult, quick shortcuts for Irish music (not always true, but very often it works):

If the tune contains two sharps (C#/F#):
And ends on D:
D major
And ends on E:
Edorian
And ends on B:
Bminor
And ends on A:
A mixolydian

If the tune contains one sharp (F#)
And ends on G:
G major
And ends on E:
Eminor
And ends on A:
Adorian
And ends on D:
D mixolydian

If the tune contains no sharps:
And ends on C:
C major
And ends on A:
A minor
And ends on D:
D dorian

If the tune contains three sharps (C#/F# and G# or Ab):
And ends on A:
A major

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

Surely the best way to get an understanding of harmony and what chords will fit in what places in what tunes (short of serious theoretical study of harmony, which would fry most trad musicians’ brains very quickly) is to tinker around with a harmonic instrument such as guitar or piano.

You can learn chords by shapes on the guitar - but along with that it’s very useful to learn the names of the notes that make up the basic major and minor chords we use in this music. (You’ll pretty well have to learn the note names on piano.) Knowing what notes make up chords, and a bit about how to accompany tunes (even in the privacy of your bedroom/basement/woodshed) will unlock many mysteries for you.

As for knowing what key a tune is in, it’s pretty simple in the case of many tunes - at least for the purposes of telling passing accompanists (or, just read your response to Ailin, for the purposes of putting sets together). In most cases all they/you need to know is the keynote plus major or minor. The keynote is usually (but not always) the note the tune ends on. Major or minor depends on the third degree of the scale - or how the thing sounds.

Unfortunately it’s more complicated than that in the case of very many Irish tunes. There are modes (usually shades or variations of minor keys for basic purposes) and there are tunes that are ambiguous or don’t seem to fit any mould. But again, for the purposes of forewarning an accompanist, you don’t need to worry about these initially.

Hope this helps!

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

The short and very simplistic answer is…the note a tune ends on…or in may cases the note a tune resolves to…is the “key” of the tune. In the case of minor keys, I’ll leave it to others to argue about relative (aeolian) minor and dorian modes and I’ve never seen, on the Session at least, discussions of phrygian, lydian, locrian modes, and they almost never show up in this music. It all gets way to complex to easily grasp in the context of Irish or almost any other form of folk music. You’ll almost always be right by calling the resolving note the “key”.

A brief guide (subject to some parsing):

Ends on D..most often key of D…2 sharps

Ends on G…most often key of G…1 sharp

Ends on E…key of Em…1 sharp or key of E dorian…2 sharps

Ends on A…Am…no sharps or key of A dorian…1 sharp. Or key of A…3 sharps

Ends on B… usually Bm… 2 sharps

Another way to think of it is:

1 sharp (F#)…keys of G, Em, A dorian

2 sharps (F#, C#)…keys of D, Bm, E dorian)

no sharps…keys of C, Am

And this is just the start…add in the other modes (there are 7 for starters) and the complexities increase exponentially. Universities offer degrees in this sort of thing! What’s above will do ya most of the time for the tunes we love to play. All of the responses to you question so far are quite good. I’ll just add that if you have even the most modest understanding of music theory you don’t have to commit anything to memory.

A Google search of “modes in music” will bring up plethora (I love that word) of simple discussions on the topic. Just remember to have a good time and don’t overthink the problem.

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

You can’t wait for the end of the tune to figure things out, though. Perhaps I’ve filed away the keys of tunes along with everything else about them, but I analyze the opening notes and deduce the key from that. FAAF BAFA is, ignoring the B, repeating the middle section of a D major chord, thus D for that tune, which of course is the Silver Spear. GFG AGA leads away from G, thus G major. It could be an E minor tune too of course, so we listen and see where it resolves to - G in this case, thus G major for that one, the Donnybrook Jig. There are more ambiguous examples, tunes with gapped scales etc., but in general I can tell what key I’m dealing with right off the bat, somehow. And of course pretty much all tunes move from key to key, if you want to get technical about it - Donnybrook Jig could be said to move to E minor in some of its bars, so what key is it in? I guess the final note of the section would be the deciding factor there. Or that plus the opening notes? Its “mood”?

I’ve always understood these things instinctively so it was a bit bizarre taking up Irish music and encountering pipers who couldn’t figure out what regulator chord would fit, etc. (Insert joke about how no chord on the regs fit. A good friend of mine had this to say about the regs the first time I played an A/F# chord behind an A minor tune in his presence: “Ah, Cajun music!” ;) )

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

It is important to note the difference between the last note of the last bar of a tune, and the note that the tune resolves to, which is the one you want.
Interestingly, very often it’s the note that the tune begins with - probably because tunes are made to be repeated, so they resolve to their own beginning. Say, the Banshee reel begins in G, end on a D, the key is G;
or Morrison’s jig starting at E, ending on a D but clearly resolving back to E - it’s in Edor.

I’ve been told that this (the key note appearing at the beginning) is why people in old classical music used to call their pieces by the keys, e.g., “An air in G” - the conductor would call the piece to play and the orchestra members could then quickly flip through their music sheets to find the one starting with said tone.

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

This subject comes up periodically on The Session. The short and obtuse answer is, “You don’t need to know the key, you just need to know how the tune goes” - if you have any semblance of musicality, you will understand what the notes ‘mean’, without any reference to a theoretical framework.

But the question asked was “how to answer when a guitarist (or other backer) asks for the key”. I’ll leave it to others to explain the theory of keys, modes and their corresponding chords (a fair bit of explanation has already been given). So, assuming you know how to determine the key or mode of a tune, how you convey the information to the backer is very important.

As has been shown in the preceding posts, it may not be as simple as a ‘major’ or ‘minor’ key - the most precise way to refer to the tonality of a tune is in terms of modes (G Ionian, E Dorian, A Mixolydian going into D Ionian in 2nd part, D Dorian with an occasional flatted 6th etc.). The trouble with that is, i. most backers don’t know the theory in that much depth - or at least, are not quick enough to process all that information whilst playing (Not intended to be a jibe against backers - but, hey, why not? This is The Session, after all ;-) ); ii. neither do or are most tune players, and they are liable to get it wrong in the heat of the moment. (I would probably have to stop and think to work out whether a tune is Aeolian or Dorian).
iii. many of us (tune players) cannot easily speak and play simultaneously, thus making us even more likely to blurt out misinformation - and fluff the tune.

The other suggested method - calling out the number of sharps (or flats) - does not strike me as useful at all, since it does not tell the backer, however musically erudite, what chord to start on. “Two sharps” could mean D Ionian, E Dorian, A Mixolydian or B Aeolian - and a B Aeolian tune with a D chord under it would sound very wrong. What is more, even an accomplished backer, hearing a wrong opening chord under a tune, is likely to get confused and take a while to get back on track.

Another suggestion I have read here in the past is calling out the name of the tune. The trouble with this is, many Traditional musicians, both tune players and backers, no matter how competent or how extensive their repertoires, do not know the names of the tunes they play.

To my mind, the most useful bit of information a tune player can give to a backer is the home chord - “D (major)” for D Ionian or Mixolydian, “E minor” for E Aeolian or Dorian etc. Perhaps, for a tune that starts on a chord other than its home chord - e.g. The Providence https://thesession.org/tunes/376 - you could say something like “D starting on A”, “A to D” or somesuch like. That should be enough information for the backer to find their bearings. The rest is (and should be) down to their ear. Backers should, like the rest of us, be allowed to make a few mistakes, but if they cannot hear their mistakes, then perhaps they should stick to air guitar.

Ultimately, a good backer is probably best left to their own devices - it hardly disrupts a session if the backer sits out for a few bars, a round or two of a tune… or a whole tune. Those that cannot figure out the key of a tune themselves need to learn to do so.

I may be the outlier here, but my personal way of being tells me that the number of sharps (of flats is really the only thing that matters. Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

2. Modes.

This differs from working out major scales, which the post above is trying to do.

Here we take a major scale (we will use C as our example but it works just the same with G and it’s F# and D with it’s F# and C#)

CDEFGABC
a mode is the same notes as this but started at a different place. And they have greek names, but you can also use numbers to help:

2nd mode (Dorian) starts on the second note and ascends normally;DEFGABC

5th mode (Mixamatosis) starts on the 5th note; GABCDEF

6th mode (aeolian or natural minor) starts on the 6th note; ABCDEFG

All these modes (usually) have the Key signature of the original major scale but resolve on the chord of the ‘key tone’ ie the D for the Dorian mode.

PS we are not dealing with dimented chords here…

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

It’s something I take for granted, but as the discussion above shows it’s difficult to put into a concise explanation.

One thing that helps figuring out keys is knowing the chords.

Many Irish tunes, or sections of them, follow chords clearly.

The thing to learn is to be able to spell out basic chords, which is nothing but being able to say every other letter of the alphabet A-G thus:

ACE: A minor
AC#E: A Major
BDF#: B minor
CEG: C Major
DFA: D minor
DF#A: D Major
EGB: E minor
EG#B: E Major
FAC: F Major
GBD: G Major

These are the common ones that you’ll use 99% of the time.

Once these are stuck in your head, you’ll always be aware of where you are in a tune in terms of the chords.

Deciding what key a tune is in is usually straightforward. The chords of the beginnings of the main phrases, say bar 1, bar 3, bar 5, or what have you, will usually lay the foundation of the key, the alternating phrases being often in contrasting chords.

As mentioned above the end of a phrase, say the end of bar 8, will often not be the chord of the tune’s key, but a contrasting chord to lead you back round to the beginning of the tune.

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

Here’s a very easy straightforward example, The Atholl Highlanders.

The first part:

|: e3 ec#A | ec#A Bc#d | e3 ec#A | Bc#d c#BA |
e3 ec#A | ec#A Bc#d | c#ae f#ed | c#dB A2 :|

Well, remember what I said about learning how to spell using every other letter? The first three beats (bar 1 and the first half of bar 2) consist of nothing but E, C#, and A… and recall AC#E is an A Major chord, so it’s obvious that the tune begins in the key of A Major. Bars 3 and 5 are the same as bar 1 so all the major phrases are likewise in A Major.

The fourth beat (the second half of bar 2) has B and D (plus a passing tone C# which we can lay aside) which means either GBD (G Major) or BDF# (B minor). Either chord is equally implied by the tune, and either one can be played at whim.

Obviously bar 3 begins also with a G Major or B minor chord, the second beat implying an A Major chord, but many accompanists will prefer to build some tension by not resolving to A Major until the first beat of bar 5.

The first beat of bar 7 is also A Major as we can see, the notes being AC#E once again. The second beat of bar 7 has F# and D (with an E passing tone in between) which implies either DF#A (D Major) or BDF# (B minor) and either chord would fit equally well.

Though bar 8 starts on C# most people would hear, for the first beat, where the notes are C#DB, either a G Major chord (GBD) or an E Major chord (EG#B). Why? Because chord progressions tend to follow certain patterns, the tonic chord (in this case A Major, AC#E) wanting to alternate at times with the dominant chord (for the key of A the dominant chord is E Major). The dominant chord has the effect of pulling the ear back to the tune’s key, and is based on the 5th note of the tune’s scale. In many traditional tunes the 7th note is flat (in this case a G natural in the key of A Major, the scale being called a “Mixolydian” scale) and the chord based upon that flat 7th note (in this case a G Major chord) has the function of the dominant chord, and alternates with the tonic chord.

The second beat of bar 8, which is the final beat of the part, nicely resolves to A Major.

All this theory stuff aside, it’s child’s play to look through a tune as simply constructed as The Atholl Highlanders and figure out all the chords.

Harmony is very interesting - but traditional Irish music - and quite a lot of other traditional music in the British isles - is melodic. You can play it very well with no understanding of harmony at all and its ornamentation exists to make it interesting without any harmony. You do not, therefore, need to know the key.

This music was created and developed when most English, Irish and Welsh people were totally illiterate and hardly any lay people had access to this kind of information. (Before the 17th century most Scots were illiterate as well). Now most of us do have access to knowledge of all kinds and it’s interesting to know the theory, but if we love this music we must remember that an understanding of music theory is not needed to play it. If people choose to play melody only - or they have some gift for devising a simple harmony without working it out intellectually - being musically untrained doesn’t stop their music from being as good as anyone else’s.

For me, it’s always been about the Circle of 5ths for sharps and circle of 4ths for flats when it comes to the key signature. Simply put start at C major 0 sharps 0 flats. walk up 5 tones to G 1 sharp. Up 5 from G to D 2 sharps and ect. for Flats you start at C and go up 4 steps to F 1 flat and then up to Bflat 2 flats in the key signature, and so forth. The relative minor for any key is 3 steps below its major key (C major-A minor. G major-E minor, and so forth) Remembering these simple little rules has made my life easier. This is the circle in it’s simplest form.

Re: How do you know what key a tune is in if you don’t read music - and harmonies?

I haven’t read through all the responses but it might help to think of why the person was asking for suggestions of tunes in D. Was it because the tune he was thinking of pairing with a D tune had a minor sound? Most of the time the tunes in D have a “major/happy” sound and pairing them with a “minor/modal” sounding tune creates a nice energy and dynamic.

For the chord players, the question to ask is not “what key is this tune in?” but “what chords do I need play or concentrate on?” The tunes in Irish music generally either fall in the classic I-IV-V type of chording (those “major” sounding ones in D or G) or the two-chord modal variety. The modal two-chord ones are like Eminor + D, or Aminor + G, or D + C.

Telling someone that Banish Misfortune or Rakish Paddy is in D will really throw them off because these tunes are actually in the G scale but you only play the V and IV chords D and C. Rather than get into that technical stuff it’s easier to just say the chords are D and C.

I get off on music theory so it’s fun for me to look at Irish tunes through that window but I can understand how others can play this music just fine without any knowledge of the compositional aspects of the tunes.
