Re: How many English Concertina players are there in Ireland?

Yes, cag. I believe that the sequence is this:

1) Charles Wheatstone sets out to produce something that he thinks will be an improvement on the German melodeon, and invents and manufactures the English concertina.

2) Other English makers get in the act, and also manufacture the English concertina.

3) The German melodeon market is hit, and to retaliate the Germans come up with the German concertina.

4) English makers counter-retaliate my manufacturing an instrument similar to the German concertina - but of much better quality - hence the term: “Anglo-German concertina”

5) The term “Anglo-German concertina” shortened to “Anglo concertina”.

Re: How many English Concertina players are there in Ireland?

As to what the English concertina was intended for - well, anything a violin can play, for a start. The first four notes on the top inner row, on the right hand side, what are the white buttons on a student model, are GDae - violin open strings. And the top note of all is a top c - giving it the total range of a violin.
I have read somewhere that in Victorian times it was not polite for ladies to be seen playing the violin ( something to do with raising their elbows/lack of deodorants/I don’t know ? ), but the concertina was perfectly acceptable as you could just sit it in your lap. Hence it could often, in its early years, be seen in smart drawing rooms for the family music-making.
The link Ptarmigan has posted leads to others explaining a lot of its early history, both of manufacture and of use.

Ragman - I’m with Dafydd on that one about not economising on the syllables in this instance - it somehow conjures up visions of “(concer)tina Turner”!

Not only has it the same range, as Guernsey says, but the same octaves as well. Good for fast runs, as you alternate sides as you go up and down the scale. But not so good for triplets if they all happen to be on the same note, for example.

Unlike the fiddle, you don’t have “all the notes in between” the notes of the chromatic scale, but that’s true for any free reed instrument.

It is actually quite difficult to play it at speed, although good players seem to manage this OK. Easier if you have a steel-reeded instrumunt rather than a brass-reeded one, as the former are more responsive.

Speaking of English Concertinas in Ireland, I was lucky enough to meet up with Alan Jones from Scotland, last weekend in Limavady, Co. Derry.

He was over playing as a guest, with his band Haggerdash, at the Roe Valley Folk Festival.

It was lovely to see these two beauties side by side at a session in Ireland.
