Tough conversations? Telling someone they are doing it wrong

Saw this last night at a festival. Big session, started by a few Irish musicians I know, who play just fine. Other people joined in, including a bodhran player who had been playing all day, and was maybe OK in the morning but was now at least a full beat off.
So my friend Al (lets call him) stops and says: “I’ve stopped because you are way off my beat and I started the tune”. Which was completely true. And the guy got very upset. Considered he’d been told off. Friends consoled him. But, you know, if you don’t say it how do people absolutely know that they are ruining the enjoyment for everyone else (except, presumably, the mates of the bodhran player, who may be a really nice guy and rescue donkeys and everything)

So the question is: Do you have Al’s interaction? Do you only do it if it is your own session and not a festival? If you started the festival session do you have any rights over it? Who here would avoid the interaction and just walk away from this situation?

NB of course the percussion one here is black and white. Would you ever do it with a melody player?

Re: Tough conversations? Telling someone they are doing it wrong

Tough topic. Sometimes a kind word is enough … until it isn’t. To my way of thinking the worst offense is arhythmic playing and or pushing or dragging the tempo. Often I might say something at the end of an awkward set like “who won” or “we ought to pass around the birth control pills ’cause the rhythm method isn’t working”. Once in a while I might offer a glance at the offender (who may not even be aware) and a firm foot stomp. A calm “don’t drag” or “keep up/don’t speed up” could be enough. Other times I might start out (always in private) by saying “ I can tell you’re trying hard to get this and I appreciate your enthusiasm. Can I make a suggestion?”. Maybe “I can see your talent, try this”. Oh, and it’s a big help to offer a little praise now and then. My favorites are “You nailed it” and “Jeez, you must have played that one before!”. Humor helps.
When all that doesn’t then you know it’s time for a firm “You. Need to do … ”This“ …. Now”

There’s a reason I choose the anti-tantrum method. I’ve been over the years the recipient of all manner of correction. The rumors you have heard are true, I’m not the success story of the century (decade, year, week, day, last 5 minutes). I can’t think of anything that hasn’t been said to me. Fortunately my attitude has always been “suck it up Buttercup” and made the effort to fit in or at least found out that I wasn’t going to. I don’t think anyone ever said anything to me that wasn’t meant as helpful or at least to bring me up to the session standard for the enjoyment of everybody. And, that’s how I became the barely-tolerable player I am today!

Re: Tough conversations? Telling someone they are doing it wrong

I guess there’s no easy way to handle the potential hurt feelings. I have occasionally been blunt with a player, but I generally try to do it in a private conversation instead of calling someone out in the middle of a session. I will sometimes make general pronouncements that aren’t focused directly on an offending player, like “we should all tune” after a particularly out of tune set, or “that was like slogging through mud. Let’s try to focus on keeping the lift in the music”, etc. Making it a general pronouncement keeps people from becoming too personally offended. The other people that were noticing the distraction will feel good that you brought it up, and at the very least, it can help get everybody to try to be more conscious about their tuning, rhythm, or other potentially offending behaviors…

Edit: Jeremy makes a good point there. I am the session host in the vast majority of sessions I play in (two a week, at least). So when I make a general pronouncement like I mentioned, it holds some weight because of that. In the larger, more open and welcoming session that I anchor every week, a lot of the people who come regularly are either students of mine, or have been involved in the weekly tune learning session that I run. So they’ll listen to what I have to say. But we also tolerate a bit more off-putting behavior, especially from visiting players. I have noticed that if we work to keep the musicianship as high as we can, the people that come and don’t fit in well generally also don’t come back. Not because we were mean to them, but because they actually realized that they didn’t fit in…

Re: Tough conversations? Telling someone they are doing it wrong

My approach with the session I host is much like Reverend’s.

If there is an issue with a specific person being disruptive, I’ll generally take them aside and have a chat.

But, we have a issue as a group of often speeding up sets, and it’s one of those “everyone is to blame” kind of problems. In that case, when it’s clear that we’ve more or less sabotaged ourselves, I’ll say something after the set, like “Wow, that got fast” and gently ask everyone to try and keep the tempos under control without singling out any specific person. They already know who they are. :-)

If it’s clear that we’ve already sped up just a few bars into a set I start. I’ll just stop, say something about watching the speed, and start over, again without singling anyone out.

The final strategy is to wear Loop Engage earplugs that allow me to hear myself (generally on pipes) quite clearly while also being to selectively focus on locking in with our backup player. I’ll start a set and maintain a steady tempo no matter what the other players are doing while the rest of the session descends into a spiral of chaos speeding up.

Eventually they figure it out, stop playing, start listening, and get back in sync with the tempo I’m maintaining. Generally gets the point across without a bunch of drama.

If someone doesn’t know how/when to play, they will degrade the music for others. This is a fact, not an opinion.

If the *quality* of the music matters, that person needs to be clearly (but politely) told what is expected, and if they can’t listen/learn/practice, they should be uninvited from the session. Life is too short.

Re: Tough conversations? Telling someone they are doing it wrong

Lynne Hayes: “This is an interesting distinction and a really good one. It would be great if sessions could be advertised in these categories so everyone knew what to expect.”

The trouble with this is, in reality, sessions come in all manner of shades of ‘inclusivity’ and ‘exclusivity’, both in terms of musical style and musical competence, and most have a certain degree of flexibility. A session advertised as ‘inclusive’ might potentially draw in certain individuals that would not otherwise have thought to attend, who might push the ‘inclusivity’ envelope a little further than is welcome; conversely, a session advertised as ‘exclusive’ (‘advanced’, ‘experienced players only’ or somesuch like) might deter competent players that are not confident in their abilities, resulting in a session populated by a small, unvarying clique (not necessarily an undesirable thing, if everyone enjoys playing together, but it might as well not be advertised). Then there is always the possibilty that someone who overestimates their abilities or appropriateness turns up to the ‘exclusive’ session and lowers the standard, which is worse than such a person turning up to an ‘average’ session.

Re: Tough conversations? Telling someone they are doing it wrong

“everyone must be welcomed” - sure, that’s fine. But there’s a difference between welcoming the person and welcoming their behavior. If someone is exhibiting behavior that’s going to make them unwelcome despite your best intentions, the welcoming thing to do is to discuss that behavior with a view to bringing them around to a way of being that’s easier to welcome.

It seems to me almost axiomatic that most people who exhibit session-wrecking behavior do not want to wreck sessions. People don’t go to the trouble of showing up at a bar on session night just to make your good time a misery. What’s actually happening is that they want to fit in but they don’t know how. The welcoming thing to do is to help them fit in. Sometimes that means having an uncomfortable conversation, but having that conversation is the right thing to do.

Of course there are some ways to have that conversation that are going to be more effective than others, but at that point we’re talking about implementation details. The fact that your efforts might not succeed has never been a good excuse for not trying.
