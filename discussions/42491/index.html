Re: why does the note A stands fpr LA and not DO, or why Do is the note C and nor A?

Aha, I think you might be talking about moveable do. Basically in Anglo cultures they sometimes teach do re mi as a sort of universal major scale. This way they don’t have to address sharps and flats and so on for a while. So if the song (it’s usually for singers) is in d, d becomes do. If it’s in a major, a becomes do. And so on.
I see it as one of those short cuts that end up taking you miles out of your way. You avoid discussions about different keys, but you end up with some very confused people when keys inevitably become a factor.

Re: why does the note A stands fpr LA and not DO, or why Do is the note C and nor A?

Isn’t that just the approach that you take.

If you use Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Si as an absolute scale pitched to C, then La will be A.
If you look at Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Si as the wording of a diatonic scale (five whole steps, two half steps) it can be applied to any diatonic scale. Do can be A if you’re talking about the diatonic scale in the key of A major.

If you ever start with La then you’re talking about the Amin scale (or any minor scale for that matter).

“In traditional music theory, most countries in the world use the solfège naming convention Do–Re–Mi–Fa–Sol–La–Si, including for instance Italy, Portugal, Spain, France, Poland, Romania, most Latin American countries, Greece, Bulgaria, Turkey, Russia, and all the Arabic-speaking or Persian-speaking countries. However, within the English-speaking and Dutch-speaking world, pitch classes are typically represented by the first seven letters of the Latin alphabet (A, B, C, D, E, F and G). A few European countries, including Germany, adopt an almost identical notation, in which H substitutes for B”

"In parts of Europe, including Germany, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Poland, Hungary, Norway, Denmark, Serbia, Croatia, Slovenia, Finland and Iceland (and Sweden before about 1990s), the Gothic b transformed into the letter H (possibly for hart, German for hard, or just because the Gothic b resembled an H). Therefore, in German music notation, H is used instead of B♮ (B-natural), and B instead of B♭ (B-flat). Occasionally, music written in German for international use will use H for B-natural and Bb for B-flat (with a modern-script lower-case b instead of a flat sign). Since a Bes or B♭ in Northern Europe (i.e., a Bdouble flat elsewhere) is both rare and unorthodox (more likely to be expressed as Heses), it is generally clear what this notation means.

In Italian, Portuguese, Spanish, French, Romanian, Greek, Russian, Mongolian, Flemish, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew, Ukrainian, Bulgarian and Turkish notation the notes of scales are given in terms of Do–Re–Mi–Fa–Sol–La–Si rather than C–D–E–F–G–A–B. These names follow the original names reputedly given by Guido d’Arezzo, who had taken them from the first syllables of the first six musical phrases of a Gregorian chant melody “Ut queant laxis”, which began on the appropriate scale degrees. These became the basis of the solfège system. “Do” later replaced the original “Ut” for ease of singing (most likely from the beginning of Dominus, Lord), though “Ut” is still used in some places. “Si” or “Ti” was added as the seventh degree (from Sancte Johannes, St. John, to whom the hymn is dedicated). The use of “Si” versus “Ti” varies regionally."

Re: why does the note A stands fpr LA and not DO, or why Do is the note C and nor A?

This is a very good question, and the discussion about doh-re-mi has sidetracked it somewhat.

The simple answer is that at one point, almost all music existed in a two octave diatonic range, and the simplest possible system could be used to represent this: A B C D E F G H I J K L M N, one letter for each note.

So what you have here is a system centred around the major scale, but with a couple of notes underneath it, which makes a lot of sense for a system centred around vocal music. The fact that it’s the minor scale that starts on A is sort of a coincidence.

As this two octave range started to be extended, the modern system of repeating the note letters developed.

Do-re-mi was an invention of Guido d’Arrezzo (sp?…) and referred to fixed pitches. Movable Do as we know is a Victorian invention and became popular in English speaking countries with efforts to encourage the singing of sacred music (there were various inventions, such as the shapenotes of Sacred Harp).

Re: why does the note A stands fpr LA and not DO, or why Do is the note C and nor A?

First of all, we should really be using the terms ionian and aeolian rather than major and minor, since we’re talking about very early church music.
So, why is A the tonal center of the aeolian mode rather than the ionian?

Well, the major scale has not always been the default in western music. Back in the those days, aeolian was used just as much if not more than ionian. So there wasn’t really any reason to not base it on the aeolian mode.

So, why does DO RE MI (solfège) start on C?
The generally accepted origin of solfège is Guido d’ Arezzo who modeled it on the hymn Ut qeant laxis:
(Ut) queant laxīs (re)sonāre fībrīs
(Mī)ra gestōrum (fa)mulī tuōrum,
(Sol)ve pollūtī (la)biī reātum,
(S)ancte (I)ōhannēs.

Notice how the marked parts correspond to Do re mi (except for Do, which was originally Ut)
In the hymn, (Ut) is a C, (re) is a D, (Mi) is an E, etc.

Short version: ABCDEFG is based on the aeolian church mode (aka minor), DO RE MI is based on the ionian church mode (aka major).

The history of western music is fairly long and complex, stretching back to ancient Greece.
This leads to a lot of confusing notation and terminology.

Re: why does the note A stands fpr LA and not DO, or why Do is the note C and nor A?

> whats so special about that particular frequency

A being 440 is a very recent thing, quite literally within living memory. Wikipedia’s “history of concert pitch” article is very good.

Short version: to begin with, no standard. Everyone just tuned to each other, or the least convenient instrument to alter. Eventually, some bright spark invented the tuning fork (before that, a constant reference did not exist). At that point, several standards appeared; A=415, 432, and 452 being common. An international convention agreed in 1939 that A should be 439 for reasons that are too tedious to remember. Finally, boffins at the BBC intervened and explained that 440 was much easier to produce electronically than 439. And that, in a nutshell, is that.

Re: why does the note A stands fpr LA and not DO, or why Do is the note C and nor A?

In the Middle Ages they began to define the concept of movable “do”. There was a Grergorian Chant that was, essentially, a major scale, that began with “Dominus” and had 8 notes (Dominus = God. My Latin’s not very good so there might be a spelling mistake here.) To help people understand the concept, they referred them to the lyrics of this chant. The first note in the chant was abbreviated from “dominus” to “do”.

This type of thing is called “solfegge”. There are two types. One is “movable do”, where the first tone in the scale is defined by the group of musicians playing. The other type is an absolute “do”, where “do” is always “C”.

Correction: I spelled the concept “solfege” wrong. It has only one “g”. I’ve been surfing the net trying to find the lyrics of the Gregorian chant that provided the solfege name of the relative notes. I haven’t found it and I would love it if someone does. If you find it please post it.

Medieval music did not use equal pitch. That didn’t happen until Bach. Now we use something called “equal temperment” (every half tone has the same mathematical ratio vibration-wise). Centuries ago, pitches were generated from a base note using the mathematical reality that if you touched a string at certain fraction of the total length while vibrating it, you got a harmonic. Touching the string at the halfway point generated a harmonic that was 1 octave above bass pitch. If you touched thea sting at 2/3 the length while vibrating it, you get a tone which is 1 octave and 1 fifth above the base note. Defining the “major scale”, they moved this fifth an octave lower to produce a scale of “do si do”. They kept doing this until they developed an entire scale. The problem was that these mathematical calculations generated fractions (which implied that the end point of the string wasn’t fixed in position). A sting doesn’t end with a vibration-- it’s fixed, atatched to a physical musical instrument. They had to keep rounding up or rounding down to accurately describe the tone (because it couldn’t be a fraction- the end point of the string was physically fixed). By the time they generated the 7th tone in a scale, the physical reality of how the string vibrated produced a hideous 5th. In medieval music, that pratio was associated with the devil (they weren’t too keen on the E-B fifth either). It’s interesting to listen to Gregorian chants with the original tuning instead of the emodern tuning we use now. Certain intervals are REALLY discordant. When the devil crops up in Gregorian chants they invoke the f-b forth. You feel stressed when you hear it-- it’s like nails on a chalkboard.

I was a piano technician before I played fiddle. I went to a Piano Technicians Guild conference once where Steinway supplied two identical pianos, one tuned the modern way and the other tuned the medieval way. A pianist played the same pieces on both pianos. At first I thought “oh boy… everyone in the room is going to lie and say there’s a difference when, in fact, they can’t hear a difference”. But no. Anyone with a decent ear could hear a profound difference.

The history of tuning has philosophical/spiritual meanings that us modern people can’t imagine. Our modern scales aren’t an “improvement”. They’re just different.
