It’s like any other real instrument IMO, it depends on whose hands it is in and what you feel about the music they are playing. I am always a little apprehensive when I see a piano accordion because rightly or wrongly I tend to associate them with the worst kind of stripped down, mechanical and by and large safe middle class country/folk music. Normally with a morris, English or country dance slant to it. Music which just does not do it for me at all, then on the other hand you have Phil Cunningham and people who play in that kind of style and I love it. Then there’s the fantastic East European stuff….and once again its ersatz and sterile impersonators.

Re: Opinions on Piano Accordions

It depends on the time and place. In a ceilidh band it’s great - the volume and oom pah pah bass chords are just what you need to keep a room full of hob-nail booted dancers vaguely in time with each other. But in a session it can be a bit overpowering. To be honest I don’t think that’s a failing of the instrument - people like Phil Cunningham prove that the PA can be played sensitively, but a lot of PAists are taught and brought up with the expectation that they will play for ceilidhs, and they don’t know any different.

Re: Opinions on Piano Accordions

Back many years ago a wise old man told me “A musical instrument is merely a mechanical device. The music is in the person.”

Yet, in ‘traditional music’ each instrument seems to have its unique associated style or set of performance practices, for example the same player will play the same tune rather differently on whistle and flute, though the same style could be played on both. It “simply isn’t done.”

I don’t know anything about the mechanical differences between an Irish B/C box and a piano accordion, but I can hear that quite different styles are usually done on each. I wonder how much of the difference is tradition, is choice, and how much is forced upon the player by the instrument. Because I suppose a piano accordion player could use the same bellows-action as a B/C box player if he chose to.

About booming bass chords, a piano accordionist could choose to not use them, could choose to play his left hand like a B/C box player.

Anyhow I really like the flowing pipe-style playing of Phil Cunningham, such as here

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XJt14MixKPk

Re: Opinions on Piano Accordions

I’ve seen some PA players who use the bellows more like a fiddle bow, changing direction for emphasis rather than just automatically opening and closing from one end to the other. But it does very much depend on the player. The basses can be very cool when used kinda like regulators or as fill-in/harmony like on a concertina, but a lot of players either get carried away or just let fly with the OOM-PA-PA (see, it even has PA in the description of that sound!! :) ), and that gets old, overpowering and dominates the accompaniment if there is any. It’s like a banjo, or a loud set of pipes, it can take over. Ultimately sensitive playing will win over the critics, and really any instrument can botch things if used without sensitivity. I used to go to a session with a husband and wife duo, he played bodhran and she the guitar. Nothing terrible about that, but they played on every tune, every week, he with the identical lift-lessly mechanical Xxx-Xxx or Xxxx-Xxxx rhythm, and she with same hopping root/fifth basic chord progressions completely devoid of imagination or a basic semblance to any sound that could be called Irish, and it was absolutely awful, so incredibly hard to listen to!! You happen to play an instrument that has strength, rage and volume and great the potential to really wreck things. If you go in with respect, listening and sensitivity then all power to you! There will always be people who roll their eyes in judgement and will never change, but they’re tools in my opinion!

Re: Opinions on Piano Accordions

Ive been playing PA for 2 years and I had some apprehensions and doubts when I started (although that was mainly due to reading threads on this website!). I think if you want to play the accordion, and want to play more or less solely Irish music, then the button accordion is the way to go. It has that punch and bounciness which sounds so good in trad, that the PA just doesn’t have. Not to say that the PA sounds bad in Irish music, like others have said, dry tuning with minimal bass can make it sound really really good. I’ve twice walked into a session, and before heading in thought to myself “sounds cracker, that button accordion is really lifting it”, to find out both times that it was actually a PA! But still, overall, I think the button accordion wins over the PA in terms of just Irish music.

However if you’re like me and you love loads of different types of music and want to play lots of different types of music then the way PA is without a doubt the way to go. It’s why I think the PA (or even CBA) is a better instrument overall. Much more versatile. I chose the PA because alongside Irish music my other musical passion is klezmer and well different forms of Eastern European trad in general (Russian, gypsy, etc), which sounds absolutely fantastic on the PA. I also wanted to be able to play classical music, French musettes, Italian tarantellas, punk, metal and even ska! I know with Irish button accordions you can technically play anything, it being chromatic and all, but I’ve heard it, and it just doesn’t sound the same.

So don’t listen to the haters is what I’ll say. I think people’s fear of the PA is (becoming?) a thing of the past, and that it has securely found itself a snug wee position in the world of trad. This style of Irish PA instigated by Karen Tweed (I think) and carried on by Alan Kelly and such has certainly proved it’s competence and suitability within Irish music.

I’m definitely not an expert at all, but this is what I’ve gathered from 2 years of playing!

Re: Opinions on Piano Accordions

I’ve gladly had my old biases challenged, after repeat bad experiences with inconsiderate and ego-maniacal piano accordion players, often thinking that things should revolve around them as the centre to it all, but then we’ve experienced others of the same ilk but different instruments, though the louder ones seem to dominate here, such as hammered dulcimer players. A few things about the piano accordion seem to contribute to the pain some of us have suffered under such negative influences. First, that it was designed as a one-person-band. All you’d need then is one of those contraptions where you knock your knees together for a cymbal, and something to bang a drum too, or shake a tamborine, and you could add a kazoo holder too, or a horn. What emphasizes this is when ALL reeds are chosen, immediately swallowing up the sounds of many instruments, such as winds and bowed strings. The combination of the flat set and sharp set especially do this, and the wetter the worse. They were designed, I’d guess, to emulate the usual setup of a larger ensemble, that not everyone is in tune. By adding a flat set and a sharp set the volume also seems to increase, but that is true for every extra set of reeds added after the first. And, most full blown piano accordions have at least five or six sets, low, high, mid, mid-sharp, mid-flat. With all chosen and sounding, and if you also throw in chords and bass, and with someone laying heavy on the bellows ~ you can kiss goodbye pretty much everyone else, all ideas of balance are steam rolled over by the dominance of the one-person-band, the piano accordion. Why bother to join in, especially if you can’t hear yourself?

And then you come across, dare I say ‘rare’, someone who plays this HGV (Heavy Goods Vehicle/Mack Truck) of a musical instrument with finesse and consideration of others, where you can hear a balance and yourself in that, each instrument coming through ~ the piano accordion player actually listening to everyone else and doing their best to let everyone have voice in the mix, not dominating, and not playing EVERYTHING SMEARED - LEGATO - MUDDIED. Yes, that last SHOUTED complaint is another of the pain-in-the-ass ways some folk choose to squeeze. Blessed are those that know the difference, and show it in their consideration of others, and for music and ‘tradition’ in their best sense.

I have chosen to just nurse pints and not play when someone clueless steam rolls a session with their instrument, whatever that instrument might be, though having a big honking piano accordion does give one that power to dominate. It does still tend to initially make me wary, and my wife too… Curiously, I have inherited my father’s whopping huge Hohner, though it will never be my instrument of choice. Mind you, it’s reeds are sweet! :-D

Re: Opinions on Piano Accordions

As far as weight goes, the B/C/C# players of “Jimmy Shand” style accordions may well be playing pretty much B/C style on the right hand whilst moving a full size Stradella accordion bass end to do so, but as Sean says, a pianobox player doesn’t have to do that. The phrasing and “articulation” has to mostly come from the fingers.

If you’re looking to concentrate on Irish traditional music, and you want to play accordion, you’ll probably choose buttonbox. Most pianobox players have fingers in other musical pies, or have come from “elsewhere.” A classical violinist or Boehm flute player can turn up at a session and show more or less understanding of ITM, but with relatively limited volume potential. The potential problem with pianobox players from other genres doing that is that they can be so loud! They may also be inclined to believe they can play this stuff. Whilst a trumpeter or sax player would have the volume issues, they are much less likely to turn up expecting to play!

PA = Piano Accordion or Public Address - for many years pre-amplification the one box did both!

Re: Opinions on Piano Accordions

I’ve worked with 3 different PA players, and they all had strengths and weaknesses, but as all of them were in some sort of dance band there was this tendency to blast out, because that was what was needed on occasion. Personally I’d try to avoid a session with one ( or more ! ) on principle that they can overpower everyone else too easily. I do hold the same opinion on tenor banjos too, from some unfortunate experiences.
As many people have said above, it’s the player and the sensitivity that always counts, not the musical instrument. And, in answer to Richard Cook, it’s how you use the bellows that counts, some people just push air through with them, others use them to subtly accent the rhythm in ways that enhance the fingers on the keyboard, so it’s not just an organ that is being played.
