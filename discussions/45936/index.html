Classical-style Irish Tune

A bit of background: I’m interested in traditional Irish music and am largely self-taught. My fiance is a classically trained violin player. I enjoy listening to the technical prowess of her and her playing, but when she plays traditional tunes with me, she says she doesn’t find any of the tunes to be challenging or technically difficult and is usually bored. I recall when we played the Old Grey Cat, she exclaimed, “At least that one had triplets in it.” I know difficulty is in the eye of the beholder and is different for different styles of music, but hopefully the background of this question makes it more clear: odd rhythms and pauses, tuples, harmonics, notes out of first position, etc. seem to make a tune more interesting to her.

What are some of the most technically difficult traditional violin tunes to play which a classically-trained violinist might find more engaging?

Re: Classical-style Irish Tune

They are tunes. Tunes are to be interpreted as they are played. The nuances are in slight variations which are subtle, yet essential elements of the idiom. The beauty of this style is the tune constantly changes yet many players can join together in unison. It’s not something which requires arrangements, rehearsals or even difficulty. The tunes can be difficult or simple. But neither needs to be boring. The player however needs to be a good listener keen on the single melody, always aware of the playing style and willing to play with others who seek this too. Anyone who is bored either is in the wrong session or not listening.

Hi Charleville, I may be wrong but my impression is that your fiancée is looking at this music from the wrong perspective. “At least that one had triplets in it”, this makes little sense in the context of ITM: you can put in a tune how many triplets you want.

Look at this video of Martin Hayes: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KGSBYzqBc5E The first tune (The Sailor’s Bonnet) is not a musically complex one and he plays it plainly the first two times. But see how he revisits the tune and plays with it the third time (starts ~ 0:59).

What is really challenging (and this is something that is not usually required from classically trained musicians) is to be able to “speak” a tune like that: make it your own and express it with your instrument and style as easily and variedly as you would express something with your words in your language.

I can relate, because at Uni I was dating a very good “classical” violinist.

However, she got very interested in Scottish fiddle music and began attending high-level workshops taught by Alasdair Fraser.

Though her “classical” technique was at a high level it immediately became apparent that, in the realm of Scottish fiddling, she was a newbie and had much to learn.

She recognised that the difficulties, the challenges, in Scottish fiddling were different than the ones she had encountered and overcome in her “classical” violin playing.

Your fiance is looking for the challenges found in “classical” music in traditional Irish music. She won’t find them there. What she will find, if she sets aside her existing knowledge and technique and experience, is a whole new world of equally daunting, but utterly different, challenges.

I can guarantee that in spite of her skill on the violin it will take a huge amount of practicing to play a single tune as played by a good Irish style fiddler. Actually I don’t believe she could ever achieve that unless she took some lessons, or did a huge amount of listening and studying of good Irish-style fiddling on Youtube.

Perhaps the greatest obstacle for her is that she doesn’t know what she doesn’t know. She doesn’t know what to listen for. Currently she’s listening for the wrong things- listening for the things that exist in “classical” violin playing- and failing to hear all the things that exist in Irish style fiddling, specifics of technique and nuances of timing and rhythm.

What she perceives as being simple and easy is actually more complex and difficult than she can imagine, at least now, in her current state of listening and understanding.

Let me make it clear that I’m talking about traditional Irish dance music as it’s PLAYED by good player, not traditional Irish dance music the way it LOOKS in staff notation, which is indeed simple.

(BTW I’m putting “classical” in quotes because we in trad music apply “classical” to people who, in fact, play Baroque and other non-classical sorts of music.)

If, recognising that difference, it does not excite her or arouse her curiosity, then perhaps you both need to accept that, much though you no doubt have in common, you just inhabit two separate musical worlds (or perhaps find some other common musical ground).

Re: Classical-style Irish Tune

The thing about notation could be the key that unlocks Irish fiddling for her, or for any “classical” player.

I’ve seen “classical” players who only know Irish trad music from sheet music they’ve seen. They play those dots flawlessly. It’s incredibly simple for them. But they’ve not done directed listening, and they don’t yet understand that there’s much more to the music than what’s shown by the dots.

Staff notation can get very specific, and it is possible to notate a performance of an Irish fiddle tune by a good player that’s specific enough that a good violinist who is also an excellent sightreader can produce a performance that’s uncannily similar to the original. Detailed accurate transcriptions are difficult to write and difficult to read, but possible.

if there is interest in engaging, (after all, we may or may not ‘click’ with a genre of music), it is perhaps worth approaching from a different standpoint as many comments above convey…
Over many centuries ‘classical’ (or perhaps we should just say ‘composers and musicians who excel in their art’) have drawn upon the wellspring of their folk music.
Turlough O’Carolan counted Haydn as a friend.
Through history, ‘La Follia’, originating as a ‘simple’ folk dance, has found it way into many works..
Vaughan Williams famously, like many composers of his time, drew on folk music.
Perhaps take a look at the Fitzwilliam Virginal book (1612). Here there are ‘folk’ (read ‘popular tunes of the day’, such as ‘Jhn, come kiss me now’!) interpreted by William Byrd, which he has reinterpreted to put his own mark upon.
This has continued across times and culture, Bela Bartok, through to Benjamin Britten and the present day Judith Weir, James MacMillan…
Great folk musicians bring their own selves to represent or reinterpret tunes that we inherit from our past or to create new music, connected to ourselves, to those who have come before and to those who will come after..
Folk tunes provide a canvass on which to bring oneself and engage with the audience. Sometimes this may be formally recorded in a score or in the ‘magic’ of the moment for the players and audience .. most importantly, just enjoy and, if it should happen, emotionally connect..

Re: Classical-style Irish Tune

People are posting links to great-sounding trad playing, but I think that skirts around the heart of the issue, which is that Irish trad as it’s notated in nearly all the collections is quite simple.

There’s no bowing indicated. To an Irish fiddler that means “use the traditional approaches to bowing that you’ve spent thousands of hours mastering” but to a sightreading violinist it means “bow each note separately”.

There are no double-stops indicated. To an Irish fiddler that means “use double-stops whenever your thousands of hours of listening, practicing, and playing tell you is the right time to use them” but to a sightreading violinist means “play all single notes”.

There are no dynamics indicated. To an Irish fiddler that means “use your sense of dynamics gained from long listening and playing to give the music rhythm and lift” but to a sightreading violinist it means “play every note at the same medium dynamic level”.

That’s what trad players so often don’t understand: leaving so many things out of standard Irish trad sheet music isn’t creating an absence of musical information, but is communicating quite specific performance instructions to a sightreading musician.

Ditto flute music. Leaving out ties/ligatures isn’t creating an absence of instruction about articulation, but tells a sightreading flutist to tongue each note. So you put O Neill’s in front of them, and guess what? They tongue every note! It’s not their fault that the sheet music is giving them wrong information.

Many years ago I attended an Irish whistle workshop where the teacher handed out one Mary Bergin reel correctly notated. Guess what? “Classical” fluteplayers and Recorder players who sightread that sheet music sounded pretty good. They weren’t tonguing in the wrong places, they weren’t breathing in the wrong spots, they weren’t doing any of the odd-sounding things they would be doing if they were reading the typical dots in O Neill’s etc.

Re: Classical-style Irish Tune

I often think dedannans arrival of the Queen of Sheba would be a good example of the differences between the two genres for someone who isn’t looking beyond the notes on the page.

In terms of more “classical” or “classical feeling” tunes:

There’s a slightly more classical feel to a lot of hornpipes. The same might be said of barndances, Germans, highlands. That kind of stuff.

Get her playing, I don’t know, the golden eagle or the laird of drumblair or something. You could move on to things like the mathematician. There’s some nice minory ones like Galway bay, the drunken sailor, poll ha’penny. Then there’s Séan Maguire stuff where he moves around the fingerboard a bit.

And some more modern stuff that’s come in. The contraction. Catharsis. Fire in their bellies. Cavan potholes. The clumsy lover. Pressed for time. Franks reel. Mac Arthur road. These kinds of tunes often appeal to violinists.

But honestly I think these often appeal for precisely the wrong reasons: because the violinist is missing the subtleties going on in a rendition of the bucks of Oranmore or what have you.

In the long run, listening, lessons, ditching the sheet music for a while, and playing with other trad musicians will make her much more of a fiddler than playing the moving cloud or the road to errogie , However fast and pyrotechnical she manages to get it.

And it’s not really like these “technically challenging” tunes are really all that much of a stretch anyway. A bit of third position or a little shuffle pattern. The real technical challenges in trad lie completely elsewhere.

But there’s some titles that might appeal more to someone with a more classical aesthetic in there. Maybe they’ll serve as a gateway drug. Either way, I hope you enjoy playing together! Whatever particular tunes you end up playing.

Hi -- I’m a newbie, just joined.

Tell her to watch Baroque opera. Those tunes are also extremely simple; most of the ones written by Haendel for the great singer Senesino are very simple and have less than a two octave range and are a great place to start. The point of those tunes is to sing them simply the first time, then go back and jeuje them up like crazy, which is exactly what the star singers of the time did. Irish tunes are much the same.

Also, if she listens to any Mozart, she’ll find the same thing applying in a different way: the tunes are relatively simple, but that means they are also exposed, to the point where poor intonation or poor expression stands out like a fart in a confessional. Oddly enough, the simplicity of Mozart makes his music brutally difficult, something that any classical musician will appreciate.

She can also approach it by remembering that any Baroque suite is basically dance music as well: allemande, courante, gigue, sarabande. We don’t dance to it now, but they used to.

I’m a Baroque fan, so the points of tangency between it and folk/roots music stand out to me in high relief. It sounds as if your fiancee might be less into early music or Baroque period performance. If you can compare it to that, it may give her a way to approach it. Tell her to watch some good performances of Baroque opera arias on YouTube (simple tunes highly ornamented) so she can see that this way of approaching music is part of her heritage as well. This can help her get over the “hump” of thinking that it’s an unschooled way to approach music and develop a greater respect for the incredible challenge of playing Irish music well, even if she decides she doesn’t care to play it in the end.

Full disclosure: I started out on the flute in ITM and ended up stopping both because I just gravitated toward opera tunes :-) and because it was too damned hard! (When the hell am I supposed to breathe?!) However, I did retain a love of Irish flute music and a massive respect for it. I could listen to Orlaith McAuliffe and Mike McGoldrick all day long.

BTW, when I say that Irish tunes are much the same, I don’t mean that they sound the same or should be treated the same, but that they have a structural similarity with Baroque arias in that you often go straight through on the plain tune, and then embroider it on the second go-around, and that the embroidery is something the musician is expected to come up with themselves, often on the fly. What I’m trying to say (although not very well) is that there are ways for her to think of ITM that will make it seem less alien to her and maybe make more sense by describing it in something that approximates her vocabulary for music.
