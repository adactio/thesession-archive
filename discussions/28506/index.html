The session “leader” is not there to “lead” and they’re not paid to be there, they’re being paid to forego anything else that might come up that night and ensure that there will be at least one solid player to keep things rolling.

If you’re certain that there will be that at least one solid player in attendance, it takes some uncertainty out of whether you yourself will turn up.

Why is one player paid, and the others not? The others aren’t promising to be there. Why not split the money evenly? Well, suppose the leader fee is about $150 (I don’t know what a typical fee is, but I’d do it for that, and not for less). Split that among five people (about the right number for a good session) , and it’s $30 each - not much pay for giving up your Wednesday nights (or whichever night) for now and forever more as long as you come to this session, is it? For one person, it’s a reasonable deal. Try to fragment it, and it doesn’t work for anyone.

As for the pints - a bar that doesn’t pour pints for the musicians is showing a degree of disrespect for them. If you’re playing, you should get a few beers. I suppose I’d suggest “reasonable” is about one beer per hour, because that’s what I typically drink. Your mileage may vary.
Bars can show respect in other ways, and I don’t say you should boycott a place that makes you buy your beer, but it’s a cheap gesture for the bar, and refusing to make that gesture says a lot.

If the musicians decide who gets to drink and who doesn’t, and they pick and choose like that, they’re showing you a hell of a lot of disrespect and I don’t know why you’d go back to play with them.

I spent a few years trying to organize musicians with the AFM (the American Federation of Musicians) and the IWW (the Wobblies, the folks who brought you the weekend).
And yes, differential pay for a gig would certainly be a problem for a band situation, but as you say (and as I said before) the leader is not there to lead a band, he’s there to ensure (simply by his presence and personality, usually, not by any persuasion) the presence of other players. Those other players are not working for the bar: they have no obligation to the bar, and the bar has no obligation to them.

A gig is an undertaking to be at a place at a particular time, and perform a service, preferably specified in detail, in writing, in a signed contract backed by a union and the force of law.
If I have a gig, and I don’t make it, the bar should have recourse to recover damages from me, as spelled out in a contract, just as if I turn up to play and the bar has double-booked, I have recourse (if I have a contract for the gig) to get paid, regardless of whether I was playing or not.

That is, a gig is a promise, not a performance. The performance is simply the fulfillment of the promise, but the promise is what I’m paid for.

That being the case, there is only one party who has thatrelationship with the bar, and that’s a “leader”, who should be paid a good wage for that work, or, more specifically, for turning down (in advance) the possibility of other paid work.

At least, that’s how I see it, as an old union guy.

>>“It doesn’t serve any purpose”

Right, but you missed what I was saying there - it doesn’t serve any economic purpose. You’re not going to promise to be there every week for thirty bucks a week - or, if you are, you’re in such precarious financial shape that the bar probably ought not rely on you. If the bar thinks $150 is a good sum to invest in a good session, they get a better return for that money by giving it to one person than by dividing it up among many players. And since the players themselves are choosing whether to turn up or not, it follows that the players agree that this is the best distribution. Let’s assume that we live in a big world with many people trying to make money running bars, and many people wanting to play in sessions. In that case, we can assume that any reasonable system of organizing those sessions has been tried: the ones that seem to work are the ones that pay no players and the ones that pay one or two players a fair day’s wage or thereabouts to come and play.
And this makes sense: the point of a wage is to get you to change your habits, to get you to do something you wouldn’t otherwise do. Someone who is likely to come out for $20 or $30 is reasonably likely to come out for nothing. There are cases where that mini-fee will make the difference, but they’re going to be quite rare, in relation to the universe of players who might come out, period. So a wage of $30 doesn’t change a lot of habits, therefore (in most cases) it doesn’t serve any purpose. On the other hand, a fair day’s wage to one musician who’s trying to make a living at musicking is likely to change his habits, so it’s an effective wage.

I know you want this to be about fairness and equity, but it’s not. It’s about getting a session into a bar. Paying a player like Sean Connor here in Boston goes a long way to getting a room full of musicians, and it means a slow night is still a good night. Dividing his pay among the musicians who turn up doesn’t do that, so bars don’t follow that policy.

It’s really simple in our system. Two hosts are paid. The hosts change every week, and any two hosts will generally get to host every couple of months. The bar has a tab for the session drinks and food, and if the total goes over the limit, then players toss in some cash. There is a tip jar that can be used to help cover any overage on the tab as well as help give a gratuity to the server and bartender. If it’s a good night with lots of tips, maybe the hosts get a little extra, and often share the extra with others who also may have driven a long way or to help pay for parking. The reason for the paid hosts is to insure that there will be at least two experienced, good quality players there no matter who else shows up. It’s not a session for newbies, generally is attended by experienced players from all over SoCal. So far, it has worked very well and I think is a good model for other sessions where the pub owners really are committed to having live trad sessions in their establishments.
