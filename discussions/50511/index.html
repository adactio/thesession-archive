Do Tunes Really Have a Nationality?

I’ve been keeping up with my Irish Tune a Day project, and sometimes viewers leave comments like “This is a Scottish tune.” Tunes like “Miss McLeod’s Reel” or “The Jig of Slurs,” for example.

I really appreciate those notes, but the truth is, plenty of tunes with non-Irish origins are played all the time in Irish sessions. For instance, the well-known session standard “The Flogging” actually comes from the Scottish “Flagon Reel.”

Scottish pipers might also assume that famous Irish tunes like “Jimmy Ward’s Jig” or “Dinkey’s” are Scottish, since they fit neatly into a one-octave range. And it’s often said that many hornpipes trace back to England or Scotland too.

Since so many tunes are shared across these traditions, I’m not sure it makes much sense to draw strict national lines once a tune is already part of the Irish session repertoire.

Do you think a tune’s “nationality” really matters?
Also, I’d love to hear about other common Irish session tunes you know that actually have roots outside Ireland!

Re: Do Tunes Really Have a Nationality?

Tunes may have a nationality because of where they originated, but those that transcend national borders can become international. Soldier’s Joy may be Scottish or English or even Swedish in origin, but it is frequently played in many countries. St. Anne’s Reel is from New Brunswick, Canada, but is part of the repertoire of Britain, Ireland, the USA, etc. I think it is interesting to see how the tunes take on different characters in different environments. Much like the many accents of spoken English, they are played with the musical “accents” of a given area and those that inhabit it. I do think it is important to be aware of where a tune has come from, but that’s my personal perspective.

I really don’t care if a tune is presented traditionally, or not (I’ll play what I like to hear), but when talking about ANYTHING ‘traditional’, I feel it’s important to try to be as accurate as possible in citing the origin.

Re: Do Tunes Really Have a Nationality?

Interesting topic, I think, and possibly more to it than simply geographic origin. I take part in a weekly ‘virtual’ session with some participants in other countries and with international musical interests. Apart from the benefit of picking up good tunes from various places across Europe, one can learn stuff. For instance, I gather that German traditional music was for a long time a no-go area because of its association with the Nazi policy of co-opting it for malign nationalistic purposes. More recently, though, there has been an interest in unearthing previously unknown or disregarded collections and discovering folk music material that predates such faux-cultural manipulation (e.g., Dahlhoff collection, and Ziemetshausen manuscripts). In the UK, it’s possibly significant that the Playford collection (first ed. 1651) emerged at the tail end of a repressive period of Puritan domination, and I imagine lent music and dance a renewed sense of permission and relief in a culturally stifled atmosphere.

I’m not a historian of these things, but I have no doubt that much Irish music was similarly dismissed, vilified, or suppressed under colonial rule - and we know that some songs and tunes were deliberately taken up as coded signals of resistance.

Always interesting to know things like dates and authorships - they help to locate tunes in the mind and in a context - but information about them can be a way into knowing a bit about their social and cultural background.

Re: Do Tunes Really Have a Nationality?

The “nationality” of a tune has to do with a lot more than just where it originated or the nationality of the composer, it also has to do with how it’s played. The nuances of the rhythms, the way it is expressed, and sometimes even the key that it is played in. There are tunes that get played in numerous different traditions, Irish, Scottish, English, American Old Time, Bluegrass, etc. But they often sound pretty different in the different traditions.

And there are plenty of examples of tunes that are essentially “Irish tunes” that were written by people that weren’t in Ireland. Many of them are from ex-pat Irish, of course, but there are plenty of tunes that were written to “fit within the Irish tradition” by people that aren’t Irish, and don’t reside in Ireland.

And I don’t have a problem with tunes that aren’t “Irish” being played in Irish sessions. But, as others have mentioned, I do like at least knowing the provenance of the tunes.

So in my way of thinking, traditional tunes have a provenance, but any tune can have multiple traditions that it fits within, so it could be considered to have multiple nationalities, I guess…

Re: Do Tunes Really Have a Nationality?

When it comes to origins of tunes, it’s never a good idea to get up on too high a horse. If you believe you’ve come across the obscure ‘actual’ origins, chances are you’ll later find that someone beat you to it, and has debunked, or at least cast doubt on your ‘discovery’. So, for example, you discover that an ‘Irish’ tune is ‘actually’ Canadian. Then you discover that there is an ‘old’ Irish tune that is very similar. Then you discover that there’s no evidence of the ‘old’ Irish tune before the ‘Canadian’ tune hit the airwaves. Then you discover that the Canadian who first recorded it learned it from his old American neighbour who had jumped ship, with the tune in his head, fifty years before. Then you discover that the title appears in a Scottish dance program from 1853, but it could be referring to a different tune …. Etc., etc. In short, it’s a mug’s game … !

Re: Do Tunes Really Have a Nationality?

I wouldn’t worry too much about this Hatao. Most people acknowledge that, when it comes to Irish traditional music, the word “Irish” is shorthand for “music played in Irish sessions”. Indeed, even the “traditional” part is strictly speaking contentious, as there are loads of recently-composed tunes that can’t strictly be said to be “traditional” but which are considered part of the repertoire.

“Repertoire” is a very useful word here I think. If including tunes whose origin is (considered to be) non-Irish prompts some historical information then so be it - that’s not a criticism (even if the person stating it might think it is). Personally I think it’s totally legitimate to describe a tune that’s part of Irish repertoire as “Irish” and at the same time as Scottish or English or whatever. That might seem like a logical contradiction, but this is the great thing about human language as a sophisticated tool: we understand different useages and applications and intentions within their context.

Re: Do Tunes Really Have a Nationality?

One answer to the question is ‘more than they used to have’, by which I mean there seems to be more interest in allocating national identities to ‘traditional’ tunes these days. In the trad musical market place there is more value in an Irish-branded tune/ song/ band/ performer than in, say their English equivalents; they just sell better to the average punter. This was even the case 200 years ago when so many dance tune collections were being published in Britain, when ‘Caledonian’ or ‘Hibernian’ labelled tune collections sold better.

On the other hand, tunes often had a remarkably mobility, especially when linked to fashionable foreign dance crazes like the waltz, mazurka and polka. You can see this if you browse the various manuscript collections being rediscovered from 200-odd years ago. Fiddlers in obscure parts of England, Wales and Ireland were playing a cosmopolitan repertoire at dances in response to popular demand. Nationalism, in the political sense, also kicked in when self-appointed cultural guardians decided to cleanse the played and published regional repertoire, to emphasise the more ‘authentic’ or ‘characteristic’ tunes and banish the now unwelcome ‘foreign’ tunes. Many of us still maintain this preference and it still sells - how else to explain the expanding gobal reach of the Irish session?

I still get funny looks when playing non-Irish tunes (even Scottish ones!) in some Irish sessions, although the hard line is softening as leading perfomers embrace non-Irish tunes to spice up their repertoires. Then it becomes OK to play those tunes in a session. Just don’t try to push your luck by launching into a non-famous player endorsed non-Irish tune!

I’m of the opinion that it doesn’t matter exactly where a tune comes from - just that it fits in the Irish folk tradition. Its impossible to be picky about this since many tunes are hundreds of years old. Tunes get passed from country to country and tunes are written in an Irish style by an English musician etc. Its inevitable really! The origins of so many tunes can become so muddled that in the end it just matters that the tune be appropriate to play at a session.

The folk tradition is really all about sharing tunes we find cool and enjoying them together, no matter how popular they are, where they are from, or who wrote them.
