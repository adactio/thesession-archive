[*All I was saying was that 153bpm (or whatever) doesn’t tell you anything much about the overall speed of playing. You also have to know how many notes per beat.*]

I see what you mean about notes per beat. Something like Moto Perpetuo or Flight of the Bumble Bee have more notes per ‘beat’ than any reel, as they are all equal in length and time value, and played separately with no slurring of notes. So 153 bpm for them is going to sound a lot different than 153 bpm for a reel. The playing there is relentless, with no pauses, unlike reels, which are full of tea breaks by comparison :)

On the other hand, I think playing a reel at that speed could be just as just as tricky, as you need to (or should) preserve the ‘swing’ of the long note-short-note pattern, unlike the ‘violin’ pieces mentioned earlier where all the notes are of equal length and duration.

Re: Frantic Frankie

“The Heifetz link shows playing much, much faster than 150 bpm, but it sounds like all the notes are in one bow, with more than a few tea breaks too” (Jim)

On a strict interpretation of bpm, that Heifetz piece (“Wellenspiel”) is 55 bars of 3/4 in 1’43", which works out as a sedate 92 bpm. However, for most of the time, within each quarter note beat there are eight one-thirtysecond notes being played, which doubles the perceived speed of the piece to the Paganini Moto Perpetuo level.

Jim, you’re quite correct in saying that the notes are played on one bow (with a few tea breaks!). “Wellenspiel”, which means “Waves at play” was composed as descriptive music of waves breaking (the tea breaks), not as a flat-out exercise in virtuosity, although technical command of the highest level is certainly needed in order to do it justice. In this piece there are no bowed runs of less than eight-note octave scales per beat, and at least a dozen three-octave scales per bar, both up and down, each one on a single bow.

The technical difficulties of this piece to my mind include achieving numerous inaudible position shifts and string-crossings in these runs, coupled with precise articulation and speed of each note. In doing this, Heifetz sets another of his gold standards (on his famous gut strings, of course). Today, this level of playing would be expected of a top graduate at any music college or conservatory.

Re: Frantic Frankie

One further point, I understand that Paganini’s original speed indication for his Opus 11, the Moto Perpetuo, was “allegro”, and not “allegro vivace” or “presto”. He was after precise coordination and articulation, which at the allegro setting of his day would have giving all the impression of virtuosity that was needed.

This suggests to me that the virtuoso performances today are significantly quicker than Paganini himself would have played, or intended, bearing in mind that the 18th century “allegro” was probably slower than today’s “allegro”. If composers of the time wanted something played flat out as fast as possible they would have used terms such as “allegro vivacissimo” or “prestissimo”, as Haydn did a few times in his symphonies.

Jim, you’re probably not all that far from Paganini’s intention :)

@Reverend
**I thought we had a discussion about this guy a while ago:
http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WFhvcYJe-hI**

If you look carefully at that guy’s left hand you should see that some of the time he is sliding his fingers on the string over a succession of notes rather than playing the notes individually. He is simultaneously executing a very rapid bowed tremolo, at about 17 strokes/sec which, in conjunction with sliding fingers, plays the individual notes of the piece at 17 notes/sec. This is obviously a technique requiring skill and practice but is well within the capabilities of many. What it is not doing is demonstrating a clear coordination of non-sliding individual finger placements with individual bow strokes, as in the Menuhin video of the Moto Perpetuo.

I do not know what the maximum physiological speed for the non-sliding technique would be for sustained clear playing as in pieces of the lengths of the Flight of the Bumble Bee or the Moto Perpetuo, but I am inclined to think it would be less than 17 notes/sec, perhaps 13-15.

Whatever technique is used for fast playing it is essential that clarity of articulation be maintained throughout.
