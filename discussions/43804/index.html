Hosted vs. unhosted sessions norms

I’ve recently had to deal with the consequences of a lack of specific communication on my part about the norms for our local session, I’m curious, in your local session which of the following standards apply:

1) The session is hosted by one or more hosts.

The hosts by default start sets and frequently invite others to start sets.

Nobody else starts sets unless invited.

2) The session is hosted and all sets start organically.

3) The session is un-hosted and all sets start organically.

Most of the more experienced and long-lived sessions I’ve been to, include the one I co-host here in San Diego, are in the first category.

I’d never really given this distinction any consideration until this recent misunderstanding, so I’m curious about other sessions outside this area.

Re: Hosted vs. unhosted sessions norms

In my experience, #1 has become the most common format in pub sessions. As the evening wears on, a regular may organically start a set but it’s mostly the host. Depends on the moment.

But in the past, many sessions I’ve participated in used a round-robin format (aka ‘circle of death’) where each person gets a chance to start a set, moving clockwise around the group.

In kitchen sessions with friends, scenario #3 is probably most common--we just hang out and play tunes.

I’ve been in sessions with ‘pouncers’ who jump in and start sets whenever there’s a lull and it can be annoying, especially when one of your other formats is in play. It’s almost like the pouncer is attempting a coup, to seize control of the session.

Can’t recall ever being in a #2 type session.

“I don’t understand, why people are so keen on ”hosting“ a session as ”session leaders“ and such crap.”

I’m going to assume that you’ve never been to a session that is spontaneously taken over by the Grateful Dead singalong guitar army?

A session host, with the support of the group, can prevent that from happening. A session host can also make the arrangements with the venue, so you have a space to play in on a regular basis. Session hosts that are too controlling are not fun, and I’ve experienced a bit of that here and there. Usually with hosts who are also teachers, who have trouble letting go of their normal teacher-class relationship. But strong enough players who join that kind of session can damp it down.

To answer the OP’s question: The most enjoyable local sessions are in category #2, but that’s only because the group has been together long enough that they don’t need much prompting by the leader, and sets happen organically. The session leader is still there to kick things into gear if we’re spending a lot of time in conversation and a tune hasn’t been played in a while.

I have never seen a type #3 that lasted very long (the session is un-hosted and all sets start organically). They’re usually taken over by the Guitar Singer-Song Writer Army or other genre drift. That may be a USA-centric thing however, since we have so many other genres of music that can be called public “jams.”

Personally, I detest the added category #4 of a strict format of people playing tunes in a circle. It puts too much pressure on people who aren’t ready to start tunes, and removes the spontaneous flow of a session where enough people know enough of the common tunes. A good session leader can track who hasn’t started a tune in a while, and ask if they have something they want to play. No big deal if they don’t.

Re: Hosted vs. unhosted sessions norms

It seems to me that this might be one of those cultural things, Irish v Americans (using the term to mean people in the USA; I know that Canadians and Brazilians are Americans too).

It comes up here all the time, and it boils down to Irish people having a more subtle awareness of unspoken codes of behavior than we Americans do. An example that often comes up is the American coming in and sitting in a chair that the Irish know is so-and-so’s chair. There’s no way for the American to know that the chair is taken, and the American might think “there’s no name on the chair, it belongs to me as well as anybody”. And indeed at many American events chairs might have names on them, or if an American brings his own chair it might have his name on it, to avoid confusion.

In the music field too, Americans often like clear-cut rules, and many American Pipe Bands have formal bylaws, boards, committees, officers, meetings, etc. One Pipe Band I was in conducted its meetings strictly by Roberts Rules Of Order- there was a copy sitting in the middle of the table at the meetings!

Coming from the Pipe Band scene as I do, it doesn’t surprise me to hear about Americans setting up Irish sessions with sets of rules, established membership, official leaders, and so forth. There are sessions here with fixed tune-lists, once again because Americans often want things spelled out.

Re: Hosted vs. unhosted sessions norms

I go nearly every month to three or four sessions in my area (SE England) and the format is similar in each of them. They’re not exclusively, or even mainly, Irish sessions, but the principle is the same. There is a recognised ‘host’ who is likely to get the ball rolling at the beginning with a couple of tunes or sets - usually fairly well known - and who then invites participants roughly in order around the table/room to kick something off. The host is essentially a coordinator - I can’t recall any local host being unduly directive. Participants may contribute something well known, or a ‘party piece’ that they’d like to introduce for others to pick up. The general expectation is that, if you know a tune, then you’re free to join in. If you don’t, then either listen (and learn) or gradually try and pick it up by playing along discreetly till you get the hang of it. (Of course, not everyone has the same idea of ‘discreet playing along’, but that’s life). It’s always o.k. to record other people’s tunes to work on them at home.

Personally, I’m happy with this style of session. It’s fairly simple and quickly clear to all how it works, and it leads to plenty of variety in material - some old standards, some tunes quite new and surprising. It means people don’t miss out if they’re not very forward (though it’s o.k. to decline to take a turn) and the thing is not dominated by anyone taking advantage of a free-for-all approach. The sessions I go to are relaxed and informal, but orderly enough for everyone to take part either as initiator or joiner-inner.

Re: Hosted vs. unhosted sessions norms

I guess I’m in the minority here, but most sessions I’ve been to (Ireland and East Coast US, primarily) have been #1, and I much prefer it that way. In fact, a session I used to go to a lot has shifted from #1 towards a more “free-flowing” format, closer to #2, and I think it’s for the worse.

I think the issue is that “no one starts sets unless invited” sounds much more draconian than it is in practice. Many hosts share starting duties very liberally, and the night ends up being at most 30-50% them, with the rest everyone else. I have rarely sat in on a session and not been asked to start at least one set.

“Free-flowing” sounds great in theory, but in practice what I’ve found is that it tends to lead to problems. One is that some very aggressive people tend to “pounce.” That’s fine if they’re strong players who can lead well, and pick tunes that fit others’ repertoire. I’ve found, however, that “pouncers” often have inflated senses of their own ability, and also often pick tunes, tempi, etc. that don’t fit well with others. So they end up dominating a session, often for the worse.

Another issue is that some people can be intimidated, and never pipe up. Without a session leader to coax a set out of them, they’d never get a chance to start. Being timid, by the way, is not the same as not wanting to start. Plenty of people would like the opportunity, they just are too caught with not wanting to be rude or stick their neck out that they won’t jump in without some prompting.

A third issue is that a good session leader will counter some of the natural ebbs in a session. Yes, it’s fine to hang out and chat for a while in between sets. But if that “for a while” devolves into longer than 5 or so minutes between each set, it’s not a session, it’s a chat group. I’ve sat at sessions with gaps so long that the barman kept putting the stereo system on again, which was very annoying. A good leader knows when to break for a while and when to keep the pace up.

Finally, a reliable host with solid skills is worth their weight in gold. Without one, you are liable to show up one evening to find three guitars, two bodhrans, and no melody players. Or, you’ll get there and some loud birthday party has taken over your usual spot. Or, you’ll get there and there’s a gig on at the bar for a special occasion. These are all things that a reliable host would be able to prevent or remedy, but a less-structured session will end up running into at one point or another.

Now, all of these suppose a skilled, reliable, friendly, and small-egoed host. One that runs his/her session like a post-Soviet dictatorship is not going to make for a fun night. Those can be very draconian and unpleasant, and I would definitely try to stay away from those. I’m sure a lot of people who dislike more host-led sessions have been burned before.

That said best-run sessions I’ve been at have all had a good host. Oddly enough, they’re usually far more inclusive than ones where you’re supposed to pipe up organically, and far less dominated by domineering personalities. They’re reliably good, no matter the week. Having a good host also tends to keep the owner happy, and they’re less likely to get displaced or turfed out. Ultimately, I think #1 is the best way to run a session.

Davidread, here’s what a hosted session looks like. (names other than the hosts changed)

Session starts. I might kick off a set of common jigs on the pipes. Everyone knows the tunes, so they join in.

Set ends. I turn to someone and ask “Hey Bob, what ’cha got?” Bob might say, “I’m still warming up”. I say “Great, let me know when you’re ready to start a set. John, you want to start a set?” John says “Sure” and kicks off a set of reels.

Set ends, chat chat chat. George (my co-host) starts up a set of jigs.

Set ends. George might ask a visiting fiddler if they want to start a set. “Sure!” they say and we’re off to the races again.

An hour later I might say, “Hey, we haven’t played any slides all night! Anyone have some slides?” And Mary nods and looks like she’s got a set, kicks some off.

Rinse and repeat. Sometimes it’s even more subtle than that, there is no “control”, there is just flow and inclusion.

davidread, using the phrase “numerous sessions show” is a weak argument. Numerous sessions also have all kinds of issues as have been described on this and the “session from hell” thread that a hosted structure solves. Numerous sessions also have issues with players that don’t have good playing or social skills, strong egos, and who dominate or destroy the session, while others feel left out or resentful. Numerous sessions have every kind of positive and negative variant.

All I can say is our hosted structure works for our scene. The players report they like it when asked and continue coming back. It seems to scale well to the size of the group, which typically is 7 to 15. If we get down to just 4 or 5 players, we’ll seamlessly change to a more organic structure, particularly if everyone is a more experienced player. While having a very supportive venue and probably some luck, that we’re going on 20 years at the same location and night says this is a structure that works, at least for us and several other very long-standing sessions I know of with similar structure.

lynnhayes, regarding newer players, I just tell them how things work. I’ll then just include them in the session like anyone else “Hey Joe, what’s that set of hornpipes you were working on last week…”

I get it from those who object to any structure or hosted management of a session, that’s fine, we don’t have to agree. As long as you can find a session that matches your needs and works for you, great.

bogman, my question was about why some people have such strong objections to the concept of a hosted session where the hosts manage the flow and offer the players opportunities to start sets. I think one can find enjoyment in a hosted, unhosted, circle of death, or any other kind of session if one sets aside any preconceived notion or agenda one might have coming into someone else’s group and just go with the flow. It’s all dancing with what’s actually there, not some idealized notion of how things should be. This is what PB’s response was about. Watch, adapt.

This may be a bit of a cross-over with the Session from Hell discussion. You get to decide.

Was there mention of what I call the passive-organic session. You know the one, where everybody sits around (even good players) terrrified of starting a tune. Then one person has to, by default, start everything, maybe considerate of the tunes everybody might know (or not). Wouldn’t that be a de-facto session host? By the way I distinguish between a “leader” who directs everything and a host whose job it is to keep the peace and help things go smoothly. Too often somebody has to step up and face down the “gawd-awful” player/backer who doesn’t know or maybe doesn’t care, and help bring the enthusiastic “new-to-things” or new to the session, guy into the fold. Maybe at least part of a host’s job is to help grow new musicians. Somebody should stand between a good time and chaos. I recently attended a session where the socially skilled “host” did all of that in one, two hour, session and as they say“a good time was had by all”. And another where the session “leader” directed, say it with me, EVERYTHING. Sessions are painted with a big brush. If you’re in one that’s not making you happy it’s not your job. or mine, to change the world. Our options are to either suck it up or walk away. Personally I’d much prefer the session where everyone, the seasoned and the unseasoned, the regular players and the visitors, the players and the backers, were all invested in each other. Wonderful things can happen. Sadly that doesn’t happen everywhere and then maybe someone needs to be a good host.

Is it always about a hierarchy though? I can appreciate being invested in the experience of playing in a session whether it’s your local regular or one you are visiting on a trip. But it’s healthy to experience different types of sessions.

How much can you absorb from visiting a session of the highest calibre once; how invested can you be in sharing the experience of the other long term players? It might be helpful, inspirational, maybe even instructive.
Often the best investment isn’t what you contribute, but what you absorb.

Now consider sessions which are a notch down. Is there an opportunity in the *lesser* sessions which might make them approachable for a good, experienced player who is willing to listen & absorb something different; just not the *best* sessions in a hierarchial sense.

To me a hosted session is hosted because there is a mix of players. Those are the ones where a host (or cohosts) help. But they (hosts) have to be vigilant communicators/observers. BTW it’s not about being reluctant enforcers. Hosting is about acting appropriately to the health of the session based on monitoring it’s vital signs in real time.

Re: Hosted vs. unhosted sessions norms

Agree or disagree with me, but of course it’s a hierarchy, like any other activity where there is a very wide variety of experience and skill levels. Unfortunately, you can’t “handicap” the newer players like you could in a bowling league, so various session structure patterns have evolved based on the specifics of the local scene.

In our case, we have a wide range of skill sets, but mostly more experienced players, and a limited number of seats, and the newer players come early and know to give up their seats to the more advanced players later in the evening. Out of town visitors are always welcome, and we try to accommodate them with seats all night if possible. So we’re more a “meritocracy” than an “hierarchy”. Those who do the work reap the benefits over time. If there are just too many players (we really only have room for 9), we’ll often just voluntarily swap out seats to give everyone a chance to play.

Regarding that word “enforcement”, if you search back through the comments both my and other session hosts in the this thread, has never been mentioned in the context of normal flow of hosting a session. It only has been talked about in how to deal with those who, either through ignorance or active malice, threaten to damage the session. No shortage of horror stories on this site about “session wreckers” and other situations that have prematurely ended what could have been thriving session scenes if someone actually had the “authority” (there he goes again) to throw the miserable bastards out.

I can find value in all sessions I attend, whether it be just sitting out and listening to the most experienced masters play together or sitting in a with a group of beginners in a circle reading out of books. I just try to go with the flow, and if there is host, I won’t start a set unless invited, unless it’s clear that it’s an organic session and I’m welcome to start sets whenever I’m inspired. Watch, adapt, never impose.
