Picking binary tunes (on the bouzouki)

Hello,

I am a brand new bouzouki player since a trip to Ireland in May but I’m a long time guitarist. I am thus coming to the bouzouki with my guitar picking habits.

My practice when I play complex or long melodic parts on the guitar is that I choose the direction of the pick depending on where I want my pick to be in certain places, and not so much on the basis of the DUDU pattern (although DUDU remains the basis if nothing else factors in, of course). Like if I have to alternate repeatedly between two strings, one note per string, at some point of the phrase, I will do whatever I can to not have my pick stuck in-between the two strings doing ups on the lower-pitched string and downs on the higher-pitched string (I shiver at the notion).

That means sweeping over one or two strings, including up sweeps, going DD when switching to a higher pitch string, starting a phrase on the 1 beat with an upstroke because it’ll be convenient later etc.

HOWEVER, as I am practicing tunes on the bouzouki I seem to notice that some very uncomfortable situations on the guitar seem to not be so uncomfortable on the bouzouki (maybe the softer tensile strength of the string, or the smaller size?) ; playing jigs make this really stand out because the DUDDUD just doesn’t really leave much of a choice. I still have to figure out what I am comfortable with and I will probably adjust a lot of things as I progress but in the meantime my question is:

What is the common or “recommended” practice of Irish bouzouki players? What would make my fictional bouzouki teacher happy, if you will?

Is it to always keep the DUDU pattern, and train to do that cleanly? Or do you do the same sort of planning I described above?

Re: Picking binary tunes (on the bouzouki)

I always advise that flatpickers should pick the tune, not the pattern, so I would say your instincts are leading you in the right direction.

>That means sweeping over one or two strings, including up sweeps, going DD when switching
>to a higher pitch string, starting a phrase on the 1 beat with an upstroke because it’ll be convenient
> later etc.

This is a good list of useful techniques, all of which should feel quite natural and obvious when they’re the right thing to do, and all of which will give your playing more fluidity and avoid of the sort of sewing-machine rhythm that tends to come from strict pattern picking.

The other thing you might consider is making more use of left-hand articulations, hammer-ons and pull-offs. They’re not great for a strongly accented note, of course, but for passing notes they can really simplify your picking.

Re: Picking binary tunes (on the bouzouki)

Absolutely spot on, jeiaz. At the risk of labouring the point, it’s also always worth considering “portamenti “ - “carried notes” - when the tune or circumstances suggest, ie sliding up or down the same string, either permitting the sound of the “carry” or just zipping up instantly to the second note. This particularly seems to work well for slow airs, when ironically pick strokes may not be in point. However, in a fast tune (and assuming you use DADG) it can be handy to, for example, slide the first finger second fret first string to 4the or 5th fret first string, This gives you an effortless move to 4th/5th position and access to likely useful notes on 7 fret on both first and second strings.

Re: Picking binary tunes (on the bouzouki)

Probably the most important thing to be thinking about before worrying about any of this, of course, is getting a good relaxed hold on the pick. Tension is the enemy of fluidity, and any decisions you make while you’re playing with a tense hand will be wrong ones.

Basically, holding the pick in rest position should involve no muscular exertion at all, and the force of your thumb or forefinger driving the stroke is all the exertion you should use. What this means is you should never be “clutching” the pick - applying pressure with both thumb and forefinger. Some people get this naturally, others struggle with it, and some people tense up at times and lapse into clutching when nervous, but when someone goes from clutching to a relaxed grip, things get a lot nicer.
One reason why this is important is the question raised earlier about emphasis. If you use a tight, clenched grip, then you do indeed have to think about where your emphasis is going to go and how to get it, because your default is “full on”. Once you get to the default of a fully relaxed grip, emphasis becomes automatic, because it’s just a matter of an almost imperceptible tightening of that grip.

So I’d probably start there - make sure that you’re playing with a relaxed grip, and if you find this is new to you, spend a little time getting used to it. Once you’ve got that, everything else will fall into place.

One thing I should add, though, is that some guitarists compensate for their clutch by using floppy strings and picks - basically, they move their relaxation out into their gear. If you’re stringing light (say, a .010“ e string or lighter) and using a floppy pick, this is a chance to try a somewhat heavier set (.013” is what I usually use) and a stiffer pick. (I like the John Pearse Studio Blacks, but the good old Jim Dunlop orange is similar and highly available). This may require a bit adjustment, but on most guitars it’ll immediately open up a lot more tone and a lot more control and precision, so it’s probably worth a go.

Re: Picking binary tunes (on the bouzouki)

Extrapolating from tenor banjo to bouzouki, if you have any of the old Sully’s tenor banjo instruction books, where he provides the pick direction for every note, there are many examples of what I’d refer to as “most efficient path” picking, often minimizing direction changes.

In this case he deviates from DUDU or DUD DUD to whatever is required to provide the most efficient path through the tune.

So, if in jig the tune goes DAB then the picking would be

DDU

or in a reel DABE, the picking would be

DDUU

Watching tenor banjo players over the years, both in person and on YouTube, it seems to me that I see this style of “most efficient path” picking done mostly by native Irish players, while the more regimented DUDU and DUD DUD patterns more common for non-native Irish players. I find that interesting.

The feel of the “most efficient path” playing seems in many cases a lot more relaxed with a lovely lift, and it does lend itself to more fluid fast playing since you don’t have unnecessary reversals.

I’m not saying that there are plenty of fine players who play strict DUDU and DUD DUD picking patterns, just that the “most efficient path” method is interesting, can produce lovely results, and is worth exploring.

In my case, I learned to play DUDU and DUD DUD early on and find that “most efficient path” messes too much with my head, but I’m only an occasional tenor banjo or bouzouki melody player these days.

“every note being picked”, that’s a good start, but eventually you discover that you only need to pick the important notes and that you can skip (ghost stroke) a lot of the extra/unnecessary ones, and make/create space in the music. “bluegrass and metal” - the time line does not work out. metal is 1970-ies, Bluegrass is Bill Monroe is 1940-ies, people played Irish music on mandolin and banjo in the 1890-ies (Edison wax cylinders) and alternate picking method was already well established.
