A Splinter Discussion

Mike Richardson’s post on how folks learn tunes inspired me to ask: How much do you have to love a tune before you will make the effort to learn it?
I emphasize “effort,” because many of us learn on the fly or by virtue of repeated calls for a tune at our sessions. I’m talking about tunes you have to take time to sit down and learn.

After almost 40 years at this, my repertoire is not extensive, but I probably know around 200 tunes I never tire of playing. I don’t know if you, gentle reader, get excited by most or all of the tunes your lot introduce, but I’m kind of picky. If I like a tune, I’ll get it down in short order. If it don’t grab, it’s forget-it city. In time, I kind of fake my way through, but that is as close as I’m likely to get.

So, what are your thoughts?

As I said in my comment in Mike’s discussion, I directly relate learning Irish trad tunes to learning a language. In my opinion, learning tunes has lots of things in common with learning a language.

I went to Ireland in 2004 with 24 years old and I didn’t talk English at all. I went on my own to the pub sessions in Galway like mad and I could play, but I couldn’t talk or understand the musicians. And It went like this for a few years. I was the perfect definition of what in Ireland some people call a “blow in”, a foreigner that comes to the session like brought by a “gust of wind” knowing how to play already.

A few years passed and my English became good. I learned the language. And I can tell you now that the learning process I went through with the language has many things in common with the learning of tunes. Specially because I learned how to play 100% by hear.

Talking about love (:-D), at the beginning I loved all the tunes. I wanted to learn them all. What I always did and still do is to learn the tune by hear, even if It’s not correct, and I practise it. If I don’t get it perfect I don’t stay there forever, I practise for a while and then I move on to another tune. With that I always  end up knowing how to play many tunes but all of them with mistakes. And I keep at it. Because I love playing even if I don’t do it well. But after a few years I ended up playing many tunes well.

The thing is that I started not loving many tunes, I became picky. Even I started going less to some sessions that play too many tunes that I didn’t feel like playing or I simply refused to play some of the tunes even if I knew how to play them.

In my opinion, I think sessions can sometimes be a bit misleading because they can give you a very small picture of all the huge repertoire that exists in Irish Traditional Music. Specially if there are musicians working in the session because it happens quite a lot that they play the same repertoire for years.

Of course there are exceptions to this, a good one I can think of It’s the session that Paul Bradley (fiddle) and Declan Corey (mandolin) used to have every week (not sure if they are still doing it) in Tig Coili in Galway city. Superb musicians. I know they had that session for at least 20 years. They play sessions completely full of very uncommon tunes for about two hours but they are able to change any time to “standard” session tunes if needed, usually when more musicians join in to the session. And they could be playing those standard tunes for hours as well.

Re: A Splinter Discussion

Like Fernando, when I was relatively new to traditional music, I wanted to learn every tune. Certainly, there were some tunes that I didn’t really ‘get’ aesthetically (and others that I was not ready for technically) but a big factor was being able to participate and contribute to the music as a whole. It seemed natural, therefore to try and acquire as many tunes as I could, to increase my chances of being able to join in a session. Whenever I bought a new trad recording, almost the first thing I would do (after a couple of listens) was write down all the tunes; I also spent hours browsing through tune collections online, playing through the tunes either on an instrument or in my head. I do not have the kind of mind that can ‘memorise’ tunes in that way, but I would often find that enough of the tune would remain lodged in my brain that, when the tune came up in a session, I would be halfway to playing it (although, in many cases, the other ‘half’ of the process is still ongoing some 25 years later…).

In fact, I’m not sure that things have changed all that much for me. I no longer feel the urgency to ‘build repertoire’ for its own sake, so the emphasis has shifted to learning the tunes I like (How much? I’m not sure how to quantify that). But a major factor that drives me to play music is communication - i.e. ‘conversation’ between musicians. As such, I still feel a strong motivation to play whatever my fellow musicians play (which, where I live now, is mostly non-Irish), even if the tune is not one from which I would get much pleasure playing it on my own; the challenge and the reward is making something great out of very little.

“What’s missing from the discussion is how difficult a tune is to learn and play. I had a bunch of tunes that I could play on mandolin that increased in difficulty…”

That comment made me pause.

My knee-jerk reaction was “all tunes are the same”. Because I can read though a big book of session reels, jigs, and hornpipes and they seem the same.

Of course on whistle there’s an advantage of being able to put tunes in keys other than one sharp or two sharps on different-sized whistles, an option not open to many/most players of other instruments.

So for the moment setting aside tunes that aren’t in one sharp or two sharps, they pretty much seem the same “level of difficulty”.

Seems to me that the matter of “level of difficulty” is related to a person’s technical command of their instrument. It’s why orchestral and studio musicians practice scales and arpeggios and other exercises rather than tunes: they never know what they will be required to play when they show up at a gig, but whatever twists and turns the sheet music throws at them they must be able to handle it. (Their livelihood depends on it.)

In like manner I think a trad player who has all the possible note-sequences under their fingers won’t be flummoxed by any session tune.

That is true, of course, but not every trad player has. First, my thoughts on this are from a performance perspective, not a Sessions one. Because we used the stage not only to entertain, but to educate (to a limited degree) the audience on the Irish and Scottish history that inspired the tune, that was a big factor in adding tunes/songs to our set list. Vocal or Instrumental it didn’t matter. We discovered fairly early on, that audience interest in the set, required a ratio of 3:2 vocal over instrumental. That was hard to accept because we had started with an emphasis on the instrumentals. So we had to adapt to that.
Pleasant and interesting, as well as challenging melodies. A Slow Air is fine every now and then, but 45 minutes of Slow Airs finds more people walking away from the performance. Singing Raglan Road plucks the heart strings, but A Nation Once Again gets the blood flowing, and the audience participating.
At the height of our popularity, we could perform almost 4 hours of music. We didn’t play for 4 hours straight, usually 3 45 to 50 minute sets, with an introduction between each tune or medley. With that much material, we could very the sets enough to insure a different show every week. A few tunes/songs crossed set lists. There were a number of tunes that were dropped during our 16 year run, but quite a number remained during that time, and several ended up with different arrangements than what they originally started out with.
Members left and joined during that time, usually to relocation. We started as a 5 piece, went as high as 8 members, and ended as a quarter, when live music died in the area in 2006, thanks to amateur Open Mic nights and bar/tavern/pub owners being cheapskates. There’s another dimension to it, and that is as a player learns a tune they will usually (perhaps unconsciously) adapt the tune to their particular instrument.

It comes up fairly often here where a person has difficulty with a particular passage and when I (and others) look at the setting referenced the problem is that they’re trying to play a version that’s specific to a different instrument. Each instrument has things that it likes to do and things that it doesn’t like to do, and adapting tunes to your own instrument’s stylistic tendencies (and your own personal style) is an essential part of learning any tune, unless the setting’s source player plays the same instrument you do.

As somebody once said “there are no difficult tunes, only difficult settings”.

It’s a quirk of how the Highland chanter works, and the partially-closed Highland fingering, that certain note-combinations that fall dead-easy under a Highland piper’s fingers are deadly on other instruments.

The very thing you mention, leaping octaves with A, and also with G, fall right under the Highland piper’s fingers. Perfect examples are the original Highland pipe settings of Clumsy Lover, and The Shetland Fiddler, chock-full of these big leaps from low notes to high a.

Re: A Splinter Discussion

As Calum rightly points out, one of the things that makes these tunes with super quick jumping between high a’s and lower notes be so Highland-pipe-centric is the way High A on the Highland chanter is more quiet than the low notes and can sort of disappear into the harmonics produced by the drones.

So, on many of these Highland pipe tunes the high a’s don’t really function as notes as much as they function as silences between notes, creating a pseudo-staccato effect.

Then when you play the same tune on Scottish smallpipes, where the volumes of the notes is reversed, those high a’s jump out as full-fledged notes, spoiling the intended effect but creating a new one.

That’s the thing with, say, fiddlers playing these Highland pipe compositions, if they bow back and forth between high a they loose the original Highland pipe effect. I’ve heard fiddlers leave out those “silent” a’s and bow the notes detached, which is the effect on the Highland pipes.

Re: A Splinter Discussion

I sometimes love playing a tune because a friend has a bee in their bonnet about it and sharing it with them is a pleasure so I’ll play it until they’ve left it by. Sometimes its a recording that I love and I find I enjoy playing the tune alone in a particular style but the way the tune tends to lean in the community doesn’t work and I let it go for regular play. There are tunes that I love and I learn, and then I cannot find the right style in my playing to get the tune to sing so it falls away. There are tunes that I learn and discover that learning it was the adventure and they fall away. I find that any of these can be resurrected and do pop back up, either it comes to mind, I hear the recording and can’t resist picking up the instrument to play it again, I meet someone new and am curious whether it’s something we share, or a friend comes ’round to a tune I’ve been testing the waters with over an age or like a good book, it’s the right companion of the moment. This excludes the tunes that I’ve dragged into local use because I fit them into my sets around other popular things, the tunes that come out of my flute, whistle or concertina when I first pick them up, or the things that I carefully pick back up periodically to examine because they evoke dear moments.
I think our relationships with tunes are like book lovers relationships to books or human relationships. There are the tunes you should like because they appear to tick all the boxes but you never quite take them to your heart, the tunes you play everywhere and they know your partner’s name and you’d share a drink but not much more, the tunes you work hard to get to know because there’s something there for you but it’s work, and then the tunes you could almost swear you were whistling last week and you can pick up your instrument and play but they’re new. Of course that excludes the popular, shiny tunes everyone wants to know but they might just be a flash in the pan, popular while their celebrity is high and then forgotten when an album gets a bit older…
