Cello?

Way back in the 1600s and 1700s, Country Dancing was THE thing to do, and, because the melody would repeat many times for a given dance, the professional musicians were expected to be able to improvise a little on each pass through the melody. My observation today is that, since classically trained musicians are taught to play the score exactly as written, many of them are not comfortable with improvisation. I live in Azle, and play really old melodies, mostly Irish, Scottish, and English dance tunes strictly for recreation (i.e., no money involved). We have no score, but do have chord charts for all the tunes in the repertoire. We are all acoustic and, for decades, have been using a cello for the bass component - the cellist would improvise what to play using the chord chart and the key and mode of the tune - its a form of composing. It is a unique opportunity. Anyone interested? My cello really wants to be played!

Re: Cello?

The cello appeared alongside the fiddle as a melody instrument in mid-18th century Scotland, in the performance of traditional dance tunes that remain in the ITM repertoire. A description from 1764 stated that, “this instrument may be consider’d as a Large Fiddle, only held the contrary way….” It was also used as an accompaniment instrument, providing a simple bass line. There’s a detailed review of its use in both roles in traditional contexts at https://loopholes.blog/accompaniment/

“we cannot know what those baroque-era cellist actually played.”
I think we have a pretty good idea what they were doing, at least in Scotland: There are a number of manuscripts from the first half of C18 (i.e. Francis Barsanti) which were published with figured bass accompaniment. The notes on the stave are the cello part, the figures tell a harpsichord how to harmonize.

Re: Cello?

If you’re referring to the article I linked to above, it deliberately doesn’t mention any present-day cellists by name. That’s because before preparing such a list, I would like to know who of those it might include are directly rooted in traditional practice (as Sharon Howley, https://youtu.be/r4FGldkj9Rk ) or started as classically trained cellists (as Caroline Lavelle, https://youtu.be/R1Bn-Xtkb2M ).

There is no intrinsic reason why an individual musician can’t move between genres. However, doing so in a musically convincing manner entails a clear, if not to say daunting, burden of becoming conversant with both idioms. My personal touchstone for the snap assessment of a cello performance in the ITM context is whether its player uses vibrato as an ornament, or turns it on automatically whenever a note is long enough to sustain it.

If it is safe to say that the majority of cellists heard at ITM sessions have classical backgrounds, the varying opinions expressed here about the value of that instrument’s contribution probably reflect the extent to which its player has accommodated the less familiar nuance. This isn’t the same thing as an aversion to the cello, but doesn’t preclude it being deemed inappropriate, anyway.

Although a topic for separate discussion, I’m quite interested in the factors that determine which of the instruments that occasionally make guest appearances at sessions, ultimately end up being accepted in the ordinary line up. The alien bouzoukis and digital pianos have certainly gained entry, and the cello seems to be well along the same path.

Re: Cello?

It takes skill, sensitivity, dedication, and knowledge of the repertoire you’re going to be playing to do well with a cello in a typical Irish session setting context. It’s far from impossible but takes focus and it takes time to develop the tunes and the chops you’ll need to not make a mess of things. By all means, experiment. Just, please, talk to people, develop rapport, learn their favorites and how you can make the group sound better, and try to do as much of it offline as possible. The finished product will be worth it if you’re all sufficiently dedicated to achieving something musical. The majority of the effort however is going fall on the cellist if the cellist is unfamiliar with Irish repertoire and style. It can be done but it takes humility and a great deal of effort.
