Not enough arms…

Now, I’m aware that this is probably going to be major heresy to many; it feels that way to me too – but at the moment, this is a purely hypothetical question, and sometimes needs must for those of us who live in places where competent and willing trad musicians are rarer than hens’ teeth… What seems especially rare is backers who know enough about the music to do more than boom-chuck on three chords.

Those of us who do both tunes and backing don’t have enough arms to do everything we need simultaneously, so the thought reluctantly enters the head about getting technology to do a minimum of it compatible with making a more complete sound (e.g. guitar/bouzouki etc) while the live musicians play the tunes.

So does anyone have any experience of having done this sympathetically enough (including while performing) not to get lynched by the traditionalists? Both in terms of equipment and techniques used, and the success or otherwise of the end result.

Many thanks for any thoughts…
Ian

I too have played many gigs of unaccompanied trad instrumentals to general audiences, often just a duo act, and we’ve always been well received. It helps to vary the music, with slower pieces between the uptempo jigs and reels, and we might do a few tunes with bits of harmonies or counterpoint. I’ve also done many gigs with just me on fiddle and a very good bodhran player. It’s a bit daunting to be the only one carrying the tunes for 2 or 3 hours, but it’s doable, and you find ways to vary things and connect with the listeners. The crux of it is that we were honest and transparent about the music we could offer. The people who booked us knew what they were getting, and it was what they wanted. In many cases, they sought us out because we were different from the usual folk, pop, classic rock, jazz, show tunes, singer-songwriter, country western, etc.

@ Stiamh, hah! I almost posted that Rick Beato link. It’s a good perspective on the wider world of music.

When I was gigging in a duo and sometimes trio on mandolin with a guitar player playing both “Celtic” and other music, we often thought about how to get a bigger sound. I tried some things, like a “foot pedal bass” thumper. I had heard Solas use that in a live performance, but it was too much trouble. And you can’t introduce that kind of thing except late in the show, because once the audience hears it, they’ll miss it if it’s not on every set.

Looping was an option, laying down more complex lines behind the melody , and I had already experimented with the hardware for that as a solo player. But you have to be really on your game with that. One misstep on hitting the pedal at *exactly* the right time and you’re screwed.

So we just decided we’re acoustic musicians and that’s what people are coming to see and hear. Loops and pre-recorded tracks can work for some genres like big stage Rock and Pop music, but if you’re selling yourself at a gig as a Folk-adjacent acoustic act, that just undercuts the whole premise.

Re: Not enough arms…

I would think for a session, which isn’t a stage performance but humans interacting having fun playing music, that introducing recorded backing would feel alien to the “vibe”.

On the other hand, back in the 1980s I played in a Ceili Band that used synthesized backup very effectively.

It was a money thing! We had four guys (accordion, fiddle, banjo, and flute) and a four-way split of the money was already bad enough.

Since a full Ceili Band sound was called for, we reckoned we needed a piano, a standup bass, and a drum. Our boxplayer synth’d those parts creating sets of 3 reels, 3 jigs, etc. and made it so each set looped, so we could play as long as the dancers wanted. The chords he used were very nice, not cretinous 1-4-5 stuff.

The boxplayer started and stopped the backing track with a foot pedal. In performance it worked seamlessly.

One brilliant thing is that the boxplayer could adjust the tempo on the fly. Say we’re playing at 116 and the dancers signal us they want it a hair quicker the boxplayer could type in 118 and the backing track would smoothly speed up to the new tempo.

“What kinds of gigs” is an interesting point. If what you play is straightforward acoustic trad, you’d be swimming against the current to book yourselves into a punk/grunge or jazz club or for an evening with the Ralph Vaughan Williams Appreciation Society.

One of the small, local trad bands I played in many years ago did all sorts of gigs, from wakes, weddings, and community celebrations to large festivals. But the mainstay of our diet was trad-oriented events: house band at a local pub, fundraisers for Irish-oriented organizations, supporting the area ceili and step dance groups, opening for touring trad bands, etc. So when people came to that pub or those events, they weren’t surprised to hear trad music.

Whenever we played more generic events or venues, we’d pull out all the stops—play out most energetic sets with as much fire as we could muster. People respond with passion to music played with passion.

Ian, you wrote: “experiences that I find rewarding . . . arranging sets of tunes together and creating sympathetic instrumental settings for them, and working on them in a rather tighter way than most sessions permit.”

All well and good. Here’s some food for thought based on long experience gigging with a friend (mandolin player) who loved those same aspects of preparing for performance. The critical key in this is whether the arranged tunes, sympathetic settings, and tight performance all serve to grab your listeners. It’s possible to put a lot of time and effort into careful, clever sets and arrangements and precision playing that leave audiences cold. Also, a band can get so “tight” from striving for perfection through over-rehearsal that it loses any spark and spontaneity. In the band I mention above, we found a compromise with our detail-minded mandolin player. Because we all worked day jobs, our cohesion came less from rehearsal and more from close listening to and interaction with one another on stage. We played premeditated sets but encouraged improvised variations, and at any time one player could call to keep a given tune going, so we had to be ready to go with the flow. This kept us engaged in the music and each other, and it allowed our own pulse and groove to build intensity when we were so moved. This helped greatly in connecting with audiences, trad and non-trad alike.

Re: Not enough arms…

Ian, glad that my post caused no pain, certainly none was intended.

Yes, it’s a challenge to play this music with respect, whether for knowledgeable listeners (where you hope they can hear that you’ve done your homework) or for a mob that wouldn’t know a hornpipe from a horned lark (where you at least hope the sots and their bottles stay on the other side of the chicken wire).

I think we all get that this is what you’re wanting to do, while not unnecessarily estranging your audiences. And this thread has been a rich one, with a lot to ponder for all of us.

A good chunk of Martin Hayes’ recently published memoir, Shared Notes, describes his struggles with this very issue. Spoiler alert! In the end, he learns to trust his roots and the music that’s deeply his own, and to “let go,” in the sense of not controlling how anyone receives his music.

To be clear, this is Martin Hayes, so his well of music is especially deep, the tradition is the one he grew up in, and he’s no slouch of a fiddler. It’s telling that even Martin was at first anxious about how audiences might respond. And who did he choose to record and perform with? A guitarist! (RIP Dennis Cahill.)

From the book, and also from earlier published interviews, it’s also clear that Martin and the musicians he works with typically go to some pains to prepare music for performance.

So Ian, your instincts aren’t wrong.

Martin also tells a powerful story of recording the album Triur sa Draighean at Peadar O‘Riada’s home in West Cork. O’Riada has composed a bunch of tunes, and keeps adding new ones as Martin, Peadar, and Caoimhin O’Raghallaigh quickly learn them and record them. Martin says he’s the slowest of the three to get each new tune, and he assumes that when the mics are on, they’re simply capturing ideas to listen to and refine to record in earnest at a later date. Martin approached it as a “preliminary exercise” and says he “hadn’t so much as checked my microphone position for recording.” Then, we he packs up to leave, Peadar says, “I think we have an album here.” Stunned, Martin is even more astonished when he receives the CD at how good it sounds. “I didn’t even think we were making a recording,” he says.

For Martin, the lesson is this: “The attitude and level of trust we bring to the creative process is hugely important and really does impact the outcome, and sometimes, attempting to control the outcome gets in the way of letting the better music just happen. For me to make good music, the singular most important thing I try to achieve is to become fully present and mindful in the act itself, fearlessly letting go and trusting the outcome.”

Yes, it’s perhaps easier to let go and trust in the outcome when you’re Martin Hayes, Peadar O‘Riada, or Caoimhin O’Raghallaigh. But I’m persuaded by my own more down-to-earth musical experiences that this practice can help us mortals play better, too. And that such music has a fair chance of resonating with audiences.
