The Pure Drop for the middle classes.

Well, what do you think ?
I’m reading of people who have large four-figure sums available for purchasing ( or have already purchased ) their next instrument, some who own several already.
Consider this; the wooden flute only came into ITM when it was rendered obsolete, and thus cheap, by the introduction of the Boehm system metal flute for orchestral music. The concertina usually played in ITM is the Anglo, because these were the cheap ones, the potentially more flexible English costing up to £20 in Victorian times and thus being well out of the reach of the working classes. I could go on.
Many people seem to have large amounts of disposable income to support their hobby in ITM. What happened to the music of the people ? Are we now a bunch of middle-class winkers ?

Re: The Pure Drop for the middle classes.

There are a bunch of issues wrapped into one.

Most folk music has its origins in inexpensive instruments. Part of the nature of the beast.

The price of a given style of instrument varies wildly over time. The wooden flute came into Irish music because the orchestras were dumping them. It is expensive now because it is hand made by a handful of craftsmen, where silver flutes are mass produced and cheap.

The instrument makers spend a great deal of time and money mastering their craft, and they aren’t getting rich off it. I’m just happy there are still people willing to do it (and that many of them still invest the time in selling student or starter instruments).

Heirloom quality instruments are, if all goes well, going to outlive their owners. Even if they are commissioned because someone fancies a top tier instrument before they can play it, barring accident, they will still be here in a generation or three.

That being said, a lot of things depend on the local musical community. If someone shows up with a $75 silver flute, and wants to learn to play traditional tunes, are you going to show them the door until they come back with a wooden flute? Can they show up with a cheap guitar and a desire to learn tunes? If they can, then the cost of traditional instruments isn’t a barrier to the music.

Re: The Pure Drop for the middle classes.

One hundred per cent completely right, Guernsey.

For a long time people think I am taking the p*ss when I say a good mandolin, £90 (Ozark), Guitar (yamaha 310) ideal for sessions, £90, world’s best bodhran £120, whistle £3, and so on, but I am serious. That’s all you need for a session. If you are going to be a serious pro, maybe spend a bit more to get a better sound, on stage through amplification, but spending £3,000 to play at a session on a Lowden guitar should be a crime. Especially after someone spills a table full of drink on it, and then knocks it over 3 times.

I too thought “is everyone rich”, and then in a music shop in Geordie land Newcastle, I realised the truth.

There was a Martin guitar, less than half price for about £500, beautiful sound. And It suddenly came to me, walk up, give them a piece of plastic called a credit card, and away you go. The guitar would have been for my son, a £35 new classical Stagg or Lauren guitars are good enough for me, even for radio performances. So it is the credit card, the answer to all, that I think is the cause. And the less money you have, the more companies want to give you a card.

From a social history viewpoint, what used to be the working class are now financially “middle” class, although it does not stretch to Sam Murray flutes or Lowden guitar, let alone pipes, in most instances. Joiners (carpenters) actually earn more than plumbers per se, and could afford such items. The working class have been replaced alas by the “underclass”, and they all have credit cards.

To conclude, for a normal session, and sitting in houses playing, cheap instruments can be very rewarding. Look out for Chinese zouks, mandolas and mandolins, Blue Star or something, which have a great sound for little money. The son’s electric guitar, Gould (made in China) is sensational for about £200.

Here endeth the lesson.
