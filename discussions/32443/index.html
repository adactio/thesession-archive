Added by bigsciota on September 11th, 2013

Why always with the reels?

I’m not a big fan of them. Sure, it’s fun to rip into a reel or two and all that, but I’d much rather play a jig, polka, slip jig, slide, waltz, or hornpipe. And the funny thing is, I’ll go to a session, start a set of slip jigs/polkas/hornpipes or something, and everyone around seems to agree with me that there should be more slip jigs/polkas/hornpipes played. Then, that session will go back to being 60-70% reels.

There must be some overriding reason for this. I’m quite far from being an expert on ITM, so would someone who is be able to enlighten me on why this is? My theories are that

A) Dancers prefer reels, the music is dance music, therefore, most tunes played are reels.
B) There are just more reels in the repertoire than anything else, so it makes sense that most tunes played are reels.
C) The preference for reels comes from their “flashiness” and ability to be played at breakneck speeds, therefore showcasing how awesome the player is.
C.2) This “flashiness” made them popular among bands, and people listen to and enjoy those bands and want to play their tunes at sessions.
D) Reels have just always been the most prevalent tunes around, and so people keep perpetuating this preference as this is “how it’s always been done.”

I know this board has had many the wind-up thread, but I’m not trying to bash reels or anything. Just wondering how they came to be the most popular type of tune played. Are any of my theories true? Or some combination of all of them? Or are they completely wrong and I should be ashamed of myself?

Re: Why always with the reels?

As a follow-up to the foregoing comment, I looked up Reels in the Companion to Irish Music compiled by Fintan Vallely. Reels are identified as the most common form of dance tune in Ireland. The book dates reels back to the 1700s. Jigs are mentioned earlier, but tunes that were called jigs were not necessarily in the time of double jigs. Consider Kemp’s Jig, which is in four. However you slice it, though, I am reluctant to accept reels in Ireland as recent after over 200 years, since most of the ITM repertoire of any stripe seems to come from within that time period.

Re: Why always with the reels?

Historical perspective: piper Tom Busby said the musicians he learned from in NYC in the 1930s mostly played reels, some jigs, a few hornpipes or airs. But mostly it was reels they liked; so this isn’t anything new. In general I’d say it’s true of the musicians on most of the old recordings I’ve heard as well, regardless of what part of Ireland they were from, excepting Sliabh Luachra of course, where you have musicians who never bother with reels at all.

Older musicians often played a wider variety of tunes too, though. Gatherings of Irish used to have a bit of everything, not just music - you’d have songs, stories, whatnot. Not a bad way to go, eh? All of these types of tunes are great fun in their own way; reels can be the most challenging, of course, but who wants to spend a whole evening being challenged?

It’s expected that you’ll know more than your fair share if you want to sit in at a proper session, mind. If you don’t like reels, well, you’re like a baker who doesn’t like flour, to throw in my own analogy.

↳ Posted by Lucy Rietmann on September 12th, 2013.

Re: Why always with the reels?

I’ve also run into that phenomenon where the individuals at a session say they prefer a more varied mix of jigs, slides, or polkas, and yet the actual session is consistently dominated by reels. Usually played as fast as the dominant members of the group can play them.

I think there might be several reasons. One is a bit of friendly competition, since reels are often seen as being more difficult to play at speed. Another reason might be a subconscious motivation to use reels as a sort of high bar for maintaining a standard at the session (i.e. “keeping the riff-raff out”), since beginners often start with polkas and jigs, and work their way up to full-tempo reels. Or it might just be that a particular group just loves reels. Hard to tell, sometimes.

Up until the 1950s 6/8 tunes were often played by US fiddlers such as Bob Walters of Nebraska. The tunes were known as “quadrilles”, and some were jigs learned from Canadian AM radio broadcasts.
