Re: Northern Flute Music: Why is it bouncy?

I know Harry Bradley cites John McKenna as a big influence but obviously there’s a broader Northern style to his playing too.

I think the Northern style is less ornamented but no less lyrical than other regional styles and possibly more agressive and exuberant too; in fact a bit like Donegal fiddle playing. Leitrim incidentally, where McKenna is from, is right next to Donegal.

There are probably a whole raft of reasons for this style, including history (e.g. influence of marching bands and music) , landscape, geography (proximity to Scotland, Donegal); if you could put your finger on any one as the defining reason then I take my hat off to you.

Re: Northern Flute Music: Why is it bouncy?

Well, hard to explain. And yes, the word “north” is a bit obscure.

I started learning whistle in Scotland, and my teacher’s bouncy style was a big influence on my playing. After I left the country, I was encouraged to play more smoothly in workshops in Sligo and some other parts of Ireland. I tried but always failed. All the Irish flute players I knew at that time were Matt Molloy, Kevin Crawford, Mike McGoldrick, Liam Kelly, and Seamus Egan, and so I thought something was wrong with my playing style. Then I got Hammy Hamilton’s solo album and found his style is similar to mine.

Of course, all the northern flute players have developed their own individual styles, receiving inspirations from the musicians from different parts of the country. I heard that Harry Bradley also argues there’s no regional style as such. And I remember the Armagh flute player in London said to me that Peter Horan was her hero. (I’m sure she is one of your friends, Conan.) But their playing styles always remiends me of my Scottish whistle teacher’s bouncy style. This is all from my personal experience.

Re: Northern Flute Music: Why is it bouncy?

LOL, LongNote, the reverb in the stairwell may have smoothed out some of your attack, but I think you’ve described your style very accurately. And it’s a lovely sound you make. But I don’t know enough about alleged regional flute styles to pin you into one or another.

I tend to group fluters (in my own mind) by how much they use glottal stops and whether those stops are used simply to articulate certain notes or are brought into play more heavily, the intent clearly being to lend pulse or “bounce” to the music.

I haven’t studied this for long--I’m much more familiar with (and tremendously more confused about) regional and personal fiddle styles. But most fluters who do the heavy glottal stops seem also to tend toward short rolls over long rolls, overblowing (“barking”), and a less florid (ornamented) style. In effect, their sound tends to parallel that of northern fiddlers, which may give rise to the sense that they sound like northern fluters even when they come from Cork.

In short, a fluter’s style depends an awful lot on how much they lean into notes with their breath and use the air column (glottal stops included) to work the rhythm. Players on the gentler end of the spectrum tend to use more finger ornamentation (more cuts, taps, rolls, and slides) than players who huff and puff and cut notes with their glottis. In my own anecdotal, purely personal, untutored and no doubt stupid experience, I’ve fallen into what feels like a ‘natural’ use of my breathe to add pulse and lean into phrases on flute. Even glottal stops came into it, on their own. I listen to fluters whose sound I like, and I’m gradually being influenced by them. But I think I lean toward fluters whose sound is closest to my own natural tendencies anyway. I suppose it works this way for most folks. Which maybe dilutes the notion of regional style some, for flutes. Or maybe I’m just p*ssing into the wind….

Re: Northern Flute Music: Why is it bouncy?

Another way to think of it is that some fluters use glottal stops and explosive bursts of air to create an energetic, aggressive pulse (as exemplified by Conal O’Grada, and also Gary Hastings and Harry Bradley, say), while others use glottal stops and more subtle changes in air pressure to ebb and swell the dynamics, often for rhythmic effect as much as to distinguish musical phrases (a la Kevin Crawford, Brian Finnegan, and Tom McElvogue). But the two approaches produce very different overall styles. Of course, most Irish fluters do some sort of overblowing or air bursts now and then, particularly when playing for dancers, so this matrix isn’t an either/or sort of thing.

Crikey, I hate to think *I’m* really shedding light on any of this--these are just my personal musings, based on what crosses my mind when listening to various fluters, after 30 years of obsessively listening to fiddlers for the same sorts of distinctions.

The same muscles you employ to do vibrato or imitate a machine gun (boys will understand this) is what you employ -- but with a rhythm that matches the tune. Re: Northern Flute Music: Why is it bouncy?

Part of the reason why what typifies “northern” flute playing is bouncey is because of its pulse punctuated rhythm. Because of marching band flute playing the articulation is mostly diaphragmic pulses and glottals so you get a very pulsey bounce to the rhythm. But then you also have northern players like Tara Diamond and Cathal McConnell that play in a relatively smooth style.

Of course nowadays regional styles are disappearing with the introduction of modern media, travel and recordings. Hence it would be less easy to discern where a flute player is from just by listening, eg. from the WFO cds where a good half just sound like they come from the region of County Molloy. The fact that Slainte was encouraged to play more smoothly in workshops in Sligo is another example of this. Listen to old styled Sligo players, Kevin Henry, Peter Horan, James Murray etc, I don’t think you would consider their styles smooth at all. Also as mentioned previously yes, there would be variations in personal style within a regional eg. with East Clare fiddlers Paddy Canny and PJ Hayes. Additionally Counties are fairly big, certain characteristics popularised by the key players in this town may be different from characteristics favoured in that of a neighbouring town.

However, despite the limitations of discussing Irish music in terms of regional style, the music of past musicians (and some contemporary players) would generally have strong distinctive factors that identify it as coming from a same region (due to being exposured to only a limited number of musicians from around the village). If you’ve done your homework and listened to old musicians, you’ll find that its hard to confuse the rhythm of any old East Clare musician with a Sligo musician, or any old Kerry musician with a Donegal musician and so on. Most notably, the rhythms in the playing are distinctively different.

Earlier on someone mentioned Matt Molloy as a hugely influencial flute player. This is undeniable, but Matt Molloy isn’t exactly a good example of someone who plays in a regional style. By the time he’s made a name for himself and gotten a whole bunch of followers his style has become the result of a mix of different musicians from different places and personal modifications.
