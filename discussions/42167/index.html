Re: Concertina Progress: Slides, Polkas, Barndances, a Slip-Jig, and a Fling.

Watch out for pushing so hard with the fingers that your joints invert. It costs you feel and also time. You only need to touch hard enough to get the button to lightly touch the bottom. Try to hold the passive end still, all of the rhythm needs to be played with the active end. Look at the way the active end changes direction. The end is flapping because you are reversing direction by pushing on the handrest or pulling on the strap. Watch Edel Fox on youtube for an example of efficient bellows reversal. She is fanning open and lifting closed. Hey, nice feel to your music…

Re: Concertina Progress: Slides, Polkas, Barndances, a Slip-Jig, and a Fling.

I like your playing, Jerone. No surprise there because you are already an accomplished musician. While the above poster is a close friend and a good player, we don’t always agree on matters of technique - such as the need to open and close the bellows in a certain way. I think it’s more important to be relaxed and comfortable and to minimize tension as much as possible. Reach for the sound you want and if you are musical and focused your technique will develop as you work towards that sound.
I don’t draw much of a distinction between learning new tunes and developing technique. In your case, playing more than one instrument will foster your ability to learn a new tune on one instrument and easily shift that tune to another instrument, mutatis mutandi. Learning a new tune often means learning new technique -- especially on the concertina, where it’s necessary to map out every tune, and where there are, for instance, three choices for the middle A and only one choice (generally) for the middle F#. My Dipper has an alternate F# press on the right hand accidental row and that is a feature worth having on every concertina.
When someone plays a tune that I don’t know I am personally offended (by the paucity of my own repertoire). The gauntlet has been thrown and I respond by learning the tune as soon as I can. As we know, some tunes take years to learn properly. The concertina, intellectually, is a difficult instrument to play. I am always in tune and it generates its own sound, and for that I am thankful. But it is a perpetual Rubik’s Cube without a solution. Learn new tunes and the technique will follow as you solve the fingering problems presented by new tunes.

I find that by just by slowing the tune way, way down and by listening carefully I can learn an amazing amount of technical information about the concertina (or fiddle, or flute). It’s part of the process of learning a new tune- to play it the way someone whom you admire plays it. Don’t learn only the bare notes, the bones, of the tune. Learn the chords and ornaments as well.

Re: Concertina Progress: Slides, Polkas, Barndances, a Slip-Jig, and a Fling.

Jerone, with the finger thing, it is important to use the heel of the hand to close the concertina rather than the fingers and I often see people aiding the push with the index finger. When you push hard enough to invert the joint you lose perspective on how “down” the finger is and have to make a large movement to lift it and will often overshoot and lift the finger higher than necessary. This does not help phrasing.

Your interpretation of active and passive is right, you can’t say left and right because some have an active left and others the right. If you ever want to play box having an active left works better.

Before I clarify the fan/lift thing, the reason for rolling the concertina forward onto a point is so the thumb on the passive side is at a point halfway across the concertina and when you push down with the thumb it neatly traps the end (the line of force bisects the concertina end) so it can’t wiggle when you apply force on the active end. This means very little of your hand is involved in holding the concertina and you can relax the rest of the hand and all of the fingers.

The fan/lift thing; in an ideal world you want every and all movement on a bellows feversal to be reflected in sound. If any of the reversal is a movement that does not move air then you will have a lag before the next note. You might not notice it but you will get off the outgoing note early in order to get to the new note on time. This makes phrasing more difficult and when you speed up your playing will be more ragged than you like and you won’t know why.

In order to examine this can I suggest an exercise? Place the concertina, rolled forward onto the point, in such a way that the folds nearest your feet are held together. Play by fanning the concertina open on the opposite side, which will be facing your face. Keep those folds on the underside together. You will find every movement is reflected in sound. I am not saying play this way all the time, as there would be times when you need more air, this is just an exercise to show you how efficiently the concertina can be played and what you are missing out on when the active end flaps. If you have trouble holding the folds together on the underside use a mirror to watch it or ask a friend to hold that side of the concertina shut.

I have taught a number of people who had prior experience and all have made significant improvement in rhythm when they learned a no flap (OK, very little flap) method.

Fan/lift. If you have the folds together you will find you can fan the concertina open without pulling on the straps by rolling the active end out and down using only the heel (the part directly back from your little finger) of your hand. You can lift it closed the same way with the heel of your hand. The part of the hand under the junction of the index finger and the palm does not do the work. That leads to the flappy thing.

The person who showed me this (Hugh Healey) demonstrated he could play the concertina this way without his hand in the strap. When you do this almost all of your hand is uninvolved in the rhythm process and is free to play in a relaxed fashion. When large parts of your hand are trying to hold a flappy thing steady then you can’t have relaxed fingers for the melody. Incidentally, as another exercise, Hugh made me loosen the straps to the point where they were essentially useless, forcing me to get control of the active end with the heel of my hand and the passive end with the side of my thumb.

Keep your small finger on the keyboard as a brace whenever possible on the active end.

The whole point is to have no movements which do not create sound, and to have a steady playing platform. You might not think the flapping around moves the buttons to different places by much but your fingers are compensating for tiny shifts all the time. You never know how far down a button will need to be pressed because while you know its location you cannot accurately predict how far from your fingers it is. When you don’t have to do this then you will free up your fingers for playing smoothly and fast.

Note that this is only one technique of many people use simultaneously, for example there are moments when only a suddenly pull on the strap will give you the attack you want.

Watch Edel Fox here https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=BEE8JBBcpUw
And even better here https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=eNaStAQQPrA

You will see she is essentially playing with the bottom folds tight. She has an advantage over you in that the concertina she is playing is much more efficient but the principle still applies. When you want to use more bellows, in a piece with chords for example, other techniques need to be applied but if you have the principle of a steady playing platform on board you will not be happy without it and will find ways. Happy to elaborate some time.

Went on a bit but it was a revelation for me, hope it helps others. Interesting to note David suggests our priorites differ and he is about concentrating on relaxing and minimising tension. These are concepts; what I have said above is a practical approach to exactly the same end.

Re: Concertina Progress: Slides, Polkas, Barndances, a Slip-Jig, and a Fling.

In general I agree with Chris, though I don’t spend much time analyzing hand position and technique. What stands out for me is when Chris says, “Incidentally, as another exercise, Hugh made me loosen the straps to the point where they were essentially useless,…”
Hugh Healey is a great player and has very loose wrist straps. Every good player that I know has loose straps. Most people have straps that are much too tight, which instead of helping you develop a sense of security and freedom, reduces the security. Tight straps are counter-productive in other ways. They lock you into one position, such that stretching your fingers in new ways is made more difficult. They cramp your hand, preventing it from moving where necessary, reducing the overall flexibility that one wants, and increasing the tension in wrist and hand.
I disagree about keeping the little finger braced on the instrument. That way, if you need to use your little finger you first must lift it from the face of the concertina, then put it down on the button. Much simpler just to do the one thing and keep your little finger free to move about the instrument.
Ultimately one has to learn to play slowly, not so fast that some notes are skipped or the phrasing is compromised. There are tricks that can be taught past the basic melody (ornaments, chords), but there is no substitute for playing slowly and perfectly. One note missed is an indication that one is playing too fast.

“About the slides, I have a very difficult time discerning slides from jigs, especially at slower tempos on the slides. When you say ”snappier“, what do you mean by that?”

“‘snappier’ - well more staccato maybe - difficult to describe, maybe contributors with more music theory than me might describe it better. Have a listen to Jacky Daly playing slides!”

Yes, listen to Jacky Daly, but also to Johnny O‘Leary, Matt Cranitch, Michael Sheehy, Padraigh O’Keefe, Dennis Murphy, Julia Clifford and all the Sliabh Luachra crowd.

Do you know the nursery rhyme ‘Humpty Dumpty’ - aka the tune ‘Off She Goes’? That’s a good one to illustrate the feel of slides as opposed to jigs. Slides are a dialogue between jerky and smooth; something they have in common with hornpipes (though of course slides are 12/8, so they always have that underlying yadda-tee yadda-tee rhythm). I think note emphasis is key to getting slides (at least to start off with). Emphasis tends to be placed on the 2nd and 4th beats of four; and most of the players I listen to generally play slides as a two-bar phrase, with the second phrase perhaps emphasising the 3rd beat as well. That’s a very heavy-handed, programmatic way of describing it of course; I’m generalising.

Also, when you say “at slower tempos”… slides are generally played pretty fast and, while I’m no fan of excessive speed in irish music, there is something about the character of a slide that IMO demands to be played faster than you would generally play a jig.

A couple of point Jerone that came up in thought regarding speed , ornamentation and feel. You’ll notice the people playing for dancers, play fast, or as we say , lively. and on stage for listening, they play more ‘relaxed’ slow.
Also the link from Matt with the excellent polka feel, yet the other links I posted don’t have that, why would that be!
The reason is that playing for dancers is rarely something a single player will do, they will have a backer to help drive the tunes. and playing for dancers, by its nature is more demanding, as you can see I think!
Ideally a we’d have both , the technique of matts link and the speed of a dance musician.
How achievable is that though?……

In your neck of the woods, it’s maybe not so likely you will encounter dancers of the experience displayed in my links. So speed is not so important. But still….

Part of the reason why I recommend a player learns to play at pace, the right speed for dancers , without ornaments, cleanly with drive lift excitement clarity and enjoyment is because dance music is functional, if it’s not danceable , it’s not functional , it might sound good, be full of clever inticasies, but it doesn’t actually work.
David Levine made a really important point, re read his post .
Yet that’s not enough, sprinters don’t train my jogging round a track.
That slow clean precise playing stripped to a core , need also to be brought up to speed. So it’s functional.
Then stage 2 is going back down and bringing in the ornaments, then eventually getting back up to functional pace with the intricacies .
The standard is pretty much set by Michael sexton in that regard. IMO, Martin Hayes plays in a soft lyrical style, but make no mistake, he can play at pace for the sets with lift drive etc…
There are many levels , the problems come when beginners try to copy the experts , try to be something they’re not.
Be yourself , be honest , be patient , be determined , be persistent, be open. Be happy :-)
