Flute tuning, tone and scale.

In a recent discussion Terry McGee mentioned the Cooper scale while relating his experience of learning to play. I had not heard of it (it seems mostly a Boehm flute concept), and so looked it up. In short it is an “unfixed” schema for tonehole placement on Boehm flutes, unfixed in the sense that it is not strictly mathematical as compared to Boehm’s own presentation…at least that is how it seems to be.

Patrick Olwell, Terry McGee and undoubtedly others noticed the discrepancies in tuning and tone that were also present in classical conical bored flutes, and set about correcting those. These corrections seem as much related to embouchure style, a darker tone for Irish music might have the effect of the flute embouchure being made effectively smaller, which would change the tuning across the scale. Obviously tone was worked on as well. Classical flutes played openly tend to be more in tune with themselves.

Not only that though, even for the more accurately tuned Boehm flutes discrepancy existed (and still exists) , such that:

“A flutemaker might tweak something here or there, but they would have been crazy to deviate significantly from the “ideal.” Players learned to adjust for the errant notes (with mixed success), creating a paradox: a theoretically perfect flute would have been unacceptable, because established players would find the low notes sharp and the C-sharp flat.”
(First link below)

Even more obvious in this respect are the earliest scales on cylindrical flutes. Somewhat amazingly on a scaled Renaissance design I have access to I find second octave B as sharp compared to first, whereas it is widely understood to be flat on modern plain cylindrical flutes (e.g. Tipple) . The use of cross fingering for the upper register is designed to bring out strong harmonic notes as opposed to open notes, however for the B it flattens second octave, which allows a fit for other second octave harmonic notes in terms of more straightforward crossfingering . In discussion I found that modern reproductions are not played using exactly the same fingering charted in the day. This could be understood as due to (the little understood) variety of patterns described as well as variables of contemporary and modern flute design. The most well known extant flutes however show very fine attention to certain details across their range.

Anyway, as I have a mixed background of embouchure styles I found I was able to play all variety of fingering patterns (including modern keyless flute) presented, and on the mentioned flute, by varying embouchure style. Obviously the scale and tone produced varied along with that, but not in a way that would make the design unworkable.

All of this just correlated with the quoted paragraph, that flute teaches embouchure teaches tone teaches fingering/tuning teaches design…in whatever order.

In practice I expect people just choose the flute that best suits them, or work out position or style to get best tone-tuning. I was surprised to see even modern Boehm flutes were not as accurately tuned as I thought they might be (second link).

As far as most flautists are concerned none of this matters too much I expect, they work with what they have available and suits best, but it is good to know how tuning is still debated, as well as having some idea of the sort of parameters at play, i.e. why one person cannot play certain flutes in tune but others do without problem, or why tuning (and its measurement) is often very subjective, and tone is so without having to even say so.

(this is usually discussed on C&F forums, I read the discussions with interest)

1) story I read somewhere. famous jazz flutist wants to learn to make flutes, but is told how it very hard and difficult, as all the holes must be in exactly the right places, or else. so he buys some materials, drill presses, safety goggles, etc, goes away to a shop, eventually returns with nice self-made flutes in his hand and exclaims in disgust, “these people are full of *, I can put the * holes anywhere I want and the * things still plays in tune!” (key word for me, “a ***jazz*** flutist”).

2) according to Mr.Boehm’s book, he did not know where to put the holes, he did not have a formula. he built a flute with movable holes and worked it out by trial and error. he did work out the formula for scaling the flute for A440/A416/etc, but his tone hole placement is ad-hoc.

3) tuning flute tone holes to make all harmonics come out at correct frequencies is similar to tuning RF transmission lines in particle accelerators (only difference, if mis-tuned, instead of unpleasant sound, you melt stuff). licenses for software to model/compute such transmission lines are above $10k/year, plus ~$10k for computer to run these models. and good luck finding people who know how to use this software to make reliable computations/predictions.

so no surprise for me that nobody knows exactly how a flute works, nobody can predict/compute exactly what frequency spectrum a given flute will produce. and of course, how it will *sound* depends on player and on listener.

a better bicycle^H^H^H^Hbetter flute is still waiting for it’s inventor!

P.S. Theobald Boehm’s book is short and easy to read. recommended to all flute players. https://www.alfred.com/the-flute-and-flute-playing/p/06-212599/

I’d like to see the next major flute design improvement be in the embouchure hole area. The embouchure holes on a lot of metal and wooden flutes, of all prices, are often a basic and easy-to-manufacture approach that doesn’t impress me that the experiential or scientific research in musical possibilities have all been explored there yet. Mass production preferences and maximized innovation could be a great odds here. Issues still to be explored would include physical interaction with the instrument, tone colour range, range of volume, range of octaves, range of response characteristics, range of notes possible. Improving the embouchure hole affects the response, and that may affect the need for further innovations of the tube end location, the conical shape of the headjoint, the lip plate, the chimney design, undercutting, wall angles, etc.

Re: Flute tuning, tone and scale.

A lot of good stuff above, too much to take in let alone comment on in any detail in one sitting. There are flutes to be made!

But on the topic of changing embouchure approach (from say blow across to blow down), yes it changes the pitch, so you have to move the tuning slide. But tuning slides aren’t magical - they are only changing one aspect of the flute, in this case the length of the cylindrical section. Leaving the lengths of the conical section and the flare at the bottom untouched. So we are inevitably changing the physical relationships within the flute. Ideally we would have several tuning slides distributed along the flute, and make smaller adjustments to each. Insane, you sputter? Then I wonder what George Rose (of Rudall Rose fame) had in mind when he included what looks like such a flute in the English patent documents for the Boehm flute?

http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Rose1847patent.htm

In many ways, we modern Irish flute makers, working alone or in very small shops, are at an advantage over the Boehm factories. We are probably flute players, whereas many if not most in the factories are not. We are not mass-production oriented (the biggest number of flutes I ever start in one batch is two, and even then I split them up to finish them separately!). And we do everything, rather than only say drill the holes, or pad the keys. So we are intimately connected with our products, whereas the factory workers just see them go by.

Against that, our challenges are steeper. With the Boehm flute, the holes can be where they need to be, whereas in our flutes, they have to be within human reach and coverage. Look at a keyless flute and it’s easy to see that the holes are in two clusters of three, rather than an equal distribution of six. And whereas there is essentially only one Boehm flute, I make five dramatically different models of conical flute to cover the range of capabilities and aspirations out there. And three embouchure holes!

Yes, I’m imagining I’m the source of the Rudall & Rose #5501 data. If not, either that flute has moved on or some other confusion has arisen! You can see the bore of the original as measured at http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/bores.htm

At the time I put together that page, I was naively assuming that the bores as measured were probably pretty close to the bores as originally reamed. That the reduction in bore diameter shown at each of the tenons and the expansion in the sockets was original and intended. After my later work studying bore compression ( http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/wrap-survey.htm et seq), I now recognise this as damage due to time, moisture and applied compression, mostly the string wrap at the tenons. So, I’m imagining that a straight line picking up the diameters near the middle of pieces rather than at their distorted ends would come much closer to the original than what we see there.

Oh, straight line except for the flare at the end of the foot. That is original and intended!
