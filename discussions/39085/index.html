> I know all instruments go out of tune it has to do with the tuning pegs being poorly fitted like you said

If I put my fiddle away and bring it out at the same temperature and humidity, it’ll still be in tune. If I have a run of consistent weather, I can go days without tuning it. And the tuning pegs *never* move.

The trick is to coil the string around the peg so the string is hard up against the edge of the peg-box. If you get this right the peg will never move, never mind how well it is fitted.

And I know not everyone agrees, but I take the view that there are very few people in this world who *need* to worry about whether or not fine tuners damage their tone.

Re: been playing for 2 years looking to upgrade

Hi;
Quality, air-dried wood is the most expensive component in a stringed instrument today.
And it is the single most important element in the making of an instrument.
A friend, who just finished making a high-end mandolin, paid nearly $600 USD for the wood and tuners.
So, I am not sure you can find a good NEW instrument for $600.

If you have a top price range of $600, look for a used instrument and bide your time. If you hear something you really like, ask the owner to notify you if he ever wants to sell. As you wait, do odd jobs and put the money in your violin fund.

Call the nearest school with a music dept. and ask to talk to the strings teacher. Tell him/her what you are looking for. There are other students looking for instruments, too. And their ‘old’ instrument could do very nicely for you. Call that teacher back every 6 months.
Does your community have an active Craigslist service? Get on it and post an ad in the ‘Wanted’ section.

Call the largest churches in your area and talk with the music minister. Or send a letter indicating what you are looking for. I once had an old couple from my church give me a nice violin and 2 nice bows and asked me to get them in good repair, and sell them. They only asked that I split the proceeds with the church, and that I try to find a serious student who would play the instruments. Call those music ministers back every 6 months.

Keep talking to people and telling them that you need a better instrument in order to improve your playing. Tell everyone how much it means to you. You wouldn’t believe how many people have grandpa’s violin in a closet and would ONLY ever part with it if they met a nice musician who would play it and cherish it.

If you get your hands on an older instrument that has not been played for years, realize that the wood will have stiffened up. It will take some time to play it in, again. Ask a luthier to check the sound post adjustment, too. Oftentimes an unplayed instrument will need an adjustment, or a new sound post. I don’t know how to put into words how to tell if a ‘tight’ instrument needs playing in, or needs a sound post adjustment. Get a professional opinion.

If you keep striking out, and have kept increasing your violin fund:
Gliga Christian, a Romanian shop, makes fine, inexpensive instruments. They seem to have access to a large supply of quality wood. I visited a former student, and heard his Gliga. I told him if he ever decided to sell it I wanted first opportunity. He paid about $850 for it (in 2001), and I told him he got a real bargain. When he called a few years later and said he bought another one, and needed to sell the G. Christian, I said ‘I will bring you the money tomorrow morning’. I did not give him a chance to change his mind.
GC has distributors and retailers around the world.

One last option: get a job - part-time or full - at a violin shop. You will learn so much by simply playing many different violins. Eventually, a fiddle will come into the shop that calls out to you. Make an offer to the shop owner and see if he will take payments over time (assuming it’s above your budget). The year I spent working in a violin shop, back in high school, was one of my best educational experiences ever.

One Last Thought: I have never, in 55 years of playing music, heard a musician say “I wish I hadn’t spent so much on my instrument”.

Re: been playing for 2 years looking to upgrade

It’s true that all instruments go out of tune, but poorly fitted pegs make it much harder to put them back in tune. Winding the string against the cheek of the pegbox isn’t a solution - not only will it dramatically shorten the life of your strings, it’s also a recipe for a cracked pegbox, and if the weather gets humid and the pegs stick you’ll find you can’t pull them out. The string should always come away from the peg just short of the peg box cheek. It’s also important to wind the string neatly with no crossed turns. The gap between the peg and the back of the pegbox (particularly on the A string) is often only big enough for a single thickness of string, if the windings are crossed it will jam, again making tuning much more difficult, wearing your strings and in extreme cases cracking the peg box.

Working without fine tuners is really only an option if you are using gut strings, with synthetic or steel you have to have some form of fine adjustment. Fitting four e string tuners is far from ideal - the practice started when synthetic strings first appeared and there was no other option available. But the shortened afterlength makes the fiddle sound more scratchy, and they have to be carefully modified to take thicker strings. A Wittner tailpiece with built in tuners is a much more sensible idea. Personally I’ve never quite seen the point of geared pegs, unless you play in a snobby orchestra and want to hide the fact that you have fine tuners, so that people think you are using gut strings. On a good instrument I’d rather have wooden pegs that I can pull out or push in as the weather changes, rather than something solid stuck in the pegbox and putting a lot of stress on it when the wood contracts.

Re: been playing for 2 years looking to upgrade

I have had Knilling Perfection pegs on my fiddle since 2009 and I’ve found them to be excellent.

My view of the pros and cons of them :

Pros :

They offer an excellent, natural, efficient, quick, easy and very accurate way to tune (easily adjust the string[s] while bowing). They have a turn ratio of 4:1, and do not need to be pressed in to the pegbox while turning them, as standard pegs do.

To the best of my knowledge, they don’t wear out. Standard pegs and the pegbox will be subject to wear if used regularly.

Cons :

They are an added expense, assuming that the standard pegs and pegbox work perfectly on the new fiddle. Although to be fair, this is not always the case, so you’d need to pay for that to be done anyway.

They will need a bit of getting used to (if you have only ever tuned a fiddle using tailpiece tuners, and not by the pegs).

As for stress on the wood of the pegbox when the wood contracts, I’m sure that would apply to standard pegs as well, in the scenario where a fiddler only ever uses tailpiece tuners, and never touches the pegs except when changing strings, and for the initial tune-ups after that.

Re: been playing for 2 years looking to upgrade

People who are selling you a fiddle won’t necessarily tell you things that might put you off buying. It’s always best if you can narrow down your choices and get two or three on loan so you can get a good player such as a tutor to try them out. If you are looking at older fiddles, some very useful information can be found by doing a web search for “correct violin neck angle”. Look at the images and measurements and rule out any fiddles that are too flat in profile because they probably need an expensive neck reset. Most old fiddles were probably originally listed in catalogues and were varying grades of imported instruments made with some kind of mass production. A couple of example catalogue pages: http://collections.nmmusd.org/oldvln1.html http://collections.nmmusd.org/oldvln2.html . This is where all those “valuable” heirlooms found in attics originated. A lot are still imported in the white (i.e. unvarnished) and then finished possibly with a bit of distressing and set up for sale (sometimes with a fancy Italian label). I looked at one like that and had it on loan. It was actually really nice, lovely tone, but it was a German fiddle with an Italian label and in the end I thought it was a little overpriced and might be regarded with a bit of suspicion if I sold it on. I am someone who buys impulsively but I have finally grown out of that tendency!

Re: been playing for 2 years looking to upgrade

Not sure what your current fiddle is like, but an Eastman model would be a good standard mid-high end student model. They tend to be rather consistent throughout all their instruments and are available from generic music stores like Long and McQuade.

If your price point is around $600CAN I would recommend finding a good used model. Unless you stumble across some hidden gem it will be hard to get a really good quality higher end instrument at this price but you can get a nice mid-range student model. If you are serious about playing I would recommend you budget somewhere around $800-$1200, invest in a good quality instrument that will have a good resale value. You are more likely to practice if you have an instrument that is a joy to play anyways.

In Southern Ontario fiddlers tend to recommend The SoundPost (Toronto) or Reidstra’s violin shop (Kitchener.) Both places have many high end instruments ($$$) and often have a selection of good quality more affordable instruments. They have a reputation of being honest and offering fair prices based on the quality of the instrument. If you found a used instrument and weren’t sure of the value they would be the ones to turn to for a fair appraisal.

If you are looking for an affordable used instrument sometimes small independent music stores can be helpful. I have seen some good quality instruments at affordable prices from small music stores like Pianovations (Blyth, ON) or Ernie King Music (Goderich, ON.) But since they are smaller stores with limited inventory it depends on what they have when you visit.
