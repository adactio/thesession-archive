Re: Is this song racist?

Well, “black” in traditional songs can refer to a member of the group formerly referred to as gypsies, so there might be a racist context but probably not the one you were thinking of.

However, if the song makes you uncomfortable as it is, you can either not sing it or tinker with it until it seems okay to you. I’ve done this from time to time - for example, John Prine’s song “That’s The Way The World Goes Round” had a few lines that I just wasn’t going to sing (“Starts drinking heavy gets a big red nose/Beats his old lady with a rubber hose/Takes her out and buys her clothes/And that’s the way the world goes round”), but I loved the second verse and the chorus. So I changed the first verse, and what I sing now is “Starts drinking heavy, gets a big red nose/Spends a lotta time with a girl called Rose/Winds up in the alley in another fella’s clothes/And that’s the way the world goes round”.

It has Prine’s rhymes, more or less, and I think it gets about the same sort of goofiness, but without the jolly violence part. I’m not a songwriter by any means, but I think this sort of surgery is something pretty much anyone can manage.

The way I see it, there’s a huge number of lovely songs out there. Re: Is this song racist?

great Q by OP! One thing for sure, there’s the opportunity for educating audiences as opposed to letting ambiguity lead them astray. It’s an opportunity to enlighten the rich beauty of Irish culture, and can add to the performance! Whatever your comfort level is. Here’s a few thoughts below I hope are helpful!

Some thoughts:
(1) Dubh isn’t (and hasn’t) been used in Irish culture to relate to skin colour. I think this is a shortcoming of English language; the translation mapping to “black” isn’t helpful.

(2) Dubh isn’t and hasn’t been used to relate to gypsies (at least outside the pale). Jon sorry for taking aim, but I respectfully refute your statement above (just in case readers do a 2nd-hand reference to your post). I’d stick my neck out and bet that the term “gypsies” was imported into Ireland in recent times. Gypsies definitely wasn’t used around my parts, and generations before me. There were many names on various travelling communities (tramps, drifters, etc) and they were respected, often transacting in day labour for food/respite, and sometimes receiving charity ex gratia. I suspect the term “Irish Gypsies” is a construct originating in the UK, it deserves little/no further attention IMHO.

(3) Gorm is indeed a colour (blue) and is the close correlation to “black man”. However it’s not an exact one; supposedly French military (especially d’Humbert & Teeling’s soldiers of the 1798 rebellion who marched through Mayo/Sligo and further afield) had a notable proportion of Black-skinned-coloured soldiers (likely from French Antilles, who were formidable warriors). They wore “blue” uniforms, and possibly this is where the correlation comes from. Teeling is a honoured hero in Sligo, and over 100k Irish people reportedly traveling from the 4 corners in 1898 to celebrate the erection of the statue west of Collooney. The Irish had the height of respect for these soldiers.

(4) Bán / Dubh colours in Irish are very old, for example “bán” sometimes used extensively with fairies (white horses, etc), and dubh being more sinister (e.g. Crumb Dubh, possibly the most notorious example I can think of). It’s possible Christianity helped enhance this colouring toward good/evil, however my guess is that it was there long before!

(3) Re: Changing names, I recall reading somewhere that the reel “Irish Girl” was popularly called “Wild Irishman”, and was changed to the former upon the publishers request (I presume they wanted to cause no offence to the Irish). So it has been done before!

Re: Is this song racist?

Context is all.
When ‘Sean Dempsey’ sings: “I lost her to a student chap, with skin as black as coal”, you might wonder why he mentions the chap’s skin-colour at all, but he’s relating another important change to his beloved city, which is what the song’s about. If Pete St. John had written: “There are more black faces here than there used to be!”, that wouldn’t have necessarily been racist, but it could easily sound like it was, so he cleverly weaves that particular change into his own story.

When Shane Macgowan puts the words, “cheap, lousy faggot” into the mouth of Kirsty McColl, he’s trying to give her protagonist dialogue which fits the time and her mood: “The word was used by the character because it fitted with the way she would speak and with her character. She is not supposed to be a nice person, or even a wholesome person”. Like John Prine’s rubber hose, it’s the juxtaposition and even shock within the context (a Christmas song, in this case) which is so good.

I know people tend to go with their first impressions these days and I’m as well-intentioned as the next bloke but, if you know a lyric isn’t just gratuatous racism (or homophobic, sexist or anything else), I say sing it as written!

The tale of the pub in Linlithgow is an interesting one, and I suppose that does just show how language changes. I’m 56 and from the rural English midlands so I wouldn’t do a double-take at a pub called that, but I can see that people might these days… Even though you really wouldn’t have to go out of your way to find out the backstory. Why Green King didn’t change the name of the pub to “The Black Dog” is beyond me.
