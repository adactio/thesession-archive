learning completely without sheets ?

When you learn tunes by listening, do you write down the stuff you just figured out ?

↳ Posted by Davidread on August 30th, 2016.

Re: learning completely without sheets ?

Nope, I never write down the stuff I just figured out (and never have done). I think the trick is to listen to the tune so much that you know it inside-out to hear and basically end up subconsciously absorbing it. Listen to it over and over without your instrument in hand - listen to it while you are commuting or exercising or cooking or whenever you have the free time to do so, until you know all of its twists and turns.

If you are struggling to learn multi-part reels, maybe it makes sense to stick with simpler 2-part tunes for the moment until you get proficient at learning them by ear.

If I was you, I’d forget about the idea of time management (unless you have to learn a particular piece in a limited time for a gig etc.) - playing this music is a lifelong pursuit and I don’t think there are any shortcuts. If there are, I haven’t discovered them. Sometimes you’ll make great progress. Sometimes you’ll make no progress for ages. You might get stuck at a bit of a plateau for a while and suddenly make some great breakthrough in your playing. Enjoy the journey :-)

↳ Posted by Cheeky Elf on August 30th, 2016.

Re: learning completely without sheets ?

I like to write down notes as I learn a tune, especially if I’m learning from a recording. It’s sort of an intermediate step between lots of listening, and being able to play from memory. It’s also convenient to have the dots as a reminder if I return to a tune I haven’t played in a good while, without having to dig out the recording. I usually just write down the first time through a tune, though, and maybe an especially nice variation. Sometimes I will transcribe several different settings while I’m trying to get my head around a tune. Sometimes I will then write out a synthesis of these multiple sources, as my own setting. But most variations and ornamentation that I might put into a tune comes from having listened to the music over and over.

I don’t think there’s anything wrong with using the dots to learn a tune. It’s really convenient. What’s concerning is when people don’t actually listen to the music very much, and their primary source is a printed score. They miss a lot.

Re: learning completely without sheets ?

Mike Troxell: “How do you learn the bowing pattern or where slurs are, etc. if you are only learning by listening?”

In this variety of music, such things, and bigger things like exactly which notes are used when, are not set in stone. In my opinion this is the crux of the debate about whether people ought to use written notes for this variety of music at all. The player has a lot of freedom to vary the tune each time they play it. But it’s possible, when learning from notes on a staff, to get the impression that those notes are the One Right Way to play that particular tune, like they would be if it was a Brahms sonata.

But you’re right, after awhile listening, you’ll get ideas for good places for ornamentation and how bowing works. But there’s never going to be One Right Place for a slur, cut, roll, etc. Even which places are better than others can be very subjective.

Re: learning completely without sheets ?

When I was learning music by ear from a YouTube rendition or from an mp3 by my teacher, I used to write down the notes once I’d learned what they were.

But now I just play along with the tune over & over again. I find at first that I’m only joining in occasionally - then I’m joining in most of it, but there are some troublesome phrases that I can’t quite work out - then eventually it all clicks. I just keep playing it over & over till my fingers can just do it automatically.

I once learned a version of ‘Farewell to Erin’ that differed from the commoner version that my teacher played for me on an mp3. I thought I wouldn’t be able to adapt to the new version because the old one was so engrained. But it didn’t take long at all - I suppose because my brain had a mind-map of the tune’s basic structure even though some of the notes or phrases were different.

The new version has now become automatic.

So although it *used* to help me to write things down, it no longer does, and learning by joining in what I hear has made me better able to adapt at sessions that I go to - I used to find it difficult to pick out what notes or keys people were playing, but I’m much improved these days. (Famous last words! :) )

I’d say do whatever helps you for as long as it does, but you may, like me, reach the stage where writing it down is no longer the answer.

↳ Posted by Cheeky Elf on August 30th, 2016.

Re: learning completely without sheets ?

Mike Troxell: “How do you learn the bowing pattern or where slurs are, etc. if you are only learning by listening?”

Bowings are very rarely notated in traditional tune collections anyway - you’re only like to see them in a fiddle tutor. As tdrury says, bowing patterns and slurring are a ultimately matters of choice. But if you are looking to imitate the style of a particular recording, then close listening should give you clues as to where slurring is used and where it is not. It may not always be obvious, as some players can do very smooth bow changes - and I doubt whether my ear alone could tell the difference between an up-bow and a down-bow - but a bit of experimentation tells you what sounds right and what doesn’t. Eventually, you develop an intuitive sense of how to use the bow to achieve whatever type of rhythmic articulation you want.

Re: learning completely without sheets ?

Over 18 years ago, Philippe Varlet wrote this:
"I always insist on my students becoming aware of the structure of melodies:
- overall thematic structure: Irish tunes are, for the most part, symmetrical in construction, so that 2-bar units will recur following common plans like AAAB or ABAC, etc.
- tonal structure: Irish melodies are built around chords which follow, for the most part, standard progressions, I IV V for major tunes, ii I ii for Dorian (mode 2, “minor”) tunes, or V IV V for Mixolydian (mode 5, “low 7th”) tunes.
- melodic patterns: Irish melodies progress primarily through arpeggios or stepwise motion (up or down the scale), with changes from one chord to another usually coinciding with stepwise motion.

All this means that, to memorize a tune, you don’t necessarily need to know exactly which note comes after which. In fact, I tend to think of it as a compression program: you take out redundant stuff when you memorize to save space, but you have a way of filling the gaps when you “explode” the tune to play it. In a 2-part hornpipe, for instance, the two halves of the first part are usually almost identical except for the cadence (the end), typically reflecting a different chord progression which makes it sound like the melody is not over when you reach mid-part but that you have reached a satisfying closure in the last bar. Then, the second half of the second part is also very often the same as in the first part, so that the only new material may be the first four bars of it. To recapitulate, if you learn the first four bars of the first part and the first four bars of the second, you basically know the tune."

(Source: https://listserv.heanet.ie/cgi-bin/wa?A2=ind9603&L=IRTRAD-L&P=R6709&D=0 )

“(…)you take out redundant stuff when you memorize to save space, but you have a way of filling the gaps when you ”explode“ the tune to play it.”

Again there’s something to agree with David50. We could use a little more specificity with how the brain retains music. Still I’d be reluctant to say that the brain fills in gaps… except for music. It may be a hypothesis to say how the brain stores and retrieves information but I think it’s a safe way to think of it based on what do know from, say, eyewitness accounts of events that vary from person to person and over time. My anecdotal experience must be vastly different than yours. There’s probably not a time that I play when I don’t find myself “making stuff up” because I’ve forgotten the precise phrase. Re: learning completely without sheets ?

There’s a big difference between filling in bits that you don’t remember properly (or worse yet, glossing over an intricate part because it doesn’t flow as well under your fingers as the rest of the tune) and careful, thoughtful variation that comes from an intimacy with the music.

But I agree with Ross, in principle. If you’ve ever done the trick where you hold your thumbs together and put your fingers up like goal posts, and then move them away from your nose to the point where the tip of one of your fingers will disappear because it has moved into the part of your eye that is blocked by the optic nerve, you know what he means about our brains filling things in. We have big blind spots and we just don’t see them, because our minds take the incoming signals and paint a picture of reality that is different than what is actually happening.

I think the same thing can happen in music. I don’t read music (although, I can read and write ABC if I want to notate something). Every one of the hundreds of tunes that I know, I learned by ear. Some of them I have notated to be able to remember the setting that I learned, but that is generally something I did long ago when I was first playing, and I haven’t done that in years. But I will occasionally play a tune with the person I learned it from, and notice how much our settings have diverged over time.

I certainly wouldn’t attribute all of the different ways tunes are played to lapse of memory, or brains filling in the blanks. But I bet it’s a good part of it. And if you think about the music like a big game of telephone (the game where you sat in a circle, and whispered a sentence into the ear of the person next to you, and see just how much the sentence changes by the time it gets all the way around the circle), you can understand how things might evolve in an aural tradition.

And I also think of this as something to be embraced! I learned a tune from Cillian Vallely recently that is an old Patsy Touhey setting of a tune that is played completely differently these days. And I embrace that knowledge, and love being able to play it both ways!
