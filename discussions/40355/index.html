Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

The music will always be around. It’s still very much a living tradition with vibrant support all over the globe. The community that supports it may wax and wane in size, but there will always be a core group of adherents passing it down from generation to generation.

Many coming into it today in the US are probably spoiled by the inflationary effects of such cultural exports as Riverdance and other such grandiose productions that inspired many to pick up a fiddle or a pair of hard shoes. I think the level of popularity that’s been experienced since the late 90s is somewhat atypical, and it might just be returning to the levels we were at before. (Caveat emptor: I’m saying this as someone who started playing during this wave and not before, my perspective may be skewed).

I can tell you that the last time I was in Ireland it was for an international summer school; there were loads of people your age and younger who were absolutely mad about the music and dance, whether it be people coming from Mexico who picked it up after watching Riverdance or Irish kids whose family’s been in the music since time immemorial. I don’t think it’s going away any time soon.

Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

“How do you think it will change … in terms of repertoire and not in terms of changing into a crappy artificial techno modern thing.”

Fears of ‘real instruments’ being replaced by machines have probably been around since the invention of the barrel organ - but it hasn’t happened. The popuar music scene continues to produce new acts playing guitars, bass, drums etc., despite synths, samplers and sequencers (and computers that do *everything* nowadays) and musical genres that rely wholly upon them; jazz and classical music haven’t gone away (people still play trad jazz, which was ‘old-fashioned’ by about 1940 - and the likes of Vivaldi whose music would have been old-fashioned by 1750); we (Irish trad payers) are still playing some tunes that have been transmitted orally since their composition in the 18th Century (or thereabouts).

Certainly, people experiment with fusion of musical genres (we don’t have to wait 50 years to hear fusions of Irish trad and ‘crappy artificial techno modern thing’), with results that may be good or bad (such is the nature of experimentation) and with results that are appealing to some and appalling to others. It cannot be ruled out that such music, if sufficiently influencial, could have some lasting effect on the general musical aesthetic. But the evidence seems to show that people just like sitting down (or standing up) and making music together.

Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

If we’re getting onto the idea of the music changing, it’s always been changing anyway. Very substantially. What’s the ITM repertoire made up of? A few very old harp tunes, a slightly larger body of late 17th century Jacobite song airs, a whole load of 18th century dance music of various national origins and a crust of stall ballads and music hall turns, all refined through a largely oral transmission process. As for instruments, it’s not that long since you had people declaring accordions were some kind of hellish innovation guaranteed to kill off the music entirely.

The one constant is that people always played (or sung) the good tunes.

Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

The first branch of Comhaltas in Cavan town was organised by Dr Galligan in the Ulster Arms hotel in 1952. He was one of the founder members of the CCE in Mullingar the previous year. I was there. A group of us lads all playing mouth organs turned up at that session. Most of the musicians who turned up that night were from the country areas. At that time I think you could count on one hand the number of traditional musicians who lived in the town. Trad was the music of the country people and most of the ceili bands were made up of people from country areas. Those of us young lads who turned up that night had got our taste of trad music by playing in the Boy scout harmonica band. Trying to learn a fiddle or accordion in those days was a hit and miss affair. Even if you could get the music for the tunes nobody read music, and tape recorders were unheard of. Even when you manage to pick up a tune from somebody lilting the chances were that it was wrong in places. As the fifties progressed, ceili dancing became more popular and consequently more ceili bands were in demand. Trad was been featured more and more on Irish radio especially on the sponsored programs and there seemed to be a surge of new trad musicians coming on stream. Our local electrical shop had started selling button accordions on hire purchase and our boy scout harmonica band had become an accordion band. Our ambition was to play in a ceili band and some of us became good enough to achieve that ambition. However the curse of emigration was always present and thousands of musicians from all over Ireland had left or were leaving for greener fields in England, America, or Australia. When I arrived in London in 1955 I found a thriving trad scene in many pubs or in the Irish dance halls. That continued right through the sixties and seventies and you could find yourself playing in the company of the cream of musicians from nearly every county in Ireland. However time stands still for no man and as the older musicians were being replaced by younger players, I found the music starting to speed up and becoming a bit more sophisticated. More complicated tunes were coming on stream and many of us old guys were being left out of the loop. In other words we found it hard to keep up. This being the age of the internet I feel that many more people from all over the world now have access to the music. like what they hear and will of course put their own stamp on. Maybe even change it to such an extent that in 50 years time you’ll be listening to tunes like The Humours of Timbuctoo…..

Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

One thing to remember is that what you and the rest of us think of as “the tradition” isn’t really traditional, in a way. Those traditional button boxes and concertinas? Blow-ins in the late 19th century, and despised by many as much as today’s techno for being “mechanical music.” The banjo and mandolin are similarly late additions.

But then again, as has been stated, the “tradition” is really a 19th and 20th century amalgamation of tunes from a wide variety of sources, beaten into a few recognizable tune shapes so people could dance to them. Some people decried the polka and waltz as foreign invasions into native music (if you notice, they’re not in the sacred O’Neill book). But of course, the jig and reel weren’t really fully Irish either, although the Irish certainly made them their own.

And some of the great shibboleths of the trad scene today? If you tried to impose some of those “rules” on group of musicians playing for a dance in the early 1900s they’d look at you funny. The tradition was whatever people wanted to dance to, with whatever instruments people had, and that varied considerably. The whole idea of a session just for the music’s sake wasn’t a huge part of it until the mid-20th century, and that concept itself (of the music being a primarily listening rather than dancing pastime) changed a lot of things about how people treated it.

As people have said before, things change, and I’m sure that whatever form the music takes in 50 years will be as interesting and varied as it is now. The technical ability of musicians, especially the younger ones coming up, is probably better today than ever before, and if you ever get a chance to go to a big festival like the Fleadh Cheoil or Willie Week you’ll have to fight through throngs of 10 year olds trundling along with their concertinas and flutes. Ask an older flute player about how much they paid for their Rudall and Rose or Pratten flute back in the day; they probably found it for 50 quid in a pawn shop. Now, those same flutes cost thousands and there are a hundred makers of new, even better ones that people line up to buy. People like yourself being able to find resources on the web means there’s way more demand for this kind of music than ever before.

I wouldn’t worry too much.

Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

I think it might be more interesting to think about ‘the tradition’ as an ephemeral thing, sometimes captured in a recording or heard in a moment. Sometimes the tradition lives in a session for a minute, an hour, a night. It lives in the hands and minds of a few and visits the rest of us occasionally if we’re diligent or lucky or it’s feeling lonely and the tools are at hand. I’m part of a tradition of music in the driftless region of Wisconsin. On a good day it’s 30% Irish traditional, 30% contemporary Irish traditional and 30% other (Breton, old time, and ‘our’ tunes). I’m okay with being a part of a tradition that’s local; built by locals and transplants from all over; a student of Brendan Mulvihill’s, a woman who learned at her mother’s knee in Ireland, and their students and friends who have been inspired by music inside and outside of the tradition. These local traditions will persist because they’re part of our lives and little bits of the greater tradition live there and everywhere people play these tunes in a real way. Is there pure tradition anywhere? I’m not sure. I can’t imagine that it will disappear in our lifetimes, in 2 or 3 maybe, but it seems unlikely because there are people still pursuing it.

Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

Late here, and not speaking specifically to the Irish tradition…. but here in Cape Breton, my sense is the tradition is healthy. Not booming, but healthy, and that’s because people pay attention to watering the seedlings. The adult musicians here are INCREDIBLY kind, and patient, and supportive of the young players. The fiddle teachers, for example, create opportunities to ease their students into playing for people, and if a youngster sits in, they will do whatever it takes to make them feel comfortable and welcome. My 16 year old daughter is easing more into the circle of adults, and I have never seen an exception. Two examples: her high school music teacher is married to one of the Barra Macneils (Sheumas), and several times a year she puts on square dances at the school, and Kyle and Sheumas play all evening. These are the kind of dances where if you have never tried it you are encouraged… so it’s more enthusiasm than skill. There are probably a strong handful of students at the school playing fiddle, and anyone who wants to gets a chance to sit in and play, along with some of Kyle’s students. Last dance, my girl played a couple of sets, so there she was, head down with one of the best fiddlers on the island, learning each others tunes before they played for the dancers. She’s played at a few now, and it’s a heck of an opportunity.

Another example: there is a Celtic music mentorship program going now for young players, only in year two. You can focus on fiddle, pipes, guitar, or keyboards, with tutors, and there is dancing and other related stuff involved too. One mentorship day a month…. first one had a busy morning, then Mac Morin came in the afternoon to talk to them and play tunes. You also do at least one Friday night house party or similar a month, and there can be a lot more than that. Last Friday, I took her to a house party in Antigonish, just off the island…. the kids were all mixed in with adults from Cape Breton and the local area. Great tunes and dancing. Saturday, there was a youth session (but open to anyone) at a local business in North Sydney, so she played all afternoon with people like Kimberley Fraser (one of the organisers of the group), Ryan J. MacNeil…. Sheumas MacNeil landed there with his wife and kids, so they all sat in. Next time I wouldn’t be surprised to see a few more Barras there. Great afternoon. The program is through Feis Cape Breton.

Someoen said something about being a student of a genre is different to growing up in a tradition… here, it feels like a tradition. My girl heard her first fiddler at only a few weeks old, at a funeral. At three or four she was asking for a fiddle. She started lessons at five, with the fiddler she heard at that funeral, whose family were all musicians going back through the years, one of the musical families, she’s still with him ten years later. Another kid in the Feis group is a MacMaster; he was in the Feis group last year on fiddle, this year on piepes, but still playing lots of fiddle with the group. His aunt is Natalie, and his great uncle was Buddy. He’s definitely, I think, part of a living tradition. http://www.feiscapebreton.com/ https://www.facebook.com/feiscapebreton/?ref=page_internal

Anyway, my point is, here, I think things are strong, but that’s due in large part to a serious and continuing effort, all across the island, to keep it strong, and nurture the young players. They’re doing a great job.

Re: Will the tradition still be around 50 years from now?

It will still be here but not necessarily recognisable or how we view things today. In fact, things have changed drastically even within the last 20 years or so.

The nature of “the tradition” will depend on what happens during the next 50 years, of course. Players will learn and gather tunes and songs differently. We already do. Most of the younger musicians learn their craft through courses, and attending the activities of various organisations as opposed to “at the knee” of older experienced musicians. In fact, the attitude at many sessions and informal get togethers is that such gatherings are not the place to learn the music. That should happen elsewhere and players shouldn’t really participate until they are ready. Also, there seems to be less willingness on the part of older players “to show things” to novices. Rather, they are advised to take lessons or to go on courses.

Of course, there are still many informal sessions with helpful older players and a formal element to teaching and learning has always existed too. However, I now feel that the divide is probably more pronounced these days. For good or ill, depending on your point of view.
