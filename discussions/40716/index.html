“I do not think it means what you think it means.”

We’ve had a few discussions recently about playing from memory vs. playing from sheet music, as well as learning tunes by ear vs. learning by using sheet music. To quote a now-famous line from the movie, The Princess Bride:
http://www.prdaily.com/Uploads/Public/i-do-not-think-it-means-what-you-think-it-means.jpg

I have never seen anyone play from written music at a session and when I asked in another thread if anyone else had, there were no affirmative replies. I therefore conclude that this is a red herring. I suspect it simply gets mixed in with a discussion about the virtues or lack of same of learning a tune from written music.

The debate about learning from a written score is what I am aiming at with my playful quote from the movie. The notions of what may happen if you learn (meaning memorize) from written music stem from a misunderstanding of what it means to be able to read music. Assuming that learning this way results in a rigid performance or one that is locked in or otherwise lacking in interpretation, flow, lift or spontaneity can only support an argument that accepts those assumptions. Nothing could be further from the truth. When a player suffers from the foregoing shortcomings, I submit that the problem is in the player’s skill, not the method used. However one learns a tune, the process of committing the melodic line to memory is only the starting point, not the final result. Once the tune is memorized (which I contend ALL, or close to all, players who attend sessions do), the process of how one will ultimately play the tune begins. From that beginning point forward, the process is identical no matter how the melody was initially learned. If the player does not progress beyond simply having learned the melodic line, either by how an aural source played it or the way someone transcribed it, the problem lies with the player, not the source.

And this endless debate is growing tiresome. My underlying sentiment is that I don’t give a hoot how you learned the tune. What’s important is how you play it: What kind of rhythm you have, what kind of phrasing you play it with, what kind of variations you use in it, and how well you adapt to playing it with others around you, especially if they play it differently than you learned it…

Re: “I do not think it means what you think it means.”

“learning this way (from sheet music) results in a rigid performance or one that is locked in or otherwise lacking in interpretation, flow, lift or spontaneity…”

Yes this is a common fallacy.

It comes as a surprise to most people that the often beautiful, soulful, and expressive music one hears in film scores is being played by people who are sightreading the music cold. (You get a couple run-throughs, but the point is that the music is being sightread. It’s not being played by people who “know” the music… it’s new music, and no-one knows it.)

It takes great musicianship to have cold sightreading sound expressive and spontaneous.

One pro told me “the secret to being a good studio musician is being able to produce the composer’s intent.”

This comes from the musician’s knowledge of the idiom.

Likewise, somebody who has a keen understanding of the ITM idiom, who is a sightreader, will be able to produce idiomatic performances by means of cold sightreading. The written notes are the skeleton which the musician has to put meat on, whether it’s symphonic or jazz or ITM.

When I’m sightreading an ITM tune I’m no more bound by the specifics of that particular arrangement than I would be if I were picking up the tune by ear. Either way I’m modifying the tune to fit my notions of what sounds good. Usually the written versions I see are further from the way I play than versions I hear, so I’m following written versions less strictly than heard versions.

This topic of producing idiomatic performance from sightreading is of course an entirely different topic than the topic of learning modalities.

Personally, my brain has two distinct modes:

1) studio gig sightreading mode: read what’s on the page. No memorisation, no learning, occurs.

2) ear mode: pick up a tune on the fly. It goes into short-term memory and will probably be forgot shortly after. Long-term memory requires repetition over time.

Actually I prefer the ear+eye mode, where I can watch the fingers. I pick up tunes more quickly that way.

“Once the tune is memorized the process of how one will ultimately play the tune begins.”

That process, for me, is there from the get-go, while I’m sightreading; what I’m playing as I’m sightreading is suggested by the notes on the page, but for sure isn’t dictated by the notes on the page. I’m seeing the basic outline of the tune and the motif-families and replacing those at whim with other members of the motif-families as they occur to me in the moment.

I sure wouldn’t waste time memorising a version I had no intention of playing.

Calum’s post really hits the nail on the head.

I just wrote something very similar, but I’ll repeat it again. When learning Irish traditional music as a beginner, there are three distinct (but of course not mutually exclusive) things that are being learned:
1. The instrument
2. The tune
3. The music (aka the style or nuances that don’t exist in written music)

The OP is arguing about how people should or should not learn the tune, but every teacher / experienced musician that I know that argues for ear learning is doing that as a shorthand to mean “You can’t learn *the music* from sheet music”. Sure, lots of people learn tunes from sheet music successfully, but to do that they know the music already (or it won’t sound like Irish music).

The catch-22 is that people who rely completely on learning *tunes* from sheet music almost definitely have not really learned the *music* yet. It’s absolutely true that one cannot tell whether someone terrible has not learned from sheet music or not learned by ear… but one can certainly tell that they have not learned!

“It is probably rare for an ear musician to go back and learn how to read.”

Not so very. I learned music and played by ear from the time I was seven. I didn’t learn to read music till I was about 30. Even now - nearly 50 years later - I find it difficult to get the gist of a tune from the dots unless I have heard the tune before and have an idea of how it goes. Some of the musicians with whom I play, who can’t read music except minimally, have a great sense of the music: melody line, rhythm, ornament, phrasing. Mike Rafferty comes to mind - I once heard him say, “Don’t ask me if it is a C or a C#, because I don’t know music that way.”

That said, it is inevitable that if you play music for years you will learn to understand the notation to some extent, and learn to “read” music. Pointing out the difference in the tune by saying, for instance, “that’s an A right there, not an A#,” is a valuable aid to learning. This is as opposed to playing the note in sequence and asking the other person to hear the difference between the A and the A#. The written notation is only one way in which our brain maps the tune, much as a street map stands in relation to the streets. Once you know the area it is much easier to navigate it from memory and acquaintance than it is to get around by continually referring to a map.

The thing that twists my knickers is when a person new to the music comes to me with a basket of tunes in a binder and proceeds to play from the dots. The playing is inevitably stiff and un-natural. Clearly, the better way to learn is by hearing the tune and repeating it by rote, bit by little bit, phrase by phrase, until it makes sense. This is the clear consensus of the posts in this thread, and one which I believe to be true.
