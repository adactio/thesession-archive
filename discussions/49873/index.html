Repetition Incoherence.

I’m sure others have experienced this, as I’m certain it’s just part of the learning process:

You’re practicing a phrase. It’s not necessarily a difficult phrase, or an easy one. It’s just something new or variant that you haven’t done before. It may not even be giving you much trouble, it’s just taking some time to develop that reflexive, automatic, motion of 2nd nature. And then you get it right. And then you get it right again. Then AGAIN. AND AGAIN. You got it. It’s there. And then… it’s not there. Your hands and brain just stop cooperating and start throwing curves at you. And you lose it. It’s time to stop and take a break or move on to something else.

Why do you think this happens? I mean, it logically follows that once you’ve figured something out and you “know better”, that you would always have it. That you would always do it right. At least, when it comes to something as simple as a melodic phrase or harmony line. But it doesn’t work that way, at all. It seems that life isn’t so simple and straightforward. It’s almost bizarre in a way, I think. As bizarre as when something kicks your butt over and over again, and I mean it gives you absolute hell; Only for it to be immediately friendly and agreeable, so much so that it’s like it never gave you any trouble at all, after you take a break from it for a while and come back to it.

Just something else i’ve wanted to talk about for a long time, but never could find the words to describe the experience. The last few seasons, I’ve been learning some due music from other cultures. One of the songs was a Colombian song with a simple IV-I-V-I chord progression. Learned it last Summer. Wanna know how long it took me to get that progression down-pat? …I’M STILL PRACTICING IT. Apparently, in all of my years of piano, I never learned any songs with a circular IV-I-V-I progression, and my muscle memory is just NOT having it. I don’t get it, it’s beyond me. And to add insult to injury, the song is in the key of C Maj. Go figure lol.

I would like to hear other’s thoughts about their experience with this phenomenon. I hope you’re all doing well at the turn of the year!

Re: Repetition Incoherence.

This is a standard thing in general cognitive psych and in language acquisition (where it’s part of ordered development and is called the u-curve).

Basically there are a bunch of things happening at the same time whilst learning tunes. Your brain is building mental representation of the tune (ie you can hum it, or “hear” it in your head), you are building new neuronal setups for movement, etc, and these things are evolving simultaneously. Sometimes because we have limited attention and brain “bandwidth,” not everything works at once.

This also happens when one over-practices a specific thing eg a phrase. Memory works best as a mix of repetition and contrast so mixing your tunes when practicing helps.

Re: Repetition Incoherence.

Here’s my approach. Generally, if I can play a tune through three times without too much fuss, I figure I “have it.” But, how to get there?

A useful idea is that most tunes have highly recognizable, playable and easily managed sections -- latch on to them, and then build the tricky parts around them -- for example a non-obvious interval that eludes you, or unexpected pattern of repeated notes, or parts that fold below (or above) your range. It takes a bit of nerve to launch into a tune alone, so best to play along with a reliable source (recording, live session, teachers, …)

There are many phrases / motifs in Irish music that recur, and I’m sure that recognizing these (in context) gives you access to a free section of the music that you don’t have to memorize. You already know it! The term “muscle memory” is perhaps overused, but I’m sure this is how the best players so easily acquire new tunes, on the second or third hearing.

And, of course, as I’m sure many on this site would agree, best to learn by ear. Can you lilt, hum, or whistle the tune you are learning? If needed, the notes and dots can help decode a difficult passage. Get rid of the notes once you have the hang of the tune. Happy playing!

I think that having to repeat something more than normal to ‘get it’ shows that there are things about it that are unfamiliar in some way. Aspects where previous experience isn’t helping us so much.

The way things get organized in our heads so that we can recall them when needed seems mysterious but their are training techniques that were used by teachers (taking a short break, sleeping on it, spaced repetition etc) that were hit upon long before modern neuroscience.

I now recognise a distinctive feeling of tiredness creeping in when ‘new things’ are having to be processed. It may be awkward fingering or a melody that does unexpected things. Time for ‘take a break’ or ‘sleep on it’.

I remember when starting out on whistle I had got to where I could pick up tunes in D major or G major fairly well and was feeling confident I was getting somewhere. Then one where the ‘home note’ was the high D took ages - I wasn’t used to using the scale in that way because it’s not particularly common. Similarly at a tune learning workshop several of us found a tune in B minor hard work.

So my thought is - if it’s hard to make it stick is there something about it that makes it an outlier within previous experience? Or maybe subtly - and not obviously - slightly different from something familiar and well embedded in the head.

↳ Posted by Jon Kiparsky on January 11th, 2025.

Re: Repetition Incoherence.

When dealing with new music, creating it or learning it off records or whatever, for phrases and longer sections, I always try to first learn the length of it straight through, and then as a foundation, get to know the rhythmic or bar structure it’s placed within, so I can feel the beat and phrase structure with its tempo, and then fit the note phrases within that…….I sense the timing of the notes, don’t rush it, don’t separate it from the musical structure it’s part of. If it’s 10 bars long, 20 bars long, whatever, learn the underlying musical structure and that can give the notes the feel they need to stay in place.

If you’re dealing with a rhythmic structure that’s difficult, with notes or phrasing that are complex, learn it bit by bit, and then run it all together later. First, just listen to it without playing the instrument. Get the musical structure clear, the feel of it, clear in your mind, dance to it, snap your fingers to it, play the drums to it, before trying to play the “notes” instrument. Is it formally composed and arranged? Improvised? Trying to learn too many parts of this process at the same time, if it’s awkward, can fry the neurons. Break it down into parts if that helps.

Re: Repetition Incoherence.

A lot of interesting ideas! Thanks everyone for sharing!

I looked into the “u-curve”, and being a language learning enthusiast, I can see the links. I try not to take for granted how “fragile” the human mind is and how truly complex learning really is; Especially when it comes to matters of physical and motor skills rather than just acquiring pure information. So many things are happening when we’re learning pure information, and there are so many facets to the information learning process. Adding physical skills like playing musical instruments, or speaking(writing), adds another layer of complexity that now the body has to deal with alongside the mind.

On the note of fatigue: It’s interesting how mental fatigue is more abstract than physical fatigue. Whereas, you can quantify muscular fatigue by depletion of glycogen, by the actual wear-and-tear from muscle usage, and by the buildup of lactic acid causing discomfort; I would argue that we’re less competent of the mechanisms that cause mental fatigue, but there’s no denying that it’s happening. I remember posting in the past about my experience with napping/meditating during long practice sessions. I think someone had posted a discussion asking about the most effective practice methods. But yea, taking a moment to breath, to clear, to distract, to not think, to not move, makes all the difference, especially in the long term.

On the note of actual tricky bits: I think that when it comes to music that does give us trouble, a number of things could be happening. On the one hand, sometimes there are tunes that have phrases so unique that you just always remember them. One the other hand, like in my example above, sometimes a phrase is composed of everything I thought I knew, but is done in such a strange and unfamiliar way that it gives me trouble. I think the simple fact is, if something is unpracticed, it needs to be learned. We think it should be easy for us because we’re only accounting for the ways it’s familiar. We’re not accounting for the one thing about it that’s throwing us off.

Thanks again everyone for posting! This was some much needed intellectual stimulation!

> Especially when it comes to matters of physical and motor skills rather than just acquiring pure information. So many things are happening when we’re learning pure information, and there are so many facets to the information learning process. Adding physical skills like playing musical instruments, or speaking(writing), adds another layer of complexity that now the body has to deal with alongside the mind.

I think I might turn some of that around.

Pure information’s actually more difficult to take in, because there’s no framework or points of reference. It’s why people only tend to retain long strings of numbers if they have to punch them into a phone every week.

Music’s not at all like that. For a start, when we sit down to learn a reel we already know a lot about it. We can predict some things it is going to do, and disregard a lot of things it’s not. That framework’s a heck of a shortcut.

But the fact that movement and gesture, rhythm and breath are involved is also part of what makes it possible to learn tunes and retain them for years. When we first pick up an instrument we start laying down those simultaneous patterns (this movement = that sound), and they’re part of the framework. Try sitting on your hands and saying aloud the notes of a tune you’ve known since you were six years old – the reason it’s next to impossible is that you never learnt it as a series of notes. Whether you were singing it or playing it, your whole body learnt it as a piece of choreography, and that’s a lot easier to grasp than pure information.
