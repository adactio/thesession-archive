Re: What does mean to a common ITM player getting in touch with the outstanding top performers?

I don’t go out of my way to play with outstanding top performers(because I know my limitations and I’m not naturally pushy) but, sometimes, you can’t always avoid it. This happens especially at folk festival sessions and sometimes you don’t always know who you are sitting next to or you might look up and “ so and so” has joined the session. Most “top musicians” who go into commoners’(excuse the expression) sessions are pretty down to earth people anyway and they are much less concerned with you than you are with them. I mean that in a nice way. About 3 years ago, Johnny Cunningham came into Sandy Bell’s one Sunday, sat down and joined us in the session. No fuss, although he was nice enough to buy a round. Great guy. Those of you who know Cathal McConnell will know that he’ll have a tune or song(and a drink) at any opportunity. There are many more like this.

I much prefer the musicians who like to get “mucked in” with the rest of us. You’ll get some who hang around on the periphery until “encouraged” to play. Invariably, it can be a “showpiece” or “Look at what I can do” performance after which he’ll retire to the shadows leaving the rest of us to carry on scraping, plucking etc in our own inferior manner.

On the whole, however, most traditional musicians are easy going people. Arguably, the top performers are even more so as they’ve little they need to prove.

John

Re: What does mean to a common ITM player getting in touch with the outstanding top performers?

My most recent contact with professional tradists has been that they were warm and down-to-earth. However, their presence at the session really made it difficult to enjoy because it changed how everyone else behaved. People didn’t relax; the craic dried up and went away and those few locals with chips on their shoulders (unchecked egos) took over and embarassed the lot with their aggressive demonstration. I gave up and went outside to hang out with others who didn’t want to play the ego game.

In all honesty, I also didn’t enjoy the internal struggle I faced: of *course* I would want someone whose albums I’ve bought to hear me play and recognize that here is another good fiddler. And I also didn’t want to give into that desire because then I’d be doing the same thing that was irritating me at the session. I’d rather we’d all gotten together for pints instead.

This was not the same in other places I’ve been - some sessions are used to having famous faces and they have learned how to relax.

Re: What does mean to a common ITM player getting in touch with the outstanding top performers?

“I’ve played on stage/in sessions/etc with ***” etc etc

One of the more disappointing things I’ve seen at both Irish and US summer schools is the number of people who push to get into sessions with the “famous players”, so that they can subsequently talk about the fact that they’ve done it. I’ve seen a lot of potentially-good sessions get killed by this kind of crowding, so much so that I know a number of people at summer schools who intentionally *don’t* tell others where they’ll be sessioning.

Of course, this is not a problem if folks just want to go and listen, but it is unfortunately a real phenomenon that sometimes some people are more concerned with pushing in, to be able to say “oh, yes, I sessioned with x/y/z last night”, than with the calibre of the music that results.

I know that’s cranky, old-farty, and pessimistic of me in the context of this thread.

chris smith

↳ Posted by 🎻Johnny Jay, Melody Player 🎻 on December 20th, 2003.

Learning over time

I’m an American w3ho has been living in Cork for a while, playing music and meeting a lot of people. My favorite session to go to is hosted by the accordion player from a ‘famous’ band. I played with those guys for three months before I even knew that they were ‘famous’.
What I got out of that session (and continue to get) is great tunes, great conversation, a few pints and encouragement to keep playing. I feel I’ve really benefitted from the high caliber of playing. It’s different than a one-off folk festival-type session because I’ve been going for months now and actually gotten to know the lads and the tunes they play.
Real learning takes time. It’s amazing to be able to say that you’ve played tunes with or seen in concert these top class players--but to be truly influenced by them is a different story.
