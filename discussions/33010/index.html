↳ Posted by Weejie on December 21st, 2013.

Re: rarely heard instruments used to play traditional music.

I too cannot see that this is a ‘debate’. It’s a matter of history & fashion, surely.

Pipes, flutes, whistles & fiddles are truly traditional Irish/ Scottish/ English instruments - portable & played among the people.
Harps are also traditional, & Victorian pubs often had one, but not now (like the piano). The small harp or clairsach is traditional & had something of a revival, but not a lot of players seem to find their way to sessions. I don’t know why.
Mouth organ & jew’s harp were popular in the nineteenth century, especially in America, but the sound is not to everyone’s taste, if not played by a virtuoso.
Pianos were a sign of gentility; banjos were part of American traditional music but became more widespread in the later nineteenth century - see the jokes in the late Victorian ‘Three Men in a Boat’.
Before accordions, there were concertinas, but they have passed out of fashion a little. They were still ‘big’ in the North-East of England in the seventies & eighties, to my knowledge.
Accordions also ‘came in’, and became popular for dances because the sound carries well; they also attract pianists to play them, because, as pointed out above, you can’t lug a piano along to a session.
Mandolins and bazoukis (and guitars) are ‘foreign’ instruments but have come into vogue since the folk revival of the mid twentieth century. Guitars are usually used for chords & percussion, it seems. Could it be the same for bazoukis? Maybe it’s more difficult to learn these instruments - a lack of official teachers or mentors you could learn from ‘down the pub’?
Percussion instruments are also subject to fashion; might have been spoons once - then people rediscovered the bodhran (presumably there was always a breeding stock of the beast in selected localities) - there are now shaky eggs & other imported drum thingies.

Surely what you encounter in a pub session depends a lot on the area? In some localities it might really be down to one or two keen individuals.

To add to yhaalhouse’s intriguing list, I suggest comb & tissue paper. That may have fallen out of favour with the demise of Izal. :)

Mollie

Re: rarely heard instruments used to play traditional music.

This whole discussion has taken on the tone of a bunch of early PDQ Bach recordings, LOL. I keep waiting for the “windbreaker” and “slide windbreaker” to be suggested along with the calliope four hands. I’m sure there are “purists” who insist upon only playing with certain Instruments, but when you get right down to it, just about every trad instrument can be traced to another culture either used concurrently or predating the use on the auld sod. Harp has the distinction of being one of the oldest trad instruments, followed by early flutes (whether bone or wood) and some form of percussion. Fiddles probably came over in the 15th and 16th century from the main. Accordeons, Concertinas and Union Pipes didn’t arrive until the 1800’s, the same with banjo and guitar (in it’s present form). Piano is a continental instrument that arrived with the Aristocracy.
To me it’s all a moot point and the proof is in the pudding. Traditional groups like the Chieftains have played with many artists and orchestras that do not use traditional instruments. Groups like Silly Wizard used electric bass guitar and keyboards for many of their songs. They and others like them were successful.
In my mind, if it adds to the texture of the song, Play It!
