Re: Overcoming a tinny fiddle sound

If you decide to go for lessons – something I would most strongly recommend - go to a teacher who will guarantee to teach you at this critical early stage how to get a good strong tone and to play in tune and in time. The ability to do these three things will pay enormous dividends as well as giving you the much-needed confidence that all beginners need.

Oh, and before you do anything else, get someone to check that the distance of the strings from the fingerboard (the “action”) isn’t too high. If there’s one thing apart from carppy strings that will make playing difficult or next to impossible for a beginner it is too high an action. Basically, a high action makes the strings hard to press down and makes it difficult to play in tune. New cheap fiddles tend to be like this. Any violin technician will fit a new bridge which will bring the action down to a usable level, and this is easy enough to do along with a change to better strings.

Re: Overcoming a tinny fiddle sound

One important possibility is the ‘soul’ of the fiddle, the soundpost. The positioning of this is crucial in achieving any quality of sound. The soul of yours will be cr@p wood.
You can choose a soul your self in the right establishment or get the luthier to do so.
The wood wants to be as tight grained as possible, Grain per mil is min, you might have 2-3 mill on the one you have.
The soul wants to be 6mm from the center of the bridge to the center of the soundpost. precisely under the E string, Have a look….
If you bounce a number of sound posts from a couple of inches up each will bounce differently, the bigger the bounce, the better the soul.
The same applies for the bridge, same grain concept, same bounce concept. You want a bridge that is signed by its maker. with a good bounce and a tight grain.

The good fiddler would probably be using his/her own bow, possibly a rather better item than the beginner’s bow. A good bow can have more effect on the tone than a change of strings. Re: Overcoming a tinny fiddle sound

“better with or without shoulder rest”

No one should tell you to use or not to use a shoulder rest, chin rest, etc., at least not unless they actually see you holding the fiddle and playing it. It’s a decision that needs to be based on how to fit the fiddle to your body.

Some people need a shoulder rest, some don’t. Some have to have a chin rest, others can do without. It’s mostly a matter of physiology and personal taste.

Personally I only use a deflated play-on-air shoulder rest (to keep the fiddle from sliding off my shirt) and no chin rest. But I have thick shoulders and a short neck, there’s not much room for me to fit in any more than the fiddle. Other people need high chin rests and shoulder rests to play comfortably.

General statements about using or not using chin rests or shoulder rests are a bit of a pet peeve of mine, because it caused me some damage as a beginner when I was convinced to use a chin rest and a bridge-style shoulder rest--not a good fit for me! It’s not the same for everyone. You have to fit the fiddle to your body, and that’s what those things are for.

Now, whether or not you use these things does affect the tone of the instrument slightly. But that’s nothing compared to the problems caused by a badly fit fiddle!

You’ll find many examples of accomplished players who have a beautiful tone with all sorts of set-ups. Some chin /shoulder rests are better for the tone than others, but it’s probably not worth worrying about as a beginner.

P.S. I’m not sure that jig was talking about this sort of thing at all, just throwing my two coppers in about chin rests and shoulder rests.

Re: Overcoming a tinny fiddle sound

Screetch, the whole of the fiddle, from the tailpiece button to the end of the scroll, is a vibrating structure. This includes the neck of the fiddle. Dampen any of these vibrations and it will affect to a greater or lesser extent the overall sound.

A simple experiment will show this. Hold the fiddle as lightly as you can with the left hand (or even drop the hand away from the fiddle if you feel confident about holding the fiddle up by using the chin). Play a steady long note on the A-string. As you play it, touch the back of the neck of the fiddle lightly with the fingers of the left hand. You should feel the neck vibrating. Now, still playing that A, hold the neck tightly, with the the palm of your hand against it, and listen to the change in the sound. So avoid the “death grip” like the plague, and keep the palm away from the fiddle. OK, I know Martin Hayes uses his palm to support the neck, but he’s allowed to because of his name :-)

An incorrectly fitted chinrest will noticeably alter the tone if the chinrest is actually on the belly, which is when it will certainly dampen the vibration of the bass side of the belly. That bass side of the belly plate is the most important vibrating part, driven as it is by the bass foot of the bridge and the bass bar inside. When I use a chinrest (I don’t always - sometimes it feels more comfortable not to use one) I make sure it is perched as close to the edge of the fiddle as possible so as not to affect the vibrations of the belly plate too much.

I won’t comment much on the shoulder rest because I never use one, but I shouldn’t have thought it affects the tone all that much because it is in contact only with the edges of the fiddle and not with the vibrating plates. The other thing I’ll say about shoulder rests is that they seem to be forever falling off at inopportune moments!

I’m not sure, either, about the direct effect, if any, of the body cavities on the sound of the fiddle (after all, it’s not a blown instrument, and the player isn’t singing), but it is certainly true that good posture and correct breathing together will relax the body and provide a strong base for the arms to work from and for them also to be relaxed. This, I think, more than anything else will encourage a strong singing tone.

Re: Overcoming a tinny fiddle sound

PK, “playing *into* the string”. Well, it’s the basis of getting a good tone, so in that respect it’s not high-level technique, but it’s the sort of thing that is more difficult to explain in words than to be shown by a teacher face-to-face. Anyway, I’ll have a go.

You need that relaxed, controlled weight of the arm from the shoulder to the finger tips, on the bow. My cello teacher, many years ago when I wasn’t quite yet into my teens, advised me to think of my bowing arm as a long, infinitely flexible and heavy rope (“hawser” was the word he used), and to imagine that the bow hairs were just below the surface of the string. The bow hold is important; for the cellist holding the bow with finger-tips (except for special effects) is out - a strong, firm but relaxed hold is necessary, with the fingers virtually wrapped round the bow but relaxed and NEVER touching the bow hairs, the thumb being in the recess in the frog and ALWAYS bent, the little finger (pinkie) always being ready to balance the bow when playing near the frog, and, most crucially, the index finger powering the bow and initiating its movement.

In much more recent times my violin teacher says virtually the same thing, advocating a fairly flattish right hand with the bow stick being more or less in the join between the 1st and 2nd phalanges (the 1st phalange being next the hand) of the 1st, 2nd and 3rd fingers. Again, the index finger is the powerhouse for controlling the bow, and the thumb and little finger being positioned and used pretty well as on the cello. A natural consequence of this hold is that the bow will tend to be flat on the string and not tilted towards the fingerboard (as in some playing styles) and makes for a firmer tone. Cellists tend to have the bow flat on the string. A relaxed right arm from the shoulder to the fingers is again the key to a good tone.

An exercise – or rather, a technique – to get the feel of playing into the string (a good firm tone with no hint of glassiness) is to place the middle of the bow on the string and to bear down on it with the weight of the arm so that the hairs get close to the stick, but without actually moving the bow. If you then gently move the bow a fraction of an inch to and fro it won’t move on the string but will move the string. This is what you’re aiming for when you start a note from silence – a very slight “scrunch” as the bow starts to move and the string starts to vibrate. This faint “scrunch” is the “attack” people talk about and is a very definite and precise start to the violin or cello sound, and is one important reason why the experienced players sound that much better than the not so experienced. If you don’t have that “attack” and the bow is already moving when you start applying bow pressure than the sound will gradually come into being (a bit like an aeroplane landing – you never quite sure when the wheels first touch the ground) and won’t be so definite and precise. It will also be that little more difficult to play into the string.

As I’ve indicated at the start of this comment, description is one thing, but you really need a live teacher to show you how to do it.
