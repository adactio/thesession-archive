Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

Last year I landed my first solo fiddle gig. I was hired to play Irish music at one of those senior citizen communities during their St. Patrick’s Day luncheon. So I regaled them with a selection of reels, jigs, hornpipes, some waltzes and polkas, and a slow air or two for an hour. I received some feedback from the audience afterwards. They remarked that it was felt more like a concert. They were disappointed that I didn’t include more of the standards that they could sing-along to - When Irish Eyes Are Smiling, Danny Boy, etc. Ah… well now I know for next time. And next time is this St. Patrick’s Day because I just landed a very similar gig. I can think of a plethora of songs, but I was wondering which ones are especially good selections to include?

Then one guy in particular came up to me afterwards and told me that I should go further than simply naming the tunes I’d played. He told me that I should have discussed the history of the tunes as well, how they got their names, etc. I dunno… this one guy certainly did, but do audiences in general really care about tune origins and etymology?

Re: Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

I play a lot for senior groups, Irish and Old Time. It’s my experience that these audiences want the “feel good” songs that came out of Tin Pan Alley, songs that make them laugh, or the “sappy” sort. A traditional dance tune or two thrown in for a diversion is OK…usually something close to what they’ve heard before. (Think Irish Washerman or Campbells Are Coming, mostly jigs and hornpipes). It might not thrill me to play them but these occasions aren’t about me. If you’re lucky enough to have an audience with a depth in Irish music, like the “one guy in particular”, you can feed them all the tunes and background you like. Just know that’s a rare day indeed. Like it or not the tunes we love to play have a very narrow appeal. I’ve learned that these people want what they want. Best to know your audience. Who knows, maybe one of these places are in my future. If it is I hope the entertainment director doesn’t book an accordion player who specializes in Taylor Swift covers.

Here’s an analogous incident. Years ago I was in a Rock cover band booked for an early Christmas gig at a major hotel in town. We set up early in the day. Just before the start we looked out and saw that we were clearly the wrong band for that group. A dilemma for sure. We sent some guys home (with pay, it wasn’t their fault) and the piano player, drummer, sax, and I (bass) played the date with mostly clean and simple jazz standards. Turned out to be a successful start. We played more often, and for more money, over the next couple of years than the rock band ever made. Know the audience, give ’em what they want.

Re: Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

You’re entertainment. Play things they can differentiate and get into. A few recognizable dance tunes are fine -- Chase Me Charlie, the Harvest Home, Sally Gardens -- but otherwise I would expect they’d prefer airs and songs. I think context is important -- they don’t want to hear about how Sean Ryan wrote the Ballyoran Hornpipe and it won the All Ireland over Father Kelly’s, they want to hear a story with a good punchline about famous musicians that will make them laugh. Depending on the age of your audience and where you live, consider a few tunes related to the local area.

Re: Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

Okay - for the Seniors, based on my experience. First of all, you do NOT have to do a full program of nothing but Irish music. Play a handful of the standards that they can sing along with - and they will, even to unaccompanied fiddle. That takes care of the Irish. Then go into any bunch of songs they know and can sing with. Throw in Irish Washerwoman somewhere, another (Tin-Pan Alley) Irish song or two - and Paddy’s your uncle.

Bear in mind that the entertainment director often doesn’t know what they’re talking about, or what they think they’re telling you and what you are hearing are two different things. So if they say they want an hour of Irish fiddling, they may well mean that they want the kind of thing I’ve described above. Or they may have the misconception that their folks will love an hour of hard-core Irish fiddle because it’s old and they’re old, so - what could go wrong?

Seems that here in the USA loads of venues want to hire “an Irish band” for St Patrick’s Day but what they really want is a Top 40 band, a pop/rock/jazz band, that wears green hats and sprinkles in a few Tin Pan Alley and Clancy Brothers songs here and there.

I cycle between Tin Pan Alley Irish, Irish folk songs, and Sean Nos songs. Each genre has its own audience, and sure enough there will be a person or two that perks up when I play the “real stuff”.

Re: Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

There’s the old saying “he who pays the piper calls the tune”.

If you dress however you feel, play whatever you like, and tell the person who hired you to p!ss off, you’re not likely to be hired again.

Back in the 1970s I had joined my first Pipe Band, and at the first gig they had me join them we had to wait around before playing and the Pipe Major said “the entertainment business sucks.” It opened my eyes to the fact that we were professional entertainers, something that hadn’t occurred to me.

In the 1990s I was doing 40 or 50 weddings a year, in addition to funerals and studio work. It was a large part of my income. I was successful because I was professional: the first to arrive, the last to leave, dressed and groomed well, played well, would take on any special tune that was asked for, and was always cheerful and eager to please. I followed the advice given me by a professor when I was at university: “the secret to success in the arts is to undercharge and overdeliver”.

Being professional doesn’t mean you have to “sell out” or “be a clown”.

Re: Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

I second the idea of doing one “standard”, e.g. Danny Boy, and then one or two sets of dance tunes, then a “standard” again, etc.

As for talking about the tunes, pick tunes with funny names, e.g. “The Floating Crowbar” or “Upstairs in a Tent”. The audience will love the story you invent about how the tune got its name, and will then appreciate the tune more.
Another good one I always do at this kind of gig is the Tarbolton set (Tarbolton / Longford Collector / Sailor’s Bonnet), where I talk about how it was recorded on a 78 by Michael Coleman in 1934 and has now been on the Irish music charts for more than 80 years, and that the audience will understand why when they hear this tune set that will make their feet tap.

A lot of useful suggestions here about the music and the audience…I am not any kind of musician, but I have been part of quite a few ‘arts’ projects where material is put in front of an audience, and it is absolutely true that everyone’s a critic, and you can’t please all of the people all of the time. I’ve also worked with various groups including people who have a number of forms of social disadvantage, mental illness, etc. My current group, in aged care, are my toughest crowd yet. A lot of it is due to things that happen to some people as they age resulting in a more cautious approach, less flexible fluid thinking, possibly more negative responses in some cases. People in aged care are more likely to have changed in these ways compred with people the same age who have not needed aged care yet. Aged care itself can further change some people, because it is an artificial environment where a person usually has less control and independence. So don’t be surprised when there is some negative feedback or even outright complaints. Even if you had happened to give more background information as one of your audience wanted, others may not like it.

Your audience also have had their own experiences in life, and their own preferences, and you can’t guess just what that might be.

Sounds like you are doing the right thing by considering what else your audience might like. So don’t let the negative feedback affect your confidence or make you give up. Do what you reasonably can to handle feedback positively, and you can always say “Oh! I’m sorry you didn’t like it…” then let the person tell you what they ARE interested in. That’s part of the event for that person. The rest probably enjoyed themselves at least a bit, and you should notice the good things about what you did too. You played decent, real fiddle for some people who needed the opportunity to hear something good they might like. It’s loads better than crap on a CD.

Re: Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

“He can do what musicians did for almost two centuries before Riverdance: Get a job.”

To many of us, music IS our job.

I was a member of the Musician’s Union for many years.

It’s a job. You show up, work, and get paid. You do the work you’re given, just like any other job.

In the 19th century, in the 18th century, it was the same: some people were professional musicians, others were nonprofessional musicians (or as the US tax laws state, music is a “hobby”).

JS Bach and Patsy Touhey were professionals. They made a living and created great music.

BTW I also take issue with the negative attitude of taking on new pieces of music for performance. It’s what professionals do.

The epitome is Studio Work, where you play whatever music is put in front of you. You’ve never heard it before, you’ll never hear it again. and you don’t care.

I find it interesting and sometimes challenging to take on new music for a gig. I’ve played Disney songs, Andrews Sisters songs, Stephen Foster songs, tunes from Riverdance and Braveheart and Titanic. I find the music and sightread it at the gig. Re: Advice for St. Patrick’s Day gig?

The point is, if you’re being paid you’re a professional, and if you want to be a professional it behooves you to act professionally. Know your audience, and do what works for them.

Nevertheless I strive also to throw in some education along the way. They want to hear Danny Boy and Irish Eyes and they’ll get what they want, but they’ll also get Ar Eirinn ni Neosfainn Ce Hi and A Spailpin A Ruin and they’ll like it. They want to hear the Highland pipes (yes on St Patrick’s Day) and they’ll get what they want, but they’ll also get the uilleann pipes and the whistle and they’ll like it. Afterwards they’ll come up and ask questions and go away knowing more than they did.
