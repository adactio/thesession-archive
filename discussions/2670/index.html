Re: Finally got a fiddle

My deepest sympathies to you Mike on your affliciton. The only cure is complete submission to the disease. :o)

You’re right about the reels you name above--not the easiest to start on. Not impossible either, but you’ll want some friendlier tunes at first to develop bow control and basic left-hand familiarity with the strings.

In your shoes, I’d start off on some straightforward jigs before tackling reels--it’s easier for most people to hit the downbeat in a jig, and you can start by just using a single bow stroke for every note (rather than slurring notes together on a bow stroke).

Some fairly easy jigs for fiddle (and I think all of them are in the tune database here) include:

My Darling Asleep
Swallowtail Jig
Connaughtman’s Rambles
Tripping Upstairs
Saddle the Pony
Stan Chapman’s

If you’re set on starting with some reels, I’d recommend:

Silver Spear
Wind that Shakes the Barley
Sligo Maids
Bantry Lasses
The Morning Star
Within a Mile of Dublin
The Abbey Reel
The Bag of Spuds (in A dorian)
The Bank of Ireland
Humours of Tulla
Castle Kelly

The advantage of these reels is that they can be played without rolls or triplets and still sound okay, and the fingering isn’t terribly difficult, leaving you to concentrate on the bow (which is where your focus will need to be for the first 15 years :o).

Of course lots of other tunes are at least as accessible to newbies. These are just the ones on my short list when I teach.

In all sincerity Mike, welcome to the fold. Keep the hair side down….

Re: Finally got a fiddle

Yep, that relaxed, effortless approach is what makes it sound so good. Crisp triplets come with economy of motion--a miniscule twitch rather than a clenching of forearm muscles. It’s funny how hard it can be to let your arm and hand relax, and how little motion is needed to get such powerful sound from the bow on the strings. As has been said here before, you’ll do better if, instead of trying to grind the tunes into the fiddle, you lighten up, relax, and let the tune lift off the strings of its own accord.

Try a search for “effortless” to find some of the old discussions here about how to do this--you’ll find most of the threads are specific to fiddle technique. There’s a lot of advice going back, what three years or more now?(!)

As for your parents, buy a heavy rubber mute--it fits on the bridge and dramatically reduces the volume. And learn to play softly--that’s part of playing relaxed. With the mute, you might be able to play in the basement while your parents sleep unawares upstairs….

Off topic but prompted by Will’s mention of mutes - I bought a large brass mute (the “hotel room practice” model) and found it had two beneficial side effects:

1) it alerts me pretty quickly to any developing twists or bends in the bridge - the mute won’t sit properly, will sit at an obviously skwiff angle or won’t go on easily.

2) it blocks my view of the point at which the bow touches the strings and stops me relying solely on my eyes to know I’m aiming for the right string - a dependency that’s probably easier for beginning trad players to develop than beginning violinists because the violinists often have sheet music to look at while they play. (Maybe that’s not really a problem but I certainly feel more in control of my playing if I don’t have to rely on seeing the strings.)

Re: Finally got a fiddle

Scales (and practising your rolls within a scale) are very useful for most people. Kevin Glackin told me a while back that he still does scales, usually every day. George Keith gave me specific scales to practise to work on specific issues in my playing, including rolling each note in the scale all the way up the strings and then all the way back down. Other teachers have recommended scales to me as well. If, as a beginner you can’t play at a sufficiently advanced level that you can’t concentrate on remembering the tune, remembering how to play, play in tune and with good tone, and going a steady and slow pace, ALL AT ONCE, then scales are extremly good for practising all of those things, because they *are* no -brainers.

(I find that experienced players have forgotten what it’s like to be a beginner and not be able to remember or do all those things at once -- and also that “slow” has gotten a lot faster for them; to the extent that I will oftentimes ask the players at our tune-learning session to start off a tune at “slow” for them, since “slow” for me is generally much faster than they should be playing yet.)

However, while some players find them a good exercise and aren’t particularly bothered by them, some players find them incredibly boring and get nothing out of them except for high blood pressure. (I tend to think that they aren’t approaching them with the correct mindset if so, but oh well.) It’s useless to work with them if you find it to be complete drudgery and don’t get anything out of them (unless you want to learn the mental discipline that is attendant upon doing something you don’t like to do because it’s good for you). At that point there’s nothing for it but the Concertina Reel as an exercise. ;)

Especially with *really* enthusiastic beginning players, I find that the triumph of getting a tune out and having it sound good even if relatively unornamented (I teach cuts and triplets first rather than rolls while teaching them a tune, but start the rolls during the same lesson as exercises first) is worth it for their self-esteem as players. After they’ve learned their first tune, we take that first tune and work it for variations and different ornaments (including rolls, long rolls first, then short). I find that students who don’t have naturally good finger coordination are so frustrated by their lack of instantaneous ability to roll well that they get far too discouraged if I push rolls at them too early within the tune -- they actually prefer the exercises first.

I do try to remember that there’s nothing wrong with simply playing the tune without much variation or a ton of ornaments (back to that “fine, unornamented style” that Professor Carson writes about in Last Night’s Fun), regardless of the scorn of players who think there is. During a group lesson once, Shannon Heaton once exclaimed, “what’s wrong with just playing the tune? Don’t worry about the rest of the stuff that you can’t do yet, trust the tune to carry itself and just play it as well as you can!”

Re: Finally got a fiddle

This is one of those sticky questions that doesn’t have a completely satisfactory answer. I agree with Michael that cuts, rolls, triplets, and all the other bits are part of the tune. Once you can play all those ornaments, they quit being ornaments--they become part of your basic dialect for enunciating the tunes.

But I assume we’re giving advice here to someone who’s wrestling with a bow for the first time, and to combine that with full-on rolls right off the bat is likely a bit daunting. I’ve hardly ever had a student who could get rolls right off. It usually takes a few days with simple cuts before they’re ready to even comprehend a roll let alone attempt one.

I’ve found that beginners respond well to being taught a tune that does work okay without ornaments--My Darling Asleep or The Morning Star, say. This lets them get comfortable with basic fingering and intonation, while concentrating on the rhythm with the right hand.

As for teaching the timing of rolls and triplets, etc., if you’re systematic about it, you *can* teach the timing by emphasizing how a dotted quarter note or eighth-note-quarter-note in combo feel in the tune, and then showing how the ornament fills that space. This way the learner doesn’t have to backtrack and relearn the tune to keep the rhythm and phrasing solid when they’re ready to put ornaments in.

I think there are two types of “beginners” here: the first is someone who is holding a bow and fiddle (or any other instrument) for the first time. I’ve found you can give such people only so much to work on for the first week--relaxing while playing a simple, plain melody with good rhythm is more than enough to ask. I’d be leary of explaining how to roll to someone who can’t yet keep the bow straight across the strings….

The second type of “beginner” is someone who has played the instrument for a while but isn’t familiar with Irish music. Here you’re much more likely to be able to introduce them early on to ornaments and other aspects of playing that make the music what it is.

Also, I know several top notch players who say they still like to learn a tune stripped of all the ornaments first, so they can anchor themselves in the simple melody, and then let the ornaments creep in.

Yep, it’s important for beginners to listen to how the music is actually played so the ornaments sound natural to them, and not some add-on. I think that’s crucial--getting that sound into their heads so they’ll *want* to learn rolls and triplets, etc., so they’ll know something’s missing in the plain version.

As a student is getting up to speed on a tune, I like to play along, starting off with the plain setting, note for note what the student is playing. And then gradually slip in a cut note here, a triplet there, a roll, anything to vary it. And you watch to see whether they notice it, and when they do, this look of revelation comes over them, and they usually ‘wake up’ and rattle off a bunch of questions: “What was that?! Re: Finally got a fiddle

Ineresting. I think it makes a big difference what instrument you’re learning. With whistle, it’s fairly easy to get a tone out right away, and the fingering is straightforward (at least compared to a fiddle or accordion, say). Flute’s about the same, except for wrestling with the embouchure. I found it relatively easy to start twiddling with rolls on whistle and flute within the first day.

But fiddle demands a lot of attention to the bow in the beginning, especially if you’ve never held a bow before and tried to drag it musically across the strings. I’d say for most people, the learning curve on fiddle is much steeper, so in most cases, it’s easier to learn ornaments after you’ve got a grip on getting a simple tune out.

Of course, the key is listening to the music, so at least you’re filling your head with the tunes as they’re actually played, preferably by players whose music you admire and hope to echo some day. You’ll get used to hearing all the cuts, triplets, rolls, drones, crans, etc., and want to include them in your own playing. In my experience, it makes more sense to learn these techniques *after* you’ve heard them used in context.

Re: Finally got a fiddle

Martin, I tend to agree with you on this, but I can see the other view too. Take Last Night’s Fun, for example. The start of the B part ( | d ~f3 a ~f3 | d ~f3 egfe | ) would sound pretty lame without some ornament on those long f’s. Rolls or triplets--anything rather than just that long sustained f. And because this music is created on the instruments that play it, there are lots of tunes that rely on stock ornaments to flesh out otherwise numbingly plain phrases.

Of course, there are also tunes that sound just fine without much ornamentation. They tend to be more melodically sinuous tunes, like the Morning Star or the Scholar.

The ornamentation also plays a vital role in giving the tunes lift. In fact, this is the primary purpose of ornaments in Irish music--to liven up the rhythm. So while it’s often possible to play a nice “listening” version of a tune without much ornamentation, you’re more likely to get people tapping their toes or on their feet dancing if you know your rolls, cuts, triplets, etc.

All of which I’m sure everyone here already knows…..

Re: Finally got a fiddle

Ottery, that’s one of the downsides to learning a tune with ornamentation already in place--for beginners at least, this tends to cement that version, and it can be difficult to change, to accept other versions or variations. But if you learn the basic melody line, and then add ornaments into the mix, you’ll have a better sense of the varied ways the ornaments can be invested in the tune.

I understand what the christmas tree analogy is getting at, but I don’t much care for it. The ornaments, while distinct from the tune, aren’t something to be “hung on” like tinsel. To me, it’s more a matter of fitting them *into* the tune. My analogy might be using stones to build a bridge. How your bridge looks, and how strong it is, depends on how you choose to place the stones. The melody is the mortar that holds it all together, and the stones are *in* the mortar.

Re: Finally got a fiddle

’s not strictly accurate in terms of rhythm, but then you already knew that:

Long roll: quaver/cut/quaver/tap/quaver

Short roll: cut/quaver/tap/quaver

Condensed long roll: semi-quaver/cut/semiquaver/tap/quaver (so yeah, it’s almost like a short roll)

My personal favourite-
The long double-cut roll: quaver/cut1/semi-quaver/cut2/semi-quaver/tap/quaver [cuts 1 & 2 are on different fingers]

Then there’s the the double-condensed long roll, the condensed short double-cut roll, condensed long double-cut roll… and the condensed long double-cut roll with cheese. (not counting all the variations :-))

I’ll readily admit, this is the outer extreme of ornamental trainspotting. I can’t play most of them yet, certainly not up to speed in a tune, but they’re there. Looming.
