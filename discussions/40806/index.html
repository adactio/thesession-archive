How long before you call it a day?

Locally, there are many people who after many many years can still not hold down tune but still persist at sessions. I have been brutally reminded of this today. This dramatically reflects on the quality of “Folk” “Irish” “Session” music.

I know this is not a unique situation. As a community fair to it’s own values and existence I ask whether those that recognise this should be more vigilant, albeit that requires hard truths to those that may be close to them?

To put this into perspective, I teach young players who are way away ahead of anything I could achieve at a similar age…a good reflection of the power of todays media…but I would die a slow death along with them if they sat in on some of the “music” I witnessed today…which in turn would sound a death bell for our music.

I know this is a sensitive subject, and controversial, among what is a friendly and close knit community, but I have grown up within this community and whilst very grateful to it, I also passionately believe in it’s forward strength and the dilution due to incompetency is not in my reckoning an acceptable or sustainable quality.

I sit back and await your thoughts, but most of all a suggestion as to what action may. :-)

Re: How long before you call it a day?

When I read the title “How long before you call it a day?” , I thought the subject was going to be about musicians who were or may have been good in their day but were now a little bit past it as opposed to mediocre players in general.

So, I thought the question may have been along the lines of how do you tactfully tell these older players when to stop or should you at all.
Of course, many older players are still excellent and even most of those who are “failing” slightly still have a great store of music and knowledge from which younger players can draw. They should be given the best of respect.
Sometimes there may be issues.. e.g. drink may have taken its toll over the years or illness(which can’t be helped). Older players are often slightly deaf and often don’t have the same intonation when playing an instrument such as the fiddle etc.
However, even in such situations, I’d be very reluctant to make comment in a session situation especially to or about respected musicians. After, it’s our choice to be there and we can always set up another session elsewhere if we are irritated by one or more musicians. A close friend may be in a position to talk to a player in such circumstances but the rest of us might be better keeping our own counsel.

As to whether a player him/herself should realise when it’s time to call it a day or not is another matter.
Personally, I believe that everyone should be allowed to play music for as long as we want regardless of our ability or present standard. However, we should also be aware when and where it’s appropriate to do so and, hopefully, realise when we may be out of our depth. It’s difficult enough for younger players sometimes to be aware of their shortcomings but, arguably, even harder for older players especially those who were keen and very “handy” players over the years.

Re: How long before you call it a day?

Have any of you here been to the session at Jim of the Mill Pub in Upperchurch, Co. Tipperary? If you have, then you know Bridie runs a completely inclusive session that welcomes players of all levels. She even shushes the always-capacity crowds so that a new player can have the floor for a solo or two. Some of the musicians/singers there are amazing, some pretty good, some pretty average, and some not so great, depending on who’s there that night, who the visitors are, who the regulars are. Doesn’t matter. All are welcome, and their participation at whatever skill level they have is encouraged and appreciated. I’m sorry, I just don’t see how that could be eroding Irish traditional music or the “traditional” session (which isn’t actually so traditional if you take a look at the history). Sessions like Jim’s build up the tradition because they are inclusive and welcoming; they epitomize the Irish tradition of hospitality, which is at least as important as the music itself.
