Re: Why didn’t jigs cross the Atlantic ?

Just be aware that Franklin George picked up the modern Highland pipes as a foreign instrument, just as he might have picked up the Sitar. In other words it’s not an indigenous survival in Appalachia, just as his playing of a modern Irish session jig is not.

We have to keep in mind that even in deepest darkest Appalachia (which I hope you-all are pronouncing apple-LATCH-uh, so you can sound like a native) musicians were exposed to various influences. My Grandfather, born in a log cabin in a holler, played what we call “mountain music” on fiddle, banjo (both 5 and 4 string), and mandolin, but also would play currently popular songs (1920s and 1930s) on piano. And he played Euphonium in the local Colliery Band.

My Grandmother, by the way, also born in a log cabin in a holler in 1896, recalls hearing pipers regularly as a small child. It was due to recent immigrants from Scotland come over to work in the coal mines.

To my ear the closest music to Appalachian music is Shetland music. There, too, it’s dominated by fiddles, with plenty of droning/chording. I’ve often wondered if both Appalachian and Shetland fiddle music are survivals of a style once common on the British mainland.

Re: Why didn’t jigs cross the Atlantic ?

“…on a related subject, any theories why bagpipes/bombardes/hurdy gurdies didn’t make it across…”

I don’t know but I’m glad they didn’t. :-)
You have to remember that many settlers were very limited as to what they could carry over here. Add to that the fact they they probably spent 99% of their waking life trying to avoid death by any of dozens of means. Any commonly used musical instrument would have to be: easily portable; easily learned and played; easy to maintain; easy to find or make replacement parts; easy to find repair if broken. I imagine many purchased or made their instruments after they arrived, and for any luthier to survive there would have to be a market for his instruments. So he built what he knew he could sell: fiddles and dulcimers. Some instruments, in those early days, were associated with the military or wartime activities: bagpipes, drums and flutes for example. So perhaps those instruments were shunned by an early population eager to shed any vestiges of pre-emmigration hostilities.

Re: Why didn’t jigs cross the Atlantic ?

There are a couple of assumptions about French music in Canada and “Scotch-Irish” Appalachian music that are at odds with the historical facts.
The Voyageurs were mostly “New France” residents with a smattering of Acadians, English, Orcadians and French mainlanders. The Company was supplying the French and English fur markets. Most of the New France population originated in previous generations in central/west France and had no relation to Brittany, its language or its music and instruments. The voyageur working life was brutal and short. Most died from malaria, broken bones and complications due to hernias. They had no time to pay music around the camp fires. They did have plenty of songs though. Most were lost unfortunately due to a combination of almost total illiteracy, harsh collecting conditions and condescension from the ruling class. The music played in Montreal and Quebec City was mostly French parlour music from the European classical tradition with some light opera thrown in. Quebec music, as we know it today, is a melange of French, American and Irish music that was developed in the late 1800s after the French American colonies collapsed into Quebec in the 1700s and Irish Famine refugees sailed up the St. Lawrence to Grosse Ile.
The “Scotch-Irish” (their name, not mine) are another thing all together. While there were Ulster planters in their midst they were a mix of Presbyterian Dissenters from the Borders, Dissenters from Europe and planter families from Ulster. They became the westward wave of protestant migration into Pennsylvania, Ohio and down into the Carolinas. They called themselves “Irish” to differentiate themselves from the local Americans but then when Irish Catholics started turning up on the eastern seaboard they adopted “Scotch-Irish” to make sure there was no confusion. If you know anything about their “culture” you’ll know that they hated music and pretty anything that wasn’t hymn based was considered the work of the devil. History shows that The War of Independence was as much a war between the crown and dissenters as it was a war of American independence.
Insofar as jigs over the pond are concerned, surely Gary Owen must be the most well known example, albeit in a 6/8 quick step version?
I am now willing to be put before the committee.

Re: Why didn’t jigs cross the Atlantic ?

The ‘Scotch-Irish’ didn’t remain a homogenous group once they got to America. There was music all around them. The banjo migrated northward from the deep south and down from the urban north east, early bluegrass gospel music still bears a strong resemblance to call and response forms of the ‘precenting’ hymns of the Scottish Dissenters which is where the bulk of the influence on the Appalachians comes if you ask me. The whole string band phenomenon went across all communities after the civil war and influenced trends everywhere as the minstrel shows took it on the road. Both sides during the civil war employed rooms full of songwriters to churn out patriotic songs and tunes, a huge number of the writers were Irish as it happened and they naturally employed their native melodies to expedite the process. The 6/8 march is common in both Ireland and the British Isles because it’s easy to march to. I would argue that rather than these American forms being extensions of existing traditions the new music that evolved was due to American exceptionalism. There’s no need to wonder why the jigs were never adopted, it doesn’t matter.

Re: Why didn’t jigs cross the Atlantic ?

Most modern banjo historians agree that the banjo, though having roots in West Africa, originated in the caribbean and was spread to the middle Atlantic States through slavery. In the 1840’s and 50’s it was made internationally popular in the minstrel shows, which sometimes included accordions and flutes as well as fiddles, banjoes, tambourines, rhythm bones and jaw bones. The early banjo tutors include 6/8 tunes often referred to as Irish jigs and where probably meant to be danced as character pieces. I think the advent of ragtime and jazz, and the change in dancing styles that came with this music, relegated the humble jig to the “old fashioned” category. The one I have seen turn up is the Irish Washer Woman. When Bob Dalsemer published his study of West Virginia dances in the late 1970’s he found the old timers playing this tune.

Re: Why didn’t jigs cross the Atlantic ?

As other people have noted tunes in 6/8 time are relatively common in various North American Fiddle traditions outside the South. Some of the 6/8 tunes played come from the common stock of ubiquitous tunes like “Irish Washerwomen” or “Haste to the Wedding” etc., some might also come from military marching bands (whether fife and drum or brass) like “The Campbells are Coming.” Tunes from sources like that are the most likely to have occasionally popped up from southern fiddlers. In the North there are many more 6/8 tunes, many of them devised in the 19th century to accompany the changes of a quadrille. This morning I was listening to my CD of Les Raber, a fiddler born in Allegan County, Michigan in 1911, which contains many 6/8 tunes and most of them are unnamed. If you want to know more about 6/8’s in the North go to http://www.giffordmusic.net/Fiddle.html where Paul Gifford has generally put up many of the field recordings he made in the 70’s and 80’s including many jigs.

Re: Why didn’t jigs cross the Atlantic ?

I re-read the OP and I noticed I didn’t really answer his question, after all if jigs are prevalent in other American and Canadian traditions it only makes it more strange that they aren’t in the South. As I alluded above some scattered southern fiddlers were recorded playing 6/8 tunes, but they usually only had one or two out of a repertoire of hundreds. Even then most of the 6/8 tunes in the South are either the commonest tunes like “Irish Washerwomen” or fife and drum tunes spread by bands associated with local militias. Interestingly a lot of traditional vocal music in 6/8 time, but I don’t know of any examples of those being transferred to instrumental dance music.
Here is Allan Jabbour playing one jig collected from Vaughn Marley and Tinsley Clapp of North Carolina
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zs0Y4ZvecCc

The funny thing is that this version is uncommon since the tune is usually played in 2/4 time as “Green Willis” in the south, Tommy Jarrell even plays it as something closer to a waltz.

There are several theories as to why 6/8 is so uncommon in the south:

The Temporal Hypothesis: This argues that 6/8 tunes were not as common in the 18th Century as they are today in Irish/Scottish/English music, so that when the settlers migrated to the south they carried a smaller store of 6/8 tunes to begin with. The strengths of this theory are that the few jigs that are played in the south are often the ones attested to date back to the 18th century and that many of the jigs (though by no means all) in Northern traditions probably originated in the 19th century. The flaw is that written sources from the British Isles in the 18th century do not seem to be lacking in jigs.

The Banjo Hypothesis: This holds that the 5-string Banjo (or at least the clawhammer style of playing it) is just unsuited to playing jigs. The strength of this argument is that the area in the U.S. where jigs were traditionally uncommon maps well to where the 5-string banjo was most popular. The problems with this argument are that modern banjo players, like Ken Perlman in the video above, can devise ways to play jigs and that after the Minstrel Craze of the mid-19th century and before the introduction of the Tenor Banjo 5-string banjos were also popular in areas of the U.S. where jigs were historically a major part of the repertoire.

The Dance Hypothesis: This theory argues that the types of traditional dance common in the south favored reels over jigs. Many of the older Contradances require particular tunes and jigs and reels seem about equally favored (Portland Fancy and Rory O’More are two examples of dances that require jigs). Since historically contradances were not danced in the south outside the fashionable elite of the coastal cities most fiddlers would not need to know any jigs. The problem with this theory is that although it explains why fiddlers would not need to know jigs it doesn’t account for the fact that southern fiddlers play many other types of listening pieces that aren’t suitable for dancing anyway.

Very interesting reading on this thread! I’m posting from western Canada. The fur traders and settlers here wrote in their diaries about frequent house parties like the one in the video above from Appalachia - rugs rolled back and the house vibrating to the stomping of the dances, followed by some late night drinking and “fisticuffs” amongst the men (http://www.winnipegfreepress.com/local/Christmas-on-the-Prairies-362955121.html). The music (and cultural background) in the Red River Settlement (about 1812 until 1870) was mostly a combination of Quebec/French Canadian music, Scottish music, and Aboriginal rhythms. The Métis music here is most representative of the mix. There’s a sound sample and historical info at https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/aboriginal-music-song/028012-3200-e.html and
http://www.virtualmuseum.ca/sgc-cms/expositions-exhibitions/batoche/docs/proof_en_the_red_river_jig.pdf
The Red River Jig is the most well-known Métis tune. Flute is less common in this music than strings and percussion, with the reason being given that fur traders and settlers made almost everything themselves and could more easily make and copy string instruments. Singing is recorded as absolutely part of daily voyageur and fur trader life (pre-1812) with instruments shared and played at the forts when they were not travelling. Old-time French Canadian voyageur tunes can be heard at https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lUHmPPPWlgY (click on “watch on Youtube” and you can hear other old-time French Canadian and Métis tunes posted).
