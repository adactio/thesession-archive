An Extensive Analysis of Learning by Ear.

Disclaimer: This is my first attempt to explain what’s happening in my head when ear training so please bare with me as this is a long post.

One thing that has fascinated me about my social experience with music, is that people have always assumed that I’m self-taught, which to me is a boast. I think I’m the farthest from “self-taught” an artist can be. But one thing I’ve noticed, is that this has always correlated with the perspective of me being an “ear” player.

One thing that I appreciated right off the bat from the underground Irish music culture, is that it admires and respects the ability to play by ear. But as with anything, there is a diversity of people and some of us don’t play by ear. Because ear players are the majority in this music, I feel that there may be some myth and delusion about our actual skills and abilities(like the myth that I magically taught myself how to play music). No doubt that these notions and delusions may have discouraged some sight-readers, if not many, that have tried to put away the sheets and manuscripts to learn how to “play by ear”, only to find themselves annoyed and frustrated that they can’t figure it out. I mean, it’s an aural skill, and we’re all aural creatures right? So what I would like to do is talk about and explain what I believe it means to be ear trained, in case some of you may already be ear trained and not even know it.

When discussing an abstract idea like a learning process or a state of mind, ideas like the ability to play by ear, it’s hard to explain what it actually is, so it’s always good to first understand what it’s not. With that being said, playing by ear is NOT “playing on the fly”, at least not in the practicing sense. Playing on the fly is the ability to pick up on the music of an *unpracticed* piece during a performance, and figure it out before the display ends. Playing on the fly is a skill that I feel relies more on the ability to *feel and follow* the music around you, improvising, because you don’t know where the music is going or what you’ll have to do to keep up. Given, you probably need to know how to play by ear if you want to attempt “playing on the fly” in a session, it’s different from playing by ear in practice.

Also, playing by ear is NOT improvising, again, not in the practice sense. Now, there is a misleading term that people use for improvising, and that is “Play it by ear”. I’ve heard this term used when someone is expected to maybe give a speech they haven’t had the time to prepare for, or if they need to make means for a project they don’t have all the materials for. What I think they really mean is “play it on the fly”, or “improvise”.

(Be advised, a few of the terms I’ll be using to describe the following skills are generic/made-up to explain what happens in my own mind during ear training)

To me, playing by ear in it’s most literally intended sense, is playing from memory a piece of music you’ve learned aurally. That’s it. Learning by ear, ear training, is simply learning music aurally, and is what I’ll be discussing for the rest of the post.

Some of it’s core skills are memory, interval, and contour.

Memory - Retaining a melodic/harmonic sequence

Interval - Understanding the space between pitches

Contour - Recognizing when a sequence ascends and descends

I call these “core skills” because memory exclusively sets us apart from sight readers and interval/contour puts us on the opposite end of the “tone deafness” spectrum into “ear trained”.

Some other skills are mental slowing, mental transposing, and isolation.

Mental Slowing - Slowing down a sequence mentally to make it more processable

Mental Transposing - Transposing a piece mentally in case it’s source isn’t in it’s original key, or if the source’s key is incompatible with your instrument/skill level

Isolation - Shutting out all other sounds and suspensing a note, or looping a sequence mentally.

An all around and invaluable skill is “pre-learning”, which is your ability to learn the music of a piece before ever touching your instrument, something sight readers could also do. The time it takes to listen to a piece by ear and figure out what key it’s in, where it’s accidentals are, and the direction it goes is probably the same amount of time it takes to look over it on paper.

For me, learning a piece by ear begins away from the instrument. Things you can learn about a tune first through is what key it’s in and if it has any accidentals or key changes. When learning by ear, relative majors and relative minors aren’t considered cause they have identical scales, so we wouldn’t call that a key change, but a common transition. After this is done, I listen for commonality and rhyme amongst the phrases. In most music there’s an underlying pattern, whether it’s rhythmic or pitch-wise, and learning this pattern is crucial for retaining(memory) and initial learning.

Now comes the hard part, note gathering, which is the actual process that’s happening when you’re learning by ear. You’re simply gathering notes. Given that a lot of the easy but necessary work is already done, sometimes it comes in leaps, sometimes it comes in inches and centimeters. This part, in my opinion depends a lot more on the music than your ability as an ear player. The reality is, some tunes are harder than others. It’s an inevitable reality.

Though I can’t really distinguish what officially makes a piece of music more difficult than the next, there are things that are a challenge and pose a definite threat when learning by ear. These things are the tempo of source, harmonic density, and tonality. Fast recordings, with a bunch of extra stimuli, in an unfamiliar(unpracticed) key is all the more harder when it comes to ear training.

First thing during note gathering is Isolation. Doesn’t matter if it’s one note, two notes, or a dozen(however far in you can hear before you start losing pitches). When it’s about isolating a single note(usually the first note in any given phrase) it’s suspending the note in your mind, like a drone, and finding it on your instrument before you lose it. If you do lose it, simply return to the source. When you make your educated guess and think you have the right note, you play it against your source to check it’s accuracy. When it’s about isolating multiple notes, or a phrase, it’s finding the highest and lowest notes in the phrase and then gathering all the notes in between(we isolate the highest and lowest note because they’re the easiest to distinguish from the rest of the phrase). Then again, when you make your educated guess, play the phrase against the source to check it’s accuracy.

All of these things are what’s happening in the mind when learning by ear. I don’t want sight readers to believe that we ear players can just up and play anything we hear, that we don’t have to go through a learning process, or that we don’t have to work and practice. I also don’t want sight readers to think they’re doing it wrong, or that it requires immense talent they don’t have, or that they’re missing something. It can take me personally 30-45 minutes to gather all the notes of a 2-part Irish tune that I vaguely already know, and this is only the 2nd part of the learning process. The third part, getting it under my fingers, could take weeks of practice on fiddle and concertina, and even longer on piano(the tune melodies are not very piano friendly). Some tunes are easy, some don’t take long at all. Some are so easy, you could learn them on the fly if the tempo was slow enough. Some are so easy you can learn them faster than you can memorize them. But my point is that even after you’ve figured it all out, the process is still there.

In conclusion, I hope all of this sheds some light on what our real experience of ear training is. As you can see, no smoke or magic, no super-powers and no cheats. Just the human mind and all of it’s glorious and wonderful brilliance.

Feel free to challenge or contest any of my statements made about learning by ear, with regard that this post is based on my personal understanding of it.

Re: An Extensive Analysis of Learning by Ear.

“ I don’t want sight readers to believe that we ear players can just up and play anything we hear.”

That’s not really true, is it? Any melody I can think, I can play without error, having never played it before. In Irish music, however, we are not simply playing a melodic line. There are nuances, ornaments and articulation necessary to render a satisfying performance, but none of this has anything to do with the ear process. Don’t confuse learning a tune with the performance of that tune; the processes are completely separate. Performance quality develops exactly the same way whether the tune is learned aurally or from a written chart. The process of learning by ear relies on only one skill that many sight readers never develop: The ability to determine the interval between notes. Perhaps, as a second skill, you might add the ability to retain a tune in your head. I suppose there are some who simply cannot retain a tune from one day to the next, but that would be a real handicap even with the sheet music right in front of you. If familiarity with a piece does not eventually lock how it goes in your head, you have my sympathy.

Let me elaborate more on my point about intervals. If I want to teach you a tune aurally, I would play a phrase for you slowly and expect you to play it back for me. We would build the entire tune that way, phrase by phrase until you had played through the entire tune. I might then record it for you so that you would have a memory refresher that you would eventually abandon once you became accustomed to playing the tune. All ear musicians learn in that manner, with slight variations of how they get the source of the tune they want to learn. However, if intervals are a mystery to the learner, repeating a phrase that someone plays for them becomes a lengthy, if not impossible, process. They simply have no notion of how to get from one note to the next. This is when dependence on written music becomes a crutch. One does what one does by rote, not through any skill of knowing each note the way one does when singing.

All other musical skills aside, the one essential skill to playing by ear is to be able to duplicate what you hear. If intervals are a mystery to you, this simply cannot be accomplished without labor that will discourage the would-be ear player from continuing.

Re: An Extensive Analysis of Learning by Ear.

Knowing intervals is not a technical thing; it is kinetic. When you sing, you don’t even know what you are doing to go from note to note; you just do it. Similarly, when you know your intervals, your fingers know the distance from note to note. You don’t do this consciously. If you must read and thus think of each note by its value as an A or a C or whatever, your fingers are corresponding to the written note with no sense of the distance between notes. As a result, without the written notes, you are literally hunting and pecking your way to the correct note that follows the one you are playing. The process of knowing intervals must be as simple and unconscious as the process of singing is if you are to be successful playing by ear.

there is a little more to learning by ear than intervals and mimicking the tune. Honestly, first time listening to something I want to learn, I’m listening for form. I want to get the road map right off. Then once I have the form, I start in on the phrases. One of the things beyond the intervals I am listening for is the harmonic space opened up by the tune. For me as a guitar player, this is one of the big clues I use to determine how I’m going to harmonize the line. After I have the form and have decided where the phrases are, I’m ready to start getting into the intervals in each phrase. So I’m not only trying to mimic the tune, but also understand the form and the harmonic context of the lines.

The singing analogy can be carried only so far, because you can sing without ever having worked at it, but the handling of a musical instrument must be studied. Developing the connection between what you hear and what your hands do is acquired, not innate. Still, I believe by virtue of simple repetition, the skill can be developed almost without effort. The more you try the better you get, even if you never become a particularly good player, otherwise.

Re: An Extensive Analysis of Learning by Ear.

I see you play whistle. If you wanted to play Mary Had A Little Lamb, a tune probably imbedded in your brain, and your fingers simply knew where to go, you know your intervals. It simply means that once you find your starting note, you intuitively know how many fingers to lift or put down to get to the next note in the tune. You know approximately or (hopefully) exactly how far in each direction notes of a tune are from one another. If you don’t have that ability, the next note could be anywhere and you would have to hunt and peck to find it.

You need know nothing about music academically to know intervals. It is a developed skill that allows you to transfer what you hear in your head to the instrument without knowing what the next note actually is in the scale.
