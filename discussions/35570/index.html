Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

Concert pitch refers to the accepted standard pitch to which all instruments would need to be tuned.
It’s nothing to do with the key you are playing with, it’s merely a basic standard reference pitch for all instruments that need to be played together. A = 440cps is the current standard, but there were times when it was markedly different. It may well have been quite a lot lower in the baroque period, and the popularity of brass instruments in the early 20thc produced a gradual raise in the accepted standard, as brass instruments sound better in a sharper pitch, until common sense brought everybody back together to one standard again.
Why we play in D, and of course also G and A and all their related minors, is entirely another matter. It’s mainly due to the ease of fingering the fiddle and similar instruments in first position. If it was merely for the convenience of whistle players there wouldn’t be any tunes that went below D. You could well ask “Why do concertina players use C/G instruments, when the tunes are in D and G ?” That seems to be entirely due to the fact that the C/G concertina was cheap and most easily obtained when they first appeared.

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

On the secondary issue of “concert pitch”, my understanding is that it stands for that pitch that wasn’t band pitch. Band pitch (promoted by the Philharmonic movement in the 19th century) was high, circa A 452-455. “Band flutes” played at band pitch (high pitched Bb, Eb and F). Concert flutes played at concert pitch (D). In my mind’s ear, I can hear Miko Russell referring to playing on the kon-sart flu-it.

On the substantive issue of why are sessions in D, the short answer is they aren’t always. You’ll find sessions in Eb in Clare. You’ll even find YouTube videos in that key.

If the question is really why are tunes played in the keys they normally are, I think we probably have to look to the instruments that are not naturally chromatic and that have a relatively restricted range, e.g. the pipes and whistles. These instruments pretty much demand certain fingerings for certain tunes. Those fingerings translated into keys. Fiddle players could tune to them.

There must have been some interesting argy-bargy when sessions first started, allegedly in the late 19th century. Piper with flat set in B, whistle player with a non-tuneable C, flute players in D and Eb, concertina player in high pitch C/G, melodeon player in modern pitch C, tenor banjo with misplaced bridge, wood frame piano player with slipping tuning pins and fiddle player with migraine.

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

Forgetting about the complications of pitch, when it comes to keys part of the reason why so many jigs and reels are in D and G probably goes back to the18th and 19th centuries, when it was commonly accepted that different keys have different characters. That notion might have had some basis in the days before equal temperament, but it still persists today amongst many classical musicians.

Generally the sharp keys were/are considered to be joyful, stirring and energetic, the flat keys were considered melancholy, sad and somber. If you look at old Scottish fiddle manuscripts you find all the keys used, but most of the dance music is in the happy, sharp keys, and the flat keys are mostly used for airs and laments.

The second part of the reason probably lies in the dumbing down of music in sessions. A lot of tunes written in other keys get transposed into D or G in sessions because it makes them easier to play on most common session instruments.

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

The old traditional pitch of the uilleann pipes was usually around B, often somewhere between B and C.

Flutes were commonly made both in Eb (for military bands) and D (for orchestras) but in Old Philharmonic Pitch which was A=452.

A=440 wasn’t adopted in Britain until around 1900.

Fiddles, obviously, could be tuned anywhere.

Seems to me that D is a compromise, lower than the Eb band flutes but higher than the B-ish uilleann pipes.

Taylor was making D pipes (often sharp, probably around 452) back in the 19th century. As I understand it the reasoning was both to be heard better, and to be able to play with other instruments (most likely in 452).

The same thing happened in Bulgaria where the Thracian gaida was high-pitched but the kaval was low-pitched, fine for the traditional solo playing, but when bitov ensembles were created an in-between pitch was decided upon and new gaidas and kavals designed and built to play together (in D, as it happens).

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

Before the end of the 19th century ITM was almost exclusively played solo (mostly on fiddle and pipes), so in which key the tunes turned out didn’t matter at all (cf. Hammy Hamilton 2008, p. 41). And the first mass-produced whistles (Clarke’s) were in C, military fifes were in Bb etc. -- everything was all over the place. So when people started to play in sessions and ensembles they had to settle on one key/pitch; and as the fiddle can be played conveniently in D/G (and the relative minors) in the 1st position and concert flutes (which became increasingly popular in ITM by this time) are in D as well, this seems to be a logical choice to me. Only then whistles were (mass-)produced in D as well.

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

I’d say it has to do with the instruments involved. As others have said, pipes and fiddle would be the main instrument up until the mid- to late- 19th century, and would have been the most popular among master musicians. Chief O’Neill noted that flutes were extremely common in the late 19th century, but were more of an amateur instrument rather than a professional one: “As most Irish fluters were amateurs, or rather non-professionals, few are the imprints which their footsteps have left on the sands of time.”

The fiddle and pipes played solo wouldn’t have had to be in any particular pitch, and I’d be surprised if fiddle players really tuned to exactly GDAE except by chance or perhaps to play with others.

Interestingly, the original Clarke tin whistles I believe were in C, and many older fashioned players of the C/G concertina such as Kitty Hayes would have played tunes in C across the C row (like on a D melodeon) rather than the more common style today which jumps between rows to play in D and G.

So the D/G/Am/Em standard that we have today probably has a lot to do with the standardization that comes with playing in groups. On a GDAE tuned fiddle these keys are relatively easy in first position, and also work well on a standard concert flute with minimal key use or cross fingering. Since those instruments would have probably been the most popular, especially among amateur musicians, the standard would have been set from them.

Not true. The Boston Symphony uses 442 (although there are rumours that it goes as high as 444), and has since the Seiji Ozawa days. I believe the NY Phil does the same, at least it did under Mehta, and I’ve heard of some orchestras in the US using up to 443 or 444. Most major non-period specific orchestras now tune above 440, especially in continental Europe (the UK tends to adhere closer to 440).

That being said, 440 is the generally recognized pitch and is probably the pitch ingrained in most sessions due to electronic tuners and standard box tunings. Really, if you’re tuning you should always try to tune to the fixed pitch instruments in your midst (when playing with a piano orchestras usually tune to it rather than the customary oboe, or the oboe will tune to the piano before giving the rest the pitch). That way you don’t get into the trouble Tansey got into a few years back…

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

Back to the OP’s question.
It really doesn’t make sense to say a session is played in a certain key.
The tunes played in a session may be played in any key but it true that many are “D” tunes.
The fact that many of the tunes are played on diatonic instruments like whistle, pipes and melodeon dictate the key but have produced some of the great modal tunes we like. For example, a “D” whistle can play the D & G major scales but also B & E minor, E & A dorian, D & A mixolydian. But you’d be hard-pressed to play a tune in the key of Bb on the same whistle. So when we play an “A” tune it’s more likely to in the key of “A dorian” or “A mixolydian” than “A major”. (BTW, there are more than just a few Irish fiddlers who really can’t tell you what key they are playing in or care.)

An interesting distinction between Irish fiddlers and Old-Time Appalachian fiddlers is that OT fiddlers are well aware what key they are playing in because they have to play all the “D” tunes first then stop and wait for the claw hammer banjo players to retune for key changes. :-)

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

To clear up my earlier mystifying post:
Gaida: type of bagpipe played in Bulgaria and Macedonia.
Kaval: wooden flute played in those same countries.
Bitov ensemble: an ensemble of mixed instruments, both winds and strings, usually containing one Gaida, one Kaval, one Gadulka (fiddle), one Tambura (octave mandolin), and one Tuppan (drum). As you can see it’s exactly the same sort of thing that’s common in Irish music.

The various folk instruments existed in various keys, but Gaidas tended to be high, often in G, Kavals low, often around B, and so forth. When Bitov ensembles and larger things, essentially orchestras of folk instruments, became common starting in the 1950s, they had to decide on a compromise pitch and build new instruments. This resulted in a new lower Gaida and a new higher Kaval (the reverse of the Irish situation).

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

It was the Taylor brothers, uilleann pipemakers in Philadelphia, whose instruments were refered to as concert pipes. This was less about pitch (they were well sharp of A=440) and all about the fact that the Taylors increased the bore sizes in their pipes so they could be heard unamplified on the American concert stages. This gave rise to the term concert sets of pipes. Upon hearing these louder pipes, many old flat sets in Ireland were often crudely rebored to increase their volume. Most were ruined by this treatment. Wm. Rowsome examinded the Taylor pipes and came up with his big bore pipes which were tuned to A=452. The tone was better than that produced by the Taylor pipes. His son Leo continued with these pipes and they are the “big bore” standard which is still copied today. Upon the adoption of A=440 in the 1930’s, Leo was required to produce pipes in the new standard. He left the bores and lengths the same and undercut or scalloped the toneholes to drop the pitch, sometimes more successfully than others. He even tried to lengthen some chanters in the same attempt. The old flat sets were not referred to as sets in B or C, the bell note was referred to as D, no matter the actual pitch. Flat sets were referred to by chanter lengths like a 17“ or an 18” set. It is only in the last 30 years that sets of pipes have been referred to as B or C sets etc., as A=440 is now the pitch standard, pipe makers have been rescaling their pipes to play in these pitches, based on A=440. Old flat sets were sometimes close to modern pitches but often were not. An 18" chanter is somewhat sharp of B-flat and became known as a B-flat chanter. Some makers have modified their copies to produce B-flat based on A=440. This is all quite modern and the conundrum modern pipe makers must face when copying historic sets of pipes.

Re: Why are sessions in the key of D?

I would look at it as the key of D with the addition of a C natural note (or the key of G with the addition of a C#). In other words, it seems like 99% of the tunes can be played using the notes D, E, F#, G, A, B, C and C#. But tunes don’t have to resolve to D (major or mixolydian). Tunes can also resolve E (dorian, sometimes aeolian), G (major), A (mixolydian, dorian) and B (aeolian). But each of these modes are essentially still the “key of D”, just resolving to a different tonal center in cases where it’s not D.

To answer your question, I think it’s a compromise between the core instruments based on the notes that the majority of those instruments have the capability of playing. Sometimes an “A” tune will have a G#, and sometimes a melody will go lower than the open “D” on the 3rd string of the fiddle, but that’s rare.
