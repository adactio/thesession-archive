Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

I am only the first of many here who will surely tell you the same thing: Starting from the sheet music or “dots” as they’re known in these parts is a terrible idea. I’m not saying that you should never use sheet music but you really want to get the tune in your ear before trying to learn it.The dots will give you at best an incomplete and at worst a false idea of the tune. So listen to the tune. If you can find a real live player to play it for you that’s best,but you can use recordings if you listen to several different renditions. Try to find recordings by well known players as they are likely to be more reliable(I mean better) than the average you-tube posting,though not always.When you find a setting of the tune you particularly like listen to it until you can hum or lilt it through from beginning to end. Once you can do that you probably won’t need the sheet music.

Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

Fraser’s a wonderful player, but he’s certainly a Scottish player, rather than an Irish player. A good man to emulate if you want to be a Scottish fiddler - and very accessible, I think, for someone making the leap from the classical tradition, but not someone who’s going to help you sound authentically Irish trad.

For example… Fraser plays “Calliope” in E. Now, a lot of people know that setting and play it on the fiddle, because of him. But D is much more common among Irish players, and you won’t have flute and accordion and whistle players sitting there staring at you waiting for you to finish!

There’s certainly a bunch of beautiful Scottish repertoire… I’m thinking of the Gow and Skinner tunes, specifically, but there are others, where a classical technique can be very helpful in bringing these tunes to life, and this is wonderful, beautiful music, too.

But that’s a very different sound than Irish trad, and I agree, while you may be able to learn the notes from the dots, there are nuances of expression and timing that you will miss UNLESS you make * listening* to great players in this style be the cornerstone of your Irish music development.

Not just casual listening either, but focused attention to details.

Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

Hello: I am still enjoying the process of learning - only about 4000 hours into the process and have been recording my impressions in a journal of what works well and what does not. The process of learning is very interesting and it requires constant self evaluation. I don’t think anyone one has the holy grail. RE: sheet music -I have gone swung to both extremes - first trying to avoid the sheet music and then staying too long with it. With your background, you are off huge head start with the ability to site read. I would not chuck the sheet music. Personally I have come to think that the following approach is best: I use the sheet music until I can play the tune fairly proficiently without it - abandoning it when I think I have it - and going back to it every now and then to see if it need polishing.

As far as playing with a recording: I would recommend Matt Crannitch’s book“The Irish Fiddle Book” which has many tunes covering all types except for the March. With the book comes a CD with Matt Playing solo. Dr. Crannitch’s book is very useful when combined with a slow down app.

I would recommend playing with the sheet music at first as much as you need to commit to memory (which will go very quickly after a while and the rhythm of the different types of tunes begin to reveal themselves to you. I would shift from playing along with the recording and then just solo - playing with the recording may give you this wonderful feeling that you are making rapid progress - until you go back to solo and begin to hear the mistakes and the nuances of your own playing.

Some classical players who turn to IT music may have a little difficulty loosening up and getting the relaxed swing of the various types of tunes. And there may be a bit of difficulty eliminating or keeping the vibrato at bay. With your background, those things might be your only impediments.

I would say finally, the best dogma is to have none - to shake things up - to leave a tune when the mistakes begin to increase and go on to something else - to enjoy the process so you can make it a lifelong honeymoon.

I am currently reading a book about the process of learning music which may be interesting to you - by Barry Green -Principal Bassist of the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra and Professor of Music Cinn. College Conservatory of Music. He renders a very good understanding at how we learn music and what we do to impede learning.

Good luck and enjoy the journey.

Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

I’m sure that if someone played a note on the violin it wouldn’t take you long to reproduce it. So --
What I do when I’m learning a new tune is first of all make sure it is concert pitch using Magix or Audacity or something similar (this isn’t strictly necessary, but it’s handy for later when I compile the tunes into sets), then I play it as a loop (probably about 20 - 30 times through) in MP3 until I can more or less sing/whistle/lilt along with it, and have a general idea of the ‘feel’ of the tune, if you know what I mean: whether it is sad or happy, a dance or a march, if there are any twists or surprises, the overall ‘shape’ of the tune.
Then I slow it right down to half speed, pick up my fiddle and play along with the recording as best I can. That way I find out any awkward fingering or bowing, as well as beginning to get the tune into my brain.
When I can play it right through more or less confidently, I speed it up in stages, never going faster than I can comfortably manage, and trying out any little variations - musical and technical - that suggest themselves. If I struggle with any bits, I isolate the phrase in another bit of software, slow it down again and loop it until I’ve figured out what is going on and can play it as well as the rest of the tune.
When I get up to a reasonable speed -- usually not as fast as the original -- I slow it right down again and write out an ABC version, keeping it as simple as possible, leaving as much out as I can until it just fits in with the tune when I play over it on the fiddle. I do this for several reasons -- one is just as a record, to keep on my PC as a reminder. Another is to give me a visual reference when I try to play the tune later, when it is no longer fresh in my memory. Eventually it goes into a playlist so that I have something to play along with at home. I alternate between the original recordings and the ABC files, until I have the tune off to my satisfaction, and my own style begins to usurp the original.
Another reason why I make an ABC version is to analyse the tune, see where the obvious variations are being played and replace them with what I judge to be the original melody. I also compare bars to see whether they follow a pattern, for example if you see FADE FACE FADE lined up one above the other, you can check to make sure the C is correct, and if so, why.
Something else I do is look for as many versions of the tune as I can find, and see what other people are doing with it.
For the first week or so after I have learned a tune, I put the ABC up on the screen and play along with the recording, until I am happy that I can play it the way I want (more or less); then the original MP3 goes into one playlist, and the ABC version into another, and I play along with the ABC version, which is easier to manipulate.
Sometimes after learning a tune, I find myself not playing it, in which case it goes into a ‘rejects’ folder. Usually it stays there, but occasionally one will be redeemed some time later.
I should perhaps mention that I started out on the highland pipes, and as such learned everything from the score -- but you can’t do that with Irish music. If you don’t know what something sounds like, you can’t work it out from the dots.
Listen, and reproduce. When you get to the stage where you are playing a whole tune rather than a sequence of notes, memorizing the notes is not needed. It’s more like hearing someone tell a story, then telling it yourself.
Take it in stages, and remember that it gets easier as you go along.

Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

Some good advice here. I have taught a number of classical violin players to learn by ear. It is only a brief mystery to them, and as for method, whatever works. There is nothing wrong with a good transcription as long as you know it is only one of many ways to interpret the tune. Listening to great players can be misleading because they will chnage the key and put in unique variations just to sound different. Listening to many great players will only compound this, they are mostly trying not to sound like each other. If you learn every tune from a different player you will not develop a style, just a repertoire. Choose one you like, then emulate him/her until you can see through it or prefer something different, hopefully by then you will have developed a sensibility of your own. People who play very fast often have the tiniest of accent variations and when slowed will not give good clues. Find recordings with slower heavy accenting.

The key to using a transcription is knowing the rhythm, and this is where all “starting as adults” and those more difficult, “starting as adults in a country other than Ireland”, or the extreme, “starting as adults nowhere near another player” people have problems.

You need to ignore the classic meaning of the word ornaments, turn your eye away from the Christmas tree, and understand you are underlining/ornamenting the rhythm more than the melody. It is common for people working alone to add time with ornaments rather than mark time. To check for this record yourself without ornaments and see when you play with yourself (steady down the back) whether you get behind at the point where you play an ornament.

There are many fiddlers who play with a fully languaged bow and sound terrific, but the found the light classical player who wants to play ITM must find the plain bow, you can add the shapes back in later when you know where they are appropriate.

Remember the essential job is rhythm for dancers, the melody is how we amuse ourselves while doing it.

Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

For people that are starting from scratch, I find that there’s a common progression that goes like this:

1. Memorizing the pattern of notes.
2. Developing muscle memory (kinesthetic memory)
3. Internalizing the contour of the melodies
4. Musical expression of the melodies

The progression gets easier, believe it or not. With #1, you have to try to remember hundreds of notes and what order they come in. While there are people who have memorized thousands of digits of pi, etc, there’s a reason that our phone numbers are written in a broken up format, with parenthesis and dashes. Because people generally have a hard time remembering 7 or 10 digit numbers.

With #2, things get a lot easier, because your fingers flow, and you have less to try to think about. What’s unfortunate is that a lot of people stop at #2, and never progress beyond it. This is why trying to add an ornament (or god forbid, a melodic variation) makes you lose your place in the tune, or at the very least, lose the rhythm.

If you progress to #3, it becomes even easier. You end up only having to remember one thing, and that is “how this tune goes”, which seems daunting when you’re not at that point, but it’s when you get to this point that the lines start blurring between what tunes you’ve “learned”, and what tunes you “know”. You start playing tunes, because you know how they go, but might have no idea whether you’ve ever played that tune before. This obviously requires a real familiarity with your instrument, and with the music itself. And it can be a disturbing thing too, because you play a lot of tunes that you don’t necessarily know all that well, but continued exposure to the music and playing with other people, you build a solid repertoire of the music. There are some things that I recommend for people who are progressing to #3. Listen to as much Irish trad music as you can, but instead of being a passive listener, be an active listener. Sing along with the tune the second time through, even if you don’t know the tune. Find your mistakes, make corrections and each time the tune is played, you’ll get closer and closer to knowing it. Your voice is an instrument, and singing can be equated to actively learning a tune on another instrument.

And then it becomes very fun as you work to progressing to #4, because then you spend your time becoming eloquent at expressing the musical ideas contained in the tunes, and you can discover new ways to breathe life into a tune. No longer are you shackled by any certain string of notes, or playing a tune in the same key every time, or even playing it as the same kind of tune every time. You can do things like take a jig, and play the same musical idea as a reel, etc. It’s at this level where you really start to understand that your learning and growth will be a life long endeavor.

For people that already play music, and are learning Irish as a secondary form, it helps to think about this as you’re learning, so that you can aim yourself in the right direction. If you play classical, you need to get rid of the idea that any written form of a tune is in any way “definitive”. It can be difficult to embrace the freedom that I talk about, but learn to be aware that it exists and learn to embrace the fact that people play the same tunes in different ways. I think it’s important to have a specific string of notes that you think of as a “base setting” of a tune. (Just remember that other people may have a different string of notes that they think of as their “base setting” of the same tune… Neither is necessarily right or wrong, it’s just a starting point.) Sheet music can be OK if you’re using it as a memory aid to what your “base setting” of the tune is.

But as people have mentioned, what makes Irish music sound like Irish music is how it’s played, not what the string of notes is. A Scottish player (Alasdair Fraser, for example) may play the exact same string of notes that Oisin MacDiarmada is playing in a tune, and he’s still going to sound Scottish, whereas Oisin will sound Irish. The notes are a very small part of what make it sound like Irish music.

So in the same way, a classical player playing a tune off of sheet music and then trying to learn it by ear from that recording of themselves may learn the sequence of notes, but is really going to miss out on the most important part, which is making it sound Irish. When you’re new to the music, you most likely don’t have enough of a discerning ear to be able to tell the difference. So this is why it’s imperative to listen to people who play the music well, because you will start to internalize what it sounds like even before you can put your finger on what makes it sound different. And it’s even better to do it in person if you can! Find people in your area who play Irish. Seek out the best players you can. Listen to them and learn from them.

And congratulations on finding the Irish trad bug. If you stick with it, and nurture it, it will be some of the most personally rewarding time you will spend in your life!

One way I learn tunes is to
1) First gather as many settings/versions of the tune that I can find
2) Make a playlist on my iPod
3) Listen to the playlist over and over as I jog, commute to work, around the house, etc.
4) Sometimes I’ll get the sheet music from O’Neill’s, or Comhaltas Foinn Seisun, or Ceol Rince na Eireann, Bulmer-Sharpley, or maybe even this website, to look at as I listen

Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

The lines between the four steps that I outlined are blurred. There isn’t necessarily any one point that you can stop and say “yeah, I’ve finally reached step #3!”

The most blurred are #1 and #2. You can start to use kinesthetic memory on one tune, and still be memorizing another…

To give you a rough idea of where I would say the progressions happened for me, I was stuck in #1/#2 until somewhere between 150-200 tunes under my belt. There were some glimmers of hope during that time. The occasional tune that I would pick up without sitting down and learning it, though. So when that happens, encourage it, analyze it, and try to make it happen more.

The line between #3 and #4 is blurry too. There will be tunes that you have gotten way more intimate with, and those ones are easier to work on variations with. At first, you might actually sit down and learn variations, or make them up, but practice them the same as you’d practice a tune. Eventually, you will get better at that, and they just become part of how you express the music. For me, that happened somewhere around 300 tunes, or so… (back when I was still counting, and trying to keep a list of tunes that I know… those days are gone now…)

Kinesthetic memory is still important, by the way. Your fingers remember how to make the music on your instrument, but by now, your brain is controlling them, instead of following them. It’s like typing. You remember where the letters are, but the words flow out of your brain.

And no, I’m not spying on you… It’s just that it wasn’t all that long ago that I was experiencing the trouble with trying to add variation, and getting lost. (Heck, it still happens sometimes, but you get better at not letting it affect you as much). ;-)

Re: any suggestions for my tune-learning strategy?

I agree with you Cheeky Elf. Having a grasp of your instrument is pretty key for getting past #1/#2. When people are first starting out, they may be juggling a lot of balls in the air. They might be:

* learning how to get sound out of their instrument
* learning to hear intonation
* learning what Irish trad music sounds like
* learning a new tune
* learning an unfamiliar rhythm
* learning how to pick up tunes by ear (or maybe learning how to read music)
* learning that playing with other people can be very difficult at first

All at the same time…

You’re going to have a difficult time making use of internalized melodies until you get past all the newness, and gain a certain familiarity with both the music and your instrument.

But as an example of how powerful listening and internalizing the melodies can be, I have a student who was a champion dancer, and had been exposed to the music since the age of 7. This student had never played an instrument before, but was able to pick up playing music at a reasonable level extremely fast, because he already had most of that list down. He had full tunes in his head just waiting to be figured out on the instrument. So he only had to figure out how to get tones out of his instrument before he was playing fairly fully formed tunes. And his familiarity with the music as a whole made picking things up by ear pretty easy for him.

People who start as adults, having never been exposed to any of it, are certainly starting at a disadvantage! But I’m a pretty good testament to the fact that it can be done if you embrace it, immerse yourself in it, and put in the time that it takes to make the journey.
