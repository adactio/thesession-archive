Ancestor Worship

Nahh, don’t worry, I’m not getting all mystical with a far-away dewy-eyed look about me. I don’t have a beard for a start. Let alone a kaftan. But were you to do a websearch on something like Celtic Ancestor Worship, you’d be amazed what it comes up with. All sorts of bods reincarnated with some ancient celtic spirit and so on. Great fun, if you have the time. Because they’re harmless, especially to themselves, there’s no need to put a section on them. But as soon as one of these purple swirly-skirted with mirror thingys on them, ladies, started to be…“unmanageable”, Hep, off to the Bin. Especially if they were working class.

Anyway, something altogether more simple.
Is anyone happy to give an outline on their forebears? Tracing your ancestry is quite an industry these days, so there must be a wealth of stories among you guys, since what we play (to whatever level of proficiency) has, in some respects, a great ancestral element to it.

As for me, I don’t know anything, really, beyond my grandparents. But I’m gonnae pump my ma and da before they go (fairly imminently, sadly).
But for what it’s worth, on my Da’s side. His faither was from Macduff, and was a Mackay. He ran away from home at 14 and was a deep-sea fisherman, first of all, around the 1900’s. Then while in Aberdeen, learnt the trade of coopering. He met his wife in Glasgow. Herself was from Bettyhill on the north Sutherland coast, and when she first emigrated to Glasgow as a young lass, she had very little English. I remeber as a small boy she had a very strong highland “Gaelic” accent, and she would lapse into the old tongue frequently…I guess she was a rather ancient dame by then.
My maternal grandfather, Daniel Henderson, was a gifted, if flawed, musician, player of piano and several brass instruments. He was an interpreter during the Great War and was at Dardanelles, but obviously, survived. He spoke, to the level of interpretation, Arabic, Turkish and Italian. Don’t ask me more. I met him maybe once that I vaguely remember. My mother’s mother, a Byrne of Galway, died when my ma was very young - she was 13 and her young brother Danny was only 6. She was traumatised for a long time and brought Danny up, I suspect my granddad Daniel had taken to the dhrink by then.

So there ye go. A tragedy in several acts, but just normal life for many working class Glasgow folk in the early part of the 20th century.

I suppose the real tragedy is that they struggled through pain and death only to have as an end-product….me.

Anyone else?

I know very little about my mother’s family because either no one knew anything or they would not talk about what they knew. I asked several older relatives but only learned that the my grandmother’s parents were Scottish (Cooper and Armstrong were the names) and had settled in Canada at some point during the 19th century. I think a fairly large family came over because my grandmother talked about relatives in PEI, Toronto, Manitoba, and Alberta. My maternal grandfather’s family was English, but as far as I know, he never spoke about his family. My maternal grandparents married in about 1910 and moved to the US (these days, they would be called undocumented aliens, I think) and settled in Eastern Oregon.

I know more about my father’s family because I was able to trace some of them while I was in Ireland several years ago. They came from around Rathkeale and Adare in Limerick. My paternal grandparents were cousins (she was Costello, he was Culhane obviously) and their parents were also cousins (same Costello’s and Culhane’s). My paternal grandfather was born in 1857 and my paternal grandmother in 1860. They came to America in 1877 and settled in the Kent Valley, just south of Seattle, following one of my grandmother’s brothers, and her uncle. One of my grandfather’s brothers was a Christian Brother and worked in a school in Brooklyn, New York. My father was the youngest of 7 children and was born in 1899. I never knew these grandparents - my grandfather was killed by a flood in 1906 and my grandmother died in 1920. My grandmother played melodeon and sang. I have an old, very beat up melodeon that supposedly belonged to her. One of my uncles played fiddle and I have his old fiddle, which is fun to play once in a while when I ’m thinking of that part of the family.

My parents met in Alaska. My father was prospecting for gold, which he never found. My mother had moved there to work as a nurse. My mother played piano and sang. My father didn’t play any music, although apparently was a good dancer when he was young. I’m the only one of my siblings and cousins who plays Irish music.

I’ve done just a bit of research and it appears that my immigrant ancestors were almost entirely Norn Irish / Ulster Scots who settled in South Carolina. My paternal grandfather was born the year of Custer’s last stand (1876) and was later chief of police of a small town in South Carolina. I have a photo of him in a uniform that looks like the old Keystone Cops. He died in 1917.

Both grandmothers raised kids, worked gardens, were minimally educated and outlived their husbands. My father was the youngest of six. He dropped out of school without completing the eighth grade because of a head injury from falling out of a tree. He was paralyzed on one side and underwent an experimental bit of brain surgery. This was at a time when, in some states, anyone could become a surgeon just by hanging out a shingle, and “Doctor” John Romulus Brinkley was stitching goat’s testicles onto men as a cure for erectile dysfunction.

My dad survived and eventually recovered, but never went back to school. For the next twenty years, he made his living in various ways on both sides of the law. During the Depression, he ran an illegal gambling establishment and made obscene amounts of money, which he spent as fast as it came in. He eventually went straight and learned the plumbing trade, but much of the rest of his life was spent in a struggle with severe alcoholism and a miserable marriage.

After I was grown and in between jobs, I spent a few months working as my dad’s helper and was surprised to see that while he worked he was constantly whistling tunes and singing snatches of old songs from his youth. I didn’t realize there was a happy side to him.

My maternal grandfather was a carpenter who kept a milk cow, hunting dogs and honey bees and played the fiddle. He had a brother who also played, but didn’t own an instrument. They would have a nip of whiskey (or was it whisky?) in the kitchen and take turns with the fiddle. One would play while the other danced. They tried to get us little cousins to dance, but we would have none of it.

My mother told me she played that old fiddle a bit, but it was before my time. I was drawn to traditional music at an early age, but not through my family. That thread was frayed down to almost nothing. Maybe I caught the infection through a mosquito bite.

Re: Ancestor Worship

My most famous ancestor is General Horatio Gates, the “Hero of Saratoga,” who led the colonial forces in that pivotal battle of the Revolutionary War. Britains miniatures even produces a tin soldier in his likeness! I was brought up thinking he was a great hero, then found him in “The Book of Lists,” mentioned as one of the 10 worst generals in history. Subsequent research found that, although he was excellent at logistics and taking care of the troops (they referred to him as Granny because of his fussiness), he was a dreadful and overly cautious combat commander. In fact, it was Benedict Arnold who led the troops in the hottest part of the fighting at Saratoga, and was wounded and ignored by the press and government for his troubles. Many feel that Gates stealing his thunder was the largest component in Arnold’s later decision to switch sides.
And after he and Arnold parted ways, they sent Gates down south to the Carolinas, where he was soundly defeated, and set a record for one of the fastest retreats in history. He subsequently was shifted to a job where his logistical skills could be used to best advantage. After the war, there were hints that he was one of the officers who advocated a military coup because of pay problems, but he retired without further incident, and lived a fairly modest life after that.
It is interesting to chase your roots, but don’t always expect to find what you were told as a youngster!
