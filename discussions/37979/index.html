Re: Designed by Foreigners for the use of Peasants

I think it is impossible to listen to or play with other instruments without being influenced in some way, either consciously or by AB Steen’s ‘seeping in’.

Sometimes the influence is very apparent: the influence of the pipes on myself and many other Scottish fiddlers is very obvious. Sometimes the influence is much more subtle, and doesn’t necessarily mean trying to imitate the instrument, there can even be a sort of ‘anti-influence’ where you try to compensate for the characteristics of the instrument you are playing with - in box and fiddle combinations you tend to find the fiddler takes on a more legato character than he would normally play, in order to try to smooth out the parpiness of the box.

Re: Designed by Foreigners for the use of Peasants

First, in defense of A B Steen, from my interaction with him here and elsewhere I know him to be a great guy who is passionate about music and also about human rights and social justice, and the last person to denigrate the ordinary decent people of Ireland or anywhere else.
Now back on topic- several years ago, my then-eighty year old father decided to try to learn the concertina, he invested in a decent instrument and a pile of books, including Aogan Lynch’s 110 Irish Concertina Tunes. Unfortunately after a few months he developed pain and loss of mobility in his left arm/shoulder, later diagnosed as a bone spur in his shoulder. After consideration he opted out of surgery and abandoned the concertina. At some point I borrowed his copy of the Lynch book. The book has been useful to me in a number of ways- as a tune book it contains a lot of tunes which are apparently staples in Irish sessions but less familiar on this side of the pond. Also Lynch writes out repeats with variations and ornaments notated, which has given me greater insight into what concertina players do.
How has this affected my playing? Well, mainly it opened my eyes and ears to different ways to execute triplets, mainly on the mandolin and to a lesser extent the tenor banjo. As a fixed-pitch instrument the concertina shares some some characteristics , and obstacles, with these string instruments. In particular, jumping octaves and other large intervals within the triplet-playing DdD instead of DDD for example. This isn’t very practical on the fiddle, at least not for me, but can work very nicely on the mando, which is somewhat limited in terms of ornaments/articulations. This has also made me pay more attention when listening to box players of all sizes and shapes especially in terms of ornamentation.
Lastly, when I am working out a tune on the mando I listen to fiddlers, pipers box players, whistle players etc much more than mandolinistas. These are the sounds that are in my head while playing ITM, and I like it like that.

Re: Designed by Foreigners for the use of Peasants

“@Ailin: I assume, since you play Irish traditional music on flute, you must be influenced at least by other flute players in the genre - even if only those you *choose* to be influenced by.”

When I talk about being influenced by another musician, I think in terms of trying to sound like them or at least borrowing their technique. I made a conscious effort to sound like Ian Anderson. When it comes to Irish flute players, I may pick up an interesting bit here and there, but I have never studied anyone’s style or approach to playing, mostly because I really don’t find there’s anyone I want to sound like.

I recently heard a young harp player say that she really doesn’t like to listen to other harp players. If I understand her correctly, I think she was saying she would rather listen to her own playing, not out of ego or arrogance, but because she has developed her own approach and is constantly building upon that, rather than trying to figure out what someone else is doing. There is always something to learn from others, but I make a distinction between learning and being influenced by. It may be a fine point, but I think the difference in how I hear Ian Anderson and how I hear everyone else, for me, makes the distinction very clear.

Because I’m a huge fan of their unique way of self-accompaniment, I actually cultivate quite a bit of influence from concertina players for my piano tunes, due to the lack of Irish piano music.

The different families of instruments have different techniques and progressions that musicians gravitate towards. So listening to a different instrument from your main(which in my case is piano) can open up your mind to something you wouldn’t have thought of on your own. A versatile instrument like the piano has very extended capabilities, and it’s our job as musicians to learn them if we hope to keep ourselves entertained and entertaining.

Re: Designed by Foreigners for the use of Peasants

“apart from the uilleann pipes, which in any case are a development of an existing type of instrument rather than an invention, I couldn’t think of any.”

It’s becoming more and more clear, as more study is done, that the original centres of uilleann pipe production were Edinburgh, Aberdeen, and North Shields, with a couple makers each in Newcastle and London, and one in Dublin. Where they originated is unknown, but an Irish origin appears unlikely.

Uilleann pipe performances, in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, were given at venues such as the Perth Gaelic Society, the Aberdeen Highland Society, and the Highland Society of London. Some performers have very Scottish names (Malcolm MacGregor) some Irish (Patrick O Farrell) though I’ll point out that a person’s name is not a guarantee of their birth-place.

Re: Sonny Brogan

Just came across this in an article, “ Irish Button Accordion: From Press and Draw and Back Again” by Graeme Smith

“Players of the old school who played the “ten key” instrument known as the melodeon had a better sense of ornamentation. The reason for that was, they had to use their imagination to get over difficulties arising from the lack of certain keys on the instrument. When they could not play the tune exactly as the piper or the fiddler, they were very clever at changing the setting slightly without changing the air. The general run of modern players have not these tricks. They have all the notes they want on the double keyed accordion to imitate the roll of the piper and the fiddler—a very difficult movement too - which could not be done on the old melodeon. The modern players have another trick that I am not so keen on. It is this triplet which they throw in everywhere that they can, especially in hornpipes, and it becomes very monotonous to listen to” (Brogan 1963:12).

Brogan, Sonny
1963 “The Man and his Music.”
Ceol v1(2):11-12.
