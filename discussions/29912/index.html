Concertina playing

Well I’ve taken a serious interest in my concertina playing lately. mostly because there are so many fiddlers here lately and the banjo playing leaves my fingers sore by the end of the evening. I can play concertina for longer periods of time without hurting my fingers but it really works out my upper arm and back muscles to control the bellows.

It’s really starting to become apparent to me what playing across the rows means. I have become partial to playing whole phrases or half of a phrase with one bellows direction and using the notes across the rows to facilitate this. Still sometimes I am limited by certain notes like low E and F#, especially low notes as the concertina becomes a chord-centric intstrument below a certain range.

It’s also got me thinking about how ITM is different than “classical” music, in terms of fiddle playing particularly. Fiddle players don’t disguise their bow direction, they use it to give the tune a special lift. One particular thing that fiddle players often talk about is the down bow on the off beat. I know some speak of this as if it’s sloppy technique and I agree it shouldn’t be done religiously but it gives parts of phrases lift before you get to the half-cadence or full-cadence.

Do concertina players also think of lift in this way, where certain bellows direction shifts are integral to the style of playing being presented? Is it a mix of switching direction along with other phrases that all go the same direction? are there certain bellows direction patterns concertina players tend toward for certain types of tunes?

I’m really wanting a discussion on pedagogy of using the physics of the instrument to achieve a certain aesthetic with ITM music. The way a flute player plans their breath to run out at the end of the phrase with a quick full breath before the beginning of the next one. There are virtually no concertina players around here to learn from so I’m just looking for some words that can shed light on the experience of learning tunes on a somewhat limited instrument. What are your techniques? how do you bring lift to music on dual action bellows controlled reed intstruments?

Re: Concertina playing

I played the fiddle before I started with the concertina.

Quite naturally, I tried to imitate the fiddle bowing on the concertina by playing notes I might play on one bow in a single bellow, so to speak. I thought that it sounded just fine.

Then I started taking lessons with an Irish concertina player, who really knows his stuff (All Ireland Champion). His comments: You’re playing a concertina, not a fiddle, If you want to play it like a fiddle, play the fiddle instead. However, if you want to play the concertina, you should play it in a way that is characteristic for the concertina and takes advantage of all this instrument can do.

It might seem simpler and might also sound better in the beginning to slur as many notes/phrases on one bellow until you’ve developed better bellows control, but it most likely won’t give you what you’re actually after.

You’re right with the down bow on the off beat giving a tune more lift (although some players do this with an up bow instead or do both). You do a similar thing on the concertina, no matter whether you change bellows direction right before or wether you’re slurring the notes before/after. You just add a bit more umph with the bellows. Again, this is a matter of developing good bellows control.

At the beginning, for obvious reasons, it will be easier to incorporate the extra umph with a change of the bellows. But you will not get around learning to do this whenever as well as doing bellows changes without any noticeable extra umph. You can practise this by playing a series of notes on a single pull or push and just pulling/pushing a little harder on alternate notes. Or by practising tunes at a slow tempo the same way. Once you’ve mastered that, it will happen automatically.

Generally, the alternative fingerings of the cross rows help play fast triplets where more than one bellows change is rather tricky, chords, octaves or avoid chopping or otherwise awkward fingering. It’s not their main function to allow you play longer phrases with the same bellows direction.

Or should I focus on using particular button combinations that work well for me ]

both. actually, choice a) ends up being including choice b). there is a method of teaching anglo concertina for irish music in which choice a) is discouraged or at least not emphasised, and the order of the day is dictating the choices for you. but it is only one teaching approach, and i don’t recommend it unless just as a starter method to give you default choices while you’re getting the hang of the thing….

yes. to truly, madly, deeply learn this instrument, you will practice spotting, and then trying, the different possible note pathways for playing a different passage. i did that for about two years on my own when i started. ideally, you become adept enough so that you can actually choose or decide which you like---which expresses the articulating, phrasing, and lift you want in a given phrase of a given tune, as well as which won’t get you trapped when it comes to being in position for the next.

[…and hope that other musicians will want to fall in line with my rhythmic interpretation.] that is actually not your business. it is not their job to “fall in line” with your rhythmic interpretation or anything else you do. it is your job to make sure your choices fall within the fenceposts that mark the outer parameters of the wide spectrum of choice available to you, within which you are still playing within this tradition, and beyond which, you are not.

regardless of whether you are playing a back-and-forth bisonoric free reed instrument or not playing a back-and-forth free reed instrument, there is a huge spectrum of choice in traditional irish music whether to break a line of notes, or keep it smooth. whether to break a phrase, or keep it smooth. whether to lean in a smidge on a note here or there, or not. in galway they’d do it smoother here, in kerry, they’d back-and-forth more there. but both are honorable options within irish traditional music. see?

then there are outer parameters beyond which, you can certainly phrase or articulate or ornament a certain way, but it won’t really be within an option included in the irish folk style. you can slur a lot of notes in a long one-directional run on concertina in given keys if you want, but you still need to know at which point you’re gonna be smoother than even the smoothest flute, pipe, or other non-bisonoric player would do it, so you know where to break the phrase, for a “smooth” sound that is still within traditional irish parameters.

finally, bear in mind, that while concertina gives you much more of these choices (provide you bother to learn and become fluid in all the alternate options) than two-row accordion, which only has two “magic notes” or one-row melodeon, which has none, concertina doesn’t even come close to the choices fiddle gives you, which are infinite. on fiddle, you can change direction on every stroke, or you can slur into infinity. concertina offers a delightful amount of options to play with, but they have very serious limitations and areas where you will NOT have these choices, and you will soon run smack into them.

bertram levy is a concertina virtuoso and teacher who is a big believer in learning and availing yourself of all your options. i believe he has a new tutor out….

When I started playing concertina I tended to find ways to minimize changing bellow direction. Then when I compared the sound I was getting to people who had mastered the style and instrument… my playing sounded flat. The first time Noel Hill heard me play (back in 1985) he said it sounded like I was playing a piano keyboard. I stopped what I was doing and learned his system… and then little by little the sound I was seeking began to creep into my playing. After you’ve worked on it for a long time you start to unravel the convoluted logic of the instrument and you exploit the idiosyncrasies to optimize the energy in the tunes.
