Re: The Fiddler’s Fakebook

“Which ones, for example?”

I’m not going to go through the whole book, but in the first few pages, Atholl Highlanders - the first two parts are close enough that you might consider them to be one person’s ornamentation/variation, but the C part is just wrong. The Bee’s Wing Hornpipe - this is a James Hill tune, so there is a written definitive version. What is in the fakebook is a simplified version, stripped down from four parts to two, and written completely straight, with no clue as to the intricate Strathspey rhythm that is the key feature of the tune. Carolan’s Concerto - I’m not sure if there is an original manuscript, but I’ve only ever hear this played very close to the version in O’Neill, and this isn’t it. And the list goes on.

I bought the book about 4 years ago and got very excited about all the lovely new tunes I was going to learn. But after a couple of days it got put on the shelf and I haven’t taken a single tune from it. On the odd occasions when I’ve taken it off the shelf to look up a tune I’ve heard, what I’ve found has been too far removed to be of any use, so I’ve fallen back on thesession.org, ibiblio.org and your own site. All of which are far superior sources, and free.

Re: The Fiddler’s Fakebook

As I said, all the tunes in the book come from recordings, so should be regarded with that in mind. For example, the “Bees Wing Hornpipe” is played rather differently in Cape Breton, with less emphasis on the irregular rhythm (and throughout FF and other collections, irregular rhythms are notated rarely). I would caution anybody wishing to play “Bee’s Wing” to listen to a recording of it.

The “Atholl Highlanders” in FF is Dave Swarbrick’s idiosyncratic version and I agree should never be played with other musicians. I would be interested to hear a New England dance band’s version of “Carolan’s Concerto”, but I wouldn’t see it as prime source material.

I think your problems are not with the accuracy of the transcriptions, but the choice of settings, and that I can understand. Having said that, I have found many of the settings given in FF just as you would expect to hear them in a session.

I had hoped that the session.org would become in its way like the Fiddler’s Facebook: transcriptions of recordings, live playing, with background settings from manuscripts and hard-to-get books. Instead, what we’re seeing lately is wholesale renditions of tunes we’ve all got already in O’Neill’s. Grump. Grump. Grump.

Re: The Fiddler’s Fakebook

Yes, I think interpretation or choice of setting is the problem, rather than inaccuracy, but that doesn’t stop it being a problem.

The OP might well be right that it is “ a pretty good tool for American fiddling at least.” and if I suddenly had to play a rag or some bluegrass I might well turn to it. But I would be very worried that it wouldn’t sound right to anyone who knows more about ragtime and bluegrass than me, because I know that if you play the Irish and Scottish tunes as they are written they won’t sound right to an Irishman or Scot.

It probably is useful for a jobbing fiddler who suddenly has to produce something that sounds vaguely Irish for a soundtrack, but I think most people who read this forum play in sessions of a specific tradition, and for them it is far better to go to sources within your own tradition, rather than look at how someone from another tradition interprets your music (or the quirky setting he has chosen to transcribe in preference to the version 99% of the indigenous population play). Even with tunes that have crossed borders like Bonaparte’s Retreat, it would probably be better to learn the local version - even if what’s in the book is a more authentic old-timey setting it would be barely recognisable to most people in Scotland.

Re: The Fiddler’s Fakebook

As an American who plays in an old-time jam, I can tell you that many people got their start with the Fiddler’s Fake Book. We used to have a fiddle class that suggested the book and lots of people will refer to it even now, but it’s not always a perfect match to how we play things in our jam. Mainly we learn the tunes from each other at the jam, often from someone who has learned a tune from a recording. The Fake Book isn’t bad. It’s just a resource.

As far as Scottish and Irish tunes go, we play them differently from what you might do in Scotland or Ireland, or even different from how I’ve heard them play at local Irish sessions. When we play them old-time style it’s not wrong, just American.

We at our jam suspect that a great majority of our tunes were once way more notey like Irish music. We speculate that when people came over here they probably couldn’t remember the tunes accurately and maybe they didn’t have the same instruments to play them on. Probably they changed them to sound more optimistic, as optimism is a strong characteristic of Americans. And of course you can’t escape the influence of African music on our style. This isn’t academic fact, though, just our guesses as we talk amongst ourselves in our jam. To top if off, our jam tends to play mid-Western music which is even happier than anything from Appalachia, and even worse, we’re in California where we probably are butchering everything a little bit.

So yeah, Fake Book is just a resource. I suppose if you are using it for Irish music it’s sort of like Irish filtered through American filtered through classical (academic/sheet music). You’re gonna lose something somewhere.

Re: The Fiddler’s Fakebook

“Fakebook” is a time-honored term for music that contains only the bare-bones melodic line so that a musician can immediately get to the heart of a tune or song without being distracted by an arrangement that is not part of the essential melody. It goes way back and in no way represents a dumbing-down, as a “for dummies” book would. Don’t think of it as a trendy term for making things easier. That’s not the intent and professionals use fake books all the time.

on the lead sheet resting in front of them. Lead sheets are often helpful though never essential

the thing about playing from a fakebook is that you can never assume that the fakebook is correct. If you are playing from a fakebook, and everyone else is also reading from the same book, then you’re probably ok. But if you are playing with someone who knows tunes and you need to play from a fakebook (which will happen sometimes on a bandstand playing professionally), you always have to listen up that first time through the tune because the fakebook is probably going to be off in a few places.

For example, I learned The Cannaughtman’s Rambles from the Fiddler’s Fakebook years ago. Then I played it one day with someone who knew the tune from playing it at Irish sessions and it because clear very quickly that the B part I learned from the book was off, so I had to listen close and get it right the next time by.

If you recall the book I mentioned earlier The Real Book of jazz standards…well, many tunes in that book have the wrong chords and since I played with a lot of old bebop men in my youth, I got exposed many times trusting fakebooks

so the moral of the story is that you should never consider a fakebook to be authoritative. Ever. Even the better ones, which I think the Fiddlers Fakebook is.

There are some recent Scottish publications of “Session Tunes” where it is claimed that all the settings have been “approved by the composer” but, you’re right, even they play their own tunes differently from time to time and they change over years. As a result, many of the tunes differ from how they are usually played in sessions and even on recordings.

In some cases too, composers aren’t necessarily accomplished readers and writers of music and their tunes have been transcribed on their behalf with permission. So, at the very best, settings are a “snapshot in time”.

Re: The Fiddler’s Fakebook

I played through the Bee’s Wing HP in the Hunter book, after a gap of quite a few years, and I remembered what might make it appear a bit tricky to play.

It’s the string crossings, from 1st to 3rd, with no pause for breath.

It’s a feature of the tune itself - you’d have that no matter what key you played it in.

Referring to previous discussion about the parts, I’m of the strong opinion that it’s essentially a two-part tune, with two sets of variations, as opposed to a ‘real’ 4-parter.
