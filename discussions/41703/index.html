Some tips:

1) Ignore any tunes submitted by Ceolachan or JackB, because they tend to change keys to suit themselves or to try them out.
2) Look in the notes to the tunes to see if any of the settings are transcribed from a recording - if yes, odds on that it’s the commonly played key.
3) Your whistle is a better bet than your concertina - it’s more versatile.
4) Ask.

Billy, “transcribed from a recording” means that the setting of the tune (the key, sequence of notes, structure, etc.) is based on a (studio or live) recording and is therefore likely to be a setting with which a lot of players are familiar.

I would agree with Nigel re Ceolachan and JackB. Whilst there is nothing wrong musically with their settings it should be borne in mind that, in the majority of cases, their settings are personal takes of tunes and so, unless you are playing with them personally, you won’t be playing from the same hymn sheet as everyone else.

Re: Playing in different keys!

1. After finding the tune on here do a google search for it to see what comes up at alternative sites
2. Stick to tunes that are in the range of your D whistle ie the majority of the notes are within the stave.
For instance take a look at Father Kelly’s Reel on here https://thesession.org/tunes/791 - the 1st setting is in D maj, the lowest note is A with all the other notes in the upper part of the stave and higher. Then compare that with the settings in G maj where most of the notes are sitting comfortably within the stave with the lowest note being a D. - The Gmaj version would be the one to learn.
3. Go a site like this - https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCydT2k8knrBXMB0_ypHqvhg and check if the tune is there and if it is check out his fingering to see what note he’s starting the tune on
4. Ditch the dots and learn by ear
6. I’ve found tunes here vary a lot so check the various versions.

Re: Playing in different keys!

BillyRaticus - which sources do you use when learning tunes on the tin whistle? Do you listen and learn, or do you read written music? (Or both?) As many have said before me, if it a tune works on a tin whistle, that is a good indicator that it’s in the right key. No notes below the D, and no tunes with a lot of notes outside D major (a few are OK). The less you have to mess with the fingerings in order to play the tune, the more likely it is that you’re on the right track. If you’re listening to something which seems to have something else than the common notes (e.g. F, Bb, G#, Eb) and the D whistle range (Does it go a ot lower than D? Or higher?), chances are that they’re using a whistle in another key.

Re: Playing in different keys!

I think the very obvious “just ask” is the best way to go: even ask the session leader to shout out what keys the tunes in the next set will be played in before they start or just ask your neighbour, in the hope that he/she knows! You’ll probably find that many session tunes are in either: major keys: G, D 0r A and minor (or modal???) A min, Emin: all of which should suit a D whistle.
There is a sort of call signal system (possibly gleaned from the jazz community, not often seen in trad sessions?) that, e.g. 1 finger up meant one sharp (G), 3 fingers up was 3 sharps (A), 5 digits up was 5 sharps (B) one finger down was one flat (F) 3 down (Eb) and so on, but that does rather pre-suppose that you know all your key sigs and scales!

I’ve always found it useful to learn any major-key tune in either D or G along with whatever key it’s “supposed” to be in. Amaj tunes often get taken down a step to G, and tunes in less common keys like Emaj or Fmaj often end up transposed to a more manageable key for keyless flutes and whistles. Likewise, learning G tunes in A is good practice and can be useful when you’re in a session with a bunch of fiddlers.

Most of my transcriptions, at least the initial ones, are taken from recordings, not just off the top of my head, or out of my quirks for playing. Offering them in other keys is also often to open up the opportunity for D limited winds to access that same tune. Sometimes the transposition are copies of the first one given, but they can also offer other options as far as how to treat a given melody. That said, they do not vary far from the original. I have thousands of hours of recordings, and more of those are not what is commercially available, but they are mostly by musicians well known and of a given area, such as polkas and slides from Sliabh Luachra, and any treatment variations are from that same tradition, not just my own invention. Mostly, also, I try to give them relatively bare bones, NOT transcribing loads and loads of ornaments. The variations given by me are usually melodic, but not, as already said, varying in some off wall direction from the basic melodic line. I don’t always give a list of the source, though I do my damnedest to try to do that, but some transcriptions are of group playing, and sometimes also for dance. I do my best to try to listen VERY closely to what’s being played and to come to some kind of transcription that does credit to those playing, to the tradition represented, including regionally, however small or large that recognized region might be. The only truly inventive extremes I might be guilty of are those tunes that come to mind and seem to have no source, but without that I will list them as compositions of mine, though I don’t feel such ownership. The Session Dot Org ~ Version ‘x’

A little history might be needed here, concerning transcriptions and notes and various transformations of this website over time. A few years back Jeremy our webmaster and benefactor did a major overhaul on this site, not forgetting minor advancements before and since. The changes he made suddenly moved a lot of material up from comments and turned them into transcriptions. It wasn’t always the case that what was moved from comments to transcriptions was a full tune ~ all beats, bars/measures, and parts. In the comments members, including myself, would sometimes just give a bar or two to show a variation, other possibilities, and sometimes other keys, or a correction, as in the beginning we couldn’t edit a transcription after initial submission. ALL of these bits and pieces, a whole tune or a part, suddenly became transcriptions, no matter how small or large, right or wrong. Also, more curious and frustrating, some things that weren’t actually ABC notation were also moved to transcriptions. The ABCs, as given in comments, were also often incomplete. Suddenly we had a ton of new material showing up in transcriptions, ABCs and the dots, whether complete or not, whether actually ABC notations or not.

Most of my submissions, transcriptions and notes were made before this great transformation. Post these changes I was faced with a slew of things I’d added in comments now moved up to transcriptions. It was and remains a bit of a mess and there was no means to simply remove them. I decided to just put the time in and do my best to make corrections, a little bit at a go. When I catch these fragments I do my damnedest to fill in the blanks, with consideration for the music, the sources, the tradition, and those that might make use of the results. I’m only ever informed of any of these when it’s one where I’d made the first submission and when someone has added a new transcription or comment. I’ve been diligently chasing up those fragments, but if it was a submission I’d made where someone else had made the first entry, I can only stumble across those now and then, and I’ll do my best to try and make things right. In a few cases this has meant a lot of variations given as multiple alternate transcriptions, where they had previously only been in the comments. The choice to move things up to transcription was not mine, it was the new scripts Jeremy had woven into this website during that great refurbishment. I’ve had a few, thankfully a rare few, come down heavy on me for multiple submissions, even getting nasty about it.

If anyone finds one of those incomplete submissions, or mistakes, or something that they find questionable, I’m more than ready to review and if necessary make corrections. I’ve asked for this kind of help several times in the past. A few people have come forward and I’ve done my best for them, for the music. In the meantime I stumble in here now and then and sometimes I find those fragments, and I’ve found some pretty awful transcriptions, some things that made absolutely no sense at all, bits and pieces raised from the comments section to the transcriptions. Usually I get a laugh out of it and do my best to remove the nonesense and to, hopefully, add something useful in its place, something intended to be sensible, informative, based on experience and on the many sources it has been my blessing to have shared time and music and much more with. I screw up now and then and am glad of the opportunity to make reparation. While I might miss giving clarification in the comments, I try to do that too. I don’t expect or require anyone to like my submissions, and if you choose to follow the suggestion to avoid my transcriptions completely, c’est la vie! ~ this world has never been short of bias, quirks and weirdness, my own included. I do these submissions out of a sense of responsibility to those that were so generous with sharing their music with me. I only wish I could give you them to you, in person, that one-to-one experience rather than secondhand, me paraphrasing and presenting the simple skeletons of tunes I submit here.

I had originally planned to do this kind of work, transcribing, for a print collection, but I wanted that to better represent the sources. I wanted to be able to record the music through interviews with those sources, interviews about the full context of this music, including the dance in and around it. I failed. I didn’t get to do those interviews like I’d wanted to. I hadn’t the resources, and I hadn’t the shorthand. I wanted the collection to mostly be about them, with the notations as seasoning, but the beating heart, the life, to be theirs. While I collected a slew of music, and recorded much, I hadn’t done those interviews I wanted and needed, that for me were so important. They had shared their lives in many ways, but I hadn’t the means to record it at the time. Even some of the recordings I did made ended up lost or damaged. I have a few that are what is known as ‘squeaky tape’. So, instead, I stumbled across this website just as I almost lost my wife to multiple pulmonary embolisms, while she was in hospital. And I started reading, and then submitting. Before I knew it those contributions had grown substantially. I met old friends on site here, and I made new ones, some who have since visited us here, a few more than once. This site distracted me for a spell from the worries over my wife. Re: Playing in different keys!

To BillyRaticus

These are the main keys/modes that you will find in ITM.
Of course, there are others, these are just the most common.
You can see how a D whistle works best.
C natural (note) is easy to finger on a D whistle so you can easily play D and G based modes:
D whistle basic fingered notes: D E F# G A B C#
Then you just learn the fingering for C, and you have G based modes: D E F# G A B C
These two note patterns will get you 90% of traditional tunes.

D based modes:
D Major (Ionian) : D E F# G A B C#
E Dorian : E F# G A B C# D
A Mixolydian : A B C# D E F# G
B Natural minor (Aeolian) : B C# D E F# G A

G based modes:
G major (Ionian ) : G A B C D E F#
A Dorian : A B C D E F# G
D Mixolydian : D E F# G A B C
E Natural minor (Aeolian): E F# G A B C D

There are people that may discourage you from learning about modes.
If you find music theory alienating and don’t want to learn it. That is fine.
But if you do learn some music theory, you will have a vocabulary that other musicians understand and communicate with. From the question you asked, I think you would benefit from knowing the main Irish modes.

Since you you have a G/C concertina, you can easily play all the G based modes.
You may be able to play some D based modes as well. Some tunes only use 6 notes of the mode (gapped scales), and some tunes mostly only use 5 notes (pentatonic scales). I don’t have that information at my fingertips, but it is something you will notice after you learn a lot of tunes. And, there are some C based modes.

Concertinas can play chords. This may be too much information at this point, but you will have this issue if you play chords. You will also notice that pentatonic or gapped scale tunes are ambiguous. For example if a D mode tune does not have a C or C#, it could be D Major, or D Mixolydian, thus some call this a gapped scale. In that case, the guitarist (or concertinaist) can make the tune sound Major or Mixolydian by the chords that they choose. If you mainly use the chords D, G and A it could sound Major. If you mainly used D, G and C chords it could sound more Mixolydian. Notice, I said “could” not “would”. There is a lot of flexibility and no set rules. You will often find widely divergent chords used for the same tunes.

If you want to keep it simple, just ask if the tune is D based or G based or C . . ..
