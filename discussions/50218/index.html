Pipers grip vs traditional grip on flute

During the last 6 months, I have been getting back into playing flute after about a 10 year hiatus. Am practicing almost an hour every day and things are coming back well. Things change in 10 years, tho, and one thing I am struggling with is grip.

For a number of reasons, I always defaulted to the pipers grip on flute. However, I am now discovering that I play much better, with better tone, using the traditional grip. It seems that this is because I hold the flute more steady with the tradition grip, and cover the holes consistently well. (Perhaps I always played sort of crappy with pipers grip and am just discovering this now!)

In any case, why not just switch full on to the traditional grip you say? First, the traditional grip is somewhat painful, at times considerably so, perhaps owing to arthritis. Plus I absolutely can’t do a b roll for the life of me with the traditional grip. I mean, I challenge any flute player to play The Curlews reel in Amix using the traditional grip…a juicy thick b roll seems impossible with the traditional grip.

So my grip is caught in the twilight zone. Anybody struggled with this? Can you really truly do good and juicy b rolls on flute with the traditional grip?

Re: Pipers grip vs traditional grip on flute

Yes the roll on B tends to be the most difficult for people.

First of all, the rolls that divide the cut and pat between the hands are always easier for people than the rolls where both cut and pat are done by the same hand. I think this is part of the reason why so many flute and whistle players use the upper-hand ring finger as the cut for rolls on E, F#, and G.

Obviously this is compounded on the flute using “classical” grip because the upper-hand index finger is curled around the flute with just the end of the finger on the hole. There’s not much “range of motion” for that finger.

All I can say is that it just takes practice to develop the necessary speed to make that C# cut crisp.

What you don’t want to do is try to make the cut faster by not raising the index finger entirely free of the hole, sort of pinching out the cut. Yes it works but it’s not going to have that crisp sound.

BTW my hand and wrist issues forced me to give up flute over a decade ago and switch to Low Whistle. But the whistle can’t give the sort of performance a flute gives, so for the last couple months I’ve been learning how to play a Vertical Flute. This thing has all the comfort of a Pipers Grip Low Whistle and the sound and performance of a flute. It’s just taking time to get good at the entirely different embouchure.

A lot of it is determined by anatomy.

Re: Pipers grip vs traditional grip on flute

When I got a keyed flute, I transitioned over from piper’s grip to classical hold, and it took some time. As regards the B roll, I found the cut was easier to get crispy than the tap, so I just practice tapping my middle finger on the hole, until it’s strong enough to manage the crispiness. So I think it’s important to identify which part of the roll is weak, and just practise that over and over. (Just silently, you don’t need to play the whole roll at first, just tap or cut a couple of hundred times in a two-minute period or so.) I was pleasantly surprised how quickly that helped.

As regards pain, there were two main pain points for me that required me to change. The first was cradling the flute on the LH index finger. If I had it too high, it would pinch off a nerve and my hand would go numb within a few minutes. Too low and it would strain that finger when trying to cover the tonehole. Eventually I found the perfect place where, after building up a proper callus, it doesn’t cause discomfort.

The second was on the right hand. I started out with a ‘hybrid’ grip, classical in the LH, piper’s in the RH. This was fine for a while, but I started to notice my right wrist fatiguing quickly and taking a while to recover. I came to the conclusion that, while perfectly comfortable for vertical instruments, having my wrist bent in that way on a horizontal flute was not the healthiest. I switched to a full classical grip only a week ago, and while now I have to re-learn how to stabilise the flute, the pain is completely gone.

A catchphrase I’ve come up with that’s applicable: “If it aches, take a break. If it’s painful or numb, you’re doing something dumb.”

Re: Pipers grip vs traditional grip on flute

There are things that I think regardless of length of arm and fingers etc are true:

1) Playing whistle (high or low), clarinet, soprano sax, Highland pipes, etc allow you to play with shoulders level, head facing directly ahead, and both forearms and hands in alignment, in other words everything in a relaxed ergonomic non-twisted position.

2) Transverse flutes present a conundrum: if your head is straight (and you play with a centred embouchure, that is, the flute perpendicular to your head) the flute sticks straight out to the side where reaching the finger positions requires extreme, perhaps impossible, twisting of the arms; but if you place the flute in the most ergonomic position to finger, that is, straight in front of you like a clarinet or Low Whistle, even twisting your head 45 degrees doesn’t allow you to blow it.

So, the vast majority of flutists over the centuries, using “classical” grip, have adopted a compromise position with the head twisted to one side and both arms in asymmetric awkward positions. We flutists get used to it but no-one can claim it’s ergonomic or ideal.

3) Irish flutists using the “pipers grip” have the head twisted even more to the side due to the flute being brought further to the front; thus it’s more ergonomic for the shoulders and wrists but less ergonomic for the neck.

All of these things have led me to learn a Vertical Flute.

Some flutes with keys are notoriously unfriendly to pipers grip, but others are just fine.

Depends on whether they are post-mounted keys or block mounted, and the specific design of the flute.

I’m able to play my 1857 Metzler 8-key using pipers grip and can reach all the keys other than the B-flat key (because of the thumb position pointing towards my chin when using pipers grip).

Why in the world would the material the flute is made of have anything to do with the grip? I have flutes of wood, glass, and PVC, and use the same grip for all of them. If I got one made of aluminum or a hollowed out ostrich bone, I’d grip it the same way.

I agree there are some fabulous players who when I see them play I think “sure hope you have a good chiropractor or neurologist”, but for every one of those there are example of those who play in either pipers or standard grip who appear completely relaxed.

I’m a huge proponent of focusing very early on in one’s playing on the ergonomics of playing instruments so that one can avoid injury and pain in the long run and why I’d never advocate for a “one-solution-fits-all” approach to flute grip and actively push back on those who do.

We have similar discussions in the diatonic Irish box community related to straps and ergonomics, but at end of the day, it’s all about being in complete control of the instrument and avoiding pain and injury.

Will, no, I’d play all of those with the same basic grip because the physics and stability requirements are the same.

Re: Pipers grip vs traditional grip on flute

“If you’re “PUSHING the flute into the chin” you’re doing it completely wrong.”

I believe Will may be referring to the 19th Century three point grip (which Terry McGee prefers to “Rockstro” because Rockstro didn’t invent it), where your lower thumb tip pushes outward against the lower part of the flute barrel, with the side of your index finger as the fulcrum in the middle, and the chin resisting the outward force of the lower thumb tip. It isn’t a strong push against the chin, just enough to minimally stabilize the flute.

Learning the 19th Century grip was the single most important revelation in my flute learning experience. I started it after getting a keyed flute to free up my thumb for the Bb key (a few tunes I play use it), but I discovered that it increased the steadiness of my hold, and also increased the freedom of motion for my lower hand fingers when I wasn’t using the pinky to help stabilize. Your mileage may vary.

For what it’s worth, I still use a semi-pipers grip on the lower hand with this three point hold. I’m 6’ 2” tall with proportional hand size, so someone with shorter fingers might not manage it. Probably depends on the specific flute model as well, which was mentioned earlier.

BTW the people claiming that the modern Boehm flute grip was invented in the 19th century are simply incorrect.

We have plenty of images of 18th century flutists, and earlier, playing the same way, and those flutes (Baroque one-key flutes and keyless Renaissance flutes) had no thumb-key or thumb-hole, the supposed reason behind the “classical” grip.

After many years of playing Boehm, I finally got a simple system and the OP’s experience is very relatable. Two things:
1) I was very frustrated with the B rolls at first but very small adjustments to the thumb and an almost imperceptible rotation of the flute gave me just enough range of motion to get now a solid, sound. I considered switching to a piper’s grip but, for me, it felt unnatural. Changing to a “small hands” flute model made a world of a difference.
