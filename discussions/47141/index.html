Seems simple to me. If a chap refers to himself - or is comfortable with others doing so - as ‘Pat’ or ‘Paddy’ when it’s an informal nickname for ‘Patrick’ (or equivalent) then it’s entirely acceptable. Same as ‘Mick’ for ‘Michael’. There are plenty of eminent players who are customarily known as Paddy - Messrs. Moloney, Keenan, Glackin, Reynolds . . . - and presumably they don’t/didn’t object when addressed thuswise by friends.

But if I saw a sign up in a bar or suchlike saying ‘No Micks or Paddies’ I’d regard that as objectionable to the point of being derogatory, offensive, and discriminatory (and - I would hope - illegal and actionable in the UK). As to tune titles . . . never yet come across one that I thought fell into this latter category.

I was once taken to task for advertising a ‘Paddy’s Night’ gig. I had intended no slight on any Irish person, Saint or otherwise. Now, had I written ‘Paddies’ Night’ it might have been different.

For further discussion, see the lyrics to ‘Erin go Bragh’ - particularly as delivered by Dick (Richard?) Gaughan.

I thought this was going to be a thread about legato vs détaché …

Anyway, my father (GRHS), who’s name was Mick, was totally happy to refer to himself as a ‘Paddy’. He also used the term to refer to other Irishmen; both in particular and in general. And he never objected to anyone calling him a ‘Paddy’. So, I was, and still am, easy with that too. However, one Xmas time I was over for a few day at my sister’s in Kerry where, during a general conversation, I happened to joke: “Every Xmas, Ireland must sink a few inches - what with all the Paddies coming home.” A friend of hers who was there really laid into me for my “racist language” and “insensitivity” etc. etc. I was rather taken aback but my sister moved the conversation on and the matter was dropped. The next evening we were at a pleasant restaurant for dinner and I looked around at the other clientele enjoying a glass of Chablis with their seared sea bass and the like, and it suddenly struck me that these Irish diners weren’t ‘Paddies’; they were middle management level or in IT or some such. ‘Paddies’ wear wellies, dig roads and shovel dirt - as did my father when he first came to England. My sister’s friend was one with the diners and I’m sure she felt keenly that to be called a ‘Paddy’ was to lump her with the bogtrotters. She dressed her objections as a nationalist stand against racism - and she probably believed that is precisely what she was doing - but I say it was just class snobbery.

Re: Slur or Not?

It was certainly used as a slur in the US in the nineteenth and early twentieth century. There’s a whole book about it, “Paddy and the Republic” by Dale Knobel. It was common to refer to “Paddy” as a way to describe all irishmen, just as it was common to refer to all irishwomen as “Bridget.” “Paddy and Bridget” were stock characters in vaudeville

From “The Nation,” 1874, and an editorial comparing “jolly, reckless, good-natured, passionate, priest-ridden, whiskey loving, thriftless Paddy, and the cold, shrewd, frugal, correct, meeting-going Yankee.” The writer insisted that “the prodigious influx of Irish during the last twenty years has created a large Irish class, apart from the rest of the people, poor, ignorant, helpless, and degraded, contemned by the Americans, used as tools by politicians of all parties, doing all the hard work and menial duties of the country, and filling the jails and almshouses, almost to the exclusion of everybody else.”

There are any number of similar examples. “Paddy” as a descriptive name was very clearly a slur, but also could be used affectionately and of course it was and is a common name. Whether or not something is insulting generally depends on context.
