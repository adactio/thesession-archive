The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

O.K, I know The Session is not really about songs – but I’d like to pick some wiser and more knowledgeable brains than mine….

Our five-piece band has two singer-players, one male, one female, neither of whom is Irish (though the gent has Irish roots). Both are experienced and very competent singers but are quite new to Irish trad, and have no relevant repertoire of their own to speak of. (Perhaps I should add that the remaining three of us are fully paid-up trad players…) But none of us has any repertoire from the depths of our family’s singing to draw on either…

We know this is ‘work in progress’ – we are hardly going to make is as great exponents of sean nos, but we need to do *something*. So far we have used songs such as Curragh of Kildare, Sonny, Do You Love an Apple, The Queen of Argyll (Scottish, not trad), Will Ye Go to Flanders (also Scottish). Black is the Colour is waiting in the wings. But at our recent gig to a non-specialist audience (i.e. the majority round here), the songs were noticeably less well received than the tunes.

We’re not sure what to do about this – past experience suggests most audiences want at least some songs, in fact seem to prefer them. We don’t want to do Irish language songs phonetically - quite apart from the work involved, it feels phoney. We want to stay away from the more politically-charged material too. But equally, we don’t want to become an ‘Oirish’ covers band by reverting to the pub classics. Would rather do stuff that is culturally and musically more interesting.

But it also seems that many songs are slow/wistful and we really need a few more up-tempo ones for generalist audiences, it seems. Even quite a few of the newer Irish bands seem to be sourcing songs from Americana and elsewhere – is this a reflection of similar issues? (thinking of people like Daoiri Farrell with Four Winds, also Full Set etc). And thinking about it further, even some of the older bands such as De Danann seemed to have some difficulty pitching their songs and singers to match their instrumental identity. I’m thinking about the sean nos on The Mist Covered Mountain and then the more populist stuff that came later with Mary Black etc.

I’ve never really given much thought to all this before, but particularly for a non-Irish language, only-partly-Irish-personnel band, the whole matter of what to sing seems much more fraught than the tunes, which are in some ways more neutral, accessible ground.

Any thoughts, or suggestions of songs/sources/directions to go in? Many thanks for any ideas… 😊

Re: The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

It’s quite a dilemma.

As you say, a lot of audiences like a song or two to break up the “monotony” of tunes. However, there is a temptation, when only presenting a few songs, to make them slow. It’s related to that old sing-around thing whereby, if you’re only going to get to sing one song, you’d better make it a “big” one which invariably means slow.

There’s also the fact that a “traditional” tune relates more easily to the present day than a traditional song/ballad (There were three sisters lived in the bower…?). For the vast majority of people, the music they hear is mainly song based and a lot of it is very current.

It’s also worth pointing out that presenting a song to an audience requires different skills to presenting a set of tunes. A lot of tune players are pretty inexperienced at accompanying songs (and it sometimes shows) so it’s something you need to practise.

I would say that, if you are going to include songs then they need to be songs that the singers feel emotionally invested in. And, the songs, even if not numerous, have to be as important as the tune sets and be full band investments. Listen to bands like early Malinky for inspiration as to how songs can be made musically exciting and relevant.

Re: The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

Choice of songs is a potential headache for any covers band, but it’s worse when a band playing “niche” music is faced with a general audience.

There are about eight Irish/English/Scottish folk songs that the masses know - the “pub classics” - and I’m afraid I don’t see any way round them, unless the audience is made up of dedicated fans of the the music. Our repetoire is basically “The Best of the Dubliners” interspersed with tunes which fit. I’m not saying that’s the only way to go, but, if you’re playing to people who only know “Whiskey in Jar”, you should probably play “Whiskey in the Jar”.

If you only play to people who know and love the music, then the world’s theoretically your oyster. Design the set so that it doesn’t sag, but don’t chop and change between ballads and foot-tappers too much. A theme can help: we had one based on the fictitious story of someone from the West who ended up in Australia via Dublin, Belfast, Liverpool etc. and, if you think that sounds hackneyed, you’d be right, but it’s entertainment at the end of the day and part of the joy is making even “The Rocky Road to Dublin” sound fresh!

It’s important to pick keys and tempi that suit your singers, not just your instruments and important that those singers are convincing. I’d get the singers to choose a couple of songs each to see where they’re at and the sort of thing they like. Good luck!

Re: The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

“…if the singer wants c# it’s pretty likely they could sing the song just as well in d or c…”

I would disagree with that. It can occasionally work, but often as not one semitone either way can make a song a lot more uncomfortable for a singer. It’s the fact that every note is moved by one semitone. Moving up a semitone and suddenly you find you’re knackered by the end of the song. Move it down a semitone and suddenly you’re struggling for breath on the phrasing and long notes.
The bands that do the best on songs are the ones that don’t make the singers compromise on pitch to make it easier for the instrumentalists.

Re: The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

I’ve played some gigs with a core group of session mates, accustomed to playing instrumentals all night long. What seemed to work best (for us) when songs were requested for a gig was to stick to songs that are also tunes (e.g., Rocky Road to Dublin, Foxhunter’s slip jig) or songs upbeat enough to play the melody as though it were a tune (e.g., Galway Girl).

Because they’re songs, that creates a sense of variety over an evening, but because they’re tune-like, everyone was in their comfort zone playing along. To my taste, this also created a happy coherence to our concert.

>>I am intrigued, though, as to why the term only applies to songs and not to tunes.

I guess it is because songs are more easily attributable to an individual human being. There is a particular voice, and particular specific sentiments expressed through the lyrics. In the case of traditional material, I can’t escape entirely from the feeling that the singer needs to have an appropriate accent, especially where dialect is used.

The thing about tunes is that they are more abstract in that sense, and perhaps belong to everyone and no one in the way a song can’t. But I would find it just as unappealing to try to reproduce the sound of, say, The Bothy Band too, much though I like it. People need their own voice!

Re: The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

“where else can you really get songs apart from recordings already made by other artists? …. It is also pretty difficult to come up with something that is not to some extent a cover of what went before.”

It needn’t be any more difficult than it was for some of those artists who made the ‘original recordings’ of ‘what went before’; in fact, with the internet now, it should be much easier. You don’t need an aunt who is carrying on songs that the Druids sang; you just need someone in the band who is willing to look at a few of the many collections of folksongs out there, or listen to the many field recordings that have been made available to the public. By way of example: at least a couple of the songs that have been mentioned in this thread came from collections made in Nova Scotia; namely, Peggy Gordon and Arthur McBride. The former somehow got to the Corries, if I remember correctly, and they made the ‘original recording’, and Andy Irvine, I believe, uncovered (so to speak!) Arthur McBride. That is to say, the versions of those songs that were ‘originally recorded’ and made popular were based on the ones collected in Nova Scotia. In each case, someone nosing around in the collections - the same way many of us nose through tune collections - came across them and realized what could be done with them.

Re: The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

I play in a band with a singer, and in a pub session with the same lineup and guests. I have also played in a band that did mostly English, and some Scottish and Irish material that mostly busked in farmer’s markets, and played in senior’s homes. Some random thoughts:

Try searching on “most popular songs in Ireland” “most popular songs in England” (data derived from download data from Spotify etc) for an up-to-date list. I found an interesting mixture of chestnuts, and new material from young artists I didn’t know. Then work with your singer and bandmates to pull songs that you want to perform from the lists, get keys that fit etc.

Story: Was playing with the English/Scottish band at an old folks home, and our singer was doing mostly uncommon songs. An audience member asked for “Danny Boy”. Our singer lambasted the guy for ignorance, said that we were performing authentic English, Scottish and Irish folk music, and that we didn’t sing crap. Unsurprisingly, this did not go over well with the home that booked us, and the singer was asked to leave the band.

Showing my age, I have a large CD collection that I listen to in the car, and I am always surprised by how many songs that aren’t from Ireland or the UK on “Irish” CDs. Being in Canada, our band does perform a couple of songs from the Maritimes per gig. Our singer likes shanties, and we do some of those as well.

I think that the real need is to make a decision about whether you are there to please yourself/sing what you want versus pleasing the audience. Do I like Wild Rover? No. Does it get everyone in the pub singing? Yes. So our band sings it. One benefit of playing in pubs with a tip jar, or markets with a hat in front of you, is that one gets concrete evidence of what people like, and what they don’t like. For tunes, getting little kids to dance is the clue for what works. For songs the key metric is whether people stop talking/eating/walking and listen/sing along/clap.

Hugh

Trish said, “And Ewan MacColl, before either of them, was reputed to have said the same…”

I think what Ewan MacColl said was that you should only sing songs from your own geographical area.

There’s also a difference between “singing in your own dialect” and “singing in your own accent”. The Freedom Come All Ye is in nobody’s dialect - nobody speaks that way (unless you’re Billy Kay or Alistair Heather) but you could still sing those dialect words in your own accent - you don’t have to anglicise them.

I took what Dick Gaughan said to mean that it was perfectly acceptable to sing songs, from wherever, in your own accent as long as you didn’t change the (dialect) words. So for an American song you don’t have to sing it in a fake American accent, for a Scottish song (in Scots or not) you don’t have to put on a pretendy Scots accent, etc…

I must confess that I haven’t always followed that advice myself. It’s very easy to veer towards the accent of your source singer so nowadays I usually listen to a song source just enough to get the rough melodic outline and then will not listen to the source recording again until I have the song in my pocket, as it were.
I’ve also deliberately changed words here and there so, for example, in Steve Earle’s “Goodbye”, “All of them nights down in Mexico” became “All of those nights down in Mexico”.

Re: The minefield that is a song repertoire for a band… Any ideas?

We seem to be going down a rabbit hole with this thread – but a productive one, nonetheless. I started with an innocent question… 😉

Richard D Cook, you have hit on what are, for me, many of the key issues.

I have a similar approach – quite happy to learn from other people’s ideas, but need and want to use them in my own way. There is a world of difference between “creative borrowing” and slavish copying, which is anything but creative.

I don’t want to produce carbon copies of other people’s music either. Surely one of the greatest assets of the traditional canon is that we have a huge repository of material that is anyone’s and no one’s – there for the using according to need, without all the issues of writer-owned music. Why, then, ignore that and just produce copies of what other people have done? That said, it’s hard to come up with something entirely different once you have heard a definitive version.

Much though I love the Bothies and some of the others, I don’t want to copy them. I might take a few ideas though – and annoyingly, they did seem to get all the best tunes!

Somewhere in all this is the question of what audiences are for. Chewed over ad nauseam on here over the years in the context of sessions.

In the past fortnight alone, I have played for Irish ex-pats who were on a sentimental St Patrick’s night out, Irish people living in England who had, with great relief found some Irish music to bring their kids to hear, English people whose son is getting married at Spanish Point later this year – and a load of Essex disco-babes for whom we were just wallpaper for their Friday night cocktails before the karaoke began. The levels of interest and response ranged from the nostalgic to the culturally interested to complete ignorance.

Basically, we play because we enjoy playing, and consider a band setting a way of developing our playing in a slightly different way from sessions. But like most musicians, we acknowledge the importance of an audience – it gives one’s efforts focus and purpose.

But not all audiences have the same needs – or skills – when it comes to appreciating music. For those of us (it seems the majority on this thread) who are not in Ireland, we can’t count on the inherent ‘patriotic’ interest you find in that country. It seems we can’t even count on the idea that people think music is something actively to listen to anymore. But if you don’t somehow please audiences, the bookings dry up…

Add into the mix the fact that somehow, I feel that we are dealing with a cultural artefact here, not just a generic form of World Music – so playing fast and loose with it is not the best idea. Somewhere in the mix, I want people to hear this great music that they may not have encountered before. Even if I am not in a position to give it the indigenous treatment, I want to make a decent representation of what the music really is, not just reduce it to some kind of bland light entertainment.

Which is where, I guess, those points about phoney accents and dialects come in. At one extreme, ITM is now a global music brand (as someone said recently) and is therefore fair game for whatever treatment anyone wants to give it – but at another could be argued that it is still the cultural property of one People, with which others should not tamper (without trepidation). At what point does it lose what it has?

Where does all that leave me when deciding who to aim my/our playing at? Somehow the whole thing seems to become more complex when you introduce voices and words…
