Would you could you do you?

OK OK, I know most like to just get down to tunes at sessions and that includes myself. But if you found yourself at a session full of the joys where good craic and drink were flowing like a mountain stream, where a few songs were being sung, would you join in at the chorus if you knew the words and it was obviously the thing to do?

I would and do, and really quite enjoy if thats the craic. A harmony where I can, provided it’s with in my range
and I don’t need a kick in the proverbial to hit the note.

I’m not talking about a tune session where some drunk tourist comes over all misty eyed and insists on singing, or some “want to be” folksinger/budding singer song writer just want shut up, I mean where this sort of thing is obviously just part of the craic and a natural part of the proceedings, as it is at at least one regular session in my locality.

Or is it, as it can be for me if I don’t know the words, just an opportunity to nip out for a smoke.
Or would you hate it and consider it just an unfortunate unnecessary interruption to what for you should just be
an evening of tunes?????

Re: Would you could you do you?

On the right night a song or two from the session participants is a nice addition to the night. Depends on the mood and who is in, some of the regulars tune players can be cajoled into singing under the right circumstances, others are occasionally unstoppable (drink frequently plays a part in both of these situations).

We also have some friends that are primarily singers that like tunes. They’ll appear every now and then and sing a couple or so songs during the course of ther session.

I like all of this.

What I don’t like very much is a “mixed” sessions where there are a bunch of singers and another bunch of tune players. In my experience the singers in such a set up tend to all want their solo spots (understandably) and this breaks the night up into a series of individual performances which might be to a varying degree accompanied by tunsters. I find this very frustrating as it tends towards a circle of death event and breaks up the spontaneity and informality of a tune session.

Of course this needn’t always be the case, but it is the general trend in my experience. I have also experienced tune sessions where the same exclusive sets get played every week, or where everyone takes a formal turn of starting sets in a circle*. These tend to feel a little forced to me, and remind me of how I feel at a mixed session.

A singers circle is a very different night from a tunes session. I sometimes enjoy a night out at such an event, mostly to listen. But I wouldn’t be attracted to playing at such a night regularly. Mixed sessions tend towards the singer circle template in my experience.

Sorry if this is very disjointed, I had to keep getting up to attend to something else :-)

- chris

*although I have insisted on this last set up at sessions where the players are mostly beginers or youngsters in order to encourage everyone to take a turn at playing.

Doesn’t that tell you something?
You, as a musician, might feel you have to be quiet for a singer, but the audience are under no such obligation. The fact that they will talk over the tunes and stop to listen to the songs suggests to me that they like the songs.

Don’t get me wrong, there has to be a balance, and it probably needs to be fairly heavily in favour of tunes. But the odd song here and there to add a bit of variety can only be a good thing. Songs and tunes have always gone hand in hand - they are part of the same tradition. Instead of sitting holding your instrument and getting bored when a singer stands up, treat it like you would any other new tune - listen and learn, when you’ve got the jist of it try joining in. You never know, you might find you enjoy it!

As for the decline in interest, compare the number of folk clubs (and their attendance levels) now to what we had in the ’70s. Certainly trad is as strong, or stronger than it has ever been, but only amongst the aficionados. It has become very closed and insular - what we’ve lost is the blurred edge of casual listeners. If it is ever going to be something more than a few guys huddled in the corner of a pub, playing for their own amusement, then we have to make it more inclusive.

My experience (small, 7 years in one corner of Ireland) is that the kind of session that is dominated by tuneheads, steam-reelers, or whatever you want to call them are the exception, not the rule. Nearly all those I went to (3 regulars, quite a few more in other places) would be “leavened” by songs and slow airs. People had come together for music, so why should there not be songs? In the more grass-roots sessions, the tunes tended even just to be treated, to my annoyance, as a warm-up for the songs, mostly sung by characters in the community.

I only came across tune-exclusive sessions A) in more tourist-oriented areas (Dublin and Doolin, to mention two - even in Killarney the norm seemed to be that at a “tune” session there would be a few songs in the course of an evening) and B) outside Ireland.

I don’t claim that my experience is exhaustive or definitive, but it is genuine.

There may be a city/country thing going on here. Or rather an “amount of musicians in relation to sessions” thing, which sometimes, but not always, correlates with the population of the area.

Some examples:

Boulder has one main session and it tends to be mixed. It’s the only game in town unless you wanted to travel further afield. I think there are enough musicians and interest to support another one but from what I’ve heard, they’ve had a b*gger of a time finding a venue.

Denver has several strong tune sessions but also several mixed sessions.

Dundee had one main session for a while that was very mixed, even though that area is fairly urban and there are lots of musicians about. A couple years ago they started a tune session and from all accounts, it’s doing well.

Oban also has one main session and it’s very very mixed. With everything. Country and Western songs, gaita, Irish tunes, Scottish tunes, you name it. But it’s the only game in town.

Skye has a fair few sessions and all the ones I have been to have been mostly tunes. If there are singing or heavily mixed ones there, I wouldn’t know about it. Like Glasgow, Edinburgh, and similar cities, there seems to be enough musicians and sessions to support both, even though the population of the island is tiny.

Glasgow, Edinburgh, and Dublin have lots of sessions. Some are 97% tunes and others are more mixed. All three cities have a big population and a big population of traditional musicians.

Inverness has tune sessions and the odd mixed session. Get closer to Nairn though, where there are less sessions, you find it heavily mixed.

I think you can correlate how mixed a session is with how many sessions exist in a given area. This is somewhat predicted by population, but not always. If musicians/singers have the opportunity to sequester themselves by what they would rather play (songs, Scottish music, Irish music, etc.), then they seem to organize themselves that way. If there are fewer sessions. everyone is forced to try to get on at the same pub.

I prefer the more sequestered scenario because it usually allows for a more connected, high energy session: everyone has a similar repertoire and wants to do more or less the same thing. You get more disconnected sessions when no one knows the same tunes and everyone wants to do their own thing.
