Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

Hi, Sharon,

Some people have the ability to learn tunes on the fly in sessions or by repeated listening of recordings, but it is seldom I can pick up a tune this way. I simply end up either forgetting altogether, missing essential phrases or nuance, or mixing up one tune with another. So, let me tell you what I do:

When I hear a tune I like, I learn it from a recording phrase by phrase. I actually do the same thing with written music. The challenge is to pick up the tune from a recording at tempo, so I use a computer app called ?The Amazing Slowdowner (’Google it - cheap and very easy to use), which allows the recording to be slowed down without affecting the pitch. You can then learn at the speed from which you can pick up the tune and, once learned, can play with the recording at a speed you can play until you gradually work it up to full speed. Learning the tune phrase by phrase helps both the learning process and also the ability to keep the overall tune in your head without simply forgetting how the thing goes.

Cheers.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

Sharon - I too played classical violin elementary through college and put it down for ten years before picking it up again to play Irish on. You should be in good shape. As to getting rid of the sheet music, I think it might be helpful to think about how you learn and remember. Don’t discount muscle memory - it’s OK to know a tune as much by feel as by sound. I usually learn one tune at a time - and you haven’t learned it until you can play it without sheet music the next day, in my opinion - and even at one tune a week that’s fifty tunes in a year. At least at first, while you’re getting the hang of it, I think it’s fine to learn from sheet music as long as you’re listening lots (preferably to the tune you’re learning!) too.

Last piece of advice - keep your fiddle out of its case, where you can see it, so you can play it right when you want to. Play for ten minutes while the kids get their ‘screen time’; play for two minutes while they brush their teeth. Tell the voice that says if you haven’t played today, or this week, that you’ll never get there, to shut up. Find a session to go to. (That last one’s important.) Learn the tunes they know. Have fun!

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

You’ve already taken the critical first step by *wanting to*. Relax. Forcing yourself to learn by ear can be just as debilitating as refusing to learn by ear.

Perhaps the most important thing to realize (and I’m sure you do) is that it won’t happen overnight; you won’t just flip a switch or follow a bunch of rote steps and be there. Once you acknowledge that it is a journey, not a destination, and that it can (and will) happen infinitessimally incrementally -- maybe over a period of several years -- then the pressure will be off, and your mind will be more absorbent to learning tunes by ear.

A person once said to me that they won’t even pick up their instrument and play a single note to learn a tune until they’ve listened to it to the point that it is so stuck in their head that it follows them around like a zombie in a bad horror movie, and they are then forced to learn it in self-defense. That’s how it was for me with Old Man Dillon. The bugger wouldn’t give me a moment’s peace, so I had to learn it into submission.

I’ve never personally used The Amazing Slow-Downer (I’m not technically-inclined enough), but know many people who have to great effectiveness. I, too am a parent with a full-time job (not at all Irish, and not steeped in the tradition), and I haven’t looked at a Dot since I can’t remember when - so please know that learning to learn by ear can be done!

Another thing to remember is that sheet-music is not the absolute undisputed scourge of Irish Traditional Music. There are many fabulous collections out there (Lesl Harker’s compilation of tunes taught to her by Mike Rafferty; a book of Paddy O’Brien compositions compiled by his daughter, Eileen; Vincent Broderick’s collection The Turoe Stone; a seminal compilation of Sean Ryan compositions; and many others). Such collections are compiled by accomplished musicians, often with the source’s blessing. So if such luminaries can abide the existence of Dot Books, then bugger the wannabes who turn up their noses at the very existence of Dots.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

This all assumes you’ve no ready exemplars and is no more than a cursory outline.

1. Learn to lilt. It really is the silver bullet for almost all learning ills and the way to get good fast. Unfortunately there’s no good online tuition but a couple of hours messing around you’ll have it if you listen to:
this http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Jjb3P2SvLQ
and this http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=akhFCiuwfA4,
and this http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rm_oaqW_qRM.
If at all possible try to steal syllables and nonsense words from the above, they’ve chosen them for a reason: they help with timing ornamentation which’ll make it easier for you down the road. You’ll feel like a complete eejit to start but stick with it. If you really can’t, then at least learn to “sing” or hum tunes, even if you have to subvocalise.

2. Don’t pick tunes at random, take the advice of a teacher. Find a recording of solo fiddle playing at an easy pace. The CDs that accompany Matt Cranich’s and Paul McNevin’s tuition books are the obvious starting points. Both are experienced teachers that have carefully chosen tunes that progress in difficulty. Seamus Creagh’s Tunes for Practice is perhaps a little advanced for you yet.

An alternative is http://oaim.ie. There’s seven days free trial of the whole site, plus the whole of beginner’s fiddle course is free. I’ve taught with Majella and would recommend her. I should mention that I know the lads behind the site but have no commercial interest in the venture.

3. Put the tune you want to learn on a loop. Listen to it on repeat say ten times, twice a day while you’re doing something else. Absorb it naturally, without effort. It’ll take a couple of days. Now you’ve got the tune in your head, getting it under your breath will happen almost imperceptibly.

4. Start lilting the tune. Play with it. Find yourself lilting away in the car, doing the cleaning, at the supermarket. Do this for a couple of days. One of the biggest mistakes made by learners from outside Irish communities is to underestimate the amount of time trad musicians spend “playing with” tunes before they try to put them under your fingers.

5. While all this is going on, learn your instrument. Learn to hear a note in your head and be able to find it on the fiddle. Learn the D, G and A scales and their relative minors, if you’re grade 2 or there abouts you’ll have these already. Pitch the nursery rhymes, popular songs, anything that you know by heart, in these keys. twenty minutes a day, six days a week is enough. Play while your children sing along.

6. Now you’re ready to put the tune under your fingers.

7. Go part-phrase by part-phrase. Trad tunes typically break into two parts, nowadays people label them A and B. Older musicians call them “the tune” and “the turn”. Each part is usually eight bars or measures. But if you listen you’ll notice that they are structured like a conversation. There’s a a “question” (roughly two bars), and “answer” (again roughly two bars), the “question” is repeated and the “answer” is with a slight variation (as if to make it more emphatic). Build up phrase by phrase till you get to the point where you can play along with the recording.

Focus on rhythm over intonation to start with. The lilting will have helped with this. Again, if you can’t play in time, adding ornaments will only make the problem worse.

8. Don’t feel you have to avoid notation like the plague. At least some, perhaps most, trad teachers going back a hundred years have provided ABC notation as an aide-mémoire for students without recording devices. Just don’t rely on it at the expense of developing your ear. So glance at it only for example if you really can’t remember how a tune starts or the key it’s in. If you find yourself doing so any more than once or twice go back to step 4.

9. Contrary to some of the advice above don’t use SlowDowner software or equivalents. As a beginner they’ll literally “slowdown” the development of your ear and handicap you when it comes to learning ornaments.

10. I really wouldn’t bother with sessions till you have the first say fifty tunes under your belt, unless you’re using them to find a teacher. They’re really not intended or designed for learners, and are a ready source of frustration with one’s progress, and bad habits like simplifying tunes in an effort to keep up.

Trad is a highly expressive art form but it’s a music of small, almost subliminal, effects. If a student hasn’t acquired the critical listening and reproduction skills to learn a simple melody by ear it’s unlikely they’ll ever be able to analyze a trad musician’s performance of a tune, let alone reproduce it, or develop their own style.

Good luck.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

Just do what you did when you learned to read music, but this time using a different input and different circuitry. Listen to a note, find it on the fiddle and reproduce it. Listen to two notes, ditto, and etc.
Start with some slow airs. The secret is not to guess (or to not guess?) at what you think you are hearing, but to make sure of it in your own mind -- even if it’s only one note -- and play it. It might seem hard at first, but that’s only because you don’t have the neural networks built up in your brain. It isn’t difficult to build them, but it takes time, so don’t get frustrated, and don’t try to take in too much. Keep it simple, and the number of notes you can take in will build into a phrase, and before you know it, you’ll be wondering what the problem was.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

Don’t try to just chuck the written music cold turkey. Wean yourself off it slowly. Part of what you need to learn is not just hearing the notes, it is simple memorization. When I was a youngster, we had to memorize poems, speeches, multiplication tables, formulas and all sorts of things in school. That seems to be less the case these days, as folks let devices remember things for them. But memorization becomes easier the more you do it. Play four bars from the sheet music, close your eyes, and play them from memory. Then do it again, and again. Then try to do eight bars at a time. Then a whole tune. Repetition is at the core of memorization, and one of the advantages of sheet music is that it lets you play something over and over exactly the same.
And soon you will get to the point where, after a few times through a tune with music, you will be able to play it from memory. And the more you work those mental ‘memorization’ muscles, the easier it gets. Then you will find that you can hear a tune a few times, remember it, and repeat it back. The ear is a big part of it, but memorization is also an important skill that requires development.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

I was in the same boat as the OP about seven months ago or so.. ex-classical player starting ITM after a musical hiatus. I’m a single mom who works full-time and has two kids, so I understand where you’re coming from in that regard as well. I knew one tune when I started, now I know just over 50. I don’t think that “getting rid of the sheet music” need be a goal for you at this point, anyway.

Here is what has worked for me:

- I’ll pick either a tune from my session, or one that I like, and work on that one. One at a time.
- I’ll listen to it for a couple days, not necessarily to the point where I can hum it, but at least so I have an idea of it.
- Most of the time, at that point, I come here and look up the dots, and play it on and off over the course of a few days, sometimes with the recording, sometimes not, sometimes looking at the dots, sometimes not.
- I have found the Fionn Seisiun recordings to be tremendously helpful for listening and learning.
- For the first few months, I didn’t concern myself with ornamentation when learning a tune. I’ve noticed, though, over the past month, ornaments are coming much more naturally to me and throwing one in doesn’t get in the way of learning the tune.
- There are a few tunes that I have just picked up by hearing them every week at my session.
- After I have learned a tune, I add the Fionn Seisiun track, if I have it, it to a playlist on my smartphone. Once a week, I will put the player on random and play from that playlist, whatever comes up, to make sure I keep playing and retain them.

Someone up there said not to bother with sessions until you have fifty or so tunes under your belt. I don’t necessarily agree with that, depending on the session. At my session, I’ve learned new tunes, I’ve met new people, made friends, and even ended up gigging with a couple of the people from my session. There is no way I’d be up to 50-ish tunes now if not for my session. That said, you do need to find the right kind of session… one that is accepting of people learning. The first time I went, there were exactly two tunes that I knew. Now, I’d say that I play along to almost half of the tunes at session.

Anyway, have fun with this. That’s the main thing.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

As for slow downer software.

Most trad tunes are simple. For a beginner learning marches, waltzes, polkas and jigs, very simple: a 16 bar melody line that stays within two octaves, tends to concentrate on only a few notes within that range, rarely jumps more than a fifth, is broken into two 8 bar parts, each typically built from a 2 bar “question”, 2 bar “answer” motif. It’s only anecdotal but in over forty years teaching I’ve yet to meet anyone, even non-musicians who have never heard trad before, who can’t be taught to lilt such a tune within about 90-120 minutes. No-one, certainly no aspiring musician, needs slow downer software to learn such a tune.

It’s a music of small, almost subliminal effects, and far more delicate than many suppose. Slowing a roll down till one can discern the pitch of the cut and the tap gives entirely the wrong message (to my mind) to beginners, as does slowing it down till the timing of the elements shifts from felt to readily measurable. Below performance speed, ornaments start to fall apart, relative weights and duration have to subtly shift to compensate; some trad musicians accordingly find it unnatural to play tunes below a certain speed. Get the sound of a roll played at a reasonable speed in your head, practice it by playing slowly building up speed till what you play matches what’s in your head. Having something less, some intermediary state, something not quite a roll in your head only serves, in my experience, to confuse students. And don’t even start me on lift, slowed down it just vanishes like dew from off the ground. Below a certain speed a reel stops being a reel, it becomes just an unmemorable attenuated melody.

Music students have always slowed down performances they want to copy, from asking their teacher “Can you play that again but slower” to using a slower rpm setting, to braking the turntable with a hand. I’ve done it myself. Trying to copy exactly what a favorite musician is doing in a recording is a right of passage for every musician. But it’s no substitute for basic live listening skills. Using slow downer software isn’t a crime or blasphemy, it’s just not, in my experience, the most effective or efficient way to learn trad. And then there’s the risk that it becomes a crutch. Perhaps my judgement is clouded by various cognitive biases but this music simply isn’t complex enough to warrant using such software till one has acquired basic live learning skills and are working at a more advanced level. And without those, even slowing a piece down isn’t going to help.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

Jim, I know you’ve nothing but good intentions so I never interpret your comments or requests as combative.
I wouldn’t though get too hung up on the instrument.

Of course “student” encompasses a range of ages, musical and technical abilities, challenges, and the style of playing that interests them. For the beginner (either new to the instrument or the genre) the most useful are solo recordings of a suitable repertoire played at an easy pace hence the recommendations for the CDs that accompany Matt’s and Paul’s tutor books.

As I mentioned above, musicians have made tapes for students for donkey’s years. Seamus Creagh’s Tunes for Practice CD set is one of the few that have had a commercial release. (Note there’s no accompanying notation. I believe they’re essentially lifted from the cassette tapes he made for Gretta McCarthy(?).) I’m perhaps prejudiced because we ran into each other all the time but I recommend it strongly to students that have mastered the basic ornaments. It has a commendable Cork - Kerry repertoire bias.

In a similar vein, fiddlers would benefit from listening to the CDs that come with June McCormack’s two tutor books (Fliúit and Fliúit 2). These are essentially tune books (with short sections of how to play ornaments) that assume the student has a basic command of the flute. Their value for fiddlers is two fold: a) she plays each tune through twice, first at an easy pace exactly as notated, then at performance pace (not ridiculously so), and b) she marks all breaths. The latter is a great help in phrasing for musicians of any instrument. As I’ve said before, the trick with playing trad well is all in the musical choices. June’s notation helps make these visible. It’s terribly, but understandably, Roscommon-Sligo though.

Progressing into intermediate territory (whatever that means) and non-teaching CDs, several of my remote students have benefitted from Gerry Harrington’s “At Home”. Martin Hayes seems an obvious entry point what with the minimal accompaniment and typically easy loping pace . But he walks a high wire and there’s a lot more going on than appears at first listen. Though a whistle player, Micho’s style is very accessible to students. So to Denis Murphy (though there aren’t enough solo tracks available commercially). I could go on and on. It really starts to come down to the personal styles that take the student’s fancy.

They’re perhaps not for beginners other than to provide a general familiarity with the genre but a highlight of Willie Week are the evening concerts in the hall. For the last few years they’ve been recorded and the best tracks put on sale as a double CD the following year. All instruments but lots of fiddle payers amongst them. Mainly solo, but also duets and other small groupings. It’s the very best trad musicians (most of whom have given their time to teach during the week) playing for the clan. I believe they’re available by mail-order from the Oidhreacht an Chláir website.

And then for the more advanced and the brave, Tommy Potts. I’ve spent more hours studying his two commercial CDs and a handful of private recording than I care to admit. And oh yes, I’ve used ASD on them.

It’s only what I can quickly come up with on a bright Spring morning but hopefully tis enough,’twill serve. Good luck.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

This has been worked into some other comments, but I’ll say it explicitly again because it’s been really important for me:

Hum and whistle tunes all the time, driving to work, doing the dishes, in the shower, on the toilet, etc. Listen again and again critically, and follow the dots too if that helps. Be honest with yourself about ALL the parts you aren’t doing correctly. I find that once I can whistle or hum a tune correctly, then it’s easy to play on the fiddle from memory. You don’t have to agree the dots slavishly, but don’t make up variations on phrases either when you’re getting started. Just do variations or versions the way you’ve heard other folks do them.

Re: How to get rid of the sheet music?

I was very much in the same boat about 10 years ago, and it has taken this long to get to where I am now with “ear playing”. Having learned to read music at an early age, I also had t0 learn quite a lot of music theory. So when I started to try to play by ear, I found it helped to think in terms of intervals - “that note’s a 4th above or a 3rd below” for example. I also used to do what I called a “mental double translation” - i.e. hear the music, turn it into dots on a page inside my head before it would come out as played notes. Now I can bypass the turning into an “inside the head” score.
It also helped me a lot going to trad music classes where learning by ear was the order of the day, even if having learned the tune by ear, we would then get given the dots to take away at the end of the class - and some folk would revert to using the dots.
Other than that, and even going back to my long-past school days, sheer practice and repetition would allow me to ditch the music score - not the same process as learning by ear from scratch, but another way of achieving the same end result.
Now, if I know a tune/song, I can usually “busk it” by ear, but that’s also partly due to being more familiar with my instrument than I was 10 years ago.
