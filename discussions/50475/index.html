Re: Use of dissonance in recordings of traditional music?

The reason why “dissonance” as an ambiguous term is because dissonance itself happens over a spectrum of harmonies to varying degress; While applied dissonance has varying degress of brightness/darkness/softness/harshness in different contexts. All dissonance really means is that the “competing” wavelengths don’t align and are at a state of tension. The degree to which they don’t align is the degree of dissonance. Just look at the interval ratios and it becomes *partially clear.

“Consonant” Intervals
Octave - 2:1
Perfect Fifth - 3:2
Perfect Fourth - 4:3

“Dissonant” Intervals
Min Seventh - 16:9
Min Second - 16:15
Aug Fourth - 45:32

Dissonance isn’t “historical” it’s mathematical.

When you start considering the complex nature of intervals, before you even get to our own human psychology, things immediately get messy. Like how if you place the Mediant(the 3rd in a major 3rd) in the bass line, it creates quite a bit of dissonant tension, even though all the notes together make the “consonant” major chord. Or how suspended chords are technically dissonant, but they are so soft and subtle, they could hardly be described as dark or harsh. Or how minor *and diminished harmonies can sound absolutely gorgeous and fulfilling in the proper context. I’ve personally been decieved by some minor chords at points because they were so gentle and pleasant in how they set up the resolution. And just the same, consonant harmonies can darken up a vibe in abundance, building dreadful discomfort, casting a beaming light on things you would jerk your face away from. Things that would make you cringe and shutter.

Sorry, I drifted off a bit…

I say all of that to say, it’s not black and white. Dissonances/tensions can add a lot of character and spirit where you may not expect them.

In brief, if dissonance is usually understood to belong to the realm of harmony, we can identify other factors such as timbre, modal scales, accidentals, position of a pitch in the melody/metric structure and its duration, and above all, perception.

《Dissonance is born out of consonance》
and vice versa.

Re: Use of dissonance in recordings of traditional music?

When it comes down to it, the mainstays of dissonance in (classical) music theory such as flattened fifths, minor seconds, tritones and similar just aren’t part of the Irish tradition.

As mentioned above, some Irish players do play the occasional flattened fifth as part of their style (though I’d prefer to call it a sharpened fourth) and there’s a whole thread about this on this site. There’s also an episode of the At The Crossroads podcast on this topic. Essentially that sharpened fourth tendency lends a kind of Lydian mode sound to some performances - personally I don’t hear Lydian modes as dissonant and neither would anyone familiar with The Simpsons theme.

Ultimately I can’t think of any traditional Irish tunes in which dissonance is integral to the melody - although I can think of some ‘experimental’ trad performers who put dissonance into their arrangements (eg Matthew Horsley, linked to above, or the band Lankum - eg the Bernard Hermann style strings in their ‘Go Dig my Grave’ song)

Re: Use of dissonance in recordings of traditional music?

This discussion has been really thought-provoking. I find myself thinking about dissonance in two ways. In conventional musical theory, I suppose you’d think about dissonance in terms of certain crunchy intervals - minor seconds, flat fifths or whatever. But actually they can sound very rich and sweet - in this video of Sharon Shannon playing ‘McGuire and Patterson’, at about 26 seconds in, she hits what I’m guessing is an F#, while the accompanist plays a C natural underneath:
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iDqqDpBh6nU&list=RDiDqqDpBh6nU&start_radio=1&ab_channel=JoeMcCabe
So there’s a flat fifth interval there which ought to feel dissonant. But because it’s part of a progression that’s moving us to the chord change to G, it feels rich and interesting rather than jarring.
I think it also doesn’t feel dissonant because we recognise that there are two different lines going on, the melody and harmony, and we expect them to move apart sometimes then resolve back together, creating then resolving tension.
But also, there’s something about dissonance and timbre which is interesting and very much part of this music. An accordion with two sets of reeds slightly detuned from each other is innately dissonant, with every note containing a texture of dissonance. Multiple fiddles are also always a little out of tune with each other and with other instruments. And beyond that, the accordion is tuned to equal temperament, so most chords or intervals will contain an extra innate dissonance.
I find that these layers of textural dissonance create a thicker, richer sound which can almost shimmer with complex, high overtones as the layers of different tunings shift in and out of each other.
I think ultimately dissonance is about expectation - from knowing the genre and knowing Western music generally, we have a huge set of expectations, and sounds that are strictly speaking dissonant can sound lovely if they fit with those expectations.
But when, for example, a guitarist plays a A7 chord (ie A C# E Gnat) under a Dmin Irish tune, it feels jarring and dissonant to me, even though the technical dissonance (a D or a Cnat with a C#) could feel very sweet in another context. I think it feels dissonant because that particular move - a major seventh chord before resolving back to a minor on the tonic - just isn’t part of the genre, and doesn’t ‘belong’, though it might sound great with a klezmer tune! So it’s the cultural dissonance that is most jarring…
