Re: Some beginner octave mandolin/mandola questions

String types: Even the best mandola strings are a lot cheaper than bass guitar strings (everything is cheaper than bass guitar strings) The tonal difference between brass/bornze/phosphor etc. is the same as for guitars and you can look it up, but on an instrument like the Trinity College the difference won’t be that great. Start off with ordinary phosphor bronze, and once you know what you are doing you can experiment if you want, the strings are cheap enough.

String gauges: Manufacturers generally know better than anyone else what gauge strings suit their instruments in the hands of most players. So start with the gauges they supply, and only change them once you have got used to it and decided that a change would suit your individual style better. The nut slots are normally wide enough to accommodate any strings you are likely to use in standard tuning, but if you fit heavier strings and find they are sticking when you try to tune the instrument, then the slots need widening. (you’ll also fing you need to move the bridge slightly when you change gauge, to keep the intonation right).

One word of caution though: The barring on Trinity College instruments is woefully light, and if you put heavy strings on, you may well find the top sinks and the instrument becomes unplayable after a very short time.

Octave G string: Why? Using one octave course is a bit like having a guitar with 5 nylon strings and one steel. Start off with unisons, and only switch to octaves if you find the bass courses are much weaker than the trebles. You’ll be able to experiment with an octave string without modification, but if you decide to make it permanent you will want to change the nut and get a rather fancy bridge saddle made (or put up with bad intonation up the neck).

GDAE/GDAD: You’ll have to answer that one yourself. It only takes a few seconds to re-tune, so try both ways and see which suits you best. For me, the loss of speed playing melody in GDAD far outweighs the gains of simpler chord shapes, so it’s GDAE for me. But it all depends on what music you play and how you play it.

Re: Some beginner octave mandolin/mandola questions

To check the shop setup:

To check nut height, fret each string between the second and third fret. Look at the gap between the sting and the first fret - if it is touching the fret the nut is too low. If there is a visible gap of more than about 2 though the nut is too high. If you aren’t sure if the string is touching the fret, tap it lightly with you fingertip. If it is OK you’ll hear it ‘click’ against the fret, if it’s too low you won’t.

To check the truss rod adjustment, fret the G string at below the first and above the 12th frets, and look at the gap between the string and7th fret - there should be a visible gap of about 20-30thou. Do the same on the E string to check the neck isn’t twisted.

To check the intonation, lightly touch a string above the 12th fret and play the octave harmonic. Check that it is exactly the same pitch as the note with the string fretted at the 12th. Repeat for all strings, and if they aren’t all exactly in tune, either the bridge needs moving or the saddle needs some work.

To check the action height, play an open string repeatedly, gradually getting louder. It should start to buzz on the frets when you are hitting it just a bit harder than you ever would in normal playing. If it doesn’t buzz at all, or only when you hit it really hard, the action could be lowered. If it buzzes within the range of normal playing volume, the action is too low and needs to be raised. Repeat this with each string. (This is slightly harder with a mandola than a guitar, because you need to be able to distinguish between the sound of a string buzzing on the frets and that of the two strings of the course clashing together. If the strings are clashing within the normal playing range, the string spacing needs adjustment).

Finally check that the frets are level. Play up each fret of each string at near maximum normal playing volume, and if you get a buzz at one or more particular frets, then the frets need levelling.

Yes.

The root of the problem is that they uses an X brace system like a guitar. That system is designed to withstand the twisting force that a guitar bridge exerts, not the downward pressure that a floating bridge and tailpiece exerts. Added to which the bars themselves are too light.

It’s like having a floor with joists that are too thin and too far apart. Under light loads that makes the floor ‘bouncier’ (which is why TC instruments are made that way - it lets the soundboard vibrate more) But if you increase the loading on that bouncy floor, it will break more easily than one with the proper joists.

If you look at the soundboard you will see that it isn’t completely flat, it is slightly convex. If the strings are too heavy (and often even with medium gauges on these instruments) the downforce on the bridge pushes the soundboard down flat or even concave, and the action height also comes down. Once the soundboard goes concave the instrument is dead ( if you push down on a convex surface like an arch, you put it into compression, which holds everything together. If you push down on a concave surface you put it under tension, which tends to rip everything apart).

So keep an eye on that soundboard curvature and the action height, and if you see it starting to go down, switch to lighter strings.

Re: Some beginner octave mandolin/mandola questions

Technically, a mandola is a member of the mandolin family, as a viola is a member of the violin family, similarly tuned a 5th down from the -in instrument, CGDA, and with a slightly longer scale. Some people argue that the -a suffix indicates the original instrument, and the -in is the later, smaller, version.
Thus, long before the folk revival, you could buy a (usually european ) mandola, with a body a bit larger than a mandolin, and a slightly longer scale length.
The tenor banjo was of course originally tuned like the mandola. The first users of the tenor banjo in Irish music played it as normally tuned, but soon re-strung their instruments and tuned them an octave below the violin, they also preferred the slightly longer scale, 19 frets as opposed to 17. Thus, when people started clamouring for larger mandolins the terms ‘tenor mandola’ and the ‘octave mandola’ came to be used by people unaware that mandola was perfectly accurate already, and ‘octave mandolin’ would be more precise for the longer scale instrument. ( Then you have the ‘Irish bouzouki’, or as I prefer to call it, the long-necked octave mandolin. ).

Re: Some beginner octave mandolin/mandola questions

Just read all these posts. There is a wealth of info here. I’m a beginner bouzouki-er myself and string my TC zouk like an octave mandolin with octaves on the 2 lowest courses. I haven’t noticed any ill effect on the top. I did adjust the truss rod a 1/4 turn and adjusted the bridge position for intonation. I’m taking note of John Pearse and GHS string sets because I haven’t found a set with gauges I need (24/40,13/28. 16/16, 11/11). I’ve been using banjo and bouzouki loop end strings to make a set. I have replaced my nut with bone and will soon replace the bridge because I have to re-do the string slots and bone sounds better. I use both GDAE and GDAD (I use lots of alt tunings on gtr so I’m used to them) for rhythm and solos. I’m finding OMs work well in blues, rock, and bluegrass as well as trad folk. My zouk, or OM, has inspired me to write tunes for it in all of them in just a few weeks. I hope you have as much fun and luck with your instrument as I’m having with mine. Thanks for the great info
