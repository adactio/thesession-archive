Re: getting through a gig

Opherman, please don’t take this harshly, but there are red flags all over your question. Don’t be surprised if some of the responses you get here grow to be a textbook introduction to the wonderful session tradition of slagging--hurling insults with a smile cleverly designed to make you smile back no matter how uncomfortable you feel.

You see, lots of trad Irish musicians carry rather large chips on their shoulders about classical or otherwise “schooled” musicians who assume they can sit down and play this music without years of diligent effort and internalizing the tradition. Sight reading will get you the notes, or a few of them anyway, but not the *music.* And some folks (even some regulars at The Session) will likely come to the defense of the music by asking (or *telling*) you not to play it if you can’t do it by heart, with respect. You don’t have to be born Irish to play this music, but a few Irish organ transplants wouldn’t hurt. Play it long enough and you’ll grow Irish ears, feet, heart, and liver.

So, your reference to sight reading suggests that you intend to play this music from the page, which only the Boston Pops ever attempt, to hilarious (and crushingly unauthentic) results. How many ITM tunes can you play from memory? (Note that this question also holds true even If you’re “just” playing backup guitar--there are no “accompnaiment tricks”…you have to know the tunes to do the accompaniment justice). Does the fiddler know any tunes by heart? Do you and the fiddler know the same tunes?

Also, despite Trevor Hutchinson’s soulful bass lines on most of Lunasa’s cuts, upright bass is not a standard in the normal ITM line-up. Nor drums, if by that you mean a drum kit or orchestral timpani. So if you’re going to play genuine Irish traditional music, you’re down to yourself and the fiddler. You might do better to find a whistle, flute, concertina, accordion, bodhran, and/or uillean pipes player.

Finally, I’m not sure what you mean by “irish type” band, and that worries me. Maybe your local pub and audience will tolerate an “irish type” experiment, and maybe not. I know mine wouldn’t.

Good luck with the slaggers…I can almost hear them baying at the scent….. *grin*

Re: getting through a gig

All right, y’all, BE NICE. *grin* And, Opherman, Will’s given you fair warning as well!

What would be helpful for you to know -- well, I would say the first thing is to go get a good recording of truly traditional music. Don’t get Lunasa (wonderful though they are) or any of the modern stuff, get something session-like and very traditional, like the Na Connery’s CDs or any number of recordings of sessions (Custy’s is a great place to do that -- http://www.custysmusic.com). Listen to it to get the feel, which is the important part.

Then go find yourself an existing session or two -- preferably of really good musicians -- and just go to listen over a pint. In between sets, strike up some conversations with the musicians. Likely, those conversations will tell you more than you ever thought you wanted to know. You don’t say where you are, but if there’s an Irish pub, it’s likely there’s Irish players around where you are someplace.

Now, after you’ve done that, if you decide you want to play traditional music traditionally, you’ll likely have some good contacts, an idea of what you want to sound like (which we promise you, will change regularly for a bit until you settle into your own style) and a way to do it (and there’s lots of good advice here about that).

If you decide you want to take the traditional music and stick elements of it into, say pub rock or some such, then be prepared to play for people who like to whoop, holler, and drink heavily while ‘listening’ to you, and know that you’ll probably not get much respect amongst traditional music musicians.

The feel is key to playing actual traditional music, as Will says, not the notes. I’ve worked with no small number of music majors who think that they’d like to play or sing Irish music, but who don’t realize how hard it is -- it’s not easy to play this stuff right, keep in mind, and you’ll be a bit ahead that way.

Good luck!

Re: getting through a gig

Opherman, no slagging here. I am not going to make the assumption that you are just starting into ITM, because it likely ain’t necessarily so.

The band which gave rise to the tradition of session playing is the
Chieftains. They were put together for the purpose of a theatrical
production. They also captured public interest, and the rest is history.

Put all your talents into your shows. Find out what does work for you, and what doesn’t. The only way to do that is to get out and try a lot of different things in gigs. Gigging is different from session playing, and the listener expects something different, so feel free to give it to them. Your group may or may not meet every person’s definition of Irish Traditional Music, but that it true for even the most traditional of Irish musicians. By all means, study the tradition, and get what you can from it, but bear in mind that today’s top performers all sound uniquely different from each other, and from all others. Being different is a good thing.

Repertoire is important. Lots of jigs and reels, mixed with as much rhythmic variety as possible. i.e. hornpipes, marches, the occasional strathspey…
Arrange your tunes into medleys of three or four tunes. Start each set of tunes in a different key than the key with which you ended the previous set.

BTW, in a lot of places, it is OK to put music notation in front of you. One of the top local fiddlers is always reading his tunes. I had the opportunity to replace him for a gig, and had to make up charts for his guitar player. We meshed so well that nobody knew that we had never played together, until about the middle of the show when he confessed that he had forgotten my name.

Have fun, and best of luck
Scott

Well. *grin* Thanks for taking the time for that post, Opherman! It does indeed give us more background info, and I don’t think you sounded defensive at all, considering how we jumped you. :) Just be glad that some of the more radically purist of us didn’t get in before Will, Mark, bb, and I did.

If you tell us where you are, perhaps we can come up with some players in your area, we’ve any number of people from just about everywhere, from Bilbao to Japan to Alaska, and it’s actually a very small global community of players…I’ve been surprised before at where there are players where I would have said there were none!

So…let’s see, for CDs -- we can do that. As a fluter (although I get the impression you learned the silver flute, not a wooden flute), you might be interested in the Wooden Flute Obsession CDs -- you can find complete descriptions in the Recordings section of The Session.

For session CDs, the two Na Connery’s CDs (if you can find them) and the Music At Matt Malloy’s CD are my personal favorites. There’s also the Paddy and Bridget CDs, I don’t own them, but folks I know in Clare like them (a Japanese couple who visited Ireland, got fascinated by the music and took up instruments, recording sessions in Clare and publishing them, and everyone nicknamed them Paddy and Bridget because the Irish are like that).

Guitar wise, it depends on the style you want to develop. John Doyle, Steve Cooney, Zan McLeod, oh gosh, there’s any number of stellar guitarists working in any number of tunings. John Doyle is my current fav, but not everyone is into such a driving, percussive style. John’s got a video at http://www.homespuntapes.com on Irish accompaniment guitar that I think is quite good…

What else? The tunes are called “tunes” and not “songs” (songs are sung, tunes are played), there are classic sets of tunes that go together, but feel free to put whatever you like together -- sky’s the limit. At a session, you don’t usually put different kinds of tunes together (ie: hornpipe with a reel with a jig), but in performance there’s very few boundaries.

You might like taking a listen to a track by my teachers, Matt and Shannon Heaton, currently living in Boston -- they’re part of the current batch of young hotshots (in the Irish tradition, you’re considered young until about your late 40’s, although Matt and Shannon are late 20’s early 30’s) in the Boston area. They play a set of tunes with Jessica Ziegler of Chicago at http://www.zinalee.com/sounds/mulqueens.mp3. All three tunes are in The Session database, ‘Mulqueen’s’ (Shannon knows the tune as ‘Charlie Harris’s’), ‘Three Steps Forward (Two Steps Back)’, which was written by Matt, and The Torn Jacket.

You might also like to listen to Matt’s playing on a quick turn they did for me on the classic slip jig, The Butterfly. They taped it quick like bunnies one morning without any rehearsal or planning. http://www.zinalee.com/sounds/butterfly.mp3

You can hear more of their stuff at http://www.siucra.net.

I’ve found that jazz musicians tend to make good ITM players -- for the most part, they “get” the whole thing much more easily than classical players tend to do (although I know plenty of good classical players who “got” it easily). They’ve usually got good ears, and they understand the things that make up variations that are part of being a good ITM player.

Anyway, feel free to ask questions and join in the various discussions, arguments and shouting matches we get into here -- you’ve certainly got the right attitude! :)

Re: getting through a gig

Opherman, to arrange sets for a performance, there’s no reason to limit yourself to the same key or even the same type of tune. Key changes from one tune to the next are quite common in ITM performance (and many sessions) because they create “aha” moments at the change, particularly when you go from major to minor, or major to mixolydian or dorian, for example.

One of my favorite jig sets goes as follies:

The Kerfunken (D maj)
Tell Her I Am (D mix)
Paddy Fahey’s (G dorian)

I’m pretty sure all three of these tunes are in the tune archives here.

It’s also fun to vary the beat by going from a hornpipe, strathspey, highland, or barndance into a reel, or from a slip jig to a single jig or a double jig. On the first track of their Merry Sisters of Fate cd, Lunasa has a great change going from Elizabeth Kelly’s Delight (slip jig in Em/Dmix) to Jimmy Ward’s Favorite (double jig in D maj/A mix).

Some other possibilities (from our band here in Montana):

Archibald MacDonald (slow air) into Tam Lin (reel) into Julia Delaney (reel)

Rambling Pitchfork (jig in D maj) into Hole in the Hedge (jig in C maj) into Sean Ryan’s (jig in A dorian).

My Love is in America (reel in D mix) into Eileen Curran (reel in G dorian)

Bobby Casey’s (reel in D maj) into Musical Priest (reel in Bm) into Cup of Tea (reel in Eminor/Dmaj)

Go for it.
