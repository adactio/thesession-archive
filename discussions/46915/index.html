The Difficulties of Piping

I’ve been playing on a WARBL for about a week or two now and I have noticed a few things. Certain difficulties have presented themselves, and I figured it would be fun to give a list to see how these things change when I get my actual set. Here’s the list:

1.) Getting my fingers to land on the holes. Coming from whistle, Low whistle and flute, becoming accustomed to covering a pinky hole can be a bit cumbersome.
2.) Getting used to the proper pressure on the bag. I know I only have a water bottle for now, and I don’t even have to feed it, but it’s still tricky. I noticed that when I’m breathing, I’m accidentally slipping into the second octave lol.
3.) Getting used to cross-fingering every single note. It seems on the pipes that every note on the pipes needs to be cross-fingered. I was watching an OAIM video, and the instructor said the rule of piping is “Always close the door behind you”.

These are just the one’s I’ve noticed. Anyone else have any others? I’m sure the Bellows will play a role when I get them. Can’t imagine pumping air into the bag being easy.

Re: The Difficulties of Piping

When I started piping (coming from 30 years on the whistle), it took me a good six months to stop treating the chanter as a whistle.

For me, a pedagogy that helped was to imagine that, on whistle, you put fingers *down* to play notes, whereas on the pipes, everything starts with all fingers *on*. Then you lift fingers to play notes, and “closing the door” to stop the chanter again (no sound coming out). It might be more helpful to think in this way rather than imagining that everything is cross-fingered. Think of the fingerings as simply native to the chanter, so that you lift two fingers for B, one for A, two for G, one for F#, two for E (ring finger and pinky working as one unit, which is another thing to get used to), and none for bottom D (chanter off the knee). Practice playing staccato in this manner, both scales and simple tunes, closing the chanter after every note. It’s hard at first, but it will help you in the long run.

Re: The Difficulties of Piping

Kellie, do you have any pipers near you? Any sessions? Even if you have no time for lessons, there are some seemingly mysterious things about the pipes that are hard to learn when you’re teaching yourself, but which may take 5 minutes for a piper to explain to you. You don’t necessarily need to take lessons for years, but you need to make sure you’re going in the right direction, at least.

As an example, like everyone else starting out, I struggled to get into the second octave. I’ve (still) never had official tuition as it were, and this was in the middle of lockdown (been playing close to 2 years now). It took me months to have control to the point where I could get into the second octave at will, and a large part of that is that I *only knew what I’d read*, in books and online. Even from the excellent Heather Clarke tutor book, you’re basically led to believe you just squeeze harder, but there’s more to it than that. Even today, when I will modestly say I’m leaps and bounds better than I was, I can get into the second octave without problems, but I’m still not sure exactly how I do it at times!

Another example: eight or nine months into my piping, out of lockdown, another piper told me to put my bloody fingers down (“closing the door”). I’m pretty sure I’d been playing the chanter like a whistle for longer than I’d like to admit, especially playing tunes that I already knew. That was a massive turning point for me. And it took him 3 seconds to say it.

Then there’s more basic, but absolutely crucial, stuff like reed maintenance and management. I’m so lucky to get to play in sessions with pipers twice a week, which has given me an intuition about stuff like bridle movement, and just knowing what’s wrong when your pipes suddenly sound a bit shit. These days, it’s very seldom that my pipes act up and I don’t know what’s causing it. I think that’s knowledge that can’t just be trial and error: at one point or another, you will *need* human or virtual assistance.

Re: The Difficulties of Piping

The difficulties of piping and the difficulties of configuring and using a WARBL as a MIDI wind controller in its piping configuration really should be considered completely independently.

If you want to master the WARBL, spend the time to learn to use the configuration tool, understand how the optical sensors work and are calibrated, understand the pressure curve and octave switching mechanism, configuration of the switches, all of it. Get familiar with the actual MIDI data streams coming out of the device using a tool like MIDIWrench, you’ll need that to use it with pretty much any synth app if you want to have any kind of real mastery of the whole system. You have to know how the data coming out of the WARBL will interact with the receiving MIDI sound module app or device as far as pitch bend behavior and range, volume and expression control messages, all of it.

Maybe some of what you learn playing the WARBL will translate to skills on the real pipes some day, but it really is its own thing.

Re: The Difficulties of Piping

As a piper, and user of numerous electronic chanters (Blair, Fagerstrom, Ross, Deger) and now Warbl, I can tell you that managing that slippery little Warbl bag as well as playing is difficult and distracting for a novice. Mouth blowing is far easier and more intuitive, at least initially. As much as I think the Warbl is an amazing MIDI device (and I really like the sounds through the Thumb Jam app, the trumpet quality is astonishing) I would not recommend it as a learning chanter. Stick with a real one for learning, it helps develop the embouchure needed for playing pipes and accuracy of landing notes with the fingers, then an electronic one for practicing discretely, then Warbl for exploring the musical genres. If you get overly frustrated you may end up abandoning it, a real shame. And yes get an instructor! or at least a competent player who can listen and assist.
