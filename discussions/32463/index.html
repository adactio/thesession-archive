Asking to Join a Session

There was a discussion recently about dealing with “crap musicians” at sessions and a peripheral topic appeared briefly that might be worth discussing. The idea of asking to join sessions isn’t black & white, and so it might be helpful to discuss it.

There are times where it’s appropriate and other times when it’s not so much. Sessions occur under a variety of circumstances and they are all different, some in subtle ways and some more obvious. It’s much like conversations; sometimes you can walk up and join in with people talking and it seems perfectly fine… and other times it seems like you might be intruding. I have found that I have to exercise the same sensibility if I’m approaching a session I’m not familiar with… when I’m visiting other locations and such. Other times, even those sessions don’t seem to require an invitation based on what seems obvious. Sometimes people will ask us at our local session if it’s ok to join and I’ll say, “Of course… no need to ask here… but thanks for being so considerate.” Some people, even in our session, seem to think having to ask is some sort of insult, but I think it will serve you well if you can ask under the right circumstances.

Learning about sessions in the outback of Irish trad here on the west coast of the US leads one to some misled conclusions. Most of the people I met in my formative years had no inclination about asking to join sessions; Irish sessions were perceived as democratic free-for-alls. There were a few Irish expats living here in SF that would have sessions here and there around town and I did notice a different attitude and approach… but they were never very frank or articulate about it… you had to read between the lines.

It wasn’t until I started visiting Ireland in the early 90s, (in the middle of winter usually when I would be the sole musical tourist), I had the opportunity to observe Irish people having sessions on their own terms. I did come across one or two sessions that were much like our free-for-alls back in SF, but for the most part sessions weren’t something you can just barge in on. It’s not like if you did you would be tar & feathered and run out of town… it was the subtleties of how you would be received. If I asserted myself in the usual way I noticed the local players had some trepidation about my arrival… it wasn’t very comfortable. But I was intimidated by the high caliber of musicianship and I sat listening unless they asked me to play something… or the rare tune I knew would come up. This was different from my usual MO because back home in SF it seemed ok to noodle and try to learn tunes on the fly. But at these sessions in Ireland no one was doing that… so I would just sit and listen… which in itself was fantastic being so close to the music, but still different than my usual MO.

On that first visit to Ireland one of the places I went was Galway and I ran into one of the musicians on the street who was playing at a local session I was sitting in at with my instrument on my lap most of the time. He told me I’m not like the other Yanks that come through and he proceeded to describe with distain my usual MO. This was a profound awakening for me… I realized my usual MO wasn’t going to win me any friends at sessions in Ireland. After that chance encounter I kept doing what I had learned from Irish sessions (in Ireland) and it really seemed to work in my favor. I started asking to join in when I approached sessions and discovered it actually enhanced how welcoming they were… I was never told I couldn’t join in. That chance encounter on the street ended up being very fortuitous. I returned to Ireland several times in the years after that and my new MO made my visits to sessions very enjoyable. When I’m back in SF I have kept some of what I learned in my MO… but it’s not Ireland so…

Anywho, if you want too poo poo the idea of asking to join sessions across the board… go ahead… but you might might find it awkward when you’re traveling around in uncharted territories.

Re: Asking to Join a Session

i agree that one approach doesn’t fit all sessions. at first i was confused at the idea of asking to join a session, but then i realized that i am just so used to playing with people i know and when i don’t know anyone it is just an automatic response to ask if i can join. minimally, i ask if a seat is taken, as i have often found people like to save seats for their friends who haven’t arrived.

when i don’t know anyone, i try to suss out what sort of session it is. i explicitly ask if i can join when i think it might be a closed session or if it is all very established players. i sit down and play conservatively at first, and don’t start any tunes until i am asked. taking this approach, i have been able to sit in on closed sessions and even sessions that were actually gigs, replete with microphones, intermissions, and audience requests. on rare occasions, i have even kept my mouth shut and listened as an audience member instead.

i have even asked to join a session and been told that i could not. this even happened to me several times at a session that i frequented for years. rather than get offended that on a given week i was asked to sit out for part or all of the session, i was gracious that they were honest with me, and sat down to enjoy the music instead. years later i actually still play with these same people, and once was even paid to lead their session while they were away. so, it (literally) pays to be polite! the request to sit out was never personal, of course, as they were practicing for high-profile gigs and i would not have known their set. i never minded, anyways, because i got to listen to music for free that would cost me $25 to listen to the next week, :-P.

Re: Asking to Join a Session

Sometimes you can be fairly sure of the situation - at a festival with a load of people playing away, hardly a gap between sets you know it’s fine just to join in.

Go into a pub where you’ve never been before, perhaps on an ordinary weekday, and there are four or five people playing. Common sense and courtesy surely should tell anyone that they should listen, then ask. Those people may have played together every week for years. It would be incredibly rude just to join in without asking.

Sitting or standing nearby with an instrument case while you have a listen is often going to be revealing as you see if there’s any response.

PB mentions different MO in his OP! I’d suggest it’s a matter of “manners” as well. I’d suggest that in Britain and Ireland a slightly elliptical approach will often do you credit. If you sit down with your instrument case near the session players, it’ll often be appreciated if you don’t jump straight in with “can I join in,” but say something about their playing, ask how long the session has been going or something. Asking the name of a tune you didn’t recognise comes pretty close to “may I join in!” It’s a pretty stony response if that doesn’t at least lead to a question about your own playing!

It does make a difference what instrument you play of course, but I’ll say no more on that!

Re: Asking to Join a Session

Why would anyone want to join a session without asking? That’s like walking into a resteraunt and sitting down uninvited at the table with a group of strangers.

If you just sit down and start playing without saying a word, the chances are you’ll play all evening without saying a word. But if you ask to join the group and introduce yourself, then you’ve broken the ice and started the conversation. The chances are you’ll go home having made a bunch of new friends.

The only situation I can think of where it might be alright to play without asking are the big ‘free for all’ sessions that happen around festivals. But if you are visiting an established local session anywhere, I can’t think of any reason for not asking.

Re: Asking to Join a Session

The last two comments are good. At festivals, people will wander about and just join sessions as they find them and then, perhaps, moving on to another if one doesn’t suit them.

However, even then, it’s sometimes better to ask rather than just sit down. Also, there are lots of pests at festivals too and it’s good to bear in mind that most good or half decent players will have already met or played together on some previous occasion. So, what appears to a bystander as someone just “sitting down” or joining a session is not quite as simple as that.

Tom B-R has hinted at this but it might even be better sometimes to “Wait To be Asked or invited” rather than ask or try to invite yourself; i.e. if you enter the pub or happen to be sitting nearby with an instrument. At least you know you are welcome.
Sometimes, of course, this can put you in a position too and it might even be prudent, on occasion, to respectfully decline an invitation if you feel you aren’t quite at the right level or the repertoire doesn’t suit.

On the other hand, as the old saying goes “Shy Bairns don’t get” and sometimes players or musicians don’t always think to invite a visitor to sit down or just leave it to someone else. They might even assume that you will either ask or just join in anyway. It all depends on the session, of course.

However, on balance, I think the best approach is to ask politely. As has been said, you’ll seldom be refused unless it really is a “closed session”. There is the possibility though that you may still be there under sufferance and they are too polite to say no. However, the best way to win people over is also by being polite and showing good manners both in terms of conversation and as far as your musical input is concerned too.

Re: Asking to Join a Session

I think Phantom’s post is pretty well spot on from start to finish.

To illustrate how caution and manners can work in your favour: 20 years ago I was listening to two well-known musicians playing in a pub (_the_ pub?) in Spiddal, blistering music on fiddle and flute. I had every intention of leaving my fiddle where it was - in the car - but after some time three more musicians turned up, one of whom I recognized, and were invited to join in. The repertoire became more accessible, temptation got too strong and I asked the boss fiddle player if I could get my fiddle and sit in. He hesitated, caught between wariness and politeness, so I smiled and promised “I won’t annoy you” and that did the trick.

I joined in, didn’t put myself forward and enjoyed great tunes and crack. I knew I had lived up to my promise when after an hour or so yer man bought me a pint!

Meanwhile two German-speaking onlookers standing behind me betrayed another point on which outsiders sometimes fail to grasp what is going on in an intimate session in Ireland. Doubtless assuming nobody would understand, one complained to the other that the musicians were doing too much talking amongst themselves and not enough playing!

The only time I ever sat in a session without first asking permission was at Phantom Button’s own session. I was in town for business, travelling without my fiddle, and having a free night, I contacted PB to find out if there was any music to be had for the listening. He responded that there was indeed a good session scheduled for that very evening. So after dinner and drinks with my work colleagues, I ditched ‘em and took a cab across town and sat at the bar enjoying the tunes and atmosphere. The music was quite good and a pleasure to listen to. At a break in the music, I introduced myself—it’s always a treat to finally meet someone that you know from cyberspace. We chatted briefly, and PB returned to his seat at the session, and I turned back to the bar, where the bartender introduced me to Redbreast, which I’d never made the acquaintance of before, either. Half an hour later I felt a presence looming at my shoulder and turned around to see PB standing there, holding a fiddle. He invited me to sit in. I told him I was quite content to sit and listen—they sounded grand. I’d had a few drinks as well, and the booze does tend to go to my fingers. PB insisted, to the point that I felt it would be ungracious not to accept. So I sat with them for the rest of the evening, joining in on only a handful of tunes and trying not to wreck them. But I met some really nice folks and got to hear some high caliber playing up close. At the end of the evening PB gave me a lift back to my hotel as well and was kind enough to give me an open invitation to return any time. Quite the host, our man Jack. Cheers!
