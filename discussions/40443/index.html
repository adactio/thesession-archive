Re: warmer/richer sounding strings?

Yes, the lack of projection and low frequencies you describe is generally a feature of 7/8 violins, due to the smaller body size. However there are usually things you can do to make the sound a bit more to your taste. It’s hard to recommend alternative strings when you don’t mention what you’ve got on it at the moment. You will want to stay away from steel cored strings, but amongst the synthetics if you want it to project more you need a higher tension, if you want to sweeten the tone you need lower tension, you can’t do both. Most popular strings are made in two or three different tensions, although finding a shop that stocks anything other than ‘medium’ can be difficult, you’ll probably have to go online. If you don’t know what strings are on it at the moment I would suggest starting with medium Dominants and then decide how you want to change the sound from that when you get the next set.

There are also things a luthier can do to the soundpost and bridge to change the sound, and which are usually more effective than just changing the strings, but again if he adjusts it to project more the tone will get harsher, if he sweetens the tone you’ll lose volume and projection, you can’t have it both ways.

Re: warmer/richer sounding strings?

Going to jump in and second what Mark M has said - speaking to an expert is never a bad thing, and they’ll be able to suggest things that the rest of us would probably never think of. One guy that I went to see repositioned my bridge, and then sanded an infinitesimally tiny corner off one side of it, and instantly improved the sound of the fiddle. I can only assume that this was some sort of witchcraft.

Do remember, though, that these things will cost. Anything that requires the fiddle to be fundamentally altered is going to be time-consuming, and therefore expensive. Take advice, but don’t feel pressured to jump at the most expensive route to altering the sound of your fiddle.

Short of that sort of intervention, there are a number of things to try. Strings obviously make a big difference, but I’m going to come right out and say this - I would not put Dominant strings on a fiddle if my life depended on it, and I would NEVER recommend them. Seriously, I cannot stress how bad these strings are… they last no time at all, and they snap without warning. A ‘G’ string nearly took my eye out (no pun intended). Yes, they give a warm sound. But for what they are, and the rate at which they ‘go off’, in my opinion, they simply aren’t worth the money or the risk.

That being said, synthetic core strings will tend to get a… less bright/woolier tone that a lot of people seem to like for a ‘trad’ sound. (Not me - strictly steel core, but then I have a French fiddle that likes the bright tone those give, and sounds like it’s made of cardboard if I put synthetics on.) Again, speak to someone who knows their stuff and they’ll advise you on the best fit for 1) the instrument you’re playing, 2) the sound you want to produce, and 3) your budget. Find some good ones and you could be on to a winner. Mark is absolutely correct about the trade-off between mellow tone and overall volume. The lower the tension of the string, the lower the volume.

De-tuning is an obvious way to alter the tone… but it’s hard to persuade a whole session to join you in A=415. More’s the pity, everything sounds lovely half a tone down. Again, that’s a way of lowering the tension by reducing the pitch, and it’ll cost some volume.

Whenever you put new strings on, it’s worth retaining the little plastic/rubber collar that comes with them. You know the thing - it’s supposed to stop the string digging into the bridge. Anyway, that can have a very slight dampening effect on the string, but I doubt it’s significant enough to alter the sound in the way that you’re talking about. Also, if the previous owner hasn’t used those aforementioned little plastic/rubber doohickies, and the string HAS cut through the bridge, that can lead to a tinny tone. Worth a look. Actually, the bridge itself is worth checking too, and can be a very cost effective way of altering the tone - lowering the height of the bridge lowers the tension on the face of the fiddle, and can thus mellow the tone.

Re: warmer/richer sounding strings?

Only joking.

Right, so… once upon a time the pitch of an ‘A’ was 415 Hz. (a shade more than a semitone lower than it is today). Concert pitch has been migrating upwards towards (and occasionally beyond) the A=440 that we have accepted as the standard today. There are numerous theories as to why this should be, some of which (I kid you not) are conspiracy theories about Nazis. Seriously.

For what it’s worth, I reckon that a number of factors are involved. The transition from wood to metal instruments (particularly flutes). The transition from wood-lined concert halls to buildings with plastered walls (which resonate at a different pitch). The need for greater volume as audience sizes increased in music halls.

In reality, it’s hard to say why concert pitch has had this upward drift, or why it should have stopped at A=440 in the last century or so. But why was it ever A=415?

Here’s something every fiddle player should try. Hold the fiddle against your breastbone. Play an open ‘D’. Now gradually tune the ‘D’ down until - get this - the sternum begins to vibrate. That’s right. If you tune your fiddle to ‘old’ concert pitch, it resonates with your skeleton. Once you’ve retuned the rest of the fiddle to be in tune with that ‘D’ it unlocks a whole new booming set of harmonic resonances that simply aren’t available at conventional concert pitch.

It must be something on a cellular or even molecular level, because most ‘natural’ sounds are in A=415… birdsong, for example. Even the sympathetic harmonics of most timbers are, too. So when you de-tune a fiddle a semitone or so, in a way, it brings it ‘home’…

Re: warmer/richer sounding strings?

I’m afraid the whole A440 vs A415 thing is much, much more complicated than has been previously posted. First of all, there is no evidence that I am aware of that “natural sounds” are in A415, whatever that statement is supposed to mean. If you have any I would love to see it.

But more importantly, while A415 is used as a convenient modern alternative to A440 when it comes to music from a few centuries back, the actual concert pitches used varied widely up until rather recently. We know this because of certain fixed-pitch instruments like organs and items like tuning forks. They could go anywhere from the 300s to the mid 400s, with no real rhyme or reason. A440 came about as a way to standardize this variation. A415 and its cousin A392 came about as alternatives precisely because they correspond fairly easily to G# and G in A440. In reality, tuning before the 19th century was highly dependent on location and many other factors.

Any flute player who has tried to buy an antique flute will tell you that they can be in a wide variety of pitch centers, and it’s customary to include “plays well at A440” in a listing of an older flute to denote which ones can be used at a session today. Terry McGee has an interesting study of older flutes here: http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/eng_pitch.html . It’ll give you a sense of the various pitches in use, which fluctuated between the 420s and the 450s in the 200 years he’s looking at.

And no, we’re still not quite decided on A440, as most major orchestras today tune to slightly above, sometimes as high as A445. Maybe we’ll keep going up, or maybe we’ll all decide to tune lower. I know I’d save money on mandolin strings if everyone decided to play at A415 or lower!

I do work experience restoring violins with a luthier.

You need to take it to a violin maker/restorer.

Its too complex and strings are just one variable.

Bridge position dimensions, setup, tail piece weight/material, sound post position/quality of sound post/minute changes at foot head of sound post, (a really good luthier can do a lot with a sound post setup), fingerboard, tuning pegs or internal inspection of the instrument can all determine many things about the sound.

You can try a half dozen different types of strings, research a luthier that has a reputation for knowing his/her stuff.

There are more radical things like setting a new bass bar/some restoration work/design repairs but you will be in excess of 250 euros for this.
Some German violins have upper and lower tables made from baked wood which produces a poor sound in certain ways eg: lack of resonance, a floor to depth of sound.

Also take a non commercial if possible recording of an instrument you like/other fiddle player as an example of what you want to achieve with the sound. It may not be possible but sometimes it helps describe what you’re trying to achieve.

Veering off topic in pursuit of physics…

@bigsciota: “the whole A440 vs A415 thing is much, much more complicated than has been previously posted”

Yes indeed. Please note that I’m not suggesting people should tune to A415, and I’m well aware that the Nazi thing is an easily-debunked internet conspiracy theory. I’m also aware that the history of concert pitch standardisation is far more complex than I’m presenting it. Nevertheless…

What I’d encourage any fiddle player (sorry all you fixed-reed untunables out there, but what the heck are you reading a fiddle string discussion for anyway?) to try is to tune the instrument to your body. Try de-tuning the D until it resonates with your chest wall. Is there any evidence for this? Oh, yes. A number of studies are referenced on this physics discussion: https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/human-body-resonant-frequencies.501607/

Now, the idea goes like this… any frequency resonates with exact multiples of itself. So if the chest wall begins to resonate at 30Hz, and an open ‘D’ (according to concert pitch) is at 293.665 Hz (according to Wikipedia), and you then de-tune the ‘D’ a semitone, it’s at 277.183 Hz. De-tune a shade more, you’re getting awfully close to 270 Hz, which is an exact multiple of 30Hz. (Actually, multiples of 30Hz give B, F, D, basically a Bminor chord). I’d love to actually test this out, but I’m pretty sure that the pitch that starts to make your sternum vibrate must be around that 270 Hz mark.

Anyway. Try it out. See what you think. I’m not suggesting that A=415 should be any sort of ‘standard’ - just that there are natural resonances that are more or less sympathetic with the human body. Give it a go. See if it sounds more or less ‘resonant’, and if it changes the way you think of the ‘sound’ of your own instrument.

(The birdsong thing is actually a reference to an article I read a while ago that suggested urban birdsong has undergone a pronounced upward shift in frequency to prevent it being masked by background noise; even in the natural world, upward frequency shift seems to be a strategy for combating ambient noise disruption. Rural birds, without these pressures, choose to sing at naturally resonant frequencies depending on their habitat. Might be interesting to know if particular trees resonated at similar frequencies…)

Last word on sternums… I’ve provided a link above to a physics forum where a number of articles are referenced discussing sympathetic frequencies of various body parts. And yes, there are variables, and yes this is imprecise science, which is why I’m not claiming that EVERY sternum vibrates at EXACTLY 270 Hz… that’s daft. However… since resonance isn’t solely reliant on size, but rather on the density and structure of the material, it shouldn’t surprise you that average/normal bone densities should be relatively similar. Lots of people of differing heights - but it’s hard to tell that when everyone is seated. So sternum size isn’t likely to be THAT much different, certainly not enough to significantly alter the range of sympathetic frequencies at which they vibrate. That would also be daft - Imagine, ”Oh, I can’t play a violin, I’m naturally tuned to C because I’m so tall"… ridiculous. Anywho, that’s all by-the-by, and it isn’t really the point I was trying to make.

Remember, I’m not saying ‘tune your D to 270 and see if it makes your bones wobble’. All I’m saying is - experiment: try de-tuning TO the point where the D resonates against your chest, and then see what the rest of the fiddle sounds like at that pitch, because I’m guessing you’ll like it. :-)
