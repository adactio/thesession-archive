Banjo Upgrade?

Hi folks, long time lurker first time poster. I currently own a middle of the road Koda banjo, bought for about 350 euro new.
It’s been set up by a good luthier, and in my opinion plays and sounds ‘pretty good’. My technique is also ‘pretty good’, I don’t struggle with getting a solid sound out of the banjo.

I’ve recently decided to sell some old music gear and am considering purchasing a new banjo with the money.

I’ve been to a few shops that would sell pretty good mid/high end models (mostly Clareen). However no matter which model I try I find that while the sound is good, I’m not blown away enough to justify the price tag (usually north of a grand, if not two).

My question is, what should I be looking at in a new banjo? What about a Clareen or Epiphone should I be paying attention to that isn’t part of the sound/feel of the Koda?

Re: Banjo Upgrade?

Welcome to the forums, Sean! I tend to agree with the others, vintage banjos can be the way to go, especially if you’re not in a hurry. The pandemic seemed to roil the vintage banjo market a bit. I think a lot of people took up things like banjo during lockdowns, and banjos got a bit more scarce for a while. Then we saw a surge of those instruments being sold again. But if you’ve got no need for an upgrade *right now*, you have the luxury of playing other instruments until you find something that speaks to you!

I am a bit of a banjo collector, and I also buy a lot of vintage instruments and fix them up so that I can lend/sell them to my students. I will tell you this, though. I currently have three high end banjos, including a very nice Clareen. But my go to banjo is a vintage one made in the 1930’s.

So I would say that you should try different banjos when you see them in music stores, pawn shops, or estate sales, and when you run into new banjo players in sessions, swap instruments so you can see what other banjos you really like. If you find something you can’t live without, then you have a mission!

I have to agree with several others…hunting down a nice vintage tenor (especially US makers from the mid-1920’s) is worth the effort. Sounds like you may be in Europe? I’m less familiar with what you may find in shops. That may slow or limit your search.

I’ve had a couple of Vegas over the years one of which I played in sessions for thirty years which I traded recently for a short scale, larger pot model.

Vegas are pretty commonly available and often in good (playable) condition in the states. I’ve had positive results with eBay choices and eventual purchases too though international shipping is pricey.

Another less common maker but one I have come to appreciate much more lately is Weymann. A professional luthier/repair man who is also a killer banjo player and whom I highly respect hard sold it to me…”Weymann’s are the best!” If Luke says it’s the best he is almost always right.

I was skeptical at first given my long favor of Vegas but the Weymann he talked me into has gotten compliments from almost everyone who plays it or hears it. Every time I pick it up I have to agree. It’s got that something special. It needed some restoration to the unique Weymann pop on resonator that is still in progress in my shop but even without the reso it is exceptional. I got it for $500 USD. Most Vegas I see these days in good playable condition are selling around $1K- $1.5K USD.

But with banjos especially set up is everything. Getting the advice of an experienced banjo luthier may be your best bet as well as a source for leads on available instruments.

Good luck…and maybe check out some of those old fuse boxes!

Re: Banjo Upgrade?

“…what should I actually expect to sound different between a €350 banjo and, say, a €2000 one?”

That’s a difficult question to answer for a number of reasons. First off, the setup of a banjo may have a bigger effect on the tone than just about anything else. The tighter the head, the more high end you will get, but often times on lower quality banjos, if you tighten the head enough to get a nice bright tone (if that’s what you’re looking for) can often make the instrument sound weak and tinny. But other setup changes can make a big difference too, including head material, string materials and tensions, and tailpiece.

Different banjos have different tone systems, and they can sound somewhat unique. The tone system is most importantly the tone ring style (which can range from just having the head stretched over the wood ring, to having a simple rolled metal ring, to the more complex systems, like the Silver Bell tone ring, Vega’s Tubaphone, Whyte Laydie, and Little Wonder tone rings, to the Gibson Mastertone system, etc.), but other things like having a resonator or not, the shape of the sound chamber, and what kind of flange it has, can be part of the tone system. For instance, my Paragon has a concave, arched resonator, which spreads the sound in the sound chamber. And many of the Weymann banjos have the “Megaphonic” tone system, where the rim itself is a bit conical…

In general the heavier the banjo, the more depth of tone and sustain it can have. What higher end banjo makers are generally shooting for is the ability to have a complex and interesting ring to the sound, with good dynamics (allowing good tone whether you’re playing quietly or loudly), without having to stretch the head too tight.

So you might have a better tone ring, as well as better geared tuners, and a better tailpiece (which can have more effect on tone than you might think) on a more expensive banjo…

Above and beyond that, a lot of what you pay for is going to be cosmetic. For instance, a $1000 B&D Silver Bell #1 banjo is going to have the same tone system and the ability to sound every bit as good as a $4000 B&D Silver Bell #6. But it’s going to have simpler inlay, no carved heel, plain nickel hardware, instead of brass-plated and engraved metal work, and it’s going to have a lot fewer rhinestones! ;-)

But again, a good setup can be like getting a new banjo! So that’s where I always start. One of the nice things about banjos is that you can adjust stuff yourself without much worry about damaging the instrument, and you generally only need a wrench and a screwdriver. So if you’re at all into tinkering, try adjusting your head tension and see what it does to the tone. For relatively little money, you can try out different head materials, different string materials and gauges, and even different tailpieces or bridges. If you find yourself tinkering with banjo heads, it might be worth picking up a Drum Dial, which is a gauge that you can use to monitor your head tension, and more importantly, make sure that it’s even all the way around.

But having said all of that, you’re still down to trying out different banjos and finding something that really speaks to you. (While keeping in mind that you could still tweak what any banjo sounds like with a different setup).
