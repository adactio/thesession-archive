Irish hammered dulcimers and the pantalon

The blog post shared below discusses the hammered dulcimer in Ireland. It cites historical sources that date its advent a fewer decades earlier than is widely believed, and considers subsequent German influence on its further development. It makes note of a newspaper advertisement for a concert in England, in 1772, where no lesser a figure than Papa Noël participated on an exceptionally large form of the instrument that can possibly also have found its way to Dublin.

The jacket notes for John Rea’s solo album (linked to below) say this about his dulcimer:

“John was the youngest of ten children, and all the six surviving boys were taught to play the fiddle by their father. In 1924, however, he had a dulcimer built for John and that became his instrument. John’s older brother, Alex, was a carpenter and the father arranged for him to take the plan from one owned by James Davidson of Buckna, a village between Glenarm and Ballymena. Another brother, Willie, learned it and he passed on his knowledge to John. It is that same instrument that he plays today.”

This says nothing about the configuration of his dulcimer but does lead to an expectation that it might have been idiosyncratic. It also demonstrates a continuity between Rea and the preceding generation of players.

Re: Irish hammered dulcimers and the pantalon

At least here in the USA both sorts of Appalachian dulcimer, the lap and the hammer, took off in the Folk Music Revival of the 1950s and 1960s and got mixed up with Irish music due to the same folkies dabbling in Old Time and Irish trad. Many of these folkies’ only source for Irish tunes was early Chieftains albums- I’ve seen a number of these bands over the years where EVERY Irish tune they played, and the settings of them, were obviously derived from them- and hearing their familiar hammer dulcimer imagined it was a mainstream Irish trad instrument. What none seemed to realise was that they were hearing a Hungarian instrument.

There’s a superb book ‘Pre-Revival Appalachian Dulcimers’ which traces traditional regions and styles of the lap dulcimer (or dulcimore) in Appalachia and makes clear its 17th and 18th century provenance with German settlers in neighbouring Pennsylvania.

What I don’t know is if anyone has done similar research into the hammer dulcimer of Appalachia. Seems that German provenance is clear in any case.

With this being my experience it comes as a surprise that hammer dulcimers existed in Irish trad prior to the Folk Music Revival. Are they a truly traditional ancient survival? Were they an Eastern European instrument connected to travelling people? I wouldn’t imagine there would be a large German immigrant community in Ireland, as there was here (we have areas where German has been the first language for 300 years.)

Re: Irish hammered dulcimers and the pantalon

I found the answer to my own question about whether or not there was a full break between the long-standing hammered dulcimer tradition in Ireland, and the instrument’s subsequent reemergence. Two of John Rea’s younger relatives carried the instrument directly into the 21st century, and were honored at something of a melting-pot event described in the video linked to below.

As to the documents attesting the presence of the hammered dulcimer in early-18th-century Ireland, many indicate a Jewish nexus. This is primarily by identifying renowned German musicians who carried the instrument northward, as such. Additionally, the earliest of the Irish players to appear on the written record, Archibald Williamson, refers to himself as someone “whom gentlemen are pleas’d to call the Irish Jew.”

Re: Irish hammered dulcimers and the pantalon

The hammered dulcimer is an ancient instrument which is found widely throughout the world, including China, India, the Middle East and many parts of Europe. It was not just a folk instrument, there are illustrations of it being played by the gentry and middle classes. The advertisements offering lessons referred to in the article would have been aimed at them, not the ordinary folk.

The linked article says “These references instantiate the dulcimer in Dublin well before the middle of the 18th century”, but there is no reason to think this is when the instrument arrived in Ireland. It was found in England going back to at least mediaeval times, and it seems to me very possible that the instrument’s presence in Ireland goes back at least as far - why should it not, given the close relationship between the two?

The panthaleon certainly seems to have been an interesting development, but it does not seem to have been widely taken up, as despite Mr Noelli’s efforts double-sided dulcimers are not widely found. The idea that the other “improved dulcimers” were based on the panthaleon is pure supposition, and could equally well refer to other developments to allow more varied playing, such as the offset bridges still found in eastern England.

As for the hammered dulcimer’s arrival in North America, contrary to what Richard D Cook appears to be saying it is not a “Hungarian instrument” and neither is it particularly German, although versions are found in both countries. It was found widely throughout Europe, and it seems likely that immigrants brought their instruments with them from a number of sources, including England and possibly Ireland. There was a strong tradition of dulcimer playing in eastern England well into the 20th century and which continues today, and this where many of the Mayflower settlers came from.

Re: Irish hammered dulcimers and the pantalon

“The panthaleon certainly seems to have been an interesting development, but it does not seem to have been widely taken up, as despite Mr Noelli’s efforts double-sided dulcimers are not widely found.”

There are numerous accounts both of the high regard in which the pantalon came to be held in the 18th century, and the significant extent of its use. This indeed does make it quite puzzling that no such instrument appears to have survived. The earliest description of it as a double-sided dulcimer is dated to 1730.

Noëlli was three years old at that time and took his place on the roster of named specialists well after the pantalon had become established in the country of its invention (Germany). He does appear to have been the one who brought it to London – in 1767. This was six months before Isaac Isaacs took his own “improved dulcimer” from London to Dublin.

This strikes me as justifying speculation about the possibility of the pantalon having in some way influenced the exported form of Isaacs’s innovation. Either way, the blog post clearly notes that “there is no way of knowing the extent to which it might have inspired his ‘new improved dulcimer,’ if at all.”

Whatever the initial success of the pantalon, it appears to have been a dead end. Perhaps it was too difficult for all but virtuosi to play. Perhaps other innovations were able to achieve similar results more easily - Mr Dowling was obviously confident his “new improved dulcimer” could hold its own when he issued his challenge to Mr Isaac. It may simply have been that the dulcimer fell out of fashion - this was the start of the Classical era which marked a significant change in the way music was composed - and became simply a folk instrument, which would favour versions which were simpler to play, and perhaps easier and cheaper to build.

It’s an interesting article, but it is also clear that the dulcimer was already an established instrument in Ireland before the pantalon was introduced, and I don’t think it sheds any light on how the dulcimer reached Ireland in the first place, neither is there anything to suggest German influence except for this very specific version of the instrument.

Re: Irish hammered dulcimers and the pantalon

The blog post was in no way intended, nor does it claim, to provide a comprehensive review of any aspect of the hammered dulcimer. It keys a chronology to dated reference works and notices appearing in local newspapers without alleging to provide an exhaustive listing. It says nothing about the initial arrival of the instrument in Ireland for the simple reason that nothing specific is known about it. Surmising that the hammered dulcimer was in established use there before goods and services related to it were advertised in the daily press is reasonable enough. The difference is that the dates on published documents are matters of quantified fact, not opinion.

The amount of contemporaneous material published about the pantalon far exceeds what is noted in the blog post. A salient aspect of that excess is condensed to the single observation that, “the refinement of a hammer-struck zither also weighed into the early development of the fortepiano (which some writers synonymously called a pantalon).” That relationship has been discussed in scholarly contexts that began while the “true pantalon” was still in active use, alongside keyboarded implementations of hammer-based devices. This type of pantalon survives in surprising numbers and variations in museum collections. Modern researchers have argued that they have been incorrectly conflated with pianos.

The manually struck pantalon did ultimately cede its musical niche to single-sided hammered dulcimers. A question remains about the extent to whether it did so without leaving a trace. It has been argued that the Hungarian cimbalom is its rightful successor, possessing astonishing virtuoso capacity in both classical and traditional contexts. Its pedal-operated damping device (also found in other designs) appreciably alters the tonal quality and sustain of the metal strings. This emulates the gut-strung register of the pantalon, adding the ability to shift nimbly between it and the metal-strung register.

Although stepping perhaps a bit far outside the scope of the present forum, for the sake of anyone unfamiliar with it, here is a demonstration of the largest of the present-day European hammered dulcimers as used in Romania.

https://youtu.be/fA0ogXBdKOc
