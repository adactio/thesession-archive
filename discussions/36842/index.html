Subconscience playing/practice

Yesterday when I was practicing, I noticed I was tensing up and actually the playing started to hurt a bit. I play in front of my computer, so I decided to multitask a bit, put on my favourite tv show (with headphones) and I would start playing again when my arms felt ok.

When I was actively watching the TV show I started playing again, I noticed three things:
1. I don’t tense up while playing - even though I was playing for about two hours.
2. I seem to make less mistakes
3. I seem to memorize tunes that I didn’t think I had memorized

An example of 3, I was just playing a tune, only after the tune ended I started thinking about which tune I actually just played and it turns out it was the tune I was working on.
So what I’m wondering here, do other people have this? Is this an effective way to practice? And is this actually a state I should aim to get at when playing with others?

Re: Subconscience playing/practice

I used to play in front of the TV like that (much to the chagrin of my wife). I think it can help with a couple of different things:

Most notably, it can help with developing the kinesthetic “memory” of where the notes fall on the instrument. It can also help a bit with cementing a core setting of a tune into your memory (But so can running the tune through your head without an instrument - the “ear worm” syndrome). I very specifically do NOT like to think of learning tunes as “memorization”. That implies learning something by rote and being able to regurgitate it, and that is not what playing this music is about. You need to do what I call “internalizing” the tune, where the flow and contour are what is cemented in your memory, but the actual notes that you play and how you play them are open to your musical expression.

A lot of people rely upon kinesthetic memory to “remember” how a tune goes. That can be a useful step when you’re first learning the music and your instrument, but many people rely much too heavily on it, and end up playing the tunes exactly the same every time. Not only is that boring, but it’s also not all that “musical”.

So you can let your “subconscious” playing help you build familiarity with the instrument, and familiarity with tunes. But don’t rely on it as your primary mode of practice. You should really vary your practice. Sometimes practice by learning new tunes. Sometimes, consciously work specifically on technique - whether it is working on rolls, triplets, etc., or working on playing tunes with different amounts of lilt and swing, etc. Sometimes practice playing tunes you know well at different speeds. Practice relaxing while you play. Practice playing the same tune over and over and over while trying to give it different expression each time, until you’ve done everything you can think of, and then keep playing it and see what new stuff might appear…

Re: Subconscience playing/practice

And to address your last question a bit more directly, it’s difficult for me to put my finger on the mental state that I am in when playing with other people. It varies, I suppose, depending on my comfort level with the other players.

Yes, by all means, you need to be relaxed, but you also need to be focused on the flow of the music. I certainly don’t let myself get too thoughtful while playing (like I might be when I’m practicing). Like Cheeky Elf says, you have to stay out of your own way. If your brain is busy thinking about how you’re going to pull off a triplet in that tricky spot in the tune, or what variation you want to try, you’re not focused on the flow of the music, and your playing will likely suffer.

So my suggestion would be to learn from your relaxed state, but don’t let your focus be elsewhere too much while you’re playing with other people.

Re: Subconscience playing/practice

I agree with all above. Correcting something that’s wrong (whether it’s technique or the notes they’re playing) should be a priority so students don’t unwittingly end up practicing (and polishing) their mistakes!

And distractions can work really well. In conventional music (let’s say with fast passages in one hand and slow passages against them in the other hand), students tend to get more concerned about the difficult, fast stuff, unwittingly sabotaging their playing by thinking it’s “difficult”. I get them to look at the slower progressions that are happening within the music and focus on those. Or focus on dynamics - or phrasing. Basically, whatever gets them to see how the music is working and the larger things going on within it.

Re: Subconscience playing/practice

Neuroscience is a wonderful thing. The more one understands the way the brain works the better one is at using those principals to learn and perform. As always, there are always a few outliers. Some very few people can, say, “see” sounds or “hear” colors. I’d be dumbfounded to personally any of them. Some very few people can really multitask. Again, I don’t know any of them either and I know a LOT of people. So let’s just say that my gross generalizations apply to those of us that live under every point that isn’t in the tiny ends of that bell-shaped curve.

In order to learn, or perform, the brain must be actively engaged and the period of active engagement is remarkably short, give or take 90 seconds. after that the brain wanders and performance wanes. You can force the issue but the returns are greatly diminished. Here’s an example. Think of ONE thing:

That’s hard to do ain’t it! Give the brain something new to do, to focus on. You get to decide what that new thing is and how much time should pass before you come back to the original idea. For instance you could play an “A” diatonic scale, change to “A” by thirds, play each note in long tones, octave skips, each for 90 seconds then repeat the whole thing a few more times and be way ahead of where you’d be if you tried to focus on one thing for 10 minutes each. I can bore you to tears with examples of how this works. You can also trust the brain to “internalize” what the brain “learned” by working below the conscious level. That’s why that thing you worked on yesterday is often easier today. If it isn’t….you likely weren’t as focused yesterday as you thought. (Don’t lose any sleep over that…it happens to all of us!) In short…90 seconds then a change-up. Ever notice that 3 times through the majority of tunes takes about 90 seconds then we start a new tune? Hmmmm. Maybe we’re hard-wired for that.

A second point. Our brains don’t always choose (or even often choose) what we focus on. Practicing when we’re tired or distracted is for most of us (that bell-shaped curve thing again) a non-starter. I can’t think of anyone who would say “well I have a performance (of any kind) tomorrow so I guess I should stay up really late, get up early, and push myself hard to get ready”. Fact is, when it’s tired the brain just can’t stay focused on any one thing well enough to learn it and it doesn’t get to choose what it does focus on and what it gets distracted by. The brain wants what the brain wants and the tired brain want rest. Same thing applies to Chemistry. While a little whisky (my favorite) can create a more relaxed state (always a good thing) it’s a short trip to “too far” and almost no-one is their own best judge.

Some people get advance degrees in Neuroscience (not me, but some people and I’m smart enough to hear what they say) and can speak to how our brains work best. In short, to get the best bang-for-your-buck stay focused, eliminate distractions, rest, relax, practice “smart”. Then you can trust your brain to internalize what you learned actively, solve the problems, and work out the bugs. When you put them together you can reach the “zone” in your playing where it all comes together. May that be where you are in all things.

Re: Subconscience playing/practice

One of my favorite authorities on practice technique is Noa Kageyama Ph.D who teaches at Julliard. In this article (http://www.bulletproofmusician.com/8-things-top-practicers-do-differently/) he points out that the number of incorrect iterations in practice is much more important than the correct ones according to studies. In addition being aware of the mistakes and correcting them as soon as possible was a technique that more successful practitioners use. The ratio of incorrect to correct iterations was the most important of the metrics.

This implies that practicing in a state of poor focus is not a good thing. This doesn’t address the issue of creativity which is separate but related in the sense that you are likely to be less creative if you don’t have the chops to begin with but this article (and several others of his) give good tips on how to practice. He also addresses the idea of “the zone” and how to improve the possibility of invoking that altered state in some of his other articles if you search through them.

I think you will find Dr. Kageyama’s insights interesting and fruitful.
