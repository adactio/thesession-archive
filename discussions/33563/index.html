Re: Frailing Banjo in Irish Music

It’s a long time since I played the banjo; but when I learned, claw-hammer concentrated mainly on single-string picking, with the back of the nail alternating from top string to a lower, and the thumb picking on the half-beat, and a hammer-on being included in just about every cycle, or sequence, if you see what I mean.
Frailing was more strummy, with the first stroke across all the strings, then a single string and the thumb, and no hammer-on except for variety in the body of the tune. There is a style now called ‘claw frailing’ or something like that, which tells me that the distinction is not as clear as it once was.
But, having said all that, I am no expert, and my only information came from books that I bought when I was a teenager (yes, I know -- I had banjo books under my bed).

Re: Frailing Banjo in Irish Music

I play three instruments, i.e. ITM fiddle, Scottish bellowspipes, and clawhammer banjo for Southern Mtn. music. I do not blur the lines.

Technically, the notes of ITM can be played clawhammer style. And I have even heard a couple of rare individuals who have sorted out jigs in clawhammer style. But, whether it can be done, and whether it should be done, are two different questions.

First, clawhammer players regularly use various tunings depending upon the key. This makes playing in IT sessions seriously problematic, as you will not be able to re-tune mid-set. The other musicians will not want to adapt to your need to re-tune, nor should they have to.

Second, purely melodic clawhammer takes away the strength of the instrument and style, i.e. the ring and rhythm of the 5th string. Why play, if you’re not using the very technique that makes Southern Mtn. clawhammer so lovely?

Third, clawhammer-style ornaments are problematic. A very, very few folks can do a pretty fair triple, but it’s difficult, and even then the triple lacks the punch and feel of authenticity.

Fourth, clawhammer is doable, but most would feel unacceptable, as a back-up instrument. It makes for an interesting, but inauthentic, sound. Anyone playing clawhammer back-up in a serious ITM session would be left watching the other musicians stop playing and heading to the bar very quickly.

I’m not suggesting that it not be attempted. But for those serious about ITM, on many levels clawhammer doesn’t work very well. Working out Irish or Scottish tunes for non-session purposes might be great fun and very rewarding, but you’d have to understand that for sessions it may not fly.

Tom Hanway and Ken Perlman are well known for making clawhammer work for Irish and Scottish tunes, and you should check out there work if my musings haven’t put you off entirely.

My clawhammer hero is Adam Hurt. Here’s of lovely youtube of him playing a gourd banjo:

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WP72CpcnJWY

Google his name any many more videos will pop up. He’s bloody wonderful.

Cheers.

Matt

Re: Frailing Banjo in Irish Music

Tom Hanway uses a systemthat is not clawhammer.
The problem with clawhammer is that traditionally it is a downward pick with drop thumb playing harmony notes, that does not mean you cannot do it, it means you will be creating a new style.
Personally, I don’t have a problem with that, I am in favour of innovation.
But some ITM players are extremely conservative,and might prefer a sound that is more reminiscent of the tenor banjo, or a finger picking style more like Hanways.
The disadvantage of clawhammer is that unless you are incorporating the ideas of melodic banjo [using lower strings for higher notes to achieve legato]with the downward pick there will always be a break between notes unless the thumb drops to play a harmony note and disguise the gap, that is why [I assume] Hanway uses a style that involves thumb of index finger to emulate up down motion for melody, alternatively a thumb pick can be used.
Here is another suggestion, the thumb melody old timey style, BUT with a thumb pick, this achieves up down motion of picking, but leaves fingers available to put in occasional drone on one[ high d] string, or even occasional forward roll for g chords, assuming the player is in dgbd.

Re: Frailing Banjo in Irish Music

Where I grew up, frailing and clawhammer were used pretty much interchangeably. Drop thumb technique was used to eke out extra melody notes to keep the shape of the melody more defined, if desired. Otherwise, just the general shape of the melody was suggested. The overall role of clawhammer banjo was to provide a rhythmic underscore to propel singing or fiddle. I love the sound. But I personally think it is like a very strong spice, to be used sparingly lest it dominate the overall flavor. To me, it doesn’t sound Irish at all.

Re: Frailing Banjo in Irish Music

“ likewise open G on the banjo, particularly for playing in C major, I also find open G really good for playing tunes in G, and DADGBD really good for playing in tunes in D, it takes very little time to tune the 5 th string up.”

But it still sounds like you’re working pretty hard to pound a square peg into a round hole.

Tuning the 5th string up and down quickly, countless times during a session, however quickly, will result in annoying the serious ITM players present, and will probably result in a broken string as well.

Again, no one is saying that it can’t be done. I enjoy, for example, playing a few Northumbrian pipe tunes on clawhammer banjo. But ITM clawhammer style in a session setting is not done quickly or easily. And you’re losing the special quality of the instrument. The various interesting tunings used in clawhammer make for magic. You lose that magic by staying in one tuning to attempt to fit in to a session. The notes might be there, but the strength of the instrument is lost. Twisting all about to play, for example, a D tune in standard G tuning may technically work, but the lovely quality inherent in playing a D tune in double D tuning is lost.

I know my view is perhaps rigid, but I think serious ITM should be played on serious ITM instruments. Why not simply have a tenor banjo handy when you’re wanting to sit in on a session?
Or, if you must play clawhammer, why not have a second or third 5-String, in appropriate tunings, to switch back and forth on? I used to play contra dances on clawhammer, and it made things far, far easier when I bought a cheaper second banjo and kept it in double D tuning.
