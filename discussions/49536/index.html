Leaders that don’t know how to follow

I’d like to discuss leaders of sessions that don’t have the ability to follow others. They can start tunes, but their ability to follow is choppy and they eventually passively aggressively commandeer the set. They play everything in a weird contra dance movement ( which is fine) but are unable to ramble like a true Connaughtman when the time calls.

Do you have advice for them? I will not change the tradition or lower my values.

Re: Leaders that don’t know how to follow

That sounds quite annoying! I would say the only real recourse is to make sure they understand your concern, and if they take it into consideration, maybe they’ll work to change. It’s possible that they don’t know that they do that, or that they feel it’s their duty or that they’re helping the session by doing it. But it’s also possible that making your concerns known will make you a pariah in the session.

In that case, the only other recourse would be to stop attending the session, and maybe start one of your own.

As a regular session leader, I certainly impart some of my own style on the session overall. But I try to be gracious and follow others when they are starting music. There are times that I will gently nudge the rhythms, when someone else starts a set, into something that sounds better to me, but I generally only do that when it is an inexperienced player whose nerves have gotten to them, or when it’s one of my students. I even get thanked for that sometimes, but I am always trying to be conscious of not stepping on any toes or egos.

But it really sounds to me like your only way of potentially dealing with that situation is to try to talk to the offending session leader about it, which is probably best to be done away from the session, in private.

Re: Leaders that don’t know how to follow

Following others—latching onto their pace, lift, swing (or lack thereof)—is a primary skill for any session musician. It’s fundamental to “playing well with others.”

This is as true for the anchor as it is for the rest of the players. It may be okay for the leader to help lift a less experienced musician’s feel for a tune, as Reverend mentions. But it’s not copasetic for a leader (or anyone else) to impose a choppy or off-kilter feel to an otherwise well-played set. I’ve sat in on a few sessions where the leader’s style was stiff and wooden—no lift, no lively pulse. It infected the whole circle. I chose not to go back to those sessions.

If you don’t enjoy the leader’s musicality, your options are to talk with them about it, or recruit the other musicians to depose the leader, or find or start another session more to your liking.

Several of us have answered this. I’ll be more direct this time.

If it’s that leader’s session, and the other musicians are good with how it’s run, then no, the leader isn’t obligated to follow the feel of anyone else.

But bear in mind, this is a two-way lane. If you don’t like the feel of that session, you aren’t obligated to go there. And if the session leader and his/her mates don’t like your playing, they’re not obligated to adapt to you. It’s their party after all.

OTOH, when a session leader regularly spoils the music or the craic, the session usually doesn’t survive. The players may regroup elsewhere, neglecting to send the old leader the memo.

If you’re actually in Connacht and want to play tunes in a strictly Connacht style, then that particular session may not be your best option. You could start your own session and see how many local musicians join in with you.

Actually, I did. I suggested talking with the leader about listening more closely to the pace, pulse, and lift of the player starting the set. Being more musically flexible. That will carry you most of the way. Of course, you’ll also want to pay attention to intonation, volume, degree of swing, phrasing, and if you want to get really tightly in synch, how the other player ornaments, and the micro-timing of notes and ornaments, and variations, and little adjustments in their energy.

Playing truly *with* another person begs for sharp focus and attention, and an open mind and a spirit of generosity and reciprocity.

It occurs to me that these same skills, this same mindset, applies not only to music but also to engaging in an online discussion.

Re: Leaders that don’t know how to follow

I agree that it is the responsibility of a session leader to listen to their fellow musicians and to support them – if they are not able to do so musically, they by sitting out, listening and giving encouragement. The fact is, however, that i. a self-appointed leader rarely has all the qualities people would like and ii. most of us are not fully aware of our own deficiencies (in music or any other area of life). These faults come in degrees, of course – some people are more self-aware than others, more dedicated to self-improvement and more receptive to criticism and advice.

Speaking as a musician of, I think, moderate temperament, I think that most of us musicians would, to a greater or lesser degree, take offence at uninvited crticism of our musicianship. How a musician *behaves* in response to criticism, however, is key – e.g. with anger; with determination to continue doing as they do, despite recognising the validityof the criticism; or with humility, accepting that they have faults, however much it hurts to do so, and working to fix them. If the musician (session leader) in question falls into anything but the last category, then my feeling is that direct criticism would be counterproductive.

My ideal approach (which I may or may not have the presence of mind to use in the heat of the moment) would be to suggest some listening material, that would help ‘open their ears’ your preferred style – presented as a friendly sharing of information, rather than advice or criticism. It they are receptive to this, you might have some success; they might, on the other hand, still perceive it as the criticism it is intended to veil and put up resistance, in which case you are probably not going to get anywhere… But it is a relatively low-risk approach.

As for Connaught/Connacht/Connachta* style, it is not quite clear what you mean, since there are at least three distinct regional styles in the province:
i. North Connacht (Sligo/Leitrim/Roscommon – strong bias towards fiddle and flute, highly ornamented, light and lively);
ii. Connemara/Mayo (Strong bias towards melodeon and C#/D-tuned accordion, very solid rhythm, strong emphasis on melodic variation);
iii. East Galway (Smooth, flowing style on fiddle and flute, more sparsely ornamented than N. Connacht style, many tunes played in flat keys, B/C accordions more common).

There is, no doubt, some blurring of these styles, and players from Connacht whose playing does not fit any one of my (very crude) summaries, but the point is simply that it is not clear what you mean by ‘Connaught style’. East Galway and Connemara music are probably closer to the music of neighbouring Clare and Tipperary (both in the province of Munster, not Connacht) than it is to the N Connacht style.

*I think ‘Chonnachta’ with ‘Ch-’ is a mutated form, only used in certain grammatical contexts. I am not an Irish speaker, but I speak some Welsh, whose grammar has many parallels with Irish.

Re: Leaders that don’t know how to follow

I’ve played both in traditional Irish sessions, for step dancers, and contradances for about 30 years now.

Regarding contradance style, in my experience it’s relatively common that bands that play for contradances tend to play mostly American old-time and/or French Canadian tunes, with the occasional traditional Irish tune.

Reels and hornpipes tend to pick up the American old-time style back-beat pulse, and jigs are played very straight with little to no swing or the sort of note emphasis, separation, and dynamics that one does playing the Irish tradition.

Now, this doesn’t apply to every contradance band I’ve played with, some actually know how to play in the Irish style, but that’s a generalization of the difference I’ve noticed when they were apparent.

Basically American old-time style players playing traditional Irish tunes in an old-time (or French Canadian) style.

sorefingers, as a child, my mentors were Sligo musicians, including an uncle who played fiddle. He shared with me the tunes he learned from his father. So those were my earliest tunes, and to this day they remain the music that feels most intuitive to me.

But I later drifted to Roscommon and East Galway musicians, and then to Clare. Where Sligo music seeped in through osmosis, it took some effort to learn these latter styles. Ultimately, however, it’s not much different from learning more than one language at a young age—one remains your native tongue, yet you can become highly conversant and fluent in others. You find commonalities while learning to appreciate the differences as well.

Because of the natural way the Sligo tunes were handed to me, I sought the same sort of connections with the other musicians I learned from. As you grow a “family” of tunes, so you can also grow an extended family of musical mentors and close friends. You get to know their parents, aunts, and uncles, and their music. The tunes come from people, not recordings and machines. And people have stories and ways of speaking, mannerisms and small rites that accompany the tunes—habits with how they warm up a flute or let a pint settle. These days, the disembodied way so many people learn tunes seems disorienting to me. The music is detached from any sense of community, family, continuity, from people at all. Where’s the heart in that?

Re: Leaders that don’t know how to follow

Zoom isn’t intended to provide minimal latency, and any minimal latency online “playing together” system is going to be fragile at best.

If you’ve ever tried to use a low-latency online “jamming” platform like JamKazam or Sonobus, you’ve probably had more than one session turn into a huge tech support session. I was a huge advocate for them, but gave up on all of them early into COVID since we spent more time trying to fix people’s systems than playing.

There are just too many factors, all of which have to be spot on for playing together to work and not all of them are under your control.

There’s the physical distance between the players, but that can be much longer than the physical distance because of internet routing, there is the audio buffer and packet loss adaptation settings on the computer hardware, driver, and software being used, and it requires cabled internet to work properly. Only the most technically-savvy players were able to make it work reliably in my experience.

Finally I just settled on Zoom and never looked back, since it just works on essentially every platform and even tech-phobic players can generally get it to work. We don’t even try to play together, we play one after another in “chains”, which during the lockdowns, felt more like passing tunes around a session than just playing by yourself in a room. And absolutely, it wasn’t optimal, but it’s all we had.

That being said, Zoom added a “Live Performance Audio” mode a few months ago that supposed provides for minimal latency and playing together if the players are close enough.

I haven’t tried it myself since my Zoom session friends are mostly 1000+ miles away. I’d love to try it with friends within 100 miles or so.

Re: Leaders that don’t know how to follow

About Zoom, it was a lifesaver during the Covid shutdown for many Highland pipe bands.

Our pipe corps had weekly Zoom meetings, and in some ways they were actually better than in-person rehearsals. Why? Because pipe corps’ job isn’t to create a melding of everyone’s individual playing approaches, it’s for each piper to mimic the Pipe Major as closely as possible, and the Zoom rehearsals were essentially a collection of one-on-one rehearsals with the Pipe Major. The problem with in-person practices is that each piper is hearing the pipers to their left and right more than they’re hearing the Pipe Major. A problem that sometimes happens is a cluster of pipers getting slightly ahead of, or behind, the Pipe Major’s tempo.

I’m guessing that a Zoom “session” per se wouldn’t be possible, but what would be possible is for everyone to be playing duets with the session leader, which if the leader is a really strong player many people could benefit from.

Re: Leaders that don’t know how to follow

Well, it’s a dance, not a session, the big differences:
- you play the tune not 3 times, but 33 times until caller indicates “2 more times” (yes, you do switch tunes for contra, but for ecd you are stuck with the same tune all the way through).
- you would go bonkers if you play the same tune the same time 33 times, session style (plus likely injure yourself by repetitive stress, happened to one of our uber fiddlers).
- so “all were in full flight playing the melody” is an illusion:
- players would drop out to rest,
- to play a harmony or a variation
- to play drones
- to shuffle-up more groove
- (established bands usually do not arrange this ahead of time, fly by the seat of the pants; less established bands could trade melody round-robin; portland’s megaband has an actual conductor, last I heard)
- the good news is that dancers (there is no “audience”) are not competition judges counting your mistakes, instead they are busy dancing and will dance through almost anything and will thank you profusely at the end (the caller *will* be confused by wrong number of A and B parts, but that’s about it)
- this error/fault-tolerance allows for much more artistic creativity for the musicians. playing crazy variations will excite dancers but will wreck a session.
- “proper contra dancing” requires 120 bpm tempo (reels, jigs, polkas) and at this speed tunes “compress” and stop working unless you have good phrasing. this is why common irish tunes sound different in contra dance videos. the technical skill translates directly to session playing (with good phrasing, at slow tempo, tunes can bloom like flowers).

“Is the video an example of contra dance ”style”? Except for the dancing, it sounded pretty much like a solid session, or even that thing known as a gig.”

one video is not representative (google blind men explore elephant). for the next dance set, musicians may put down borons, fiddles and flutes, and grab saxophones, electric guitars and full drum kits. dancers love variety.

some bands are known for consistent style, i.e. play more traditional-sounding music (wild asparagus), or more modern/experimental/crazy sounding music (giant robot dance, perpetual e-motion, toss the possum). other bands will play anything (we once danced to the tune from the angry birds game).

dancing is the common feature, and dancers vote with their feet (ahem, dollars). bands that provide better dancing play a lot, bands that provide so-so dancing play once. whatever makes the feet move.

P.S. contra dance music, especially “new england style”, used to be associated with piano-heavy rhythm (Seattle’s KGB), 1 piano + 1 fiddler used to be the “standard” contra dance music. I think this was very true in the 1990-ies, 2000-ths, but is much less so these days. dancers want variety, musicians are perpetually looking for “the new sound” (yes, “this sounds like an irish session” could be “the new sound”).

@ Michael Eskin “ boom-CHICK back-beat feel”. If only it were that simple!

Irishtrad dance tunes have evolved over the centuries way, the heck away, from its European neighbor’s umpah marches. It will take a non native player as long to get there as it took to discover the native emphasis in simple Irish jigs. And in most cases, IMHO, it never happens at all. In another debate, among similar forms O/T like Bluegrass, has the same difficulty, similar but not the same. Just isn’t, and never will be. Nor should it be.

The whole point of American evolved dance tunes such as ‘Did You Ever Go To Meeting Unlce Joe’, V Irish-Scot ‘Miss McCloud’s Reel’ in Scottish / Irish folk, is precisely the change in emphasis put on leading notes to create the ‘boom-CHICK back beat feel’. (Once explained me as the Yanks are playing 2/4 the Irish/Scottish 4/4 ). Played, even in Scotland, there is no attempt to do that, in Ireland the big phrase points are emphasized even though not within umpah layout. Piano backing has, over time, refined the music even further along that path.

Historically Ireland got a wee bit of a leg up when it accepted Scottish refugees in the 1690’s - 7 Ill Years. As well as their food/drink preferences, these new immigrants brought Scottish Fiddling. And from it the use of the ‘short stroke bow’ took hold instead of ( still evident in far western Ireland ) the slurred long style. A great example, Packie Dolan, playing ‘The Keel Row’;

https://www.allcelticmusic.com/artists/Packie%20Dolan.html

benefited from that influence, his snappy bow sounds so exotic when we listen to such as Paddy Canny (RIP) today.

In effect, as above, there is nothing at all wrong with players down stripping Irish tunes of complexity in order to sound better - to their way of hearing. Yet there is absolutely everything wrong with thinking it is authentic.
