Fixed sets v. winging it

Random musings…

On the chat for a session I sometimes go to, it was suggested that they work on more of their fixed sets of tunes so they have a nice wee set list of them and smooth changes. Although I am not regular, I weighed in just to say that I really like spontaneity in a session.

But I realise may be the weirdo here. I have a strong aversion to playing fixed sets of tunes myself, although I think most other people prefer having some fixed sets. I get bored if I’ve started the same trio or pairing too many times, and then I divorce all of them and play them with other ones. In a (different) session today, I was contemplating this -- for the handful of sets I started, I winged everything together, as always, and realised I like doing it that way because it’s kind of like a low stakes adrenaline sport. You don’t know if you’ll successfully make that change to some tune you haven’t played in years, but it’s a buzz when you do. And if you crash it, the only thing that gets injured is your dignity.

I do have ‘safety tunes’ that I can throw in anywhere. If I’m playing Rolling in the Ryegrass, you’ll know I had a deer-in-headlights moment during the one before it and couldn’t think of something else.

Other people’s fixed sets don’t bother me, most of the time. Especially when I don’t go to a session often enough to remember that so-and-so always plays those three together, or if they are peppered around the more amorphous sets and there aren’t hunners of them.

I also like not knowing what other people are going to play. Will it be something I forgot I knew? That tune I haven’t played for 10 years? Will it be something I haven’t heard before and want to learn? If you always know that Sligo Maid comes after Maid Behind the Bar, then you lose that. Well, I think I lose it. I think other musicians are much happier when they know what’s coming.

I did once go to a session that was so fixed in their sets that they were annoyed at me for playing one of ‘their’ tunes in a different set. “Oh, now we can’t play that set because you just played the Earls’ Chair (or whatever the hell it was).” “You could play the other ones with something else,” I said. Apparently not! Having not been to the session in question before, my psychic powers had not told me that the Earl’s Chair can only be played with x and y. But that’s kind of the extreme case.

Re: Fixed sets v. winging it

I’m 100% with Dr. SilverSpear. I am usually not bothered by other people’s fixed sets (I reckon they can strengthen the identity and cohesion of the session group), but I am kind of allergic to play premeditatedly the same set twice, except for a few sets that I might have in common with one or two other musicians and that for some reason get played often (but not always) the same way (an example would be to help a beginner musician to integrate into the session).

I tend to think of sessions as the meeting place between subjectivity and communality (I am borrowing these two terms from the title of Augusto Ferraiuolo’s book on sociological aspects of Irish trad sessions). Proposing a set and establishing the tempo is one way of expressing subjectivity in an otherwise much communal musical practice. Proposing the same set at every session to me seems much like always saying the same things every time you meet old friends and join the conversation.

One of the top qualities of traditional musicians seems to me that of being able to play and blend tunes together on the fly, in a much spontaneous and extemporaneous way, and I constantly work on this aspect when practicing and when playing in sessions. Most of the people I play with, however, have joined Irish traditional music after playing in a band or in other musical contexts and often bring the logic and habits of those worlds to Irish trad sessions. Some of them are good friends, on the other hand, and I can live with that…

Other things that quitely (!) irritate me are: shouting a badly pronounced “hup” in much unspontaneous way to signal the tune change; deciding in assembly the list of tunes to be played in the next set; when someone imposes the next tune on someone else’s set; when someone accelerates on someone else’s set.

I once took part in a session where a list of fixed sets was circulated in advance and I was explicitly asked not to pick one of the tunes that were in the list, if I were to launch a set. - Not a session at all.

On my first trip to Ireland this summer I was lucky enough to join great sessions with top musicians in the Doolin/Miltown Malbay/Lisdoonvarna area, and I was relieved to find that sessions go pretty much another way!

My ideal session would be a mix of both types of sets -- some “known” sets where the flow is predictable, and then others where someone starts a tune and you don’t know what will follow. You have to sink or swim with the next tune. Of course this works best in a more advanced session where everyone has a large enough repertoire to jump in often enough on the spontaneous sets, and players have to be comfortable sitting out tunes they don’t know.

It’s easier to have everyone participating with pre-announced fixed sets, but an entire session of that type would be boring for me. I’d rather sit out a few tunes where I can’t follow the changes and just enjoy listening to the rest of the group.

I also think a few traditional sets could use breaking up and refreshing. The Tarbolton Lodge reel is too great a tune to be forever frozen in amber with the following tunes. Free the Tarbolton Three!

For myself, if I started a set, I will usually try to call out the next tune with enough warning that those who are strong on the tune will slide into it seamlessly, which takes away a lot of the stop/start feel of a set when everybody stops to hear what the next tune is. Re: Fixed sets v. winging it

“…these are usually the folks who have played a long time and know a ton of tunes….”

Yes, over a long lifetime of sessioning, you end up with hundreds or thousands of tunes clamouring to be played. Stringing a few together is less a matter of searching for something to play next and more like squeezing the firehose to narrow the torrent to just one tune at a time.

Premeditated sets come with an opportunity cost—all the tunes you could’ve gone into but didn’t. Robert Frost said it beautifully:

Two roads diverged in a yellow wood,
And sorry I could not travel both
And be one traveler, long I stood
And looked down one as far as I could
To where it bent in the undergrowth;

Then took the other, as just as fair,
And having perhaps the better claim,
Because it was grassy and wanted wear;
Though as for that the passing there
Had worn them really about the same,

And both that morning equally lay
In leaves no step had trodden black.
Oh, I kept the first for another day!
Yet knowing how way leads on to way,
I doubted if I should ever come back.

I shall be telling this with a sigh
Somewhere ages and ages hence:
Two roads diverged in a wood, and I—
I took the one less traveled by,
And that has made all the difference.

Except with tunes in sets, it’s never a choice between just two paths. Part of the fun is the mental acrobatics of rifling through your personal rolodex of tunes as the previous tune careens toward the finish line. Time, and timing, is of the essence. For me, premeditated sets deaden that electricity, that frisson of the unexpected.

Most people go through an early phase of sticking to premeditated sets—it’s safer in the beginning, when so many other challenges demand your attention, and you might have only some dozens of tunes under your belt. Some people plan their holiday/vacation itineraries down to the minute. Others step out the door on a whim, with almost no idea where they’re headed. With any luck, they’re not married to one another.

Re: Fixed sets v. winging it

Of the sessions I used to go to, before hearing loss and other problems prevailed, many of them did play “fixed sets”, and I am another who prefers to be a wee bit adventurous and play something “new” now and then. The chances are, as many of us go to the same trad classes, there would be others who would know the same “new” tune, which might have come from last week’s class. Hopefully those who weren’t there would pick it up in due course.
Some of the sessions have large databases of tunes (talking several hundred here), and will put out a flexible set list of tunes that may be included in the next session in advance of it: some will use scores, paper or online, others not.
But I think I would still go with the view that it’s good to know in advance or even as the set starts what you are going to be playing if you are anything under advanced level. If you are one of those who has at least 500 tunes registered in your head, and super confident on your instrument, and in a session of similarly talented people, then wing away!
