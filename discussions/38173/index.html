Reel notation, 2/2 vs 4/4

When played for dancing, at least with regard to ITM, reels are in 2/2 time. There are only 2 downbeats per measure, meaning that if notated in 4/4 only the ‘1’ and ‘3’ count land on a downbeat. This lead to some confusion when I started to learn to play these tunes, as I was considering them as 4/4 tunes at 200 plus BPM, which was leading me to place note emphasis in the wrong place, often erratically.

While 2/2 and 4/4 are mathematically equivalent, I’ve been finding it more appropriate to use 2/2 and count ‘1 e and a 2 e and a’, where ‘1’ and ‘2’ fall on the downbeats. It seems a better reflection of how the tunes are actually played.

Re: Reel notation, 2/2 vs 4/4

People sometimes get all riled up about how things are notated, claiming one and only one way is “proper” or “correct”, but we have to keep in mind that music notation gives the scribe a number of equivalent options. Which option one chooses is more or less arbitrary, I think.

Seems that the most logical “art music” way to notate a reel would be Cut Time.

I’ve done a lot of “studio gigs” and it’s interesting to see, when a composer writes something that’s intended to sound like an Irish reel or jig, how they notate it. It’s almost never how the traditional Irish collections do it.

For example they usually notate what’s intended to sound like a jig in 4/4, with four groups of triplets in each bar. Likewise I’ve seen things intended to sound like reels written in 4/4, with four groups of four sixteenth notes per bar. In other words, the composer uses half as many bar-lines as the traditional Irish collections do. Though sometimes they use twice as many, putting reels in 2/2 time, jigs in 3/2 time, etc. Usually studio scores are written in 4/4 though, no matter what the actual rhythm sounds like to the listener. Several old Hymns are like that: they clearly are phrased in 3/4 yet they’re notated in 4/4 with the emphasis thus shifting all the time.

Yes how experienced players and/or listeners to Irish music tend to tap their feet might serve as the best guide.

About now somebody will say we should be guided by the dancers, and yes watching ceilidh dancers will show where the pulses are for sure. What watching dancers doesn’t tell you is where the bar-lines should go, which depends on how large a phrase you think should be included in each bar.

This sort of controversy comes up all the time in Scottish pipe music, for example 2/4 marches which could be just as properly written in 4/4 and use a lot less ink.

Re: Reel notation, 2/2 vs 4/4

“The beaming gives you all the ”missing“ information anyway. There are clearly two groups of four quavers each bar, regardless of the time signature.”

The beaming doesn’t really give you any information. Both 4/4 and 2/2 beam eighth-notes in fours. It does NOT imply two beats to the measure. The beaming is purely a visual guide to show half measures. Some music DOES use beaming to show musical groupings, but 4/4 and 2/2 do not necessarily do that. In 4/4, the eighth-notes are not usually beamed in pairs.

Re: Reel notation, 2/2 vs 4/4

yes, AB, that’s important to remember: time signature doesn’t mean “time feel” at all. It means simply what it means…top number is how many beats in a bar, bottom number is which note represents the beat. You can notate all sorts of time feels in the same time signature. And the converse is also true: you can often notate a single melody in more than one time signature.

Re: Reel notation, 2/2 vs 4/4

Not necessarily, although unless told otherwise, that’s what any musician would do. My experience has been that many players confuse time and tempo. They think if you play 4/4 fast enough, you get the reel sound you seek. This is emphatically untrue. Of necessity, if you count a reel in 4/4, it will sound very choppy regardless of how you accent it. You don’t have to see the music to know what the time signature is if you know how to count what you hear. The sound of cut time in reels is unmistakable, even if you play with no accents whatsoever. Try this: Without tapping your foot, say baba baba over and over again (2/2) with no emphasis anywhere. Now say ba ba ba ba (4/4) repeatedly. Be sure to run the “ba’s” together only when there is no space between them and I think you will immediately hear the difference.

AB - What Jim gave you is exactly what I gave you with my ba ba’s, the exception being that I eliminated the accent on the downbeat since you seemed to think the accent may have an influence and it doesn’t. In Irish music, the accented downbeat does make it sound better, but it is not essential to count the time correctly. Using ba’s instead of numbers I thought illustrated the point better. The reason is, as Jim posted, some will count ONE two Three four thinking they are counting 4/4, when in reality (Jim) they are counting 2/2 by using different numbers. You can count using any numbers you like, or say Good Dog, Good Dog (as opposed to Hop On One Foot, Hop On One Foot) and they still come out as 2/2 and 4/4 respectively. It’s all a matter of how many beats you have per measure. I hope that doesn’t confuse the issue. :)
