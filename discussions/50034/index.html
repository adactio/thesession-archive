Session venues: Where is the best sound?

Is this a question for anyone else? When I play in sessions the sound is ideal only under certain conditions. Short of that there are multiple factors which can impact things like *hearing other musicians*, *distraction* from variable room noise & basically seating and *configuration* of players. Not to mention strobe lights & smoke machines ~ Kidding! :-P

A bit of background. I’m thinking about issues with sound particularly in pub sessions. Also in any session which may begin with a few players early on but grow in numbers once more people show up. The result being that overall sound suffers.

We all want a session where we can hear ourselves and most of the other players. How have you found this formula (or not) under the best & worst of circumstances?

Thanks in advance!

Re: Session venues: Where is the best sound?

Depends partly on the composition of the room. Rooms with lots of hard surfaces - glass, for example - tend to have a pretty chaotic and difficult sound. Those rooms can work, of course, but as you get more players and the volume goes up, you can expect more problems than you would get in a room with less reflective surfaces.

Of course, you can’t always choose your venue. If you’re in a difficult room, the biggest thing you can do to help is to try to keep the volume down. A corner, as Michael suggests, is doubly nice because it tends to amplify the sound and throw it out into the house and also it tends to limit the available space for players. Another solution is to set the musicians at a table, which is another way to put a soft limit on the number of players and thus on the overall volume.

If the venue has a stage, you’ll often be asked to play there. This can feel a bit weird and exposed, but acoustically it often works nicely, if only because you’ve got a bit of extra distance between you and the conversations. The unfortunate thing is that this tends to encourage players to face the room, which I find less appealing than the standard circular configuration, and also less conducive to the sociable music-and-chat of a session.

Yes, a corner is good if you can all fit into it. Even in a big, sprawling session, I suppose, having some of the musicians in a corner enables them to create a tight core for the outliers to follow.

Carpets, soft furnishings, soft walls and ceilings create ‘dry’ acoustics, which can be very unsatisfying to play in. A space with very ‘lively’ acoustics (typically with a hard floor and a high ceiling) can be beautiful for a small group to play in, without any background noise – but when there are more than, say, 10 players, and when the venue is busy with punters, it becomes impossible as the notes mash together, along with the general hubbub.

If I could choose any space for a session, it would be a small pub with a wooden or tiled (or some combination thereof) floor, stone or plastered walls, a comfortable ambient temperature, punters that have at least a passing appreciation of traditional music (enough not to put the jukebox on or talk loudly over it) – and a couple of good soft drink options (i.e. not J2SO4 or anything consisting mainly of artificial sweeteners, flavourings and colours). We can but dream…

“I think Mr. Eskin is pushing at windmills. It’s a side gig maybe(?)”

I have very low-friction bearings.

“I don’t want to go up against the tradition of playing sessions in pubs … if pub venues are becoming increasingly rare what about alternative venues for sessions?”

Yes, and since traditional music was played in kitchens and at crossroads long before it was customarily played in pubs, there is not really that much of a tradition to go up against. We have been allowed the use of a room in a large private house to ‘rehearse’ (there is simply no use trying to explain the concept of a session to muggles) once a month. It is a very nice space to play in, with no distractions.

But the pub session still has a certain appeal – even for me, as a non-drinker. If I can be forgiven for plagiarising myself, there was some discussion by email last year between my local session mates about this very thing and I made the following points:

There are some obvious disadvantages to playing in a pub:

1. The risk of encountering unruly behaviour, verbal abuse, low flying furniture etc.

2. Competing with background noise or (the thing that offends me more than anything else) other music.

3. A general conflict of interest with punters and/or staff.

4. The expectation of some punters to be entertained, or of the publican that we should pander to their punters’ every whim.

But a session in a pub also has its positive points:

1. It can be posted on public listings, so that musicians (and listeners) visiting or passing through the area can drop in. It can also happen that someone (musician or listener) just happens upon it. These things may not happen all that often but they do happen – and are worth holding out for.

2. Having genuinely attentive and appreciative listeners creates a special dynamic, even if it is just one or two amid a generally uninterested crowd, or against a wall of background noise (just not other music!). Again, it may happen seldom, but is something worth holding out for.

3. Although it does not apply to myself, I believe some musicians like to have a pint or three of draught beer whilst they play (at least, in between bouts of playing). As a stickler for freshly brewed coffee, I can appreciate that there is a difference between beer drawn straight from the cask and bottled beer from the supermarket.

Re: Session venues: Where is the best sound?

> practice playing louder

It seems counterintuitive, but when the room is loud, I suggest playing softer instead of louder. The idea is pretty simple: the room gets louder because individuals in the room are reacting to the overall noise level. That’s a positive feedback loop, and playing louder will only accelerate it.

Dropping the volume can make it easier on everyone - if there are people who want to hear what you’re playing, then the overall noise level will go down. If there aren’t, well, sessions are about chat as well as tunes, so maybe just catch up with some of the other players for a bit.

That was my post, not Jon’s.

Yes, “dumbed down” in that the repertoire, speed, style becomes like a lowest common denominator in an attempt to include everybody. This isn’t really helpful in the long run as even less able players will have to adapt to this as well when they could actually perform better on their own or in a more intimate setting. So, nobody is really satisfied.

The other extreme is that a few of the fast and furious players just take over and everyone else including many really good players either struggle to keep up or just feel unable to contribute.

The “happy medium” is for the better players to take turns in leading sets of tunes at a reasonable..even usual pace… and the other musicians can just join in as and when it suits.
There needn’t be a strict format about who plays and when, of course. In fact, it’s better that things happen naturally just as long as one or two players don’t constantly dominate.

All the above applies to overly large sessions which aren’t ideal, in my opinion. Ideally, we should have much smaller gatherings and more of them.

However, there is an increasing tendency these days to have a specific location where everybody seems to flock.
This may be partly due to a lack of session friendly venues or the fact that some players enjoy “safety of numbers” or a bit of both. As I said earlier, it also happens a lot at festivals where organisers like to organise….

Tin Whistle Samurai - Regarding the “quality floor”, that’s very unfortunate.

We welcome newer players, but since we have a very limited number of seats available in the space we play, we strongly suggest they come early and we’ll make sure we play some tunes they know.

We also tell them that as the more experienced players arrive later, if we run out of seats, we may request they give up their seat and sit nearby in the pub and listen for the rest of the session.

So far it’s worked out very well and helps reinforce the idea with newer players that listening is as important and as much a part of the whole session experience as playing.

Our session scene here in San Diego skews older. The folks who hosted the sessions I first attended in the 1990s and early 2000s now come to the session I host, and many are now in their 70s and one of our anchor players just turned 80. I figure I have hopefully at least 10 or 20 more years of playing (I’m 63) in sessions, and I want to make sure I have folks to play with in the future.

I’m always looking for new players to invite to our session either through our local Comhaltas chapter sessions (where I teach each month) or through other local Irish arts and culture groups.

A session with a “quality floor” is very short-sighted if they want to sustain a scene for the long term.

Now, if it’s a high-level session run by and for the experienced players who just want to work their craft, with no concern about the longer timeframe, sure, I get it.

Unfortunately we don’t have that luxury here in San Diego.
