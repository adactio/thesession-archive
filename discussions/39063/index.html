Re: Slow airs for tin whistle

Having competed in a few All-Irelands in tin whistle slow airs (as well as umpteen regionals & all-Britains) what I can say is this:

@WillEvans: Though technically any slow tune that is the melody of a song would suffice, none of the songs you name would satisfy the ‘unwritten criteria’. And the second two are definite no-no’s. Songs with a discernible rhythm tend to be poor choices.

What are they looking for? They are after the melody to sean-nos songs. Songs from the unaccompanied singing tradition. Comhaltas’s website has a resource of these songs (I believe). There are quite a few which are ‘done to death’ like the Lament for Limerick (aka ‘Marbhna Luimini’). Avoid these.

Finding and learning the slow airs - Best to find a version from a ‘reputable source’. So, don’t for example learn Cormac Breatnach versions of slow airs - they tend to be too jazzy for the adjudicators. What you’re aiming for is to imitate the ‘pulse’ of a voice, so if possible learn them from a singer or recording of a singer. If not, then get them from a trad whistle player like Mary Bergin or a trad flute player like Matt Molloy. As a general rule, if there is backing on the track, forget about learning this version of a slow air. You’re wanting to learn the air in phrases. Note where Mary Bergin for example holds notes, and more importantly draws breaths. That’s why I say learn from singers or whistle/flute players. Also and this is important… DON’T LEARN THEM FROM THE DOTS!!!! What goes for dance tunes goes ten-fold for slow airs.

So, this is a list of slow airs I’ve taken to junior and senior All-Ireland, and a few my brother has taken to all Ireland level on the fiddle.

From Matt Molloy’s CD ‘Stony Steps’ I learned:
O’Rahilly’s Grave (spellings various)
The Parting of Friends

From Mary Bergin’s ‘Feadóga Stáin 2’ CD (there are a couple on her other CD as well)
Aisling Gheal
Seolfaimid Araon Na Geanna Romhainn

From Seamus Ennis (I think he sings it on ‘A Bonny Bunch of Roses’)
The Bonny Bunch of Roses - This is actually a great one to learn as your budding whistle player can learn the song in English and therefore will get the phrasing right.
An Leanbh Sidhe
This CD has a couple more slow airs as well.

On the fiddle CD, ‘ar an bhfidil’ by oisin mac diarmada I learned this air, and it’s a gem.
Bean A’Leanna

From my band’s CD, ‘Lads of Lash’ by Choonz, my brother plays ‘Cape Clear’ on the fiddle.
https://paulgarner-lowwhistle-irishmusic.bandcamp.com/album/lads-of-lash

There’s a lot more to know about slow airs. Which whistle to play them on (play on a standard tin whistle not a Chieftain/ Overton/ .. low whistle.) You can enter Miscellaneous with low whistle though! … You have to play all your slow airs on the same whistle for some reason so pick wisely. There’s nothing to stop you playing on a Bb or C or indeed any key whistle you fancy, (though I’d stick to playing nothing lower than an A alto and certainly nothing higher than an Eb tin whistle - sounds too shrill.) I’d stick to playing ‘tin’ whistles - Generations, Shaws, Feadóg - nickel-plated brass, tin or brass-bodied instruments, not plastic-bodied instruments like Susatos. I play a Dixon when I play tin whistle, they are pretty good as well. Customising them to sound better is an article in itself - i never bothered myself, but it can be worth doing.

Best of luck!

Re: Slow airs for tin whistle

Books like the one you list - my Dad has it - contain a number of ‘airs’ that aren’t really. A lot of the O‘Carolan tunes for example aren’t considered airs for two reasons. Firstly, they are Baroque classical compositions more than Irish music tunes - in the same way as Bartok’s ’folk dances‘ are not really trad. Secondly, they are instrumentals not songs. BTW Many of the tunes on that book’s list *are* slow airs. The first one for example - ’lament of the three mary’s’.

Tomas O Canainn is a reputable guy but i suspect his book would omit a lot of these tunes if he was thinking in terms of CCE competitions. He’s ostensibly writing a book of mostly slow tunes that don’t fit a recognised dance-tune meter - i.e. tunes that don’t have a home on the Session’s tune categories! The reason that they won’t say ‘sean-nos slow airs’ I suspect is that it would then mean (to my best understanding), ‘tunes derived from the unaccompanied singing tradition’. Which would still require the participant to be intimate with that repertoire to know what that truly means. And don’t trust ‘the dots’!!! learn by ear!

I have played many competitions, and one of the principal lessons I’ve learnt is that many adjudicators have their own interpretation of what is ‘canon’ - so play it safe. You won’t go wrong with any of the ones I’ve suggested, and they are beautiful airs too. Some adjudicators might welcome tunes like ‘Carrickfergus’ or ‘For Ireland I Won’t Tell Her Name’ for example. The vast majority won’t. Slow airs competitions bring out the inner snobs in competitors, competitor’s parents and teachers and adjudicators!! If you make it to an All-Ireland or even before, you will find the unseemly sight of everyone Gaelicising their names and all kinds of other things! I love slow airs and I hate the posturing all the more for it. It would take too long to list what (my opinion of) what aren’t slow airs and why. Better to start with a few, get to know them, and you’ll get an idea what are and aren’t likely to be germane.

My advice in a nutshell is learn three of the ones i listed (think it’s 3 slow airs for most of the juniors). If you want a few more suggestions, i’ll think of some. Of my list, I’d avoid O‘rahilly’s Grave - the breath control and phrasing are quite demanding, as are all the sustained high notes. I’d think about ’seolfaimid aaraon na geanna romhainn’ - mostly in the lower octave, and good clean phrasing will make it sound accomplished without a lot of ornamentation. Parting of Friends and Aisling Gheal are also approachable.

Jut thought of another one - absolutely fantastic slow air. Very, very hard, and I don’t recommend this for a first dip into the competition circuit, but this is proper. Absolutely the type of thing they would want. For this, you’d have to have serious ornamentation skills, and phrasing. It’s a song about his home town.
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1Kk1hIr7Tw

Some other things. Just as with the dance music, styles vary across regions. For example, some really love ornamentation, some are relatively plain. I am English myself, so I just learned the airs I loved, that spoke to me, irrespective of region. I tried to find out what they were about, where possible. Oddly, the ones I loved the best, i generally found the lyrics spoke to me too, when i found them. You won’t need to hit the dexterity of Eoin Warner’s ‘Baile Bhuirne’ to win even a senior All-Ireland if that’s not your thing. Clean phrasing and good tone are the no.1 requirement, then picking the right airs, and after that, *not* doing the wrong ornamentation (i.e. vibrato - it sounds horrible and doesn’t belong in this music) and equally importantly, doing the right ornamentation (cuts, rolls, cranns etc.) well - choosing when to do them appropriately, and giving them their appropriate length- which is harder to judge than in the dance tunes. This is why slow airs are their own category, with the tin whistle slow airs All-Ireland titles as highly regarded as the tin whistle title. It’s not just there so you can double up your medal haul :P

I am not sure what you meant by ‘what’s that about happy C?’
If you are talking about whistle keys, a nice C tin whistle can strike a good balance for a mellow sound (compared to the standard session key of D). Eb will sound brighter. But most Eb are painfully shrill.. poor quality construction really doesn’t help a non-optimal whistle size. If you play a Bb, people may assume you are trying to hide behind the lower sound. C is somehow very pleasent sounding - quite a few flute players will play whole albums on a C flute.

An accomplished slow air player is a master of their instrument - and of the tradition - as it requires all the technical skill, draiocht and learning as the other. There are some fantastic musicians who can rip through blazing sets of tunes all night and who are listless slow air players, likewise some great slow air players who are quite ordinary dance-tune players. And some who are astonishing at both. I think to be truly great at either the dance or the air requires some great quality of the soul - for want of a better description - but just as in life itself, as some are drawn to the plaintive and some to the exuberant, sometimes a musician will feel called particularly to one or the other.

Wishing you and your daughter luck. :) If you have other questions, or want a bit of feedback, just PM me here. Tin whistle slow airs are hard, fighting nerves to keep your fingers still and breath steady in competitions is tough… even when you’ve nailed a tune in practice! However, your daughter is too young for nerves, which is good!!!!!

The late Tomás Ó Canainn wrote a fine book as noted above, with accompanying CD. His idea of a slow air is more in keeping with the general tradition in Ireland. However, as also noted above, the official CCE have odd ideas about these things.

I always think it’s an anachronism to have kids playing the sort of laments and airs that they favour. You need a bit of life experience to draw out the music and children at that age ideally have no business with the experiences of death and loss.

But if you must, the most important thing is for your daughter to pick a piece she likes and enjoys playing. This recording by Matt Cranitch has a good selection, https://thesession.org/recordings/1707

The first tune on this, An Raibh Tú ar an gCarraig? is very playable on whistle and not too hard. Well known but good.

Another piece, not on this CD, but also handy enough on whistle, is https://thesession.org/tunes/1758 If you Google around, you’ll some nice song versions of it, start on the note A on the whistle and tell her to follow it.

Re: Slow airs for tin whistle

I know nothing about the competition side of things.

My only observation is that I play a number of sean nos airs, and I generally feel that they go best on the pipes (uilleann pipes of course).

There’s one that I much prefer on whistle, ar Eirinn ni neosfainn ce hi, due to being able to play it in the key of D, using a full range from F# in the low octave up to the third octave D. Yes I know one doesn’t need to use a range that wide, and I’ve heard people play it in the key of G where they dwell on Bottom D rather than doing the turn there you can do when playing it in D.

I’ve also heard people play it in D and have high B as the highest note, leaving out the brief 3rd octave D. For sure I even play the tune on the Highland pipes where the whole thing is compressed into one octave! But I prefer the wide range the whistle gives.

And yes my uilleann chanter has a High D key and I can use it… but I don’t like the sound of it as much as the same note on the whistle.

About “airs” in general, I don’t consider Carolan pieces or the Irish Folk Music of the 1950s through 1970s as being “airs” in the strict sense- to me the word means sean nos airs and rubato or non-metrical rhythm.

Re: Slow airs for tin whistle

A lovely slow air? Try this one. However, there is one major catch in that the melody requires a single low C#, a semi-tone lower than a D whistle could produce. A keyless D flute player could produce it provided that their embouchure is in tip-top condition in order to bend the bottom D down by a semi-tone, but a whistle is another matter. An octave jump up might be the only way out of such a situation.

@tony whistle I’m pretty sure (without resorting to the liner notes) that Mary is playing the tune on a high F whistle. It comes out sounding in D minor. I haven’t a source for the notes, or the time to transcribe it, but you’d have better luck learning it by ear if you used an F whistle. The fingering would be the same as playing in B minor on a D whistle. To learn it by ear on a D whistle, you could use an app to lower the pitch by 3 semitones. Or just memorise how the tune goes and fumble about :-)

PS It might or might not help you to know that it’s basically an elaborate version of the same tune to which The Star of the County Down is sung!
