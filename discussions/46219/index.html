Re: Does using the C foot keys murder anyone else’s bottom hand?

Hi FirexAir, is it an embouchure/breath issue or, as per subject, you struggle to operate the keys with your bottom hand? In this latter case, as Chet suggests, it might help to specify what type of keys you have.

I have block-mounted padded (not pewter plug) C# and C keys and the key touches for the Eb, C#, C keys are positioned at the same length along the flute, with the Eb key being on the player side and the other two slightly further from the player. With this configuration, when I assemble the flute I rotate the foot joint so that the arm of C the key is more or less in line with the finger holes. This brings the C# and C touches “above” of the flute and the Eb touch more on the inner side (with respect to the player), so that to operate the Eb key I have to “push” the touch with the pinkie with a forward/backward motion rather than “press” it with a top/down motion. (Not sure my English is correct, but I hope it is clear what I mean…). When I am not using the keys I use to anchor my right-hand pinkie to the Eb key block, just above the Eb key arm. At the beginning this was slightly painful and I had the impression that I had to stretch my fingers to reach this position, but in a couple of weeks I could play comfortably in that position.

Firexair,
I feel your pain.

I spent a week ( well 4-5 full days ) trying to get silicone pads to seal on C & C sharp foot keys.
They work beautifully in isolated mode. Getting them to work together with a light touch as needed for ITM is difficult. ( Shimming , pad heights, registration, lever bending…… )

Take your flute to a professional, it’s non-trivial to get them sealing as they should with a gentle touch on C key…..

But yes, you SHOULD be able to get C & C# to both seal with only a light touch on C lever. Anything else is too hard in ITM, shouldn’t have to flex the low hand at all IMO.

( No need to mention that you need to be sure the low end has no small leaks ( F nat, foot joint, D# )
I have booked my flute ( a Pratten ) into a pro, to address just this issue.

Finally if they are too “high” they will affect the D tone and perhaps even tuning…..

Re: Does using the C foot keys murder anyone else’s bottom hand?

“I can use the C/C# on the foot of my Boehm with zero effort, but the keys on my simple system are murderous to the point where I’m not even sure I’m capable of using them.”

If we’re talking original c1830-1890 wooden 8-key London-made orchestral flutes, these flutes were designed and built for those keys to be regularly used, and the orchestral and band players who played these flutes professionally did regularly use them.

Thus, these keys as originally made worked flawlessly and effortlessly.

My 30-odd years of fluteplaying were split between two instruments, an original c1830 Rudall & Rose (London) with blockmounted pewter plug low C/C# keys and a c1860 Koehler & Son (London) with blockmounted cup & pad low C/C# keys, and both flutes played these notes flawlessly and effortlessly, with good clarity and volume and perfectly in tune.

Of course the 19th century professional players demanded properly working instruments! So that’s what the legit London makers made.

This being the case, how can we explain modern players having troubles? I can think of some reasons that an original London-made 8-key flute might be giving foot key trouble:

1) The flute might be perfect, but the player is holding the flute improperly.

2) The flute was made perfectly, but the foot has been rotated improperly, making the foot keys difficult to reach.

3) The flute was made perfectly, but is damaged, out of adjustment, or has had aftermarket alterations.

Now for an entirely different topic, that of modern flutes made specifically for traditional Irish music.

If it’s a new flute, are the foot keys exactly copied from 19th century originals, that is, flutes which were designed and built to have the foot keys regularly used?

Or has the new maker tinkered with the design of the foot keys, putting them in awkward positions?

Or has the new maker, assuming the ITM users won’t be using the keys much, made them overly stiff?

We have to keep in mind that to the original 19th century maker ALL the keys had to work smoothly and effortlessly because all of them would be in constant use.

Also, the original 19th century makers were making fully keyed flutes fulltime. Of their professional flutes, every flute was made with all 8 keys. Keys weren’t an out-of-the-ordinary thing, an occasional bother.

Let’s say you’re a modern Irish flute maker and you’ve made hundreds of keyless flutes, and a few with some body keys. Now an order comes in for a flute with footjoint keys. How many have you made? None? A few? How smoothly will these keys work? How well will those low notes be voiced? I’ve tried modern 8-key flutes where the C/C# foot keys would hardly play.

Re: Does using the C foot keys murder anyone else’s bottom hand?

FirexAir, here are several possible avenues to pursue. My antique 8 key has post mounted keys, including the Boehm style C foot. I agree with Richard Cook that these instruments as originally built were full on Orchestral instruments, with fully functional foot keys. One difference I would note. with today´s pads we do not have dedicated, purpose built flute pads. . .we use pads intended for clarinets, saxophones, and oboes. I think there are two problems with these type of pads as used for foot key pads. The felting is too hard. It is difficult to get good seating. The ´skin´ over the pad is too porous and not nearly as formable as the ´gold-beaters´ skin as supplied in the 1800´s. So, what to do.
First, the seal of the Eb key impacts the performance of the C# and C foot keys. While the Eb key can perform adequately by itself, its seal can degrade the performance of the two lower keys. This can be established by using flute clamps, or small plastic hand clamps on the keys. Reseating the Eb key helped the performance of the two lower keys. Then using the clamps, which exert considerably more force than than your fingers, I could get an astonishingly strong sound from the C# an C.
It has been suggested that using PTFE plumber´s tape on the face of the pad will help provide the formability, impermeability, and seal. A simple way to test this in a reversable, non-destructive fashion is to gently slide a piece of the tape across the face of the pad. Sometimes two or more thicknesses may be needed.

Re: Does using the C foot keys murder anyone else’s bottom hand?

FireAir
Some useful commentary been provided above.
It’s informative to remove the C# key and plug its hole with BlueTak.

Then you can test how well the C will sound ( and how much pressure is needed ).
It should pop out a nice solid low C with only a light touch to it’s lever as postie described it.
You can’t play a D now, as the C# is always on, but at least you know how the C lever and pad behaves.

It’s possible that with the C# installed ( while C# might play in isolation ( ie using it’s lever and assuming no footjoint/D# leaks, and that you are not leaking air from your right hand in reaching for the lower notes…. ) ) that the action of the C lever on C# is not accurately seating both pads. That’s the hard part.
( You can test it with audio cassette tape, looking for leaks around the edges while gently closing only the C lever. )

Also immersing the foot joint in a jar of water ( with the end sealed, cork or bluetak ) will allow you to do a bubble test. I use an olive oil dispenser to seal the top of the foot joint, and allow me to blow air in. This will show you where air is escaping ( including any unseen hairline cracks ).

It’s difficult to get both pads sealing all the way around both holes ( especially towards the lever/upper side ) at the same time, with only a light touch on the C lever.

Needs bending and shimming practice on flutes you don’t care about, not a prized player.
That all might be trickier ( removing C# ) on post mounted flutes, I have no experience with those.

Best of luck
Pat
