If you want to sing Irish music and accompany yourself, your options are fairly limited. There is not a whole lot of harmony in the genre. Re: Mandolins

Setup or set up is the mechanical setup of the instrument. String action/height, neck angle, nut and bridge shaped properly, right strings/tuning, frets/fingerboard even - that sort of thing. Any decent player knows that a stringed instrument straight from the factory needs tweaks and fine tuning. Most of us fiddle/guitar/whatever players have a good luthier who does such things for us. On a new instrument, this takes a couple of hours(ish) worth of work to get it into good playing condition, ~$100-$200. Depending on many factors, of course.

Setup is critical. Bad setup can give you buzzing, tonal issues, intonation issues, long-term damage issues, personal injury (one violinist I read about got nerve damage from strings that were too high) etc. Those are worst-case scenarios, and not all of them are terribly common. The typical problems are difficulty and frustration in playing. Buying from an unknown source or big box store or any place that doesn’t specialize in mandolins is rolling the dice.

I think just about everybody would agree that buying from a reputable/competent/knowledgeable seller is the best approach for most people. The above issues are all resolved before it leaves their shop.

Re: Mandolins

I think that was addressed above -- with any stringed instrument, you’ll want it set up by an experienced luthier who will make sure the bridge is positioned correctly, the neck angle is right (if applicable -- I don’t know mandolins), and that the string action (height) is set well and the frets are all the right height and in the right place. They should also make sure all the innards of the instrument are correct (most stringed instruments are not just a hollow box). In short, they will set the instrument up to make it sound its best and not be difficult to play (this is where string action is cruicial). It’s important to find a luthier that works with mandolin-family instruments, not just a person who is good with guitars.

Re: Mandolins

F and A-style will be exactly the same in terms of what it takes to play them and how they are used, for the most part. The distinction really just refers to the scrolling and shape of the body, which doesn’t affect the sound of the instrument but will jack up the cost of an F-style, since they tend to be much more ornate.

There are some subtle differences in sound quality and projection depending on the type of sound hole, with round holes typically producing a slightly rounder, richer, and louder sound that projects better, while F holes tend to give you a brighter, crisper sound that is slightly quieter but more cutting. If you’re just getting into it this likely won’t be a huge difference for you, but if you’re looking to play accompaniment I would lean towards a round hole.

In general, the range and bright sound of the mandolin makes it difficult to use to accompany yourself, and believe me I’ve tried. A bouzouki would probably be better if you want to play accompaniment while keeping some of the characteristics that set these guys apart from other instruments you might use instead, as has been mentioned. However, if you do go the mandolin route, open chords will give you the most workable sound for accompaniment, a lot of cheaper instruments tend to get a thinner sound and poor sustain if you try to bar and move too far up the neck.

If you’re still shopping around, another brand I’d recommend trying is Morgan Monroe, or at least their mid-range models. It’s another fairly inexpensive Chinese make, cheaper than most Eastman’s are new, but I have found the quality to be decent. Mine has served me well for a long time with only minimal adjustments to the bridge and the nut, even as I’ve picked up “better” instruments. However, and I really mean this, be very careful with the real cheap starter mandolins. I’ve had awful luck with them, especially in terms of intonation, which I couldn’t resolve very easily even after extensive bridge fiddling. My guess is that the shorter scale length gives you less room for error with your adjustments and makes them more vulnerable to manufacturing quirks, but I’m certainly not an expert on that end. Good luck!

Good mandolins from low to high price are Kentucky, Eastman and Weber or Collins. Irish Traditional Music is usually played on an oval hole A-model but F-models are somewhat louder and more balanced (IMHO). I prefer arch-top mandolins over flat top mandolins and prefer f (fiddle-style) holes over oval holes. There is little or no difference in sound between an arch-top F-style and an arch-top A-style with f holes. There will be much disagreement with the previous four sentences among the ITM community so make up your own mind after taking with someone like Dennis or his sales people who inventory and sell many different brands and configurations. For the money, I think any of the Eastman MD-500 series mandolins are excellent sounding and easy playing instruments. Mine compares favorably with the three Weber mandolins I own.

I found I could learn and play the melody of Irish Traditional Music tunes on a mandolin fairly quickly. Playing well takes longer.

I’m surprised no one has mentioned the possibility of either a mandola or tenor guitar. The tonal range of a mandola would be better suited to using to accompany singing - Andy Irvine sings and plays mandola, and Marla Fibish uses a mandola to accompany herself when singing too. The downside is that your options finding a mandola may be limited as compared to the mandolin. Tenor guitar would be another possibility as you can play chords to accompany yourself. They can be tuned CGDA or GDAE, but for your interests tuning CGDA would likely work better, plus you can always capo it at the second fret to be able to play single note melody and be able to use “mandolin” fingering. Kala makes a decent entry level tenor guitar that goes for about $329 new but frequently are sold second hand for about $200. I had one for awhile and was quite impressed with it given the price point. Martin make an entry level tenor (or used to make one, possibly discontinued now), the Martin LXM. I’ve seen them going second hand for about $200 as well.

"Bear in mind that there is a lot of difference in volume between various mandolins - and experience also plays large part. In sessions, I’ve only been able to get EXACTLY the sound I wanted from but two instruments - neither was my own. Funnily enough, one had costed ~€1000 and the other ~€35. I’m certainly not known as a loud player, but at these two sessions I’m thinking of, I had NO problems being heard.

The very instruments were definitely of major importance, but also my 20+ years as a mandolin player. I’m not sure I would have been able to get the same sound in the early nineties (nor would I have had any idea that it could sound like that in a session)."

https://thesession.org/discussions/33420#comment714204

But if we’re talking about playing comfortably, I prefer my own (~€150 back in 1991).

Re: Mandolins

The only problem I had with The Loar mandolin was a set up and too thick layer of lacquer on the it. Good mandos for the price. On mandolincafe there is a guy Rob Meldrum, he compiled an ebook on how to set up the mandolin. It’s a little bit of work but if you like to experiment you can do it yourself or course if you have required tools. If you don’t, well, I’d suggest getting professional set up - some shop have that in their offer, check with your guitar center where you’ve seen the mando.
Also I’ve made an arm rest for my mando with a cheap fiddle chin rest, just cut off part of it and sanded it and polished it. it costed me around 12£, incl. shipping.
