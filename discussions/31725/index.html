[Traditionally, the woods from which these [wind] instruments are made are dense, have a fine structure, and a high dimensional stability, particularly when exposed to high levels of moisture. Before the arrival of tropical wood species in Europe, boxwood (Buxus sempervirens) and fruit woods such as pear (Pyrus communis) and plum (Prunus domestica), and in exceptional cases the material of the oldest surviving flute, ivory, were used. Since the arrival of the denser tropical species, African blackwood or grenadill (Dalbergia melanoxylon), Brazilian rosewood (Dalbergia nigra), and Macassar ebony (Dyospyros celebica) have been favored for oboe and clarinet, while the large bassoon has always been and still is made of maple (Acer platanoides) (Table 2).
Why were these woods chosen? Their mechanical properties differ vastly, by almost a factor of two between the least and the most dense. The reason for their use is their structure. All these woods can be turned and drilled with great accuracy, and they are sufficiently dimensionally stable under the influence of moisture. These characteristics are critical because for these instruments, the material determines the sound quality of the instrument not by vibrating itself—the acoustic pressure of the standing wave in the air column is by far too weak to excite and couple to vibrations of the thick wooden tube of wooden instruments to produce audible vibrations—but the sound quality is determined by the interaction of the material with the enclosed column of vibrating air.

The tube material influences the sound of the instrument and its playability significantly and mainly by two mechanisms: by vibrational damping due to air friction at the tube walls and by turbulence in the vibrating air at the edges (Benade, 1990⇓). Vibrational damping is lower in tubes with a smooth finish. Turbulences, which also dampen the vibration and affects the tonal quality of an instrument, are reduced when the edges can be cut precisely and finished slightly rounded as for finger holes, for example. The best wood for a wind instrument thus has a high density and a fine grain to obtain an optimal finish of the tube walls and finger holes. Because the breath of the musician introduces significant amounts of water into the air column, the material should further absorb a little moisture since water droplets on the tube wall spoil the sound. At the same time, the material should be dimensionally stable when exposed to moisture, so that the instrument remains tuned over a significant amount of time. All the woods named earlier have these qualities to a larger extent than others. Their resistance to moisture is frequently further improved by oiling or by impregnating with paraffin. ]

The problem is that the density only tells part of the story. It is true that a lot of good tonewoods tend to be very dense, but there are many exceptions - both heavy woods that are ‘dead’ and light woods that respond well. Those exceptions are enough to make density a very unreliable indicator of how good a particular wood will be for instrument making. Density is only one of a number of factors that govern how wood responds to sound - stiffness, surface hardness, grain structure and several other parameters are equally important.

When you tap a piece of wood you are assessing not just density, but all those factors that affect the sound, and the way they interact in that particular timber. Tapping a lump of wood gives you two bits of information: the pitch tells you what part of the frequency spectrum it responds best to (which equates to the timbre of the finished instrument), and how well it rings (which equates to the sustain of the instrument). Unfortunately, how it rings is pretty much impossible to measure, all you can hope for is a subjective description. But the acoustic velocity, which equates directly to the pitch of the tap, can be measured and expressed as a number.

If you look at timber listings from tonewood dealers, they will almost always either describe the tap tone, or quote an acoustic velocity figure. Either of these gives you far, far more information about how the wood will perform acoustically than just the density alone. For guitars the ‘ringing’ factor, which tells you about sustain, is important, but for woodwind which, like violins, are constantly excited, it is far less important and it is really only the pitch or acoustic velocity you need to consider.

BTW, that paper by John Backus is seriously flawed - weejie’s not going to like this, but anyone with some understanding of physics should be able to see the truth in what I’m going to say:

His research and findings are no doubt correct - “However, recent work has shown that the sound radiated from the walls is negligible compared with the sound normally radiated by the instrument, and that the effect of the wall vibrations on the internal standing wave is also negligible….” - I can’t argue with that.

But his conclusion is a complete non sequitur: “….It follows that the material of the (woodwind) instrument is not a factor of its tone; if a gold flute sounds different from a silver one, it is because of differences in the structural dimensions and not because of the difference in the metal. ” - He has made the assumption that the only mechanism by which the body of the pipe could colour the sound is by vibrating in sympathy and re-radiating. But we know that isn’t the case. All the body needs to do to colour the sound is to absorb some frequencies, taking them out of the air column, and reflect other frequencies back into the air column.

All materials tend to absorb low frequencies and reflect high frequencies. how much they absorb or reflect, and the frequency where they change from absorption to reflection varies from material to material (and correlates directly to the decay rate and frequency of the tap tone). So using different materials for the pipe will inevitably alter the tonal character of the sound. Whether the range of change is discernible to the human ear or not I could not say, but I can say with certainty that there will be some colouration of the sound according to the body material. (Gut instinct and experience with stringed instruments would lead me to expect that the difference between say, blackwood and maple would be discernible.)

The situation is exactly the same with the back of a guitar - the guitar body is a container for a vibrating air mass, exactly like the pipe body. But in this case the pressure amplitude is much lower, so there is absolutely no question of the back vibrating sympathetically and re-radiating (besides, it’s usually so heavily damped by the player’s beer gut that it couldn’t vibrate even if it wanted to). But no one (with possibly one exception) would say that the wood chosen for the back doesn’t affect the timbre of a guitar. What is happening is that the back absorbs or reflects differently across the frequency spectrum, according to the properties of the wood. This same effect MUST happen within a pipe (the laws of physics don’t change). The only issue is whether the difference in sound colouration from one wood to another is sufficiently large to be discernible. My guess is that it is - I can’t see any other logical reason why wind makers would choose the woods that they do.

>>“”All the body needs to do to colour the sound is to absorb some frequencies, taking them out of the air column, and reflect other frequencies back into the air column.“ What is the mechanism of this absorption in a flute ?”

Surface deformation.

First of all, in answer to one of your earlier posts, sound waves are pulses of pressure, and because pressure exerts a force equally in all directions, it doesn’t matter whether the pulses are moving towards the surface, as in sound bouncing off a wall, or along the surface as in a pipe, in both cases, the pulse of pressure is bound on one side by the surface, which will either absorb energy from it or not.

Now imagine the pulse of pressure is a steel ball. If you drop it on a totally rigid surface it will bounce. But there is no such thing as a perfectly rigid surface (if both the ball and surface were perfectly rigid, no energy would be absorbed, and the ball would continue bouncing for ever, without losing height).

so let’s consider non-rigid surfaces. If the surface was perfectly elastic (ie a spring), the ball would hit it, the surface would deform, but then it springs back and the ball is ejected, again with no energy lost, so it still bounces back to the original height. But if the surface was soft, the ball would sink into it, all it’s energy would be absorbed, and it wouldn’t bounce back.

For any real solid material, the surface exhibits a combination of elasticity and ‘softness’ or damping. The ball will bounce back, but how high it bounces (in other words how much energy is absorbed or given back) will be dependent on the ratio of elasticity to damping. And the surface will absorb or reflect energy in the same way, no matter what hits it - it could be a steel ball or it could be a pulse of high pressure/density air.

It gets more complicated, because all materials also act a little bit like Silly Putty, or a corn flour paste. their elasticity/damping ration changes depending on how frequently you hit them - they are absorbing energy from each impact, and if they don’t have sufficient ‘recovery time’ to dissipate that energy before you hit them again they lose some of their damping and become more elastic. That is why materials reflect sound at high frequencies, but absorb at low frequencies, where they have more recovery time between pulses.

There is also another situation to consider: when you drop the ball on the surface, the surface deflects, then springs back, ejecting the ball, but like a rubber sheet it bounces back past its original position (using some of the energy absorbed from the ball). If you drop another ball at just the right time, so that it hits the surface just as it passes the top of its movement and starts going back down, some of the energy from the first ball gets transferred to the second ball and it will actually bounce back higher than you dropped it from. Drop a series of balls at regular intervals, so that each one hits the surface at just the right time, and energy is constantly transferred from one ball to the next. It only works if the timing is just right and the balls are always hitting at the right point in the cycle. This is resonance. When it happens the surface becomes very reflective, as almost all the energy absorbed from one ball is given back to the next. Because of that its effect is much more profound than general reflection/absorption, but it is generally a hindrance rather than a help to the instrument maker, because it only happens at specific frequencies, causing ‘blips’ in the frequency response, or wolf notes.

It is this resonance, or sympathetic vibration, that was being investigated in the papers weejie referenced, but hopefully you can see that it is not the only way that wood can colour sound.

Re: Bloodwood vs Blackwood

Yes. If you touch a vibrating string your fingertip absorbs energy (which you feel). Because your fingertip is soft (very well damped) the energy is dissipated in the flesh, not reflected or re-radiated. There is also an effect on the string - if it was plucked, it stops resonating almost immediately, because your finger soaks up all the vibrational energy very quickly. If it is being bowed it is constantly being fed energy, so it continues to vibrate, but the amplitude is reduced and the tone is changed.

But if you touch the string with something hard (like a fret) the vibrational energy isn’t absorbed, but reflected back into the string, so it keeps on vibrating with no loss of amplitude or change in timbre.

And exactly the same things happen if it is a column of air that is vibrating rather than a string.

There is one complication to all that. If you touch your finger to the string at a nodal point (exactly half or a quarter of the string length) it wont stop it vibrating. In the case of the first node (centre of the string), at the fundamental frequency that is the point of maximum movement, so you will stop it vibrating at that frequency. But at the even harmonic frequencies above that ( ie the same note in higher octaves) the vibrating string doesn’t actually move at that point, so you don’t absorb any energy and it keeps vibrating - the end result is that touching the string lightly in the middle raises the pitch by an octave, rather than killing it - the harmonics that most guitarists are familiar with. Exactly the same thing happens if you replace the string with an air column, and it accounts (in part) for the uneven spacing of the finger holes in woodwind: logically you might expect the finger holes of a whistle to be all the same size and spaced like the frets of a guitar - after all, their job is to shorten the air column just as the frets shorten the string. But if you made a whistle that way, then on certain notes the soft fingertip or keypad would land up at a nodal point, making the whistle play a higher harmonic rather than the fundamental of that particular note. (A soft key pad that isn’t at a nodal point will still absorb energy, but at all frequencies, so its effect is just to make the note quieter and will be even across all notes). A big part of the woodwind maker’s art is in positioning the holes so that they don’t coincide with any nodal points, whatever note is played, then sizing them to put the instrument in tune. (And when an instrument is overblown by an octave or a fifth, the spacing between the nodes reduces by 1/2 or 1/3, which makes them more likely to hit holes, which is why some ‘not quite right’ instruments play fine in the fundamental octave, but fall apart when overblown)

>>“ I was thinking about energy transfer because I am a bit confused about this ‘tap tone’ business. ”

When you tap a piece of wood, it resonates at its own resonant frequency, and the decay of the vibration is controlled by the internal damping of the wood, so you hear a tone, and you hear how long it rings for. Those two parameters are dependent on three properties of the wood: The density, the elasticity or spring constant, and the hardness or damping factor (The pitch is also dependent on the dimensions of the wood). The pitch is a function of density and spring constant, the length of ring is a function of spring constant and damping factor. From the tap tone you can’t measure any one of those properties, only the way that they are working in combination (Just like with guitar strings, you can’t tell the gauge of the string from the pitch, it might be a light string at low tension, or a heavy string at high tension).

The pitch of the tap isn’t really what we are looking at (as well as the properties of the wood it is also dependent on the dimensions, and for a guitar back we are going to plane it down so thin that the resonant frequency drops off the bottom of the audible range). But the pitch gives us information about density and elasticity. Likewise with the ring time, how long the wood itself resonates isn’t important, but it gives us information about how elastic and how hard it is. And the hardness, stiffness and density all affect how it absorbs or reflects sound, as in my last post. So if you choose a wood with a long tap tone, you are choosing a wood that is hard and stiff, so will reflect sound well, choose one that has a low pitched tap, and you know it is also heavy in relation to its stiffness, so frequency at which it crosses over from bass absorption to treble reflection will be correspondingly low. I haven’t explained that particularly well, but the thing to realize is that it’s an indirect sort of thing - the way the wood resonates when tapped tells us something about the properties of the wood, and knowing about those properties then tells us how the wood will absorb or reflect sound.

Specific gravity is an ABSOLUTE measurement, not a relative one. If you say “substance x has an S.G. of 1.2”, I know exactly how dense it is, If it was a relative measurement (like dBs - db on its own is just a ratio, to make it meaningful you have to specify what you are measuring against: DBV, DBmW, whatever. And as soon as you do that, it becomes an absolute measurement, not a relative one.) you would always have to relate it to a base point (“substance X is three S.G.s more dense than substance Y”).

What I think might be confusing you is the fact that S.G. can be treated as a UNITLESS measure. Because specific gravity is defined as the density of the material/the density of water, whatever units of density you are using for density cancel out on the top and bottom of the equation, leaving a number with no units.

The nice thing about that is that it doesn’t matter what units of density you are using, whether it is grams per cubic centimetre, pounds per cubic foot, or carats per cubic furlong, as long as you know what the density of water is in your chosen units, you just multiply that by the S.G. to find the density in your chosen units. (and if you use SI units you don’t even have to do any multiplication - because the density of water is 1 gram per cubic centimeter, you multiply the S.G. by one to give the density.)

But being a UNITLESS measurement is not at all the same as being a RELATIVE measurement. S.G. has a fixed base point (the density of water) and any measurement of S.G. is a measurement of deviation from that one fixed point. A relative unit does not have a fixed base point - it can only be used to measure the RELATIVE size of two values. If a measurement unit can be used to describe the absolute value of something, without reference to anything else (which S.G. can) then it is an absolute measurement. If a measurement can only describe the relative value of two things in relation to each other, without putting an absolute value on either (like a dB, but unlike S.G.) then it is a RELATIVE measurement. This isn’t even ‘O’ grade, kids learn this in academy 3rd year.

>>“Do you agree on this: when blowing the ‘all holes closed’ note on a flute is the sound at the two open ends in phase or out of phase ?”

I’m not quite sure what you are asking - it is a single standing wave in the pipe, and a single wave can’t be in phase or out of phase, phase is a relative relationship between two wave forms. If you are thinking of the forward and reflected waves that combine to create the standing wave, then they are always in phase, at all points in the tube (if they weren’t you wouldn’t get the standing wave.

If you are thinking about the pressure and velocity waves, they are always 90deg out of phase at every point in the tube, including the ends.

If you are still thinking about what you can feel with your finger, you need to be very careful: If you put your finger exactly a 1/4 wavelength away from the end of the flute (at the pressure anti-node) you will feel the pressure wave. If you put your finger right over the end of the pipe the flute then behaves as a closed pipe, which has a pressure antinode at the closed end, so you are still feeling the pressure wave, but the fundamental frequency has dropped an octave. If you put your finger somewhere in between things get complicated - because the end of the pipe isn’t sealed, you get an open pipe reflected wave from the end of the pipe (but at a reduced amplitude in comparison to what it was without your finger) but you also get a closed pipe reflection from your finger. Your finger is further away from the mouthpiece than the end of the pipe, so this wave is phase shifted slightly behind the open pipe reflection. If you move your finger you change both the relative amplitudes of the two reflected waves, and the phase shift. This is the physics that is used by jazz trumpeters when they do that ‘wah-wah’ thing with the mute.

To understand the bit about the finger near the end, you need to remember that the standing wave, with its nodes and anti nodes, is actually made of two traveling waves - there is one wave traveling from the fipple (L to R in the diagram) at the speed of sound, when it gets to the end it is reflected back, so an identical reflected wave is traveling back from R to L at the speed of sound. As the two waves pass each other, at each point along the pipe, at any given time, their values are added together. It turns out that at certain points (the nodes) , when one wave is +ve, the other is always -ve by exactly the same amount, so they always cancel out. At other points (the anti nodes) they always have the same sign and size, so when two peaks arrive, it results in a peak, when they are both zero the result is zero, and when they both go negative the result goes negative. That is very hard to explain without some heavy duty maths, but you could probably see what is happening by drawing two sine waves on strips of paper, moving them step at a time past each other and plotting the sum of their values at a number of points with each step (or google ‘standing wave’, I’m sure you’ll find a graphic explanation somewhere - standing wave theory applies to all sorts of other things besides flutes).

Once you’ve grasped how that standing wave is being made from two traveling waveforms, you can probably see what is going to happen if you add a third wave - reflected from a thumb placed just past the end of the flute - its still going to be add to the primary wave and enhance or cancel it, but because it is coming along a little later (the time it takes for sound to travel from the end of the pipe to the thumb and back) the places where it always cancels or always enhances are going to be shifted along the pipe a little, away from the nodes and anti nodes of the original standing wave. The end result is a standing wave with a non- sinusoidal waveform.
