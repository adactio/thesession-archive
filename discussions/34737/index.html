Do The Irish Really Own Traditional Irish Music, And Ballads.?

Added by Michael Dineen on September 22nd, 2014

Do The Irish Really Own Traditional Irish Music, And Ballads.?

This has been on my mind, for some time. Our instrumental tradition, is an Irish thing, there is no doubt about it. However, I feel that it belongs to Ireland, and the Irish were generous and passed it on. The minute someone musical hears jigs, reels, and hornpipes, they would know that those tunes are associated with Ireland. In fact a lot of trad instruments, are not made in Ireland at all, but there is a special Irish way of playing them. Ireland’s song tradition is in the same boat. Songs like The Mountain Dew, Kelly The Boy From Killane, and Mc’Alpines Fusiliers, are certainly truly Irish. They have travelled along with traditional tunes, and found their way to other countries. I would also have to ask, does any country own a particular music style.? Irish musicians have played jazz, but we are just borrowing it. It really belongs to New Orleans. We have also imitated country music, but it is borrowed from America. What are the views on this topic.?

Re: Do The Irish Really Own Traditional Irish Music, And Ballads.?

Of course, the origins of this music are diverse and the history is complex. But what we are talking about is a kind of music that is playe *now*, comprising a unique mix of influences, that is widely recognised as ‘Irish’, that evolved into its present form in Ireland, that has more-or-less distinct varieties from different regions of Ireland and that has been played, taught and learned by people, in or out of Ireland, that identify themselves as ‘Irish’ for generations.

Perhaps, rather than ‘ownership’, we should be talking about ‘guardianship’. As a student of Irish Traditional Music, I would put more trust in a teacher whose family, friends and neighbours have played the music for several generations, than I would in one who, like myself, has taken up the music as an interest outside their native culture, regardless of how accomplished a musician they may be. I will always regard myself as a *guest* to Irish music - at best, a welcome one (there has been the odd occasion I have felt more like an intruder) - and I would not want it any other way.

When we talk about ‘The Irish’, we have to keep in mind that the vast majority of Irish people have little knowledge of or interest in traditional music, let alone play it, so it makes little sense to endow insdiscriminately a whole nation with the status of ‘guardian’. Furthermore, there are a great many Irish players of traditional music who would, I think, regard themselves more as guests than hosts.

<<I would also have to ask, does any country own a particular music style.? Irish musicians have played jazz, but we are just borrowing it. It really belongs to New Orleans. We have also imitated country music, but it is borrowed from America. What are the views on this topic.?>>

“Ownership” of jazz: since you mention it . . . this is really important: I am sure you know New Orleans is not a country, nor has it been the only home of jazz by any means. the tradition spread *quickly* from New Orleans to Chicago, Kansas, City, NYC, etc.,—not to mention the related and influential practice of blues in rural and urban areas both—to the point where there was even a “West Coast” school by the 50s-60s. More important, though, the more fraught question has not been which country/region but which population within, given the African-American roots of the music and the various ways in which participation by Caucasians and others have taken shape and have been received. So the notion of a “country” is not necessarily the marker.

The mention of N’awlins brings up an interesting nuance: I think some would say that much of the jazz one hears there these days is “museum-y”—not all, to be sure, but the stuff in the street and so on, whereas the jazz tradition has twisted and turned in so many ways since the New Orleans birth. Round about the 80s there was some debate over whether someone like Wynton Marsalis was more “genuine,” because he reached back to earlier styles, or whether someone like Steve Coleman was, because he . . .didn’t.

There is much ethnomusicological and post-colonial writing on this topic if you are really interested. In my own studies I have observed that it is *often*, not always, the “legit” practitioners of a given music who are more content to see it embraced by “others,” which sometimes those outside of the tradition in question want it to stay as a museum. That can lead to objectifying the material and its practitioners. More generally, the idea of stasis has often been said to be applied in a way that is objectionable.

And the question of dominant/nondominant cultures is high and fraught.
