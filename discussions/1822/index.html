Re: High

There’s a certain tolerance level that most techs recommend you stay within. A new bridge should be carved specially for the fiddle by an experienced person so that the curve of the top of the bridge matches the curve of the top of the fiddle and the feet fit exactly to the save curve. The nut will also influence how high the action is (and of course you should make sure that there’s not any gutters carved into your fingerboard, too). I’d say take the fiddle to a good tech and ask if the bridge and nut are carved correctly.

If your fiddle has never been set up by a good tech, have it done at least once so you can see how it should be set up. And be prepared to notice a marked difference in how easy your fiddle is to play and sound good!

Good luck --

yes, some people do shave down the bridge a bit, but only from the top edge, as it (should have been) fitted exactly to the curve of the top by a skilled craftsperson. Oh -- and I just noticed that I never answered the last question (I’m a little distracted, due to some house-selling stuff, sorry). Most experienced players (experienced enough that they carve their own bridges, anyway, have different ways of carving the bridge. Some not only change the top curve to match the fiddle’s belly and the feet to match, but will also open up some of the scrolls and such. Many fiddle makers will set up a fiddle the way they want it to sound, including a specially carved bridge. Never put a bridge blank directly onto a fiddle. It’ll be too tall and most likely the feet won’t fit properly onto your fiddle. I had one of those newish bridges with the “automatic” feet that swivel in a little round socket to match any fiddle, and it was okay, but when I had the fiddle reset by Dale Morris, he told me that, while he’d never seen one of them before and thought it was an interesting thing, he thought the sound was inferior.

And, by the way, I know the diff between a well set up fiddle and a not-well set up fiddle because Dale set up my fiddle after I’d been playing it for two years. It was like getting a new fiddle!

Re: High

Carol, a concert violinist told me that it is indeed best if you have the action as low as possible without the strings buzzing on the fingerboard with strong bowing. It really does make it that much easier to play the difficult bits, especially in the high register. The same advice applies to the cello and viola.
The only thing to be aware of is that if you cut the bridge down too low the tone can lose its brilliance somewhat. If that is an important issue, as it will be for a soloist, the preferred strategy is to raise the fingerboard without altering the bridge. This is done by putting an insert between the neck and the fingerboard. Obviously, this can only be done by a specialist craftsman who knows what they’re doing. I’ve seen 18c violins that have had this modification done in order to improve the brilliance for modern performing.

Re: High

Andee, my post which just preceded yours showed how to reduce the action without impairing the tonal quality. Very flat profile bridges were common in the baroque because there was a lot of chord playing and multiple stopping.
The height of the action also depends on the type of string you use. If you use steel-core strings then you can afford to have a close clearnace between strings and fingerboard because these strings don’t have a very wide amplitude of vibration. On the other hand, if you use polymer-core or gut-core strings than you need more clearance because the amplitude of vibration is wider and the strings are physically thicker. When a new bridge is installed the strings will tend to bite down into the bridge and so the clearance between string and fingerboard will gradually reduce over a perid of time. This is especially so with steel-core strings. A good fiddle craftsman would take this into account when installing the bridge. A while ago, when I had my old German fiddle refurbished, the repairer specifically asked me what strings I would be using so that he could cut the bridge accordingly.
Anyway, as a cellist, I don’t notice these minute differences in fiddle action :)

Re: High

If you bring your fiddle to someone to get it set up & they say there is only a right or wrong way to do it then go someplace else. Setting up a fiddle is a very personal affair. The trade off between for lower action is volume, the lower the action the quieter you’ll get. You can offset this by using louder strings, but you don’t loose loads of volume either. The soundpost & bridge are both important to the tone, but the bridge has an effect on the action the radius (curve) of the strings etc etc.
Try cutting some of your own bridges, blank bridges & sandpaper are both cheap, if you mess up a bridge or don’t like how it turned out than you can scrap it. Here’s a quick way to sand the feet to the belly of you violin, first take all the strings of it. Put 100 grit sand paper on the belly of the fiddle (sand side away from the fiddle!!) then run the bridges feet over the sandpaper which is the exact shape of your fiddles belly. Make sure you keep it perfectly perpendicular to the belly. You can use a right angled piece of scrap for this, or you can buy a jig that does the same thing(Bridge Foot Fitting Tool http://atlanticviolinsupplies.com/MakingSupplies.asp ).You don’t want to take too much off of the feet, you just want them to fit perfectly to the belly.
As far as the top of the bridge the traditional rule of thumb is to put a 4mm spacer where the e string will be at the bridge side of the end of the finger board. Put a 6mm spacer where the G string would be. The take a straight edge from the nut & mark where the edge meets the bridge for both the E & the G. Than draw a curved line which matches the radius of your fingerboard. & sand down to that. You might want to take it down more than that cause that will give you fairly high action. But it’s a ball park to get you started. Do some more research & give it a shot. You won’t do any harm to the violin by cutting another bridge. They go on & come off easy as well.
Good luck

↳ Posted by Will Harmon on June 30th, 2003.

Re: High

One point about the high action that hasn’t been mentioned before is that if you’re playing in the higher positions, as the classical (and jazz) violinists often have to, and the string isn’t all that close to the fingerboard, then pressing the string right down onto the fingerboard will sharpen the pitch of that note, because you’re stretching the string and increasing its tension. Of course, if you’re used to it you’ll compensate naturally, but rapid passage work in the higher positions will be just that little more difficult to finger. Cellists are well aware of this because of the higher tension and thickness of their strings compared with those of a fiddle. So, if you need to reduce the action, rather than lower the bridge, which will tend to adversely affect the tone, the better thing is to have the fingerboard raised slightly by a wedge insert, as I mentioned in a previous post. Obviously, this is a job for the skilled craftsman and not the amateur, because there is precision work involved.
Trevor

Re: High

The sweet spot? I wish I knew. It’s just sort of there, except when it isn’t. Mostly, you imagine the rich, fat, dark sound you want coming from your fiddle, and then you relax and let your bow create it. I’ve been told by some fine players that it’s somewhat an inconsistent thing, except when they’re in the zone, so I no longer feel bad about those days when I can’t find the sweet spot.

When I’m in that sweet spot, I notice that I’m all over the bow, from tip to frog, doing whatever feels right for the sound I want. And my hand and arm are relaxed, and most of the contact comes through my index finger, like I’m playing the strings with my finger instead of this long stick and hank of horsehair. There;s also this sense that the sound is coming from my chest, like the fiddle is just an extra resonating chamber. And it just happens, effortlessly, like breathing at sea level.

As for how often to change a bridge--if it’s a good piece of wood, and you like the tone you’re getting, don’t change it. Do keep an eye on it for structural problems. Some old bridges start to cup or curl. Eyeball it from the side--the back (side facing the tailpiece) should be straight up and down, at a 90-degree angle to the top of the fiddle. If it’s just tilted like “\” or “/” loosen the tension on the strings a little and gently straighten her out. But if it’s bent into a “)” or “(” shape, it’s time to replace it. You can also run into problems with the strings cutting down into the top notches, going too deep over time and becoming pinched.

My golden rule for fiddles is--if you like the sound and feel, and it’s not falling apart, don’t change anything.

About the sweet spot. I’m a classical player turned fiddler and I’ve also taught young ones for 21 years. We call the “sweet spot” the highway…sort of goes along with the bridge. Anyway, if you were to draw an imaginary line from the top of one f hole to the other, that would be your spot. The idea is that your sending the vibrations into the violin so they can bounce around and come out again. You can experiment by playing starting near the finger board and slowly moving toward the bridge. You can definitely hear when you hit the “spot”. The violin just comes alive and wakes up. Of course, playing in tune and with a straight bow all work to enhance that sound.

Re: High

I agree with Suzy that there’s a place on the strings where the bow gets the most bang for the buck, and that’s where you want to be to project your sound and cut through a session.

But the “other” sweet spot has more to do with how the hair contacts the strings, and this can work even if you’re playing right over the end of the fingerboard--something Irish trad players often do. There’s a difference between the sound violinists aim for and the one that trad players prefer, analogous to the difference betwee the polished, hollow sound a classical flutist gets compared to the reedy bark a trad flute player strives for. And this sweet spot has more to do with bow speed, nuances of pressure, and using different parts of the bow to accommodate the different sounds produced by different strings.

That’s a topic we haven’t touched on yet at thesession.org, to my knowledge. In short, I usually use a lighter touch and a bit more bow (typically nearer the tip) on my e string to help soften its voice, and more pressure at the start of the stroke, less bow, and nearer the frog on the G and sometimes D strings to maximize their fat growling tone. It’s similar to how a flute player has to slightly tweak his or her embouchure, air column, etc. to get a strong low D or E versus the “easier” higher notes. You can’t just assume that the same technique will produce the same quality of sound out of every note.

Re: High

Don’t forget that the “sweet spot” will move as your left hand moves up the fingerboard. It doesn’t matter much in ITM because virtually all the playing is in the first position, but if you do need to move up the firngerboard then you’ll find the “sweet spot” moves proportionally towards the bridge. Basically, the “sweet spot” is in the region of the 7th-9th harmonics, about 3 octaves up the string from the note being played.
A useful way to check where you’re bowing on the string is to first clean all the old rosin off the string, using isosprop or similar. Then rosin your bow and play for a few minutes WITHOUT looking at the fiddle. Then inspect the strings for the region of rosin to show you where you’ve just been bowing. Repeat process until you get it right :)
