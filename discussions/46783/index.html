Humorous (in retrospect) musical accidents

….And I don’t mean accidents as in accidentals.
Many of us have had things like a plectrum falling down the sound hole of a guitar then after much shaking and holding it upside the plec finally emerges. That kind of thing.
Getting the ball rolling….
Recently I was peeling an orange over my accordion when one of the pips fell down into the crevasse between the belows. Much swearing later I did eventually extricate the offending pip. Moral of the story? Bleedin’ obvious innit? Don’t peel oranges over yer box.
During lockdown, as part of the clapping for the NHS thing (still amazes me that people did that in Tory constituencies….anyway…) in our very friendly/neighbourly street, we went a bit further - myself and some neighbours (proper, classical orchestra players) got together and played some tunes. When I started playing my flute, no noise came out. Allofasudden I realised I still had the fold-up flutestand inserted in the foot joint (which is where it goes when transporting the flute, before you ask.) Much to the amusement of the other players and the audience - mostly my neighbours. Funny now I think about it.
Anyone else?

Re: Humorous (in retrospect) musical accidents

This story is not concerning Irish music, but me in my alter ego as a klezmer musician*. There was a small street festival in my town in October, with a (covered) open-air stage, for which I had been roped in to man the mixing desk. This was fine up to 3pm, but my klezmer band was due to play at 5pm and desperately needed to rehearse, as we were two key members down and those of use that were available had not been in the same place at the same time for several months. Even though I had given plenty of advance warning of this, nobody had been recruited to take over from me; I tried my best to find someone, but the most eligible candidate was already settled into a day of drinking and could not be persuaded out of the pub.

So, we came back from our last-minute rehearsal and set-list-trimming session to find the desk deserted. Since we were already a bit behind schedule, we just had to plug in, turn up and hope for the best. As it turned out, the balance was not too bad. Then it came to my one mandolin number in the set (I play mostly piano accordion in this band)… As the keyboard player lauched into his customary intro, the colour drained from my face as I realised I had omitted to plug in my mandolin!

Now, it is important to mention at this point that, unlike ay other band I have ever played with, I wear a tie when I play with this band (not sure why particularly, it has just become my ‘habit’).

As piano-man patiently cycled through his intro half a dozen times, I hurriedly found my jack-jack leads, plugged in mandolin to preamp to desk, pushed the fader up to a plausible position and put the strap over my head. To my relief, it sounded fine and neither inaudible nor overpowering. All was well for the fist few bars – until my tie, which had apparently been resting on the upper rib of the mandolin, suddenly flopped down over the strings, so that I was physically incapable of playing them. I had no choice but to stop mid-tune, tuck tie behind mandolin and pick it up again. Fortunately (or unfortunately), nobody else seemed to notice – which perhaps says something about the dispensibility of my playing.

It seems that all the best stories are the things that nobody else notices. I used to play in a London-based ceilidh band. We were playing a wedding ceilidh somewhere in the environs of London in a marquee, with the kind of flimsy plastic garden chairs that go with the territory. We were all seated on the said chairs on a raised stage. Bear in mind, this was not a ‘proper’ ceilí band like the ones in Ireland and its cultural diaspora, but more of a hotchpotch of traditional tunes, popular Irish and non-Irish songs and terrible jokes. One of the staples of our repertoire was Dingle Regatta, complete with the corny stand-in/sit-down caper in the third part (for those of us that were not too proud or rapt); I was game on this occasion. I was positioned towards the back of the stage and, unbeknown to me, with every up-down, my chair was inching backwards, until, on one enthusiastic re-seating, I disappeared between the back of the stage and the wall of the marquee. It could have been quite nasty if not the fact that i. the stage was only raised about 3ft off the ground; ii. the landing was on grass; iii. the chair was flexible and absorbed most of the shock. As it was, I climbed back onstage with a slightly grazed elbow and carried on playing. Everyone was completely oblivious except for the drummer, one of the wedding guests (who probably thought it was part of the act) and myself.

*‘Klezmer’ is simply Yiddish for ‘musician’ (from two Hebrew words meaning ‘vessel of song’), so ‘klezmer musician’ is tautological.

About whistles in the wind: I usually play my Parks Walkabout, a three piece whistle. I’ve found that by turning the top joint or head around so the window faces my throat, I can often play in a stiff breeze. In fact, I’ve played that way while coasting downhill on my bicycle!

About performance flubs: Years ago I was asked to play fiddle or violin for a family wedding in Florida. I traveled 900 miles south to rehearse with another family member, a young woman playing oboe. We worked up some lovely music, both classical pieces and some Irish airs - Give Me Your Hand, for example. I thought we sounded lovely.

Until the day of the wedding, that is. It was held outdoors. We began our set list as the crowd began arriving, then as the ceremony proceeded. But the oboe player apparently lost her mind! She began playing wrong notes, going silent, jumping back in on the wrong measure, missing repeats, even playing the wrong piece! As I soldiered on, she leaned over and said “Something’s biting me!”

She was in a long formal dress with much of her back and shoulders exposed, and some species of fly must have been attracted by her perfume. The entire program was a musical disaster. Yet afterwards, at least six attendees came up to us and said the music was absolutely beautiful!

Apparently, people don’t really listen. Or they can’t tell the difference!

Re: Humorous (in retrospect) musical accidents

This one illustrates the dangers of being a pianist. You are at the mercy of whatever instrument is available at the venue. Like Tracie’s story, “it came out alright”. I was the accompanist for a choral group who had been engaged to perform some Christmas music at a luncheon for one of the the service organizations (like Rotary or Lions). The luncheon was at a very old tavern, the kind that George Washington slept at, and there were many small rooms. We had been assured there was a piano. There was, indeed. Two rooms away from the luncheon space and around two corners, tucked into a closet that had been built around the instrument. There was no possibility of removing it from the closet and the chorus could not sing 2 rooms away from the luncheon guests. The only solution was to snag one of the singers as an extra conductor. He positioned himself about 10 feet away from me and in view, through an archway, of the actual conductor who stood in another archway not too far from the singers. And, Lord help me, we were doing “Sleighride” which has a rambunctious piano part.
I had to bang the bejeezus out of the old clunker just so the singers could even imagine they could hear the piano. Somehow, it worked. But I learned a huge lesson that day about vetting ISOs (instrument-shaped objects) before a gig!

our chamber group was playing in a very small church and we were supposed to be masked. Even our flutist had a nifty mask that went over front of her flute. Being the highest-pitch instrument, she usually had the melody…when suddenly, while we were playing, she just went silent! And started laughing!

Somehow she had “insucked” part of the mask into the flute and it got stopped up!?! We just decided she was going to play Unmasked - they were just going to have to live with a few breath particles!

Re: Humorous (in retrospect) musical accidents

A friend was playing a ceili back in NYC. She plays flute and always sits at the edge of the stage, stage right, next to the kitchen door. After a few sets the box player said he was thirsty. While they were in the middle of playing the next set, a large tray was delivered with drinking glasses and a big pitcher of ice water. My friend set down her flute and poured glasses of water for everyone, passing them on down the stage. The box player took a big gulp and said,”This water tastes off!” She looked down and realized the pitcher was directly under the end of her flute.

Now the agreement is to set the pitcher and glasses in the middle of the stage.
