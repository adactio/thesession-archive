Added by Eimead on July 18th, 2014

How do you handle public folk trying to run your session?

Was sessioning last night, and the bar was fairly quiet but there were a few heads about, in particular there was one couple, middle aged and generally nice people but every time we finished a set or a song they’d be roaring to play this and that, and at first we kinda obliged them with a song or 2, but then it gets to the stage where they’re calling for “Ride on” and “The Wild Rover” and stuff like that, and you just want to play tunes. We kinda just smiled and fobbed them off saying “We’ll do it in a while” or “Ah, the lad that usually sings that isn’t here tonight” and anything else we could think of.

I’m aware that our money is coming from the bar owner who is going to be putting the satisfaction of his customers first so I can’t exactly tell them to go and shite, so my question is, do any of you have a tricks, tips or diplomatic solutions to when you’ve got people like this in? Something to the effect of “We’re glad that you’re enjoying the music, and we don’t mind doing a song or 2 for you over the course of the night, but go on shut the fuck up?”

Re: How do you handle public folk trying to run your session?

If we know it, we usually sing it for them. Telling them we don’t know it and offering to let them sing it works nine out of ten times, and in that one time they take you up on it, either you are pleasantly surprised, or it is over within a couple of minutes. If they persist, and want to take over the session, we tell them we are sorry, but we came here to play tunes, and get back to business.
In the end, the public nature of a session works best when everyone gets a bit of input into what happens. Just like when a newbie shows up, you let them pick a couple of tunes, and play them at their pace.

Actually not really.

If a college lecturer prepares a lecture on confocal microscopy, he won’t want to take requests on talking about quantum mechanics. In a session, much the same, your session gang will usually only play the sets with which they as an ensemble are familiar, unless someone occasionally wants to lead a set on their own. “If you do not want to entertain the public, the best thing is to have sessions in peoples houses … By accepting free beer, you have entered into an obligation to keep people in the publicans bar”

It should be said that not all pubs hosting sessions are so generous with their drinks - or any kind of material reward. The positive effect of a session on bar takings varies from place to place and from pub to pub. If a session happens *purely* for the benefit of the musicians, then whether or not the publican offers any reward is a matter of decency rather than obligation.

Taking a situation where a session does draw in punters and boost drink sales, and the musicians are rewarded in drinks, food, money, accommodation, whatever… , I think it is fair to say that, of those punters that come to listen to the session, most appreciate it for what it is, and enjoy the very fact that the musicians are playing what they want to play, when they want to, in the way they want to. Whilst pandering to requests might appease a certain crowd, it could alienate the preferred audience. A publican that is in tune with their clientele would understand this.

Re: How do you handle public folk trying to run your session?

John, I don’t think anyone here is advocating snubbing the folks in the pub, and just playing what they want to (although I know a few people who are just that rigid in the way they approach things). Instead, they are talking about things you can do to compromise between what the musicians want to do, and what the others in the pub want to hear. After all, a few beers is hardly the kind of wage that turns you into an employee of the pub. We are talking about striking a balance.
Those compromises, and give and take between all present, are often part of what makes sessions special, as they break down the walls that often separate “performers” and “audience” and make everybody part of the fun.
(And on a separate topic, in the band I am in, I write down every request we receive, and each year we go out of our way to learn at least one or two songs that have been requested. Over the last decade, we have built up a set list that allows us to accommodate nearly every request that comes our way. And in a situation where you are a paid professional, that is an important ability.)

Re: How do you handle public folk trying to run your session?

It is a very good question, for this brings up a good discussion, what a session essentially is.
One word to sessions in general: They are a gem of the Irish culture. No entrance fee, no fee - just a few beers - we can only dream of something like this here in Germany. And also the publicans can dream of it. If an entrance fee is being charged, it’s a business thing. A session is something different.
My opinion is: I’ve never heard about a session being a request concert. What I expect, when I go to a pub to listen to a session, is to listen to the music that is being played - no matter, which program the musicians have on their list. I enjoy being surprised. If I like the music, I feel enriched, and if I don’t like the music, I enjoy my beer or go to another pub. But if I want to be sure, that I’ll get the music that I like, I should go to a concert of my favourite band.
Well, if somebody comes to you and asks you, if you could play a certain song for him - no doubt, why not do him the favour? It is part of a good mood in the pub - as long as it remains in certain boundaries.
But the situation is completely different, if somebody is roaring to you to play this and that. There are good tips in this discussion to solve this problem politely (and that’s why I love “TheSession”). But if they are of no avail, it’s due to the landlord to settle it. He’s got the authority to stop indecent behaviour. And that’s what it is, when somebody is roaring his wishes right across the audience.
There is a “session etiquette”, and if that isn’t obligatory for the audience as well, then this is the beginning of the end of the fine Irish session culture. https://thesession.org/search?where=&q=session+etiquette
Maybe it is useful, to think about clear conditions. What we should talk about with the publican before we start to play. And it could be useful, to announce that to the audience as well (“We are playing a couple of jigs and reels tonight …”).
