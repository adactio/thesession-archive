Music notation as a prison?

Here’s a metaphor to play with, a prison for both the music and the musician ~ bars everywhere, two-tone, two dimensional?! I prefer songbirds out of the cage, free, in their own wild, natural habitat… The colours of their feathers and songs are so much brighter, enjoyable, moving, magic..

I’ve found myself using this metaphor from time to time. Recently I used it when someone responded in surprise and criticism when being confused by the playing of a friend, “You’re not playing what’s here on the sheet music!” My wife thought I was being a bit too hard in using this metaphor, and that those on the recieving end of it may have taken it more harshly than I’d really intended, or maybe it was the tone of my voice, or the situation?

While I’m critical of this ‘prison’, I am also in full admiration and respect for this means of communication and its usefulness in many regards, it’s history, including its ability to raise our understanding graphically of this thing we love. Life is rhythm. Life is music. The sound of a heartbeat moves me… ;-)

Yes, I know, we’ve been here before, and we’ll surely visit it again and again…

Maybe you need a metaphor with more positive connotations, which still points out the limitations. How about:

The sheet music is but a mere seed of a tune. Like the DNA of a living being, it is a simple code, which contains the building block information of something with unlimited potential. When the seed is germinated in the mind of a person who has experience nurturing tunes, it can grow into a beautiful flower – each instance unique, and beautiful in its own way.

Early on in the process, if you allow yourself, you can learn to shed the husk of the seed, and let it grow in your mind. The growing tune still has all of the information of the seed, but doesn’t need to look back. When I began playing the piano at the local Irish sessions in 1995, I already had many years worth of experience playing jazz, blues, and ragtime as well as various types of popular music such as rock‘n’roll on the piano.

Re: Music notation as a prison?

“The dots are usually wrong” can be true in two reasonably trivial senses:
a) they are not a very good representation of what was transcribed.
b) what was transcribed might be what you hear at your session, but there’s no reason to suppose that that’s the case, and in fact it’s generally not the case.

These are trivial, if you’re a good player and can use your ear to correct for minor (or major) discrepancies of timing or phrasing, or differing versions. (Do you see the lurking question behind this “if”?)

However, I think you have two incorrect starting points.
-What I hear more often is “it’s wrong to learn from the dots”. That is, learning from the dots is not a good way to get this music into you. There are plenty of reasons given in plenty of threads on this topic, so I won’t rehearse them here, just pointing out that you may be misrepresenting the position.

- It’s not quite right that “there isn’t really a wrong way to play a tune”. That’s actually quite wrong. There are in fact many, many right ways to play a tune, but many more wrong ways to play it. Even if you get the notes in the right sequence, with the time values as given on the piece of paper, you may well be playing it wrong if people experienced in the music look at you funny and say “oh, that’s nice, what was that?” and you meant to play Banish Misfortune.

So no, no contradiction here. Nice try, though.

Re: Music notation as a prison?

To give the dots their due, they are a very good way to get a sketch of a piece of music across to someone who has to learn it in a hurry. They also serve to present a summary of the music for extended study, if the music in question conforms to certain conventions which the reader knows well.
When travel was more difficult and sound recordings nonexistent, they were the only form of recording that we had, and so they made possible exchange of musical ideas that would not otherwise have happened.

In addition, sales of sheet music were a source of funding for many musicians - this is why so many transcriptions of symphonies for four-hand piano versions exist.

For a working musician needing to run in to the pit at the last minute and play the third horn part, sheet music is a great thing. For a section player in a big band with a large repertoire, it comes in handy. For a student of classical music needing to visualize an entire symphony at once, it’s essential.

But for the purpose of learning to play Irish music, sheet music is of dubious value.

Re: Music notation as a prison?

Jerone, sheet music is limiting for some people because they are so wedded to it that it inhibits them from relying more on their ears.

Look, the main reason the dots get a bad rap among traditional musicians is that we’ve all run into a painful stereotype: the formally trained musician who’s a whiz at sight reading, and who earnestly believes that this enables them near-instant access to this music. Which they then butcher, because they haven’t spent the necessary time actually *listening* to this music.

Frankly, most formal music education places too heavy an emphasis on notation, fostering both a dependence on it and a strong prejudice *for* it. I know too many formally educated musicians who can’t play without the paper in front of them. That’s tragic--this is an *aural* art form. Nothing can change that basic fact. So when people get stuck on the dots, they never gain the fluency that comes from closing your eyes and listening, and playing responsively from there.

Will,

I have to say… your post allowed me to make a realization that is of quantum proportions. Especially this comment:

“Which is what strikes me as worrisome when a self-confessed jazz major says this: ”Notation is a written language and like any other good book you have to use your imagination to get the most out of it."

I am approaching this from the viewpoint that I do spend hours upon hours listening to this music. Sometimes I will find a tune and listen to it two or three times in a row. I categorize it, dissect it in my brain, anticipate changes, and try to feel for emphasis. Sometimes I will hum the tune to myself all day long at work to truly get a feel for it. I do this with every tune I listen to. Many times I’ve heard good recordings, mediocre recordings, and fantastic recordings of a single tune. Which allows me to understand the good, bad, and ugly of ways to play these tunes.

That said, sometimes I find a tune I’ve never heard of on the session or I see it in a discussion. I pull it up, look at it and make comparisons to the things I’ve heard in the past and anticipate how I feel it was played. I try to look at it and feel how the phrasing should be and how I can apply it to my limited technique without butchering it too badly. Then I play the tune through… although to be honest all the above steps only take a couple of minutes.

My past experiences of listening to other tunes allows me to sight read these tunes and work out most of the kinks by reading the notation only, comparing it to cues of other styles I have learned in the past. We used to call that theory and application. Take what you know and apply it to something unknown.

If I do play that particular notation only tune at my session I listen to it for the first few measures before I join in. Since I spent the time and effort committing it to mechanical memory, I can then focus on the exact timing and phrasing without worrying so much about where I’m putting my fingers.

This was a pretty common habit in the jazz world, a way where those with less talent (like myself) could keep up with others much more talented. Although the advantage here is that there is actual notation in trad music, much better than most jazz music. With jazz, it’s usually the equivalent of a few chord changes written on a cocktail napkin with no sense of time or meter. Talk about sink or swim!

I think the crime of my assumption is that others devote this much time to their hobby as well…

Additionally, I think the assumption by those against notation assume that people like me only read the notation with no other preparation whatsoever.

Re: Music notation as a prison?

ceolachan, this ‘prison’ analogy doesn’t sit well with me, either. Mostly because it gives notation too much power or authority. I know some people have a hard time freeing themselves from the habit of dot reading, but that’s all it is--a habit.

I tend to think of the dots as an optical illusion, one of those black-and-white drawings (http://www.michaelbach.de/ot/) that convinces our brains we’re seeing something that isn’t there.

I have a mantra for music students who are stuck on the dots:

“Music is sound that we hear in our heads, not images on paper. If you don’t have the sound (a tune) in your head, then all the inky paper in the world isn’t going to give you the *music.*”

If they don’t grok this right away, I ask them to sing a familiar melody (Happy Birthday, Amazing Grace, You Are My Sunshine) in their head. Everyone can do this, ***convincingly.*** Then I hand them sheet music for a simple but totally unfamiliar tune (I pick something from a genre they have little to no experience with, and the name and other information has been removed from the page--it’s just the key signature, meter, and the dots). I’ve never had anyone be able to play the unfamiliar music convincingly.

Dots represent a mnemonic device. Symbols written on paper are not music - listening to music to learn it clearly involves the music itself. Dots require an additional layer of interpretation, but have value nevertheless.

Re: Music notation as a prison?

cboody, from my experience teaching private fiddle lessons and frequently getting students crossing over from violin, I’d say there are still a few problems with formal violin pedagogy.

The biggest problem is far too strong an emphasis on long-bow tone, to the near exclusion of anything remotely resembling rhythm, let alone pulse. Frankly, learning to manage the bow from frog to tip and back is more than a lot of students can muster, so they either quit, or they spend year after year plodding through reams of half and whole notes wondering why their bow bounces all over down by the frog and why it whistles and whispers up by the tip. The idea of creating a beat or a pulse with the bow is absolutely foreign to them. A shame.

While I recognize the demand for good tone and full use of the bow in much orchestral music, I think it’s a shame that students are taught more about short bow technique--use of the fingers and wrist, less arm, etc. It’s much easier to manage the bow and get decent tone with short strokes in the middle third of the bow. But I routinely get 3rd, 4th, and even 5th year violinists who’ve never been taught anything about short bowing.

Another big blind spot for formal violin pedagogy is the continued adherence to the notion that “high” “art” music is inherently better (more sophisticated, more worthwhile) than all the folk or “low” forms. At best, this attitude is only implied, at worst, some teachers still actively promote it. They continue to spout misinformation about fiddle music (“never shifts up the neck,” “improper bow holds,” etc.) and to underestimate the value of playing by ear and being able to improvise. In any case, many students end up with an ingrained snobbery that really gets in the way when they discover that they enjoy listening to other genres and might even consider playing them.

Mark O’Connor’s new violin curriculum, based on American fiddle music, could go a fair distance to correcting the course of the old approach. Time will tell.

Compared to these concerns, a violinist’s addiction to the dots is relatively easy to fix…..
