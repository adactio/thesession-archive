Transmission

Hi folks,

I went to a lovely event last night which was a combination of Indian music and storytelling. The storyteller began by telling us that there is a tradition in storytelling that the person who first told the story to the teller is standing behind her as she tells…

…which reminded me of the agitation I’ve been feeling of late around how I learn Irish music, and how I feel about playing in the local session.

I learn a lot of tunes from the internet and printed music these days, and then go to the session and play whay I’ve learned. Sometimes people know the tunes, sometimes not, and sometimes people know different versions. Sometimes, I ask the name of a tune I like and go home and learn it from said sources, but all of this seems to my mind to be putting the cart before the horse.

I often feel resistance to going to the session, and one of the reasons is that I don’t feel genuinely connected to the other musicians either socially or musically. It would mean a great deal to me to be taught some of the tunes that the others play in the traditional oral way, which for me would mean making a little time and effort to get together out of the session, since my ear is not good enought to get a tune down at break-neck speed.

I believe this would make a profound difference to my experience of the session, since I would then have musical and social connection to the other musicians beyond what seems sometimes like a collective showcase of individual home-study.

I would suggest that what can be learned either from the internet or the printed page is simply not the same entity as what is taught in the genuine oral tradition. Like with the storyteller, the tunes I have been taught orally have a personal meaning for me which is about my relationship to the person who taught me, the place it happened, the emotional content of the transimssion and much more besides - basically the spirit of the music as a living bridge genuinely connecting us with our ancestors as well as with our current communities and locations.

I’m not saying that the internet and printed music don’t have their place in transmission, but I would like to suggest we seriously consider what they are replacing, and what the real cost of that might be.

Happy diddling,

Robin.

Re: Transmission

I fully agree that immersion is important - you are never going to learn just from an hour or two ‘lesson’ each week. But immersion isn’t the be-all and end-all of it - you need mentoring too. And that is where slow sessions come in.

Remember that pub sessions as we know them today are a fairly new phenomena. Traditionally sessions were just a few friends playing in the kitchen, and if the kids or learners were there, they would happily slow down, stop, go over a tricky passage a dozen times. In short do all the things that happen today in a slow session, but not in a pub session. So that is where slow sessions fit in today - not as a substitute for ‘immersion’ and listening to all the music you can get your hands on, but as a substitute for the father or cousin who can show you the techniques and tricks to bring you up to speed.

“Its all about sound at the end of the day. Being music, it can’t be about anything else. ”

This is the crux of my concern - I disagree strongly with this statement. For me, it’s about a constellation of factors: acoustic, social, environmental, spiritual etc., and these constitute something far greater than the mere sound alone.
In flamenco they have “Duende”, in Indian music “Unstruck sound”, in classical music they have “Grace” and I guess the equivalent in ITM is “craig,” and they all mean the same thing: The magic that happens which is behind and beyond the mere physical vibrations.

So I’m concerned that people are coming to believe that all that ITM is, or any other music for that matter, can be transmitted through non-interactive means such as print/internet.

My meditation teacher puts it well when he compares this kind of thing to the difference between the experience of having real sex with a real person, and merely looking at pornography. One is shadow of the thing, the other is the thing itself…

Re: Transmission

Robin, maybe this example will help.

I host a weekly tune learning session on Monday’s here in Helena, Montana (about a third of the way around the planet from Prof. Prlwytzkofsky’s neighborhood). Our Monday circle is mostly the same people who play at the regular Thursday pub session, but we meet in a music shop, after hours, to learn a tune, to play other tunes, to talk about the music. It’s also a time to catch up socially, tell stories and jokes, raise a glass, etc.

I don’t think of it as a “slow session,” although when teaching a tune, we go slow, phrase by phrase, by ear. But by the end of the evening we’re playing the tune at maybe 75% of session speed, and we play other tunes and sets at “normal” speeds.

This all works very much like sitting in someone’s kitchen to learn the tunes. This learning session helps people gain the skills, tunes, and confidence to sit in at the regular session. But just as importantly, it brings people into the social circle of other people passionate about this music. When you live outside of Ireland, or far from the Irish neighborhoods of NYC, Boston, London, Chicago, etc., it takes a little extra attention to develop a sense of community that includes the music.

The beauty is that, once the community exists, it exists everywhere, not just at the learning session or down at the pub once a week. Some of us work together, most of us recreate together (hikes, river trips, movie nights, non-musical potlucks, etc.), and we’re well connected to the local Irish language program, ceili dance group, and step dance schools.

In such an otherwise isolated town, I think it takes at least one person, someone who will “champion” the music and the craic, to get the ball rolling and keep the momentum up when it periodically flags until the session community gains critical mass and sustainability. In our case here in Helena, there were two of us initially who kick started things. It began with small kitchen sessions and quickly grew to pub sessions. People with direct ties to the larger tradition came out of the woodwork, people who had learned their music directly from Kevin Burke, John Vesey, Martin Hayes, Liz Carroll, and so on.

If this sort of thing can happen and grow here, in a far-flung town of 28,000 bureaucrats and cowboys, it can do so almost anywhere. If your town doesn’t have a “champion” for the music, then become that champion yourself and draw people to it.

Re: Transmission

Romanticized crap.

LOTS of traditional-idiom dance music was created in urban centres by at-least-semi-professionals who got paid to entertain the elite. We know quite a lot about the social history of this music by now, and “friends and neighbours” or antecedents of the present-day session scene didn’t play a significant part in creating it.

But folk transmission did play a part in licking tunes into shape after their composition. This is a well known and thoroughly investigated phenomenon with broadside ballads gone into oral tradition, and it happens with tunes too. Tradition often loses things entirely, often improves them out of all recognition, but rarely leaves them unchanged.

You see this more dramatically in Cape Breton music. Most of their repertoire was Scottish in origin, and transmitted on paper. In most cases we can identify exactly which published collections (all produced in urban centres) formed the basis of each tune played there today. But print didn’t determine the style, and the music has gone its own way since they acquired it.

Regardless of whether the players could read music and had printed collections - it’s not exactly rocket science to use a printed score as reminder of how the tune goes, even though what you play is wildly divergent from what’s printed, and it wouldn’t have been any more difficult for someone to do the same 150 years ago.

Here’s a relevant bit from Brendan Taafe, quoting Liam O’Flynn and others on Junior Crehan, just as one example.

“In a recent conversation, Liam O’ Flynn said, ”I often heard Junior talk with regret at the loss of the house dance and how the clergy, the church really, were responsible for the demise of the crossroads dances and the house dances. It was the center of their social lives and existences. Those house dances were wonderful, community events. When the dancing moved to the dance hall it had to change, of course."

The Dance Hall Act, in forcing dancing to larger, licensed halls, gave rise to the ceilidh bands, and in the 1950s, Junior was a founding member of the Laichtín Naofa Céilidh Band, which included Willie Clancy and Martin Talty from Miltown Malbay. The Laichtín Naofa won the Oireachtas Gold Medal in Dublin in 1956. But Junior was a farmer, by reputation a skilled and meticulous steward, and traveling to competitions was difficult as “no one had yet invented the five-day cow.” As the music gained in popularity and people began recording commercially, Junior “felt a mixture of delight and a strange curiosity towards the end of his life,” this from Liam O’Flynn, “that the music was becoming so fashionable, where it had been anything but fashionable when he was young. The whole commercialization he would have found difficult, for he was someone who only ever played for the pure love of it, and now there are powerful commercial interests involved, selling celtic this and celtic that. That word celtic had no meaning for Junior. He never would have described his music that way.” …

In short, Junior Crehan was a *farmer* who played for house dances in his local community. Many of his tunes (that he composed) are considered gems of the tradition today.
