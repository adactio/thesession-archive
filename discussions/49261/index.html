The Recording and Broadcasting of Music.

This is partly a response to the recent ‘Recording Trad From A Tech Perspective’ (JoeEvans) and also an additional personal rant about recorded and broadcasted music generally and how it’s devalued what musicians do.
I explained during the other thread why I believe recording this music is counterproductive.
“One should learn tunes live. They should be routined playing along with others in a session. Learning from recordings means one hears the same version/ interpretation over and over again and this imprints itself on your playing. No tune is ever played the same twice in the real world.”
Meanwhile I also have this thought…

For something like half a million years humans are believed to have made music.
It developed from stick clicking (or wotteffah) and chanting into all the intricate threads and genres we know today from around the world.
Once if one wanted music for pleasure or dancing e.g. weddings, birthdays, wakes, religious festivals et cetera, one would have to get musicians to come and do it or learn to play oneself.
Even if it was just a small birthday in a pub one might have to book e.g. a piano and sax player.
In 1824 and for the next 75 years the only way to have heard Beethoven’s Ninth was to actually be at a gig. There were reductions for piano* and so one might have heard some of the tunes but not the whole glorious thing. To have heard Nelly Power or Franz Liszt one would have to seen them live. Music was greatly enjoyed and sought after by the general public and this meant professional/ competent musicians were in great demand and could earn a living and respect doing it.
This situation existed for c.499,850 years!
Then suddenly in the last century and a half, Marconi and the application of the inventions he ripped off (recording and radio) comes along.
Suddenly music is available to everyone at the touch of a button and slowly the number of routine gigs starts to shrink.
The near death strike came in the 60s when the mobile discotheque became possible and musicians’ gigs started to dramatically disappear.
Why bother with a band or musician when a talentless bloke and a big record player can do it?!
I believe this even happens at times with some Irish dancing.
Nowadays DJs are revered by hoi polloi as musicians were.
We hear music of some sort all the time, whether we want to or not: at home on TV, in the street, passing cars, shops, adverts, work places.
It is taken for granted that one can hear music for free at any time.
And with the arrival of www. one can hear absolutely anything at all for free at any time.
This has cheapened what musicians do.
Music is not taken seriously (or musicians aren’t!).
It’s in the same category as fresh air!
One has been expected to work for nothing, the exposure or even pay to play!
Imagine a world where (e.g.) accountants, plasterers, tailors and train drivers were expected to do the same!
Mind you, one day soon I assume the tech will catch up and devalue what they do as well. Train drivers and accountants must almost be there already with AI and 3D printers/ Star Trek style replicators must be on the heels of the others (when they are invented!).

Thank you for reading and I look forward to any comments!

* I’ve been thinking how the effect of sheet music/ written notations fits in this discussion!

Re: The Recording and Broadcasting of Music.

Generally I agree*

* Live musical performance, from the performer’s experience outward and from the audience’s experience “should” always be slightly different than the recording. As a lyric writer and music recorder and blah blah blah at Open Mics and concerts, I think there’s good proof that with all creative artists, a song is essentially just never done. Many popular bands have shown that while the first recording is a suitable treatment of the song, after they play it live themselves dozens of times or more, they find ways to improve how it’s presented. That’s an organic process very much a parallel to how the song was created in the first place.

The only concert I’ve been to that disappointed me in any way was Supertramp, because they essentially played the songs very well but absolutely identical to the recordings, which me and my friends were on guard for, and disappointed, because we’d been to so many other concerts where the band had altered the music somehow and it worked better while being performed live. I go to concerts, Open Mics and gigs of all types because I want to see the people enjoying the performance of it.

The first concert I ever went to, where I was old enough to buy the ticket and go myself, was a Gordon Lightfoot concert. It was a personal revelation and spiritual lift up and graduation point, because after hearing about 3 great albums at home, Lightfoot and his band showed up as real human beings playing real instruments and putting full conviction into the lyrics and instrumental playing. A simple folk band setup, no screaming guitars or huge amps, just human performance for 90 minutes and a great selection of songs. Here indeed was guitar picking, song construction, and conviction of the highest order. People were hanging on every word and note and the evening felt like rafting along on the Amazon river on a lazy sunny evening with the best of friends. That set a standard with me.

After that, I soon went to a PDQ Bach concert, which is not only some talented classical players, but ludicrous, humorous and risky music and some insanely funny instrument creations. But it was all done with the utmost professional development and delivery and was a huge hit. That too, set a standard with me.

After that, quite a range of concerts and music styles, from rock, pop, classical, funk, LOTS of jazz, First Nations, world music from all over (especially love Hawaiian and Egyptian), but in each case, including the Open Mics I visit, the goal is to see people enjoying music played their own way. I would prefer to see people play their own songs, but a cover song done with conviction that the performer can relate to, tends to bring out valuable experiences to witness if the performer is projecting how they feel about it. Sure, for a lot of performers, cover songs are a necessity in starting out. Great; just show up yourself too! Pure duplication (hey, Supertramp), isn’t so hot.

Recordings have their role, no lecture here from me, but I just wanted to point out that recordings, for the artists and audience, should not be seen as “the” finality, but a launching point of possibilities.

Happy Didgereedoos to everyone.

> I’m not sure it’s the existence of recordings that results in musicians being devalued.

Think of how many parties, nightclubs, etc. have a DJ rather than a band. That’s one person getting paid vs 4 or 5 or more. How many bars and restaurants have background music playing rather than a live musician. The lounge pianist or “house band” are now nearly dead, you might find them in certain places but they’re much much rarer than they used to be. I don’t think there is any doubt at all that recordings have devalued the work of musicians. Music was never lucrative except for a select few, but recordings have concentrated the money so now that select few makes more, the “industry” (labels, Spotify, etc.) makes more, and the rest make very little because the gigs dry up. Before the 20th century you had to be within earshot of a playing musician in order to hear music. Now you can hear anyone anywhere on command, and most people would rather listen to a recording of Taylor Swift or the Rolling Stones over a local musician. And don’t even get me started on how it has devalued and denigrated the role of the amateur musician!

Re: The Recording and Broadcasting of Music.

@yhaal house - thank you for this provocative thought experiment!
Recorded music is a different form to live music, in which musicians are able to create something that wouldn’t be possible to hear live. On a basic level that’s because you can hear music when there are no musicians in the room, but we can also listen to - and learn from - great musicians who are dead, or bands who no longer perform together, or musicians who live and work on the other side of the world.
Recording and production techniques and technologies also enable us to create sounds that are impossible live. Each instrument can be mic’d up in a room by itself, for perfect reproduction of its sound; the same musician can layer many tracks of their own playing; a producer can edit, tweak, apply effects; and new genres can be born that can only exist as recordings (electronic dance music, for example).
It’s analogous to the creation of images using film, photography, painting or collage. We make something with artistic value that can be enjoyed by large numbers of people, and although it might represent something that really happened, even those present on a film set don’t see what the movie will look like.
I think that the idea that recorded music is bad for musicians is manifestly wrong. Firstly, before recorded music came along, being a musician was about as low-status a job as you could find. “May your daughter marry a musician” was a comic Jewish curse, and in fact across Eastern Europe, wedding musicians were mostly Jews or Gypsies because nobody else would do such a badly paid and low-status job. Most music was played by people who either weren’t paid or who were paid in food or beer, and most of those who were paid were paid badly.
And right now, recording music has direct and obvious benefits for musicians. I recently recorded an album with my harp/double bass duo. We can sell CDs at gigs, bringing in maybe £50-£100 extra income per gig. I can say to a promoter, “have a listen to our album at https://open.spotify.com/album/104TTvXSY1p2xxL215KfNB”; they can listen to it and see if they like us, but more importantly they can share it with their audience, so that people will buy tickets for an act they’ve never seen or heard of. The benefit in booking gigs was a major motivation in creating the album.
But also, we just wanted to make something permanent - a specific work that captured a little of what we wanted to do as musicians and that can be shared widely. Recorded music is made by musicians, in fulfilment of their creative urges and aims. It’s something we do because we want to, not something that has been inflicted on us.

Re: The Recording and Broadcasting of Music.

I am well acquainted with both sides of this argument. For many years when I was younger, I was in Rock & Roll bands playing 80% original music. There was a time in the 90s where live music was appreciated and there were gigs to be had, even for bands playing their own songs. Nowadays those opportunities are much fewer and far between as DJing has become ubiquitous and Rock music has gone out of favor. Most venue owners would rather pay 1 person $100 to DJ for a night rather than having to pay 5 people in a band $100 each and having to deal with the “human element”. The punters don’t really seem to notice the difference. Sure there are SOME people who still care about live music, but for the most part it’s much more of a niche thing now relegated to certain areas/scenes and clubs.

Also, the proliferation of recording/streaming technology like Youtube, Tik Tok, Spotify and Apple Music has made it possible for people to see children who are better than I will ever be playing music, and hear the entire history of recorded music. A typical intermediate musician is no longer appreciated in the same way, and people seem to only be interested in about the top 1% of players. It used to be if you stumbled across a competent musician in the “wild” there was appreciation for the skill they had developed and an interest in seeing where they could take it. Now it seems that people aren’t really that interested unless you are a master. And even that isn’t always enough. Just look at some of the recent threads which are somewhat disparaging to the “old masters” and their technical skills. I imagine some would not be that impressed if they ran into a Tommy Potts in the local pub these days.

That said, if it wasn’t for recorded Trad music, I never would have been drawn into this scene. I live in a small-ish town in North Carolina. Without Fiona Richie’s Thistle and Shamrock, I probably never would have been exposed to Irish Traditional music. I never would have discovered The Chieftains which lead me to Planxty and The Bothy Band, which lead me to Mary Bergin, Donnacha O’Briain, and Micho Russell, which then lead me to Seamus Ennis and Willie Clancy and Michael Coleman. Which lead me to seek out sessions where the music was played in a live setting and opened up the world for me. Most of the sessions around me are monthly and unless I stumbled into one of the 2 or 3 places where the sessions were happing on that one particular day of the month when the session was going, I wouldn’t have fallen in love with the music.
