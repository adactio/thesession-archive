Re: Why are sessions “bad”?

Sessions have their “pros and cons” and, as we have often discussed, are all different.

They could be gatherings of professional or very seasoned musicians just relaxing and having a tune. Or just good amateurs or, maybe, a mixture of all three and more.

They will also include players of various abilities who have no great interest in “performing” music on stage, making albums, or wanting to make a career out of it. I’m sort of in that category although I do like to “perform” from time to time but, usually, in the safety of numbers.

For those of us in the latter category, sessions are only *bad* in the sense that we are arguably less ambitious. Not necessarily in terms of skill or in the desire to add more tunes to our repertoire, of course. Many of us still practise a lot and enjoy learning new tunes. Others are more lazy, of course, but that shouldn’t really matter as long as they are adding something to the overall experience.

For more technically proficient musicians, sessions can be *bad* as they are often limited in what they feel they can do and feel obliged to “go with the flow”. Many fancy techniques and variations don’t always “fit in” or there is no opportunity to do something different. Also, it is probably not a good thing to be seen to be showing off either.
Of course, a good player will still shine through regardless and that’s probably enough.

The Benefits of Ensemble Playing:

1. Playing with others exercises your listening and time-keeping skills.
2. You may be more inclined to learn and practice music if you’re dedicated to a musical community.
3. You’re accountable to a certain standard for the sake of the community.
4. You have the opportunity to build relationships with musicians of a higher level, with more experience and resources.
5. You get to meet musicians who play other instruments and hear their styles and takes on tunes.

Re: Why are sessions “bad”?

If the OP meant the question to be, “Can playing in sessions be detrimental to one’s growth as a trad musician?” here are some potential issues to be aware of.

- Some sessions “streamline” the tunes, simplifying them to smooth them out for ensemble playing, and to make them accessible to a wider range of abilities. They may also avoid variations in the false hope of everyone playing in strict unison. It’s possible to get lazy and learn only these less-intricate, static versions. At worst, you end up not playing tunes but just strings of notes in a wall of sound. At best, you miss out on the intricacies, nuances, playfulness, and endless variations that make this music come alive in the hands of the better trad musicians.

- Some sessions struggle to play with good pulse and lift. This can deaden your own sense of pulse and lift. It’s not easy to play with lift when you’re dragging six other people along and their music is as buoyant as sacks of wet sand. Such sessions also tend to homogenize the music—every reel or jig has the same feel as the last one. The same degree (or lack) of swing, drive, lift, etc. What’s the point? You might as well just play one tune all night. Better trad musicians get to the heart of each tune and can express each tune’s unique character. Doing this takes time “living inside the tune,” exploring what sets it apart from other, even similar, melodies, finding and highlighting the emotional gist of each tune.

- Some sessions tend to play tunes in the same sets week after week, year after year. If you want to play in other sessions that are more freewheeling, you’ll need to develop the skill of letting tunes bubble up and stringing tunes together on the fly. We learn this by doing it, a lot, over time. You have to learn how to hear the next tune in your head while you’re still playing the one coming out of your fingers. You have to imagine the transition before you get to it. You have to be able to say “hup!” or give a nod to signal the change. And you’ll want to get comfortable with all this while playing at pace in public. Most importantly, you have to learn to trust your intuition, your ability to knit tunes together on the fly.

Of course, you can address all of these shortcomings. When you play alone at home, don’t follow the herd. Develop your own skills and musicality. And find better sessions. As your ear develops, you’ll hear elements that slid under your radar before. Seek out better musicians, better sessions.

All that said, you can also learn a lot by playing in almost any session:
- They can offer a chance to play with musicians better than yourself, to wallow in their musicality, skills, and creativity.
- They can broaden your repertoire of tunes and of different settings of the same tune.
- You can hone your skills at playing in a group—sustaining your playing while listening to what everyone else is doing; latching onto the set leader’s pace, pulse, swing, lift, expressiveness; picking up variations on the fly; how to blend your instrument-specific ornaments with what other instruments are doing; and more.
- They can push you to play at speed with relaxation for hours on end.

Not least, sessions are one of the best places for understanding that this music can be intensely personal and also deeply social. The experience of a good session is a great way to learn about and immerse into a wider, diverse world of music and musicians.

Re: Why are sessions “bad”?

There are only two “bad” things about sessions IME.

One, it is easy to “play along” half-assedly and get the illusion that one really know the tunes. Too much of this (ie too many players who do this at a session) and it sounds like mud. We are only as good as we sound when we record ourselves playing solo.

Two, if you cannot play at your session’s speed, you are not going to benefit from trying to play faster. Speed (whilst playing well) is a byproduct of practice, and not something you work on. IE, trying to play fast won’t make you faster, but really learning the tunes might. If this is the case listening is the best option.

Re: Why are sessions “bad”?

I read the whole thread so far, and was formulating things I wanted to mention, only to have gimpy steal basically all of my thunder! ;-)

I am one of the people who truly believes that sessions are where you do the real learning. Yes, you have to learn your instrument, techniques for articulation and ornamentation, the idiosyncrasies of the rhythms and style, at least to some degree before you try sitting in a session, and that’s a lot of work. But for me, sessions were like the practical master classes in how to put all those things together to become more musical with my playing, to intertwine with the other musicians in a playful, meaningful ways, and to develop my own voice in the chorus. That is what I meant by “real learning”…

Gimpy mentioned most of the potential pitfalls I had in mind to bring up. Some sessions can be pretty stagnant, and if that’s the only exposure you have to playing this music with other people, you will likely stagnate as well. So don’t think of mastering this music as being a destination that you will reach, think of the music as a lifelong journey of self discovery, learning, and growth.

It’s ok to push yourself. Even if it makes you feel like you’re taking steps backward. Sometimes progress requires some regression. It’s not great to try to push yourself to do too much too quickly, like get your tempos up, but it’s also a sign of you understanding your current limitations. The desire to get better is an important part of that lifelong journey! So you want to make sure that you’re not losing things as you push yourself, like dumbing down the hard part of a tune just so that you can play it faster. People often say that playing slowly and purposefully is what will lead you to being able to play faster, which is quite true to an extent. But when it comes down to it, the only way to really learn how to play faster is by trying to play faster.

The ideal learning situation in a session is where you are playing with people that are better than you, but they aren’t playing so far beyond your ability that you can’t even try. A lot of good session leaders will know your limitations, and will intentionally push you just beyond your current ability. So even if the session is generally beyond your ability, it’s good to be there, experience it, absorb what you can, and get to know the other players, and let them get to know you. If you’re fun to hang out with, that’s as important as being fun to play music with! And the better they know you, the more likely they are to try to include you, and hopefully help nurture your abilities.

And finally, I will just point out one more potential pitfall from sessions. Playing this music is not a competition. Everybody is on their own journey, and you shouldn’t feel embarrassed that you’re not at the same level as someone else.

Re: Why are sessions “bad”?

Gimpy raised a number of things here that made me think a bit.

“Some sessions “streamline” the tunes, simplifying them to smooth them out for ensemble playing, and to make them accessible to a wider range of abilities.”

For sure the session versions are streamlined, or levelled, in the sense that they need to coalesce the individualistic approaches of different sorts of instruments. They’ll be a pure-piping version and a pure-fiddle version and a pure-banjo version, and they all will want to have some of the quirks of the individual instruments suppressed to get a smoother session sound.

About “simplified”, I’m not so sure. The session versions I hear aren’t artificially simplified- that would make them sound un-idiomatic to Irish players. They have rolls where the rolls are expected to be etc.

“They (the session versions) may also avoid variations in the false hope of everyone playing in strict unison.”

For sure there’s a time and a place to throw in blatant variations. But they can work brilliantly even in a session setting as long as they don’t clash with the regular version. (It won’t work if your variation is in a different key, though variations done by soloists sometimes are.)

“the intricacies, nuances, playfulness, and endless variations that make this music come alive in the hands of the better trad musicians.”

Yes the heterophonic nature of ITM is essential- absolutely strict unison, no matter how well done, ends up lacking the thrill that spontaneity brings.

Another member here messaged me to say that the issues I raised were frustrations she faced in her own local session. In reply, I suggested some remedies, things she can do to help lift the musicality of her session mates, and also lift her own playing. Richard, these ideas address many of your points.

"At times, I’ve lived places that didn’t have a robust session scene. We make the best with what we have, where we are, yes? So I’ve faced some of the same challenges. Here’s how I dealt with them. (Bear in mind that for these ideas to work, you have to be able to carry the tunes on your own despite your session mates sounding like a loaded dishwashing machine tumbling down a staircase.)

- Speed. When the rest of the crew isn’t up to the pace you’d like to go, put as much pulse and lift into the tunes as you can at the slower tempo. It’ll sound better than just plodding along, and maybe it will help others improve on those fronts. It will also teach you a lot about generating pulse and lift, distinct from tempo. Another trick is to play at the common tempo, and then, after a longish pause, start the tune again but at 1o bpm faster, just enough to give it the life it wants. If you hit the tempo right, most people will try to play along. Regardless, everyone will hear how much better the tunes sound a notch above a dirge.

- same old sets. Play the same tunes but in a different order. So instead of X, Y, Z, play Z, Y, X or Y, X, Z, and so on. This may annoy some, but keeping the same tunes in the set softens the blow a bit while still encouraging greater open-mindedness.

- no variations or detours. Again, this is an opportunity to emphasize pulse, lift, and phrasing. How good can you make a tune sound without varying any notes or ornaments? That will help your own playing. But I also like to layer in some small variations, just to give learners a chance to hear how this music is really played. One strategy is to start with a tune that’s relatively new to the group. Announce that you’re going to play it 6 (or 10!) times because you’re still drilling it into your own memory, and to give everyone a chance to really latch on. Then, the first 3 times through, play it exactly the same. On the 4th round, do one small variation in Part A and another in Part B. On the 5th round, keep the previous variations, and add in one more to each part. On the 6th (and subsequent rounds), you can either go back to the plain setting, keep the variations in, or add something new. This really helps people adjust to hearing variations while holding on to their own playing, and better players will start to pick up the variations. Follow up this first tune with one or two tunes that everyone knows, three times each (say “Hup!”). This is one of the best ways to free up people who are otherwise locked into sheet music or the “That’s not how the tune goes” mindset. It may also help to tell them that not getting derailed while someone else is mixing in variations is a skill of its own that will improve with practice. It’s also a norm in Irish trad—other sessions expect it.

Unless your session is more of a performance for an audience, these ideas should work well without detracting from the overall feel of the session."

I’ve been fortunate to session with many brilliant Irish trad musicians over the years. But at times I’ve also lived in places remote from this music. Even when I barely spoke the local language, the strategies outlined above helped a struggling session or two pull themselves out of the Muzak into something that sounded much more like lively Irish trad.

Well, when I said, “at a lope,” I wasn’t thinking at 72 bpm all night long. Loping can occur at different speeds. Just not racing. FWIW, I like a wide range of paces over the course of a session, reels, say, at anywhere from 60 to 136 bpm. And it’s fun to take a tune that’s often lashed like a racehorse and instead take it for a languid stroll.

At one of the smaller sessions I attend, we often warm up on just a single tune played fairly slow a dozen or so times. It gets these old neurons firing, fingers loosened up, and helps the three or four of us get in synch. We vary which tune gets this treatment, and it’s interesting to hear the phrasings and variations that come out of such a meditative approach.

Once the session gets cooking, it’s also not unusual that we all lock in on a given tune in a set, and we’ll play it six or more times out of pure enjoyment, taking turns riffing on it, or sometimes just in thrall to the tight pulse. The eventual segue into the next tune can be breathtaking like a cold plunge after a sauna. I’ve noticed that even non-musicians in the pub are drawn in by the intensity and then the rush of the change. Good fun!
