Re: Flute help

I would recommend avoiding keys for a first Irish flute. They use a completely different system then a classical flute and, in addition to being unnecessary for most Irish music, can add distractions when learning how to play a new kind of flute. I play on a Casey Burns folk flute and I love it. I’ve had it for 4 or 5 years and rarely have wanted keys. When I need to play in a different key I pull out one of my different size whistles.

I’ve also heard good things about flutes made by Gary Somers. I played someone’s once and very much liked it also. Since they are made of delrin they are also extra durable, but they still have a lovely sound and the heavy, hard plastic has a nice feel to it.

Another advantage of a less expensive, keyless flute to start with is that you can learn what you like in a flute and have a point of comparison to start from if you want a more expensive keyed flute later on.

Hope that helps!

Re: Flute help

What key? D.

As far as the holes, that is more of an aesthetic difference for keyless flutes. 8 hole flutes have two holes on the foot joint that would be covered by keys on a keyed flute. They remain on some keyless flutes because those flutes are made based off of keyed flutes and because of tradition. 6 hole keyless flutes just get rid of the vestigial holes and keep only the necessary ones.

For $1500 or less you have a huge range of choices for keyless flutes, but I would probably stick with ~$500 and get a folk flute or other similar flute. That won’t hold her back and will give more flexibility if she decides to get a fancier flute later on.

And as far as it being a different instrument, that is an interesting question. If she plays tin whistle and classical flute she should be able to pick up an Irish flute and play songs on it within a few minutes or an hour. The basic fingerings transfer over: a D or an E on an Irish flute in D is essentially the same fingering as the same note in a classical flute. But to properly play the instrument as it is meant to be played takes a lot more practice.

I studied classical flute for about 12 years as I was growing up, but over the past few years have been working on switching to Irish flute. It is almost entirely a different instrument if you approach it with the intention to play it traditionally. The technique and embrochure is similar in many ways, but different in many other ways. However, I did play my Irish flute for several years in much the same way as my classical flute. It takes a little while to learn how to cover the holes properly, but it will make music and sound nice (if not traditional) if approached like a classical flute. I certainly loved my folk flute and how I sounded on it before I started approaching it from another direction to try to properly play Irish music.

Re: Flute help

You can get along ok with both keyed and keyless when coming from a classical flute, as long as you stay in the keys of D and G (and their equivalent minors). When I’ve played on a keyed flute, I’ve found sometimes that a few of the keys get in the way, but that would just take some getting used to.

The keys are not used when the flute is played in the key of D, so the D scale would feel similar to a classical flute and identical to a whistle. But the farther away from the key of D you go (on the circle of fifths), the more things change. Playing a scale in Eb would be totally different on a keyed Irish flute than on a classical flute, but the fingerings are fairly straightforward and would just take getting used to.

So if you got her a keyed flute (which could be in your price range), it would be the similar to keyless flute to get started on and would give more options as she learned it. Those options are not so necessary for session playing, but could be nice if playing other styles of music. However they can also be distractions. But I usually get out my classical flute for anything like that.

Re: Flute help

For a keyless flute, the bottom-end pricing is about $400, any lower and you get into some risky purchases. Delrin flutes by Gary Somers come well-recommended in that price range, and I think you can get Casey’s entry-level “folk flutes” for about that.

The medium-end pricing is $900-1,000, and that should buy you a really good flute. I notice that the Whistle & Drum shop has several used flutes in stock $1,000-$1,100 from well-recommended makers.

You can find Keyed flutes as low as the $2,000 range (+/-) and I think that price gets you a high-quality instrument. 4-key flutes might be a bit lower.

Just a tip: comparing the Rudall style to the Pratten, The Ruddal has smaller holes and may be a bit more responsive. You’ll have to ask to be sure, but looking at a picture, the Pratten typically has all six finger holes on one piece, while the Rudall style splits in two pieces between the hands.

Personally, I wouldn’t buy the “small hands” or ergonomic flutes. My hands aren’t that big, but the normal conical-bore flute has reasonable spacing for the fingers with straight-line holes.

Re: Flute help

Don’t know anything about your wife or her musical tastes and background, but this might be helpful. Something no-one speaks to very much on this site is the awesome flexibility of the 8 keyed flute. Let’s admit that the flute wasn’t invented for Irish music. The 8 keyed flute was around as a mainstay in the orchestra long before it was adopted (adapted) for use in folk genres. No slight intended toward folk music, the music of the people, just a recognition that folk music isn’t all music. Even now there are local groups and orchestras that play with the older, traditional instruments.

While you can play just about any Irish tune and most American Old time tunes on a keyless flute, the 6-key makes some of them a bit easier, and the 8-key even more. With a good 8-key a player can play in just about any key and even get well into the third octave (not quite easily but it can nicely be done). Not only extending the range of the instrument but the range of tune/genre possibilities. If Irish is the end game, your wife can play successfully for the rest of her life and probably never miss a keyed flute. Cross fingering and half-holing will do the job nicely enough and keys won’t make her a better player. All they will do is allow her to extend her musical range should her interests change.

I should add here that I don’t own an 8-keyed flute…yet. I liked my unkeyed Pratten from Gary Somers so much that I had 6 keys added, and regret not adding the bottom two. That’s about to be rectified. And I’ll likely leave my Sam Murray as it is with no keys. Frankly as I go out the door to play I know I’ll have a grand time enjoying a few tunes in good company with whatever flute I have with me. In the end (other than going real cheap) you and your wife just can’t make a wrong decision here. Good luck

Re: Flute help

8 keyed flutes - great! However, if your wife already plays the Boehm then she might want to keep that until she is ready to get the _right_ 8 key simple system (“Irish”) flute. Yes, the 8 key can play anything, but working through a few of them on the way to finding your _own_ flute can be very expensive…

For now, learning to play the 90% of Irish tunes that can be played on the keyless flute is a good idea and if you really want to play a tune with missing notes then use the Boehm - I presume that is what you mean by “band type, metal” flute…? And for 80% of the Irish tunes that don’t sit easily on a keyless (for a total of 98%), a C whistle can work wonders… But, really, don’t worry about all that stuff. Get a good keyless and start playing. Use every opportunity to try out other people’s and other Maker’s flutes. Learn lots of tunes.

Of course, the missing keys become more important if you start to play Scottish, Breton or Modern “trad” Fiddle compositions… But no keys will give you the low notes that the fiddle has anyway… Realise that, although the fiddlers have tunes that are easy for them and a pig on the flute, there are also other real flute tunes with the opposite gradient in difficulty.

Well, you’re getting lots of suggestions! Two thoughts, after reading many of them:

I would not buy a keyed flute (and emphatically not an eight-keyed flute--it simply isn’t worth an extra $1000 for two extra notes you don’t really need for Irish music). The fingering system has very little in common with the Boehm flute she played. The embouchure and techniques for playing Irish music in an idiomatic style are already pretty much like learning a new instrument; keys only add to that, and they’re not needed for like 90% of the repertoire.

Getting your wife to weigh in on the selection is a good idea, up to a point. First, it’s hard to try Irish flutes, as they tend to be made to order, which means you can’t wander into a shop and try them out. Secondly, she doesn’t play Irish flute (yet) and it sounds like she hasn’t played Boehm flute for some time, either. Everything will feel weird to her for starters, so she isn’t really in a position to know what suits her best.

With that in mind, I don’t think you can go wrong with a Windward, so stay the course with that plan--but have your wife in on the selection process. Have her pick the flute she likes the look of best and have her chat with Forbes and Yola. They’ll steer her right.
