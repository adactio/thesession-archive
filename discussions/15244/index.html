Celtic is a word with a lot of different meanings.I’m personally most interested in what it means when it is connected to the words ‘fingerstyle guitar’,which I suppose is a subset of what it means in the larger context of ‘celtic music’.

I’m beginning to think that the best way of understanding the word now,(as illustrated above by TheMuse) is as a completely new word for a musical genre.Thus a novel usage,unconnected to the ways the word has been defined in the past,in popular language,and by historians and by students of linguistics and culture,etc.

IMHO the novel usage of celtic,now,is as a label for a musical
genre.
In this context it’s just like the words reggae or hiphop or bluegrass or acid rock.
It does not to have to have connections to any of the other previous usages,any more than ‘rock’ music has got any connection to geological specimens.Celtic just happens
to have become the label for a certain type of music.The argument,then,is how is this NEW genre of music defined or delineated.That may not become clear until people
can look back over time and see how it developed and mutated,absorbed influences,rejected allied styles,and so forth,in the way that say,New Orleans jazz developed,
carried along into unpredictable avenues by figures like Charlie Parker,DjangoReinhardt,Miles Davies,etc.

There are probably many objections to this line of thought,and I’d be happy to reconsider if anyone has a convincing critique.

Re: Google Earth: You are here . . . & here . . . & here . . .

“Celtic” seems to me as reasonable a catch-all word as one can expect to find for this music. It means to me in the main trad or closely related music coming from all parts of the British Isles that aren’t England, and taken fairly directly from there in recent decades (via albums, etc.) by enthusiasts in other parts of the world. This is a rough-and-ready definition for sure - and any definition can be refined and argued over for ever, partly because trying to find a watertight one is probably impossible and partly because some of us just like mulling such subjects over ad infinitum.

My local HMV, which has a tiny clutch of relevant albums, labels them “Folk” or “World”, not “Celtic”.

There will always be off-putting peripheral stuff marketed (and Enya and Bothy members, respected traditional musicians, did help start the New Age Celtic thing), and people will always bring some strange and lurid ideas with them as they engage with Celtic music, history or culture. Not much one can do about that.

I think I see what you’re after.

I’d say the defining feature was the range of characteristic rhythms, speeds and repetitions: you’d think there was nothing more natural in the world than reels, for instance, but other cultures don’t seem to have them! (Mind, this leaves out the Bretons.)

I’d also say that Celtic music, from dance music to pipe band music to English language songs to Sean-Nos, sets out to seize the attention and in some way captivate the listener. These days, it’s usually to rouse; in the remote past, the harp was used to lull; maybe the New Age merchants saw a gap in the market here.

But I imagine much trad music anywhere is designed to seize the attention; and Celtic music’s diatonic base is not unique to it, nor is the use of bagpipes.

Re: Google Earth: You are here . . . & here . . . & here . . .

Hello all, Mario here :-)
In Galicia, the term ‘celtic’ usually refers to bands such as Lúnasa, Flook etc… also to local bands such as Luar na Lubre or Berrogüetto, or Asturian bands such as Llan de Cubel. They play Galician and Asturian tunes, of course, as well as tunes from other cultures.
‘Celtic’ is never applied to Galician pipe bands -bandas de gaitas- nor the more traditional form of just one pipe and one drum.
On the other hand, there have been ‘celtic festivals’ for many years wich may include any of the above.
The first popular use of ‘celtic’ in Galicia was unrelated to music.
It was a flag of the 19th century romantic stream, mixture of poetry an politics, led by the poet Eduardo Pondal.
Quoted from
http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduardo_Pondal
Pondal considered himself a “poet of freedom”, wanting to raise his people. He imagined a past of freedom and independence, which he tried to recover with his poetry, renewing History. Unfortunately, Galician-Celtic mythology was almost completely lost in those days, so Pondal had to guess and re-invent it, based on Ossian’s poetry, quotations from the Leabhar Gabala and Murguia’s analysis. Pondal created a fragmentary mythology, using as his archetypes o Heroe (the hero) and o Bardo (the bard).

wolfbird -The Romano-Celtic people of Britain definitely called themselves “British” - “Britanni” - in the early post-Roman period, at least in Latin texts like that of Gildas. I wouldn’t know when the Celtic-speakers of Wales and maybe elsewhere first called themselves “Cymraeg” (“Comrades”).

I think your first remarks are very true. And with Arthur, there may have been things the British wished not to emphasise. A historian called John Morris wrote a book called “The Age Of Arthur” in which he argues out of all the evidence available that Arthur lived round the end of the c5 AD and fought to keep the Anglo-Saxons penned up in Eastern England, having his base perhaps in Colchester; and that he taxed the British in general to do this. But those in the West, the North and Wales - where Arthur lore later became conspicuous - seem to have resented Arthur’s taxes or levies, on the grounds that the Anglo-Saxons were a remote problem which they shouldn’t be obliged to pay for. Eventually Arthur seems to have been killed in the North, maybe by people he’d been trying to bring onside and tax. Rather grimy, ugly stuff - that is, if Morris’s speculations are true.
