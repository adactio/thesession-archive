Chords vs. tunes

Where do chords become a part of ITM? The usual reponse is the tune is king.
Yes, the tune is king! But nothing about what makes a tune a tune negates someone playing harmonies.
Or does it?

For me every tune is king. Considering the tune without accompaniment does not diminish a tune. Quite the contrary. A tune without accompaniment suggests *infinite possibilities* for harmony.

It’s only once a harmony is introduced that the tune and the harmony become intertwined. And harmony isn’t only chords. But the introduction of a simple harmony leads to who knows what. Chords are soon to follow.

Should chords be avoided to keep with the concept of trad music being exclusively about the tune being king?

Or not?

Thanks in advance,
Respect

Out of humble respect I have not capitalised the term king when referring to tunes.

Re: Chords vs. tunes

I mean, it’s traditional music, which means that what it becomes is just the aggregate of what people do with it. So at one point, the tunes were played without chordal accompaniment, though probably with some harmony lines from time to time because people can’t seem to resist that sort of thing, and that was the tradition. Then people started playing the piano along with the tunes - I suspect this was an innovation for recordings in America, but I don’t know if that’s true or just a supposition on my part - and when other people kept doing it, that became the tradition.

That’s sort of the point of tradition: it doesn’t care about sticking with the past. Tomorrow’s tradition is just the consensus of what people today do with yesterday’s tradition, just as tomorrow’s English is just the consensus of the rules that small children today are making up based on what they hear of yesterday’s English.

Just because I find the endless banging away on a guitar or a bouzouki to be at best a distraction from the music and at worst a complete disruption of it doesn’t mean it’s not part of the tradition. It is part of the tradition, and fortunately so is hating the awful racket and wishing they’d stop.

Re: Chords vs. tunes

"Its seems to me accompanists need to learn the tune in the same way melody players do, note for note. They do not need to get it to a performance standard in the same way. But they need to go through the tune slowly and have enough knowledge of harmony to be able to work out what the chords are and which of them work.
A lot of accompanists will say I do not need to do that, I play by ear and it sounds fine."

I am not defending substandard accompanists here. But I do think that a good accompanist can listen to a sequence of notes and hear instantly what chord is implied by it. That is, I admit, not the same thing as understanding the whole tune – it might take a few more listens to become familiar with ‘how the tune goes’ and to construct a chord progression that complements the contours of the tune well, is consonant with the notes in any given melodic phrase *and* hangs together well in its own right. But there is a considerable
difference between hearing the implied harmony in a sequence of notes and hearing (and reliably playing) every individual note in that sequence.

I am under no illusion that these skills cannot be developed without years of practise and familiarity with the music (and of course, the Dunning-Kruger effect comes into play just as it does with any other skill – there will always be ‘accompanists’ that have complete faith in their own skill level when, in fact, they barely have a clue). But that applies equally to learning melodies – anyone that has been playing this music for a few years knows that the more tunes you learn, the easier and quicker it becomes.

Of course, the Dunning-Kruger effect comes into play just as it does with any other skill. There will always be accompanists that believe they know what they are doing when they don’t.

Yeah, but there are some tunes where you might expect a standard I-VII-I progression but it takes a different turn. Or where you might expect a I-V progression but the V is a minor rather than a major. Or where a tune doesn’t quite resolve at the end of a part - accompanists generally expect some resolution (correctly in most cases) but a bad accompanist will persist in sticking one in even if it’s clearly not there in the melody. You’d have to know the tune already to get those things first time round - but a talented accompanist could clock those quickly and be on the case for the 2nd play through.

More important from my point of view is when a tune is in a nice mode, not necessarily either major or minor, and an accompanist picks one or the other. That’s an instance when I just wish they weren’t playing, or knew the tune well enough to stick to plain fifths or fourths. More partial chords in accompaniment please! Nothing wrong with leaving out thirds of the scale.

If the melody is king in this music - and I agree it is - any accompaniment must support that melody (and the rhythm, of course) and not distract from it. That’s why I favour the fewest and most basic chords possible when backing a tune. And (as many have said) it’s vital that a backer knows the melody.
It’s not a backer’s job to try and add “colour” to the music, or try and force it into a parallel mode or otherwise shout “look at me!”, unless perhaps they’re arranging tunes for a band. It’s why I loathe DADGAD or other tunings which lead to ambiguous voicings. Bugger off with yer counterpoint and yer sus2 chords!

Matt Milton: “Yeah, but there are some tunes where you might expect a standard I-VII-I progression but it takes a different turn. Or where you might expect a I-V progression but the V is a minor rather than a major. Or where a tune doesn’t quite resolve at the end of a part - accompanists generally expect some resolution (correctly in most cases) but a bad accompanist will persist in sticking one in even if it’s clearly not there in the melody.”

I agree, and it can be very annoying… But that is just a question of skill level. Allowing for the fact that every human makes errors sometimes (and we all occasionally jump to incorrect conclusions when we should have sat back and waited for more information), a good accompanist would hear that they had not quite got it right and correct it next time round, or hang back and play something ‘neutral’* until they have got it – whilst someone less skilled might fail to hear their error, or fail to find the correct alternative and just keep ploughing on with something that they deem ‘good enough’.

*I realise that the ideal would be to play nothing until you are sure what to play. But dropping out of accompaniment once you have started is likely to be more off-putting than carryong on in an understated manner. By ‘neutral’, I mean something that uses a minimum of chord changes (none, if possible) – perhaps the tonic or dominant chord, or a single-note drone, depending on the tune. Correcting a ‘wrong turn’ is a skill in itself.

Re: Chords vs. tunes

I enjoy small sessions, just two or three or four players, preferably all playing the melody. Without accompaniment, you can easily hear and respond to what the other melody players are doing, right down to the smallest nuances and shifts in pulse and swing. In good hands, that makes the music tighter, more in sync, and more fun.

Note that you can do all that, play tunes all night, and get people tapping their toes or even up and dancing without a backer of any kind. The tunes have everything needed to bring an evening to life, no chords, strumming, or thumping required.

The trouble with accompaniment is that any sound that isn’t the melody—a thudding goat skin, clattering spoons, chords being strummed or keyed—comes with a high chance that it will muddy the music. So a backer has to be *just as sensitive* as the melody players are, heeding every nuance, and not smothering their articulation of the tune and pulse.

I’ve been fortunate to play with some stellar backers and certainly enjoyed it. But they are too few and far between. They’re vastly outnumbered by the guitar toters who know a three chord trick and the small army of tipper owners who happily whack a jig rhythm on a drum regardless of the tune type in play. So it’s no wonder sessioneers are wary of strangers bearing large or circular cases. We try to give newcomers the benefit of the doubt, but most often it ends with us soldiering through a tune only to ask them not to continue their enthusiastic contributions.

To answer Bazza’s question, if it’s one of my local sessions, and if the backing is distracting, we suffer through the first tune and then politely ask the person to stop. If I’m at an unfamiliar session, I either go with the (lack of) flow because the session has redeeming qualities, or I pack up and go for a walk.

That said, I can think of a few instances where the “accompaniment” was so outlandish that we egged it on for several sets just for the legendary stories we could tell for years after. You really don’t have room to complain about accompaniment until you’ve rollicked through a long set of reels while a tipsy young woman with a mason’s hammer randomly and arrhythmically wallops a 1-meter brass gong tuned to somewhere slightly south of Eb. Its sheer volume and sustain were impressive. As was her unmusicality. The other four of us switched from pints to a shared bottle of, if I remember, Knappogue that night.

Re: Chords vs. tunes

On the subject of bodhrans, I’ve noticed that the switch to the currently fashionable half-barrel drums has really made the bodhran a much more irritating instrument. The new-fangled drums generally have a sort of “thoom”-y tone that sustains almost like an organ, and they really muck up the sound a lot if the player doesn’t know how to control their tone - which most players in sessions don’t seem to have figured out.

For bodhran players, you want a percussive sound, not a sustained sound. If you’re playing a new-style drum, that means really damping the skin a lot more than you’re probably doing. Basically, if you’re tempted to tune the drum, that means you need to work on your playing a lot.

Also, the bodhran should be playing with the tune, not trying to keep the beat. This doesn’t mean trying to play the notes of the tune, but trying to sync up with the rhythm of the melody and work with that rather than just pounding out the main beats of the meter like a trap drummer in a rock band. The drummer in a rock band or a jazz band is there to keep people in time. If you need to do that in a trad session, it’s best to stop playing and let the musicians work out their time issues amongst themselves.

[Edited: Cross-posting with Jon, who makes excellent points.]

Given the OP: ”who here considers chordal accompaniment to be acceptable in the tune sessions you attend & who rejects it?”

My previous post hints at this, but my answer is too big for the question. It’s not a simple matter of a blanket acceptance or rejection of chordal accompaniment. It depends—is it my session or am I a visitor? Is the chordal accompaniment tasteful and sensitive or uninformed hammering?

I have my own preference, but that’s all it is—a preference. Not a rigid iron-clad rule. My preference is based on my decades of experience that in a session chordal accompaniment tends:

1. to muddy the nuances of articulating the tunes and punctuating the pulse.
2. to make it harder for the melody players to hear and respond to one another.
3. to erase a tune’s lovely modal ambiguity, or to cement the tune in only one of its personalities.
4. by doing #3, to constrain a melody player’s choice of notes.
5. to add unnecessary loudness, which then escalates.
6. to create an soundscape regretfully similar to the canned pop music everywhere we go.

A good accompanist knows these are pitfalls and plays in such a way as to avoid or reduce them. It helps if they’re also good craic.

I think we have to take it as a given that, even though the vast majority of the general public like their music harmonised, there is a small, but not insignificant, cohort of trad players who prefer the tunes unaccompanied. And that’s exactly what it is - a preference. No one is claiming (or shouldn’t be) that it is better one way or the other.

What I find, though, is that, even if no harmony instrument is present, I still often hear a harmony, especially with some more recent tunes that have a particular harmony associated with them. I’m sure I’m not alone in that.
So, for example (Scottish, I’m afraid), if I’m playing Ross Memorial Hospital, I also hear Phil Cunningham’s left hand in my head. And if I’m playing The Johnstown Reel, I’m hearing Tony McManus’ accompaniment in my head.
It’s like if I’m singing “All My Loving” in the shower (don’t worry, the shower room is sound-proofed), when I get half way through the first verse, I hear the note on the word “true” going from being the third of the chord to the fifth of the chord.

So, for me (especially because I can hear most changes), I rarely hear a tune as just pure melody. But it doesn’t particularly bother me if nobody is playing chords I imagine.

“…why do uillean pipes have drones and regulators and not just a chanter, and why does an accordion have chords and basses on the left hand?”

The obvious answer would be that harmony can be great when it’s the melody player choosing how it’s done, and not by someone who isn’t playing the melody. ;)

I would add the mandolin to that list of instruments, because one of the reasons I still play mandolin and octave mandolin while trying to be a better flute player, is that I like being able to add doublestops and partial chords within the melody line when playing a tune. I don’t overdo it, just a little added color here and there. I still focus on flute for the greater range of expression and ornamentation, but when I start missing a bit of harmony, I pick up my mandolin or OM.

So it would be hypocritical for me to take a stand against competent and supportive backing by a guitar or zouk player. With emphasis on supportive and not distracting. Less is more, don’t force a minor or major feel if the key is ambiguous, etc.

Re: Chords vs. tunes

For the old tunes. The tune is King. For modern tunes. The accompaniment is King
Many of the old tunes had very arpeggiated structures. The chords were built into the tunes. In this way the melodies would be vivid and clear without the need for accompaniment.
Most modern tunes (without accompaniment) sound bland, just a run of random notes floating in the air. But add the right chord or two and the melodic magic is revelated.

Re: Chords vs. tunes

My tuppence:

All accompanying sounds should follow the melody, and in that I mean they should be a milisecond behind, and never ahead.

And sonically they should sit ‘under’ the melody playing, reacting to it and never ‘leading’ or taking centre stage. Barely noticeable in other words.

A guitarist (or, heaven forbid, Bodhrán-er) should not be or consider themselves to be 40 or 50% of the session, as tends to happen - a sort of ‘me and this assortment of melody players’ mindset.

I have a favourite story I heard somewhere a few years back which might be relevant to a discussion like this. The blues harp player Charlie McCoy [ intro music to “The Old Grey Whistle Test”, in the 1980s ], was invited by the producer to play on one of Bob Dylan’s albums. He asked Dylan what he wanted him to do on the songs he was to play on, and apparently got the less than helpful advice, something along the lines of “just do what it is you do, man”.
The bass player had overheard this exchange, and told Charlie this [ not word for word ] :
“What you need to remember is that the song is the painting, and you’re part of the frame. The frame can be very simple, or very complex, but should never detract or interfere with the painting”.
That particular piece of advice has always stuck with me. Something to consider, IMHO.
