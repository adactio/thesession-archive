Re: Post-structural trad

In my sessions, we don’t take “breaks”, as in having everyone switch to some mode of accompaniment, while one player starts improvising upon the main melody. But players are encouraged to sprinkle in tunes that nobody else knows, so that they’re playing solo for that tune (other than accompanists). We do this because this is how we expand our repertoire these days. Invariably, people will ask what the tune was, and often that will be the impetus for another person or two to learn the tune, and soon enough, it becomes a new tune in our shared repertoire.

“where in your world view do you place the latter music?” If you’re talking about “breaks” like a bluegrass session, then I place it fairly firmly outside of the Irish tradition. If it happens once in a blue moon amongst good players, then whatever, it’s fine. But generally I think those players would consider it to be outside the Irish tradition, too.

“Is anything beyond your local session relevant? ” Absolutely. I play music just about everywhere I travel. I go to festivals, sessions in Ireland, sessions in NYC, etc. And every one of them is relevant to me and my impression of what our tradition is and isn’t. But I can’t say that I’ve ever been in an Irish session that had players improvising breaks in the middle of tunes… One possible exception to that was a session in Nebraska about 15 years ago, where the players were primarily bluegrass players playing Irish tunes, and they were somewhat horrified when we didn’t let people take breaks…

I would also echo the Reverend’s opinion that improv lies outside the Irish tradition. I would add that, unlike bluegrass and jazz, which offer a firm chord base upon which to riff, Irish traditional is melody based. If anyone has the ability to riff on an Irish session, it would be the chord accompanists: piano, harp, guitar, octave mandolin.

Re: Post-structural trad

In an Irish trad band performance you can get a little closer to the Bluegrass approach of spotlighting one player. Here’s an example with Colin Farrell, Kevin Crawford, and John Doyle:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eJ_FySsvayM

Jump to 6:59 if you want to see the “improv” bit. They open with a jig played in unison, then at 6:59 Kevin sits out for the first reel, where Colin takes off on something similar to an extended Bluegrass break. He doesn’t depart as far from the melody as a Bluegrass or Jazz player would with scale-based soloing away from the heart of the tune. It’s more like what Liz Carroll does in her extended riffing on some of her tunes, playing games with the rhythm pulse and a few melodic variations. Then for the third tune, Kevin jumps back in and they finish with unison melody. This three-part set could be seen as an analog of a Bluegrass song that starts and ends with everyone playing together, with a featured performer taking a solo in the middle.

You can do this in a band. It doesn’t work in an amateur Irish trad session because the “social contract” is an agreement on unison playing, not a scripted series of people rotating between being the star performer and the rhythm section.

The other issue is that trad sessions have more instruments of the same type than a typical band, and many of them only play linear melody. What are a half-dozen fiddlers and a few flute and whistle players supposed to do, if someone wanted to take an instrumental break? How many Irish fiddlers are skilled at drone/harmony backup? I’ve met a few, but it’s somewhat rare on the amateur level. Even more rare for flutes, and not practical at all for high whistle players. In practice, it would be like what happens when someone is asked to sing a song at a session. The melody players end up sitting on their hands, because they’re not skilled backers. And even if they were, there are too many of them playing linear melody instruments.
