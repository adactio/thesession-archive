Re: Identifying Keys

The four most commonly encountered modes in Irish traditional music are Ionian (Major), Aolian (Natural Minor), Dorian and Mixolydian.

And here are the most commonly occurring keys:

G-Major – 1 sharp
D-Major – 2 sharps
A-Major – 3 sharps
F- Major – one flat

A-Minor – no sharps or flats
B-Minor – two sharps

A-Dorian – one sharp
E-Dorian – two sharps

D-Mixolydian – one sharp
A-Mixolydian – two sharps

When hearing a tune for the first time, the first thing to do is to work out the tonal centre. Unless it’s a tune that doesn’t “resolve”, this would be the last note of the tune. Another way of working out the tonal centre is to imagine which note would be suitable as a drone to be played throughout the tune.

The next thing to do is to look at the key signature. So if (for example) the tonal centre is A and there are three sharps, the key would be A-Major. If , on the other hand the tonal centre is A but there are only two sharps, the key would be A-Mixolydian.

The above is just the basics, but should be enough to get you started. Other complications are tunes that used gapped scales (e.g. hexatonic) and tunes that modulate (i.e. change key and/or mode within the tune).

But you only really need to understand the above if you are a backer. If you are a melody player, all you need to do is to play the tune.

Re: Identifying Keys

The keynote of a tune is the note on which the tune seems to come to rest, or feels ‘stable’ - as manxygirl says this is not necessarily the last note of the tune (many dance tunes are *cyclic*, i.e. they are not ‘designed’ to come to rest at the end, but to lead back into the first part). So the essential thing is to be able to recognise the points in the tune where that ‘stability’ is reached. Once you recognise the note* played at those points, you can identify it with reference to your fiddle strings.

Having identified the keynote, you then need to determine whether it is (broadly speaking**) major or minor. You might find that your ear will tell you this, once you have the keynote. If not, the most important determining factor here is the third step of the scale - it will be either 3 semitones or 4 semitones above the keynote. If it is 3 semitones above, it’s minor; if it’s 4 semitones above, it’s major. Using a real example, if you have identified the keynote as D and the 3rd is F-natural, then it’s in D minor; if the 3rd is F#, then it’s D major (or just ‘D’).

*If you find there are two different ‘stable’ notes in the tune and you can’t decide which is the keynote, the chances are, you’re not being stupid - this is where modes (too big a subject to go into in depth in this post) come into play. There are many tunes that are of ambiguous tonality - if you were to read through all the comments in the tunes section of this site (something that would take you several lifetimes), you would find several arguments between members over what keys or modes certain tunes are in.

**The 3rd step of the scale determines whether the overall feel of a tune is major or minor. But whether it is a true major or minor scale or one of the common modes is determined by variance in the 6th and 7th steps. Furthermore, minor scales (according to classical music theory) come in three types: melodic, harmonic and natural - although you are only really likely to come across the last of these three in Irish traditional music.

In brief:
Mixolydian mode - major scale with flat 7th
Dorian mode - natural minor scale with sharp 6th

(You beat me to it, Mix. Still, two slightly different takes on the same thing.)

Re: Identifying Keys

It’s already been answered but I would say as long as there aren’t any modulating notes (tunes with modulating notes are my favorite though) then you can use the number of flats or sharps to narrow down the key. For instance if there are no flats or sharps then you know it is either C Major, A minor, D dorian, or G mixolydian. Which one of those four it is can be determined by where the musical phrased begin and/or end. For instance the Fisherman’s Lilt generally has no flats or sharps and starts its first two notes on G and E in the first part. The part then resolves to a C note at the end of the phrase. To me at least that says C Major.

The next phrase then starts out on a high A note and kind of stays in that modality for a while. So I would consider using A minor chords here, maybe throw in a G chord, but the part ultimately ends on the C note again. The part is pretty much still in C Major but seems to really hint strongly at an A minor modality for a while. It’s not really changing keys per se, so this is why a lot of guys say to check the last note in a tune.

Most of the time though you can actually go by the first note as well. Many many tunes start on their tonic note. The tonic note is the note that shares it’s name with the key signature. A major’s tonic note is A. Usually if the first note is not the tonic, the tune will still start out on one of the chord tones of the tonic’s diatonic chord. So for A major, it’s diatonic chord is A, C# and E. Foxhunter’s reel can pretty much be said to be in A major (I’ve never heard anybody say it differently unless they are actually transposing it for their box or something). Foxhunter’s does not start on an A note but instead starts on the 5th note of the A major scale. Or if you made an A major triad in root position (A, C#, E), the first note of the tune would be the top note in the chord.

Hello Liam! I just made this up. It might help, but I remain skeptical until I hear the proof & have tasted the pudding.

Listen for the tonic (keynote). *If* the tune being played has a full octave (does it?), no more; no less, then you can probably sort the key, the mode, the major, the minor, etc.

*If* I have this written the following correctly it shows 4 modes, beginning with the tonic (T) & proceeds up the scale alternating accordingly in whole tones (W) & semitones (S).

If I have it *wrong* we’re buggered, but this is the internet so Mother Earth will go on like she always has …

[DISCLAIMER:Tunes don’t always conform to preconceived notions, theories, mores, pompous attitudes, mustard pitstops, weights &/or measures, etc.]

T W W S W W W S ~ Ionian … 2nd to third is major, 7th to octave is minor
T W S W W W S W ~ Dorian … 2nd to third is minor, 7th to octave major
T W S W W S W W ~ Aeolian … 2nd to third is major, 7th to octave is major
T W W S W W S W ~ Mixolydian … 2nd to third is major, 7th to octave is major

Key of E major ~ Ionian
E is the tonic. The third, (G#) is major. Seventh (D#) is a semitone lower than the octave.

E Mixolydian
E is the tonic. The third (G#) is major. Seventh (D) is a whole tone lower than the octave.

Key of A minor ~ Aeolian
A is the tonic. The third (C) is minor. Seventh (G) is a whole tone lower than the octave. [6th is minor]

A Dorian
A is the tonic. The third (C) is minor. Seventh (G) is a whole tone lower than the octave. [6th is major]

Re: Identifying Keys

I found this description (from my link earlier) quite handy and easy to follow :

• The Ionian mode is the same as the major scale itself, eg
C D E F G A B C (Scale of C major)

• The Dorian mode is a major scale started from the second degree eg
D E F G A B C D (Minor mode)

• The Phrygian mode is a major scale started from the third degree
E F G A B C D E (Minor mode)

• The Lydian mode is a major scale started from the fourth degree
F G A B C D E F (Major mode)

• The Mixolydian mode is a major scale started from the fifth degree
G A B C D E F G (Major mode)

• The Aeolian mode is a major scale started from the sixth degree
A B C D E F G A (Minor mode)

• Finally, the Locrian mode is a major scale started from the seventh degree
B C D E F G A B (Minor mode)

All of these examples are based on the scale of C major.

For all the other 11 keys, the notes of the modes are incremented accordingly.

Regardless of the key, the relative intervals in the modes will always be the same.
