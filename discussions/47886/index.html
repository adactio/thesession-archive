Re: To read or not to read

It is due to a totally different ethos. Classical players are taught to always read the music and specifically not to remember pieces (until you become a soloist). Also the music is a set piece with no variation. In folk music we go to great lengths to play pieces without the dots even to the extent that some purists only learn tunes by ear. In addition tunes are sequenced on the fly (and can be played with considerable variation) so there is no set running order to enable a score to be generated. It is generally frowned on to use dots in a tune session or to read words at a song session. In the end it comes down to a determined effort to learn the music by heart for the reasons stated above.

Re: To read or not to read

Simple answer to the question - they don’t need to - they know the tunes.

Classical training places high value on being able to play a piece of music perfectly from a musical score with having previously heard or played. ITM doesn’t.

A typical classical music piece isn’t played singly through end to end by a single instrument. Instruments come in and out as the piece advances. ITM doesn’t.

Classical music is multi-part and an instrument may play a lead line or one or other of harmony/counterpoint lines. ITM doesn’t.

For all of the above reasons sight reading (and playing) is the modus operandi and a large amount of the learning effort is expended in acquiring the necessary skills. ITM emphasises aural skills over reading.

You don’t NEED to learn the piece by heart in full because you will always have the score available when playing. Familiarity with the piece will usually be helpful. ITM is generally hostile to scores in sessions. It’s a heads up playing.

The ergonomics/setup for classical musicians readily allows for the additional space and segregation to facilitate the placement of the sheet music and reading stands. Concert hall or large venue versus pub or small room.

For folk/traditional music most of the above are opposite. Simple 16-bar tunes, limited modes and keys and within a narrower range of complexity lends to easy memorisation. For ITM single part melody without harmony and played straight through from beginning to end. Played often in tight circle and cramped space without room for music stands and sheets of paper. A somewhat chaotic running order of tunes would have players flitting back and forth in Tunebook. Also players will likely have a large core repertoire (typical 200-500 tunes) played regularly.

“Playing from” (as opposed to learning from) sheet music is frowned upon.

Re: To read or not to read

I was brought up to be a reader, but have made myself also play by ear. I go to some sessions where no-one uses sheet music, and others where nearly everyone does. I will inevitably get more right notes in when using the sheets, and I don’t think I play any worse, or even differently, when I use them. Playing instruments that have both melody AND accompaniment, it does help to have the sheets to know what chords or other accompaniment I should play: for piano, I only have single-line melody and chords, no left-hand prescribed part or right hand fill-ins: I just make that up to fit the chord structure.
But I also enjoy the freedom of going out to play without a bag full of folders or my iPad. Generally those tunes that I can play without the sheets have initially been learned from the dots and committed to memory, rather than being entirely learned by ear.

As for playing in orchestras, yes, I used to do that: I remember one score which had 397 bars’ rest before my next crucial entry - no cue written in the score at all. I think I did try to count it all out the first time, but was foiled by the conductor stopping midway and re-starting at heaven only knows what bar!

↳ Posted by Respect on February 12th, 2023.

Re: To read or not to read

Contrary to popular thinking, orchestral musicians definitely memorize music, whether they’re soloists or not.

A good reason to have a score in front of you as an orchestra/ensemble player is that orchestral scores are written for a specific constellation of instruments. Only rarely will your group be composed of the right instruments in the correct numbers. So a musical director or conductor will adapt the score to fill in blanks and reflect the makeup of the ensemble. So you might find yourself marking up your score to pick up parts that weren’t originally yours.

…and, the musical director or conductor may just want to put their own creative stamp on the piece, so you’ll be taking notes for those changes as well. For example, the conductor may want certain violins to play a certain passage, and the other half to rest. Lots of lining things out and note-taking.

For a smaller ensemble - say, a quartet - if you don’t have a score written explicitly for your - say - violin, viola, flute, cello constellation, you’ll be scribbling all over whatever closest-matching music you can find to arrange the music for your instruments; deciding which instrument gets the oboe’s solo, for example.

After enough repetitions, though, sheet music often becomes archival, or a ready reference for a substitute player.

Like ITM, classical music is also repetitious. So it can also be helpful when, say, the (rare instrument) player next to you is staring down page after page of whole note rests, to let them follow along on your sheet music; helps them to stay oriented.

One year, our son came home from summer classical violin camp. We asked what he had learned. He picked up his violin and blazed through the whole six weeks worth in a half an hour, without sheet music. And it was a lot of music.

The next year he came home and showed us everything he’d learned in tramp (trampoline) camp, but that’s another story.

"There is a session which I sometimes visit in Dufftown when I’m on my travels. It’s held in The British Legion on a Monday and, for sight readers, it’s actually a very good night.

The players seem to be mainly Fiddle and Accordion or Fiddle Society musicians and are, in the main, very experienced. Re: To read or not to read

Ideally, you’d want to be able to play by ear, memorise, and sight-read, as appropriate. Scenario one: a session without dots, somebody plays a tune new to you, three times through, first time, you identify what key it’s in and one or two prominent features, second time, you’re half getting it, third time, a bit nearer. After the set, you ask what the tune’s called; later at home you look it up on the net and read it to learn the twiddly bits you didn’t quite get by ear. Next session, you can play from memory.
Scenario two: RSCDS musicians’ day workshop with an evening dance to follow. Sixteen dances, at least four tunes per dance, eight in some cases, so total 65-85 tunes in all, some of which you may know but many of which you won’t. Only possible if you get the music a couple of weeks at least in advance and read and practice (and in my case mark each set with where I’m changing whistles) but then on the day the reading is only an aid to finger-memory. For the dance you need to be up to speed, which for Scottish reels is quite fast.
