Sessions are for a company of musicians to gather & play tunes as they choose?

The header of this thread feels awkward. Yet, I am posting it because I consider ITM sessions (as we know them) to still be evolving.

We discuss because we can. We play because of everything else. The former is easy. The latter is personal, social, aesthetic, musical (& all that goes w/matters of music & art).

Playing music in Irish traditional music sessions is simple! Yet, we have this forum which is not an ITM session. Let that sink in; thesession.org forum is for discussion. Sessions are where the rubber meets the road.

I could state the cliche, “What happens in (thesessiondotorg) stays in the (local) session.” I just did. Point is the lines are blurred *here* everyday; or this forum could not exist. But thesession.org is open to the world; unlike your local Irish session.

This forum inevitably is *not* a substitute for playing in a session. It is a good place to sit at the bar & discuss the session from a distance, visit the jacks & then return to the session & play tunes…

I think discussing sessions is vital to the directions sessions’ have to assist in making their choices. Those choices exist in every session. Whether members of a session talk about them is a choice for any established or nascent session. However, sessions are not geared to discuss such matters. Could not this forum be geared to fill the gap?

Not wanting to make this discussion political, because the tunes are my why I go to sessions, I’m about to post a link which directly addresses nationalism in trad music. You’ve been warned.

Again, *not* wanting to bring politics into a thread; but since it is already immersed in the idiom I have little choice & will push the button ~ https://quod.lib.umich.edu/m/mp/9460447.0003.205/--defining-the-nation-confining-the-musician-the-case-of-irish?rgn=main;view=fulltext

Re: Sessions are for a company of musicians to gather & play tunes as they choose?

Everybody is different - that’s the good thing about humanity - but to feel that we ‘belong’ we must align with a common set of values, behaviours, choices which express the identity of the thing(s) we want to be belong to. I assume that most of us who contribute to this site have some sense of common ground even if it gets a bit ragged at times. For example (1), is Scottish music ok for a traditional Irish music site? (then ask the question again and substitute the word ‘Scottish’ with any of ‘Breton’, ‘Cape Breton’, Welsh‘, ’Manx‘ , Bluegrass’, ‘Jazz’, etc etc)

For example (2) is ‘learning by ear’ (whatever that means) the one true way to learn a tune (what if you learn it ‘by ear’ from a person who learnt it from a book? Could you tell?). Do people who claim to ‘play by ear’ really simply play from memory?

For example (3) Is there really only one ‘correct’ version of a tune? (Well obviously not but where are the boundaries ?). What happens when one person plays their ‘correct’ version of a tune and somebody else recites their different ‘correct’ version simultaneously?

There are rules everywhere but mostly not written down. You have to figure them out. You have to figure out if the same rules apply in different places. And who appoints the custodians and gatekeepers? Or do they just appoint themselves? Who is entitled to dictate to the rest of us?

‘Sessions’ (whatever exactly we all understand that to mean) are all different. Not just do sessions in different places differ, but successive sessions in the same place differ, even when the same people play the same tunes. Sometimes things ‘click’ and are fantastic, sometimes they don’t quite work. Sometimes you can just sit out a set of tunes and listen. Sometimes you can experiment with different improvisations or chords. Sometimes you just can’t hear yourself play above the din of conflicting noises and it probably wouldn’t matter what noises came out of your instrument. Sometimes a particular combination of instruments makes magic.

In the end what you do in a ‘session’ is up to whoever turns up on the day. Where things come unstuck is where people don’t read the room. The boundaries for what is ‘ok’ for a particular session are unlikely to be written down anywhere. They may not be rigid. The repertoire (for want of a better word) is not static: new tunes come along, old ones drop off. A few songs may be ok but ten songs may be too many in one session, and twenty songs risks homicide. Another session may have no songs at all just tunes (if only…) another may have mostly songs with a few tunes dotted in between. Some sessions rotate politely and rigidly around a circle. Some sessions are ‘led’

The real key is to think about who is hosting your ‘sessions’. Some tolerant pub staying open late so you can play even more tunes, in spite of hardly buying any drinks for over four hours. The pub regulars who don’t get to watch the ‘big game’ on the pub telly because the session is ‘on’. We are not entitled to just turn up anywhere we like and start a session. So the answer to “Sessions are for a company of musicians…” (whatever the question actually was..it wasn’t entirely clear) is what your hosts would like and then, in as democratic a way as possible, what the majority who turn up want to do

Re: Sessions are for a company of musicians to gather & play tunes as they choose?

There is of course an uncomfortable truth is what the OP suggests, that some might be unaware of through ignorance or geographic distance or who just prefer to ignore and pretend it doesn’t exist.

And that is that trad music in Ireland has always been a bit about the expression of our culture as opposed to that of the historical occupier. There is and always has been a nationalist element to it, sometimes just expressed as a pride in national identity, other times more aggressively. For example, anyone familiar with the ballad tradition here will be aware of the sentiments pushed by the Wolfe Tones and their modern incarnation Kneecap. I mean who turns up at pressers in armoured cars and wearing balaclavas? These people have a serious following now north & south of the border with those in 20s & 30s. State media love em because they promote the gaeilge, a sacred cow.

There has been a considerable growth in gaelscoileanna, where all teaching is done through Irish and of course where the music is also emphasised. There are various reasons for this but not least that many Irish parents want their children to be educated with other Irish children and not with the rapidly increasing number of children from other cultures. Immigration both legal and those arriving as refugees and asylum seekers/ economic migrants is a hot topic.

So playing a bit of trad and meeting others to play a few tunes is increasingly a way of holding onto an Irish identity. Not so much now to do with the historical occupier but more to do with the rapidly changing nature of Irish society. And it always been thus, the principal reason there is a session scene in London or Chicago etc is that ‘birds of a feather flock together’.

As for Danny Boy, it’s a fine song and melody. Much loved.

Re: Sessions are for a company of musicians to gather & play tunes as they choose?

“Irish traditional music session”. it took me a long time to understand that what makes a session “Irish” and “traditional” is not the Irish-ness of the tunes and songs, but the idea of musicians getting together in a public setting and playing the music together for ourselves, with an active/passive participation of the punters and with active/passive support of the publican. if these elements are present, you can play Scottish, Breton, Playford and old-time tunes all day and Irish visiting from Ireland still recognize it as an Irish trad session (“this is like back home in Ireland” is the comments I get). of course, another key element is a certain quality to the music - vigorous, upbeat.

Say like it like it is. There are close links between trad music and the desire to identify with others of the same cultural identity. There’s nothing to be ashamed of in it, even it can be viewed as an expression of nationalism. The chap who owns this site is from down Cork way, but living over in England for many years if I’m not wrong and one assumes his interest in starting this site was partly driven by a desire to hold onto a sense of national identity and share it between others in the same boat.

When we Irish travelled/ emigrated for work in the decades from the 1950s, it was natural to seek out others who shared some of the same experiences and music. That’s changed now, for better or worse, Ireland is a relatively well off country thanks to membership of the EU and investment from the big USA multi national and tech companies.

Now our children emigrate partly to get experience abroad but also that they can’t afford to rent or buy a house here now. And instead we have this large inflow of people of other cultures. When I visit the streets now where I grew up, I recognise the buildings but not the people about me much anymore. Don’t get me wrong, travel is grand to go and visit other countries and experience other cultures. But I’m an Irishman and living in Ireland, I want to live in an Irish cultural landscape. And that includes playing a few tunes. These are the things that we have to hold onto.

Re Culture - Respect, you might be interested in that Helen o’Shea book.

It’s interesting because she compares a couple of sessions in East Clare -
one is described as hierarchical (inner chairs reserved for elite local musicians), more closed to outsiders, and intent upon preserving a certain local rhythm and repertoire.
The other is described as inclusive and jolly…with stories, jokes, newcomers welcome and invited to play, and punter participation…Helen seemed to prefer this style.

So “a company of musicians gathering to play tunes as they choose” is not quite as simple and obvious as it seems…
