Re: Jig Rhythm Help

Jigs are written in 6/8 timing. While I play banjo I play guitar accompaniment more regularly, and when playing the rhythm for a jig I play a 1,2,3 1,2,3 beat in the space where someone tapping their foot would count out 1,2.

As for the notes, they aren’t necessarily laid out the way you’ve said above, so I wouldn’t stick to that as a guide for how they work.

I find timing and rhythm hard to explain in words, but that’s as close as I can really get it!

All you really need to do is listen to someone playing a jig and tap your foot along and the rhythm will come naturally with it :)

Re: Jig Rhythm Help

If you want to analyse it, you usually lengthen the first quaver by about a third, and then shorten the next one by the same amount. The last quaver keeps normal length. You also leave gaps between the notes if you want the music to bounce.
Or listen to someone who you think is good and copy them!

Re: Jig Rhythm Help

Jigs are in 6/8 time but are counted as two groups of three. One and a Two and a One and a Two and a etc. That is the conventional way of writing jigs in Irish music. Note that the three notes of a grouping in a jig are not a triplet but merely three quavers.
You asked about a comparison comparing the “classical” style of playing a jig and the Irish style.

Re: Jig Rhythm Help

Don’t confuse the ‘conventional’ way of notating jigs, and the traditional way of playing them.
This business about two groups of three is all well and good when writing tunes down, but it bears little or no relation to the way the tunes are played.
Some players actually shorten the first note, or should I say stop short (ie instead of an eighth note they shorten it to, say, a tenth note, and a little silence) rather than ‘usually lengthening’ as described above.
If you want a starting point, I would say play every note absolutely the same length, without swing, and group the notes one-two-three-FOUR and-a one-two-three-FOUR and-a by virtue of dynamics. It depends a lot, of course, on the tune and whatever phrasing you want to give it; but I would most definitely avoid the bumpity bumpity rhythm suggested by typical notation.
I find it easiest when learning a tune to play along with a recording I like, until I feel I have the rhythm right, then stop and listen for a few bars. If what I’m hearing matches what I have in my head, I carry on. If I hear a change in emphasis, or something unexpected, I try to figure out what the difference is. What I do not do is play every jig the same way, and most certainly not 1-2-3 1-2-3

The video here might illustrate what I’m saying -- note the uniform rhythm of the second dancer’s taps, as compared with the first dancer and the piper.
http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-ijk4Yox4r0

Re: Jig Rhythm Help

«I am starting with jigs, as they are easy to learn so far, will try to learn the correct reel rhythm once I get a hang of it.»

They are generally easier to memorise than reels, but getting a decent jig rhythm is much more difficult than people realise. And possibly harder than reels, based on my observation that many people who have been at it for years and can play reels acceptably continue to make an awful ballux of jigs. At least you are asking the right questions.

The mechanics of how you go about getting a good rhythm vary depending on the instrument you are playing. But more important than the mechanics - and much more important than having ideas about relative note lengths - is hearing in your head how a jig should sound. Once you know the sound you want to make, finding the right mechanics is the easy part. That’s my contention anyway. :-)

The first and the third notes can have emphasis, so don’t think it’s as simple as BING-bang-bang. It could be BING-bang-BING.

There’s no one-size-fits-all formula, but in the one-two-three, one-two-three format of 6/8, rhythm guitarists often think of the rhythm as three-one, three-one, etc. , leaving the two-beat to take the least emphasis.

Re: Jig Rhythm Help

I think of it as a polyrythm. It’s a 3:2 feel superimposed over a straight 6/8.

That’s three notes over two beats, combined with 6/8.

You don’t hear the 3-over-2 feel expressed overtly very often. It’s subtle, but it’s there.

Check out this recording from Kevin Burke and the late Michel O Domnhaill: http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qcQDyob6b9k

Right around 1:10, as the set transitions from the first tune to the next, Michel temporarily drops the 6/8 aspects of the polyrythm and expressly states the 3-over-2 idea.

It’s difficult to write out in this format, but if you can tap out three notes evenly spread over two beats of a jig, that 2nd note in that 3:2 should fall where the middle note in the 2nd quaver hits. Best not to try to count it, though. Try to feel it.

Re: Jig Rhythm Help

“the middle note in the second quaver” Huh??
What you are talking about is a hemiola. In other words three quarter notes (crochets for you Brits) played over six eight notes(quavers.)When played in strict time the second quarter note will always fall where the third eight note does. Simpe math.
ONE-two-three-FOUR-five-six
ONE-two-THREE-four-FIVE-six
