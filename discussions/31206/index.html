Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

start experimenting with playing across the rows , so playa scale of g up your c row, go to the g row for just the f#, then …
start on the c row again, this time experiment by playing an 8 note scale of g major, using only index and middle finger, start with your first button on the c row with your index finger whichever is the g note first, then reverse on same button, then first button on right on c row, out in, then first button on left on g row and reverse on same button, then first button and reverse on right on g row, you should now have played a scale of g major, using your 2 strong fingers all the time.. index and middle. learn a few tunes this way you are now playing across the rows, this is useful as is… playing up and down the row.

learn also to cross finger with the same button, use two fingers on one button to get a legato [flowing sound], learn also to play a scale very staccato, this is all about getting dexterity and control on the instrument, ignore pillocks who say that staccato has no place in irish music, you might at some point want to imitate a whistle player tongueing or play northumbrian pipe music, so it is a skill worth acquiring. one bad habit that some players have particularly…. English concertina players is keeping a finger down before another is released, this is ok for airs or songs , but generally [imo] not good for lively dance music

Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

I always hesitate to give advice.. but I think you need to balance the need for early success against long term technique. By this I mean, that it’s straightforward enough to play many tunes in key of G or key of C for that matter. You get playing quickly and can knock tunes out. However the key of D, it seems to me is the one to crack on the anglo. If you can play tunes smoothly in D, you can adapt more easily to tunes in G rather than the other way around. That’s my experience anyway and I am mainly speaking of a ‘standard’ 30 button in Wheatstone layout. Instruments and people differ so this may or may not help.

a common mistake, especially starting on a 2o button, would be to play G tunes on the G row only. Think of the C row as your home row. Also try to avoid using the same finger on two different buttons one right after the other.

i believe,noel hill advocates a system for beginners which involves cross rowing and in certain circumstances uses one finger to play two different buttons one after the other, example index finger left hand side might play a g on the c row, and then an e on the g row with the same finger, the disadvantage of this is there is a gap between the note the advantage is that you are using your strongest finger for both buttons.

Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

Ornamentation is important.
Rolls on C through A on the C row left side, use the first two fingers on the first two buttons on the C row right side, no change of bellows direction:
C{ec}C
D{fd}D
E{ec}E
F{fd}F
G{ec}G
A{fd}A
where the notes in brackets are tapped very quickly.
Some of these can be used with the main note coming from the G row left side. For some, you could use the first two buttons on the G row, and for some you could use the second two button on the C row for the notes that are bracketed.

Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

I’ll repeat what I wrote above, because it’s worth repeating. If you want to play concertina with other people in session situations, you’ll find a lot of tunes go in the key of D. This inevitably involves more jumping around the keyboard and it is not as intuitive as other keys on the instrument. So try and make it intuitive, get comfortable playing in D.

Alternatively, if want to play more in the ‘traditional Clare’ style, then play your tunes in C etc. and their various related modes i.e. more along the rows. Same music, same tunes - just not that common when you go to play with others. That’s standardisation I suppose..

Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

My first concertina was a 20 button 2-row C/G, but the moment I realized I needed the C# on the 3rd row I traded up. If you stay with the 20 button you will always have people tuning down to you to play with you, or you’ll end up getting an instrument that’s D/A. The problem with the D/A tuned ones is they are more limited for tunes in the other keys. The 3-row C/G is your best bet to be able to play most tunes in most of the usual keys. Also… there’s a difference in the sound as it relates to the tunings and fingering systems. You should listen to as many concertina players as you can to get an idea of that difference and then make your decisions thusly. I have known many players who continued with tunings other than C/G only to become doubly frustrated when they finally realized they had to unlearn one system and learn to play a C/G to get the sound they really wanted.

Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

Hi Erin, this comes back round to my first point. It’s arguably better to learn to play comfortably in key of D i.e. have a ‘default’ set of buttons that suit playing in that key. Then adapt this to playing in G etc. Because in my humble experience, it’s harder to go the other way. You can play comfortably on the 20 button in C and also in G. Playing in G, you can just play along the row but it would be better to learn the cross row technique using notes from both the C & G rows. You can quickly get comfortable on this and knock out lots of tunes.

However, when you want to play ‘standard session repetoire’ with other people, you’ll find a lot of tunes are played in D major. So you get a 30 button with a C# on the accidental row, but it’s not that simple. Because of the way the notes and phrases flow, for want of a better word in key of D, you’ll find yourself having to use alternative buttons for B and probably A as well as C#, particularly if you have a Wheatstone layout with just one C#. This requires a rethink, the same movements that you have instinctively learned to play in G to play certain phrases, now require a different set of movements.

I know someone who takes beginner/ intermediate classes here in Ireland and many of the tunes are in D. At first I wondered why? Why make it more awkward than it has to be at the start. But that’s the approach of this teacher, get comfortable with this and then adapt.

It’s also my experience - I’m more or less self taught for a number of years. I had played B/C accordion previously so found it handy to play along the C row and switch to the G row for F# and the higher octave on the right hand. I could play along comfortably enough in G without thinking too much, the notes fall under the fingers. But I struggled more so playing in D and I gradually found that I’ve adapted my default fingering to suit this key - I would have been better learning at the start to get comfortable playing in D.

Of course, this only applies where you want to play the standard session keys. If you’re playing for yourself etc., it matters little.

Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

….well, i’ll be a contrarian and advise that while the staccato and back-and-forth style often advocated by such as noel hill is great and kind of a signature of bisonoric concertina, the fact that anglo concertina has multiple notes in both directions gives you vastly more choice than, say, a harmonica or one-row melodeon player, or even a two-row bisonoric box player, as to whether you want to phrase more fluidly or more back-and-forth. and the itm pasture has a lot of grazing land at the smooth and fluid end where you are still legitimately within the traditional style before you’ve wandered outside the fence into “classical” or whatever that is so smooth it’s beyond even itm’s smooth end.

the rub is that the kind of stylistic leeway that is granted and even almost codified as to fiddlers, pipers or flute players---you’ll hear of “long bow” or “short bow” fiddling, “open” or “closed” piping, etc, --for whatever reason is often denied to free reed players by the stylistic gatekeepers. i find this silly. if you want to phrase your concertina like the flowing east-galway flute players, or the flowing, “open” style Traveler pipers, or the “long bow” slurry fiddlers of clare and east galway, go for it, whether noel hill would think it “sounds like a piano” or not. noel himself has an extremely “back-and-forth” or in fiddle terms “short-bow” style despite his prowess patterning “across the rows,” and it’s brilliant. but it’s not the only way to phrase or articulate the concertina and still be playing traditional irish music.

if you listen to the fiddlers, some are very staccato and some are extremely smooth and slurry. concertina players like packie russell or the late john joe casey (who was a fiddler) played very smooth, doing a lot of notes in one direction before switching, and still sounded very traditional. you get to do the same if you want. you just want to train your ear as to where the “outer limits” are in itm’s wide stylistic spectrum by listening to as much great traditional music as you can by players of a variety of instruments…..

Re: Bad habits on the Anglo concertina!

An example of along the rows would be, play a G scale up from the middle button on the G row LHS, in/out,in/out,in/out, then RHS out/in.

The same thing cross rowed would be, and this is only one variation among many, (capital letters for G row, lower case for C row) G,A ,b,C,d,E,F#,G. The lower case b and d are on the C row RHS.

You will notice the AbCdEF# are all in the same direction. It is easy once you have found the notes to make them fast as well as smooth. It has a lot of uses, one is to help balance up in with out to minimise bellows use. In the end though the only important things are phrasing and rhythm; cross rowing is a major tool for these.

The distinction between across the rows and along the rows is one only made by beginners. It loses its meaning when you have been playing for a while. In the end you need to be able to both play along the rows and across the rows, and of the two, along the rows is harder to make smooth. Some people have trouble learning across the rows because the concept is not laid out in front of them like a piano keyboard and because the solution might be slightly different every time. You do have to puzzle it out a little more. It is half the charm..!

I think the best progress you might make is if you found a teacher. They will save you years. There must be someone in that big city of yours who can help. You have to be careful about internet advice, most of the people answering you here are totally on the ball but we all come from different viewpoints, styles, experience. One is an English player and I often find his utterances tangential or just plain silly. Better to learn a coherent style from one person for better or worse until the moment comes when you think, mmm I’d rather do it this way.

I can’t remember much about the tunes other than that the Cliffs of Moher is an unusual version. The rhythm is typical, strong backbeat and using a transfer to frontbeat for effect, unisons, chords, rolls cuts etc. There is one thing he does with this instrument I love, he hits a low backbeat note hard enough that it starts bent down and then allows it to come up into tune on the last beat of the bar.

Hope this answers some of your questions…
