Itm snobs

Ive been around here for a while now, been to many a session, ditto for concerts and fleadhs. I can’t help but feel like there’s alot of people involved in Itm that seem to think they have a right to run down other types of music and musicians. For example, songs are every bit a part of our traditional heritage as tunes, yet most here show distain and ignorance to such songs. Also, who gives anybody the right to say what instrument is allowed and whats not. The constant badgering of guitar, bodhran and bouzouki players annoys me. Its very unfair. ‘tunes that lose bad guitarists’ is just insulting to guitar players. When in fairness, there are plenty of fiddle and accordion players I’ve seen lost at sea in sessions. My main point is people should stop being snobs and enjoy the music. Encourage newbies instead of berating them. End of my thought for today

Re: Itm snobs

Zoukibanjo…I couldn’t agree more with your sentiment. Thanks for speaking up. I host a weekly gathering at my home and the only people who aren’t asked back are the critical.

Seems to me there are three thoughts here. First are those who criticize other music styles and traditions. I suppose that for some people the only way they can add value to their own thoughts is to run down the thoughts of others. Best to shake ‘em off. Trying to change their way is like pissin’ up a rope.There are many kinds of music I don’t like and so what. Lot’s of players don’t like ITM (hard to believe but true). If there’s not room for everybody in music then there’s no room for anybody. As Alasdair Fraser once told me, “when you pick up your instrument you must choose which language you want to speak and you’re the only one who has to like it”. Nobody, now think hard about this, nobody has the right to judge or criticize another’s musical choices. You have only the right to be somewhere else. Move on.

Much the same thought applies to songs. Songs are not all of the same species. Pub songs, love songs (mostly unrequited it seems), songs of protest, jokes, Tin Pan Irish songs (you know, the ones written mostly in New York by Jewish composers for Bing Crosby) are just a few. Personally I like many of them and they all have their place. That said, where I live ITM is greatly underserved when we do gather we come to play and as lovely as songs and aires can be they do eat into our limited playing time. I confess to a bit of selfishness here and no I’m not an A-list player.

Then there’s the issue of instrument choices. I know that some people’s Underoos are just too small but I’ll exclude them out of hand. I doubt that anybody thinks poorly of anybody else choice of instrument when they’re good at it, or at least trying to be…when what they play sounds like, well, music. Everybody, regardless of the weapon they choose, should be encouraged and supported…as long as they’re making the effort to get it right! That means understanding the melodic and rhythmic structure of the tune. If the tune isn’t in your head you can’t play it and you can’t accompany it. That’s where many guitarists get annoying. And they may be great guitarists in another setting. You just can’t jam 12 bar blues changes into ITM and get it right. Drummers that can’t keep time, box/flute/banjo/fiddle players who can’t find the melody or rhythm, and don’t even try, are equally annoying. I guess that’s the heart of the matter. Respect the tune and the other players by learning and you’re usually (but, sadly, not always) welcome. At least that approach has let me keep my chair over the last few years. So when someone suggests “tunes that drive out bad (…insert instrument here…) players” I believe that’s what they mean, the disrespectful lout, not the instrument or inexperienced. (Disrespect will get you dis-invited to my house,slamming the “newbie” will get you chased out the door.) By the way if you substitute any other genre for ITM you can use he same words.

Thanks again for your thoughts. Enjoy the music…and enjoy the musicians. Peace and joy.

Re: Itm snobs

As has been said once or twice before on this board, a session isn’t a performance. If I’m on stage and a guitarist buggers things up I think I have a right to be annoyed. But at a session? For me a session is about enjoying yourself in the company of other musicians, not making great music. At every session I’ve been involved with newcomers have always been welcomed and given a chance to join in. But what happens after that depends entirely on their attitude, not their musical ability. Those who keep a low profile and blend in with the group do fine, those who are desparate to show how brilliant they are generally have a tough time of it, regardless of what instrument they play or how well they play it. Most sessions I’ve been to will embrace and encourage low level players if they are genuinely interested and trying to improve. But they’ll shun those who aren’t interested in learning and just want to show what they can do.

The instruments that most often cause mutterings are bodhrans and guitars. It’s not the instruments that cause the problem, it’s the people behind them. With bodhrans the problem is obvious - some people with absolutely no musical training, knowledge or experience think they can pick up a bodhran and become part of the group without putting in any effort t0 learn the music or the instrument. The problem with guitarists is slightly different - you can get some very good guitarists from other genres who cause problems. Because their normal way of working is to improvise over a chord progression they assume that will work in ITM. And because they know they are good guitarists they assume their talent will be appreciated, and that they are bringing something new to the music, rather that screwing up what was already there. It’s not that they can’t play, just that they don’t understand the music or what is required of them. Somewhere on the interweb there is a wonderful anecdote about the train wreck that ensued when Keith Richards tried to play a slip jig with the Chieftains.

@ Mark M
Certainly there are sessions that are about making great music. Not every session is the same. Not every session participant has exactly the same objectives. But there is something to be said for those moments during an evening, when everything comes together, and the sound is exactly what you dreamed it might be -- and to be contributing to that is a wonderful thing.

This is not to say there is anything wrong with social gatherings, and friends getting together to enjoy good craic. I enjoy those sessions. But I also love going to sessions where a small group get together and try to make very good music -- not as a performance for an audience, but for themselves. I don’t think this is snobbery. But if everyone except one person agrees on what it means to be good music, then the one person who disagrees might (mistakenly) feel like he is sitting with snobs.

Re: Itm snobs

The issue is often painted as one of ability but is actually one of self-awareness. I am quite grateful (as I’m sure we all are) for the people who suffered through the sets I led when I was just starting out, and helped guide me to be a better player. I’m grateful to the people who gave me pointers when I was just another guy bringing his guitar along to a session, before I learned a melody instrument, and who helped me better feel and hear the music. And so I’ll help anyone who comes along and wants to learn more, and who wants to improve. But one also has to be aware of one’s abilities, and be able to sit back and listen when the music being played is over one’s head.

My view is that it’s up to the regulars and established players to be patient with newcomers, and it’s up to the newcomers to be respectful of the established players. If a guy is bashing a guitar or scraping a fiddle, it’s better to offer constructive criticism than to be gruff. But on the other end, it’s infinitely better to take that advice to heart than to continue doing something in the wrong vein. The only people who annoy me are the people who are quite obviously set in their ways, and either unwilling or unable to play nicely with others.

Re: Itm snobs

“So what makes a bodhran any less welcome or useful at a session?”

Like Guernsey said, it’s the player, or wannabe player. There are several types:

1) The TV watcher -- plays half-heartedly while keeping an eye on the nearest TV -- hey, here in America it’s hard to find a pub without at least a dozen. TVs.

2) The rhythmically challenged -- a person who learned enough to get started, but didn’t go any further. Plays loud, hard, and off tempo. And frequently *ahead* of the beat.

3) The repeater -- plays the two basic figures, one for reels and one for jigs, on everything.

4) The hot dog -- someone who knows their way around a drum and wants to make sure everybody gets to hear every lick and chop he or she possesses. Plays loudly, with a driving energy that keeps everyone on edge.

5) The grifter -- a newcomer who shows up early and places a drum on one of the chairs arranged for the players, then goes to a table and orders dinner. Since dinner is on the house for the core players (this is a true story) this person and his family run up a major tab with food and drinks, with the player sitting down and playing every once and a while. In fact - this player doesn’t get out the door without paying, and doesn’t come back.

Re: Itm snobs

We could set aside the word “snob” for a minute- it’s intended as an insult- and accept the fact that many people most enjoy playing music at their own level of accomplishment, and most enjoy playing with people at their level. Sometimes it’s even more enjoyable to play with people a bit above one’s own level.

And many people don’t find it enjoyable to play music at a level below one’s own. Possibly the further below, the less enjoyment.

I don’t think such people think of themselves as “snobs” but just as people who want to enjoy the act of musicmaking.

More rare (it seems to me) are the wonderful people who are great players but don’t seem to care about this sort of thing, who truly enjoy making music with anyone and everyone.

In nearly 40 years of doing this music I’ve only run into one person, as best as I can recall, who deserved the title “snob”. This guy was insufferable. It’s hard to put his attitude into words. He regarded the tunes he played as an exclusive domain, and expressed surprise when a player he deemed beneath himself knew any of them. On the other hand, if anyone played a tune he didn’t know he would literally sniff at it… you see, if he didn’t know a tune it was because the tune was beneath his dignity, beneath his rarified position. (Of course a person like this wouldn’t just admit that he’d never heard it… I know, I threw a couple tunes out that he couldn’t possibly have heard before, and got the same attitude.)

Re: Itm snobs

The essence of tradition is in the music’s connection back to its simple sources in a particular land, a particular language and a particular way of life. The most important thing is to listen to the rhythms and the melodies before joining them. If drummers come in with the wrong rhythms and guitarists with inappropriate chords and melody instruments with weird, confusing harmonies then they aren’t listening and they aren’t playing in the tradition. Unless they are playing in a messing-about session they are in the wrong place. A player can be a total novice or a graduate of the Royal College of Music. They can come from any point on the globe. It doesn’t matter as long as they can immerse themselves in the tradition in which they find themselves before they play a note.
