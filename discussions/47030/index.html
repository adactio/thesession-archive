Re: My Progress with the WARBL

Firstly, to Brian: I listened to the recording, and it’s worth noting that Austin isn’t actually “fooling around” with the tune, i.e., changing it on a whim. Clearly he knows it. That whole thing was an argument over his choice of words. Which matters, of course, and it’s not a wording I would have used, but he certainly doesn’t treat the Mountain Road like a toy.

Now on to Austin’s question. I have no prior experience with the WARBL and only listening experience with the pipes. That said, my main point in feedback is that all-important one in Irish music: rhythm, rhythm, rhythm. While it might be natural to slow down and speed up on difficult and easy passages, respectively, I think there’s something to be said for picking a slow speed to practice with and then getting the rhythm down pat. I don’t know the first tune well, but certainly for the mountain Road. I get you were just playing it loosely for fun, but I personally find it more fun to play it at a consistent tempo under the fingers. But perhaps this is an issue with WARBL - I haven’t listened much to your whistle playing, so I’ll give you the benefit of the doubt there. I like your roll on the F in the Mountain Road as well. For the first tune, I couldn’t really discern the rhythm, or rather what it was supposed to be. Focus on the phrasing, and if that means slowing down, so be it.

Now for my rant. I admit that I read Brian’s comment before listening to the recording, and it made me think about something I’ve come to realize more and more recently: overconfidence kills. Austin, that goes for defensive attitude too, but more on that later. I’m in a somewhat similar situation to you, about your age, playing about as long. And I’ve found that I need to keep in mind that this isn’t just music, to a lot of people. It’s tradition. Yes, a tradition made for dancing and merriment. But tradition nonetheless. People will take that aspect seriously, and they may well take your honest effort to contribute something new, or to show your playing unbidden, as bluster or mild arrogance, and you may be implicitly or explicitly criticized for that. This has happened to me too. I make a point here on The Session of transcribing missing tunes that I feel should be here, or else little-known tunes that I like and I feel could go well in a session. And I’ve been told many times, sometimes gently and sometimes with exasperation, that some of what I do is lackluster, misplaced or unnecessary, though not in so many words. And yes, sometimes I resent that. But that’s what comes of a serious interest in traditional music. People know more than you, and if they’re having a good day or you’re really new, they’ll be more civil about it, and if they’re having a bad day or you’re overstepping, they may be less patient. I think Jeremy’s rule about being civil is enormously helpful, and I can’t think of any time when people have actually been uncivil to me, but people do lose their patience with us younguns sometimes, and that’s okay. I’ve made plenty of blunders here, even though I’ve tried to be careful about what I do. I always appreciate a kind pointer in the right direction, but as much as it can bite, I try to take the constructive criticism to heart too.

I believe I’ve come to feel this way especially because of the most recent session I went to and how I played there. The few sessions I had been to previously were among the best experiences in recent memory for me, and I can’t imagine how they could have gone better. Playing in those sessions greatly increased my confidence - I’d been very shy about people even hearing me play before, and to some extent I still am, but those sessions really convinced me that I was good enough to go and play. In reality I’m probably about half-decent, if that.

Anyway, at the most recent session I went to, I led two sets. The first was a set of classic Sligo–New York reels, the Reel of Bogie/McGreevy’s Favourite/Kiss Me Kate. All great tunes, but I rushed them a bit, and my rhythm was off - bouncy single-bowing and a bit jerky, like a poor imitation of Donegal style rather than Sligo. I guess that’s what comes of my obsession with Donegal fiddle. But I’d call it passable.

The second set of reels I played was an unnamed Larry Redican tune, Finbarr Dwyer’s, and the Donegal Tinker. For the record, I think it’s actually a wicked set, but my execution just sucked. Firstly, I expected people to join in on the second or third tune, but they didn’t, so I was left with a solo set, which isn’t good form. But second, I was rushing like crazy, and all the problems of the first set just became worse. It sounded like a lame attempt at a showpiece rather than a set of dance tunes. And my intonation, which I struggle with at the best of times, was pretty awful.

At the end of it all, they said they liked my playing, but that I should take it down a notch and not rush so much. I immediately saw that they were right, and probably understating things. I’ve recently come to see my rhythm as really pretty bad - I’ve probably developed a lot of bad habits I’ll have to unlearn. All of this is just to say that, while I wouldn’t dream of considering myself knowledgeable about anything here, as I’ve only been at it a few years, I have become overconfident in what I do know and it’s come back to bite me. And the worst possible thing for me to do, if I want to respect these people and their tradition, is to become defensive about their criticisms of my attitude, actions, and playing. Now, I have been having discussions with Alan Ng of Irishtune.info, just through email correspondence, about certain of his tune identifications that I find inconsistent, incorrect, etc. I feel like we have a good relationship, and over lots of these I have come to see how different people interpret the tradition and have learned a lot more about it generally. I’m not always right, and we have respectfully disagreed on many of my arguments, but we have agreed on many, too. All this to say that I’ll continue to try my best, but I also need to realize when I’ve gone too far and not get defensive.

Austin, I don’t think people hate you, at all. I think they may see you as a bit pushy, or not very humble, like some of them probably see me. But that’s okay, because I can be that way. It doesn’t mean I’m worthless or won’t amount to anything, just that I’m still a novice and not really a good player, and I need to understand that fact and interact with others accordingly. So, just try and let your guard down a little, and if you really need to let off steam, playing a tune often helps.

Re: My Progress with the WARBL

Go to page 14 of the manual, you will see advanced register settings , the bag defaults are probably where you are now, so experiment with the first 2 settings. If you’d set it to be harder to hit and keep the second octave you will mimic a hard Reed, if you set it to as easier to hit the 2nd octave you mimic a soft Reed,
With a hard Reed you will build stamina and muscle strength with a soft Reed you will learn to be very sensitive to arm pressure.
At a guess the Reed will come to you in your chanter hard . It’s not an easy job fettling reeds but it’s a good skill to have for a piper!
Lessons….. a lot can be self taught, but that requires a lot of practice and hunting, better get a teacher even if it’s occasional, in the long run it’s quicker. Good luck.

Waves hand at AB! Here’s one person’s response. I’m not a piper, I followed the progression from guitar to mandolin to “Irish” flute in this music.

I bought a WARBL just for kicks, because my S.O. is a fiddler and she likes the sound of the pipes, so it’s a way to play some tunes together with that sound without a 20 year commitment to a different instrument. I play the WARBL in whistle mode for fingering and octave shift with breath pressure, not the bag. I tried the bag supplied with the gizmo, but unless you’re going to dedicate yourself to that mode as a quasi-pipes substitute, I think the whistle mode is far easier.

Since I’m not a piper, I can’t say if this is a good learning substitute compared to just getting a practice chanter. As just a fun thing if you can play a whistle, it’s great. Just don’t expect it to be a full-on synth substitute because it’s still a diatonic instrument. If you want to get into synths, a keyboard or some other polyphonic instrument is better (IMO). Also, realize that you’ll need a synth playback device. I use a last-gen iPad Mini with the Celtic Sounds app because it still has an analog audio port. The newer ones with Bluetooth have too much latency.

I will only ever play the WARBL at home and not at sessions, but I do enjoy it as a break from the flute and mandolin. I’m actually playing the WARBLE less recently, because I recently acquired a 6-keyed low Bb flute and that’s another hill to climb. Only so many hours in the day…

I keep inserting a term & it does not seem to be what the replies are focusing on. I have no doubt the device is multi-dimensional; capable of emulating a number of acoustic instrumental modes. The term is *practical*.

By practical I mean, “what was the original intention of WARBL?” I think any device is more practical to certain modes based on it’s fundamental physical design and software configurations and less practical to other modes. The WARBL is such a device & methinks the practical use of such a device falls on a spectrum ranging from spot on practical use (which may be uilleann pipe mode/or not) to less practical (which may be tin whistle mode/or not).

Sorry, but if we simply agree that this device can do everything equally well it’s a done *deal* and everyone should buy it. I just hope the WARBL gets a fair shake and anyone thinking about one mode (vs multi-modes) has a reasonable understanding of how practical the device is in individual modes (as opposed to WARBL’s multi-functional design).

KISS ~ Keep It Simple Stupid

Re: My Progress with the WARBL

I use mine 95% in standard 6-hole whistle mode for the purpose that has always been at the top on its web page “WARBL is a plug-and-play USB MIDI wind controller that allows the traditional musician to practice with headphones or …”.

For silent practice of tunes to be played on flute or whistle it is 100% practical (but see note **)

I have changed the settings from the defaults (which I think were suggested by Michael Eskin) very slightly to suite a (taped over) Generation Bb head that vents a little more than that originally supplied.

The other 5% is as a chromatic 7-hole whistle to cross-finger accidentals in tune and also to try out 9-note bagpipe fingerings.

** It’s not the same as a whistle or flute because it uses next to no air so the breathing is different. But leaking air through the nose or round the mouthpiece if required soon became a habit. I find myself using more finger articulations and less air flow articulations than on whistle or flute. It is much less forgiving of sloppy fingering than flute or whistle - but I regard that as an aid to practice.
