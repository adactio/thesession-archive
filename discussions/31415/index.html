Low D Whistle tuning problems

Simple question, long story.
Simple question first: Is there such a thing as a Low D Whistle that is ‘in tune’?
Now the story:
I’ve played the flute (classical keyed), penny whistle and recorders most of my life on and off and haven’t really suffered from tuning problems. I bought a Dixon Low D whistle about a year ago as I love the unique, breathy sound you get from Low whistles. I know that Low Ds do require some practice to get the hang of but I’m finding it difficult to sound in tune with other instruments in the small group I play with.
I got around to checking the tuning against a tuner and discovered that the C# is distinctly flat, by almost a quarter tone. I sent it back to Tony Dixon and he has tweaked it and there is a very slight improvement, but the only way I can get a true C# is blow as hard as I can, to the point of distortion. All the other notes are about right (some slighlty sharp and others slightly flat) and don’t require much of change of breath - apart from the upper octave, of course.)
I went to a music shop and tied a couple of other makes (the most expensive being a Chieftain), but they all varied hugely in their tuning. Some were a little flat on some notes but OK on others, some were a little sharp here and there.
Is there make of whistle (Goldie?) which can be played in tune without significant breath changes for each note - or is it a ‘characteristic’ of Low Whistles that they are all out of tune?

Re: Low D Whistle tuning problems

I’m a big “Low D guy” because I play Low D whistle as my primary Irish instrument. I’ve played most of the major brands of Low D out there. Because I play both in trad Irish situations and “legit” situations I’m acutely aware of the tuning issues and, like you, the quest is for the most in-tune Low D possible.

First, though, as a general thing in Irish wind instruments the C/C# thing is tricky because both notes share the same tonehole. In my opinion having the intonation right for the crossfingered C natural is far more important that having the intonation for the open C# right. In my opinion it’s a mistake for makers to make that hole really big so as to make C# in tune, because it ruins the crossfingered C natural.

On the vast majority of Irish flutes and whistles, having the crossfingered C natural in tune using oxx ooo or oxx oox means that the open C# is a hair flat. I’m very used to giving C# a little push to get it up to pitch. But as you say it becomes a problem if the open C# is too flat.

OK I just pulled out a Low D by MK, a Low Eb by Burke, and a Low E by Bernard Overton, and on all three the crossfingered C natural is in tune with its surrounding notes (B and Middle D) but the open C#, if given the same force of breath, is over 20 cents flat, but can easily be brought up by blowing.

That’s exactly how I expect whistles to work. It would be easy to carve out that top fingerhole to get the open C# to play in tune with normal pressure, but that would 1) spoil C natural and 2) possibly spoil the vented Middle D.

By the way, not only does one encounter different tunings for the scale across various makes, but even more so does one encounter different approaches to the tuning of the octaves. My two Tony Dixon Low Ds and all of the various Overtons I’ve had have the 2nd octave tuned flat so that you have to underblow the 1st octave and strongly blow the 2nd.

MKs are the reverse with a sharpish 2nd register.

Burkes and Susatos are in the middle.

For my way of blowing the most perfectly tuned Low D I’ve tried is the Reyburn.

Re: Low D Whistle tuning problems

First off, a couple people have used the term “even temperament” and as far as I know there is no such beast.

There’s a huge number of ways of tuning a scale but at least with Irish instruments it comes down to Just Intonation, or Equal Temperament, or oftentimes some combination or compromise between the two.

Just Intonation is how Highland bagpipes and uilleann pipes are usually tuned. In Just Intonation each note of the scale is adjusted to create a completely beatless consonance with the drone. Just Intonation is very common in folk music and is probably quite ancient. As I understand it the ancient Greeks wrote about it.

But in the Baroque Era composers started wanting to modulate from key to key, and Just Intonation is perfectly in tune for one key only, and out of tune for any other key! So they came up with Equal Temperament, arbitrarily dividing the octave into 12 mathematically equal segments. The good news is now every key sounds the same; the bad news is, as an 18th century critic put it, “every key is equally out of tune”.

The trouble for somebody playing whistle or flute is that there are situations where Equal Temperament is “in tune” (remembering that being “in tune” is always relative to the context) such as when playing along with a guitarist or other fretted-instrument player, or a keyboard player. And there are situations where Just Intonation is “in tune” such as when playing along with an uilleann piper (assuming that he has his chanter tuned “Just” as pipers usually do).

Since I do “legit” gigs, my uilleann chanters and my whistles are all precisely tuned to Equal Temperament. Yes I’m off a bit when playing along with trad players tuned “Just”.

The difference between Equal Temperament and Just Intonation, for the scale of D, is this:
D 0
E +4
F# -14
G -2
A +2
B -16
C +18 (the 9:5 “5-Limit Just minor 7th” though Highland pipers use -31 7:4 “Harmonic Minor 7th”)
C# -12

The + or - is the deviation in cents from Equal Temperament.

Re: Low D Whistle tuning problems

I don’t know exactly how each of the various whistle makers tune their instruments; but mostly there’s a bit of a compromise somewhere. If your whistle is tuned in even temperament, i.e. every note in tune with a piano, it will be out of tune with someone playing the pipes. If your whistle is tuned like the pipes, ie every note tuned to harmonize with the drone (D) it will sound out of tune with a classical flute (by these amounts in cents)
Back D - 0.00
C sharp - minus 11.73
C Nat - minus 3.91
B - minus 15.64
A - plus 1.96
G - minus 1.96
F sharp - minus 13.69
E - plus 3.91
Bottom D - 0.00

If you want to tune to concert pitch, ie to A -- move all the notes down 1.96 so that A=0. and you will see that both your Cs are getting to be noticeably flatter. The B is flatter still, which is why there seems such a gap between your B and your C.
Incidentally I noticed a while ago that nearly all the whistles I have have a bit of tape over the B hole, which suggests that they are usually a bit sharp for my liking. The point is, I suppose, that there is no such thing as a true scale. It depends on who you are playing with, if there is a drone, if everyone else is in tune, how many of each instrument are playing, etc.
Bear in mind, also, that once you get something like this into your head, it starts to sound even worse. Record yourself playing with a drone, or a bit of reverb, some slow tunes and some faster, and listen to the whole thing, not just for that one duff note. Does the overall sound seem OK, that’s the main thing. Experiment with bits of tape over the holes a little, just to get a general idea of what’s going on. But as I said before, the you are unlikely to improve on what Tony Dixon has done.
And throw your tuner away :)

Re: Low D Whistle tuning problems

About why the crossfingered C natural on uilleann pipes, flute, and whistle is more important than the open C# the easy answer would be to say “it just is!”

For example, the uilleann pipe chanter in nominally in D… but the C on the regulators is a C natural, not a C sharp.

In traditional Irish dance music and airs, at least in the tunes I’ve played over the last 35 years, C natural not only is far more commonly heard but is often an important note, a note often dwelled upon. C sharp doesn’t occur as often and when it does it’s often a passing tone. In short C natural is usually a stressed note, C sharp usually an unstressed note.

A friend who is a classical composer and orchestral arranger once pointed out to me that on a particular solo uilleann pipe CD which I lent him, EVERY tune on the album was in the key of G!

And there is a vast number of uilleann pipe tunes which are nominally in D but all, or most, of the C’s which occur in the tune are natural.

The only air I play that has a C sharp in a stressed position is My Laggan Love. Luckily my uilleann chanter only requires a slight boost in pressure to get the C# in tune.

Re: Low D Whistle tuning problems

“I can understand how Csharp or C might be slightly out of tune with the rest of the whistle but not how a whole octave e.g. the second octave can be said to play sharp or flat”

In order to get the second octave, you have to blow harder. The variation between different types of whistles comes with the degree of difference in pressure change. Some people like to have to blow quite hard in the lower octave to stop it jumping too readily, while others like to be able to get the second octave more easily. Of course, when you blow harder, the pitch goes up, so the maker compensates for it in the design. The internal structure of the whistle also has an effect on the pitch as well as the quality of the sound. The C hole presents particular problems due to the nature of the fingering, but in the main the flatness or otherwise of the notes is well within what is humanly acceptable. There could be a particular problem if the whistle is new -- not because it sounds wrong, but because you are not used to it, and are expecting a different pitch. You quite quickly get used to a new instrument, so I would persevere for a few weeks until the novelty wears off,

Re: Low D Whistle tuning problems

Well I don’t know how my post got posted three times… honestly I only clicked “post” once! The computer did wait a long time to post… now I know why.

As Gam says, different makes of Low Ds have different approaches to the tuning of the octaves. No one approach is more “correct” than any other, and a good player can play the octaves in tune on any of them.

It’s just the relationship of blowing pressure required to play the two octaves in tune to each other.

I’ve owned, now, five different MK Low Ds and even within this one make there’s some variance from whistle to whistle. In general though MKs have a relatively sharp 2nd octave so that to play the octaves in tune you must blow the 1st octave very strongly, nearly to the point of the notes breaking up to the next octave, and you must underblow the 2nd octave somewhat, nearly to the point of the notes falling back to the 1st octave. In other words, there is nearly the minimum possible differential of pressure between the two octaves, which I really like. The two octaves play at nearly the same blowing pressure, in other words. What’s nice about this approach is that is evens out the natural volume differential that’s present in all Low D whistles (on which the 1st octave is softer than that of the flute while the 2nd octave is louder than that of the flute). The MK is great for session playing because you can really blast full-out on the low notes, blowing them to maximum volume, and be in tune.

All of the several Overtons I’ve owned (all Low Whistles C, D, Eb, E) have had a rather large gap between the octaves, meaning that to play the two octaves in tune you must blow the 1st octave rather more softly than it is capable of handling, and must blow the 2nd octave rather strongly, far stronger than it takes to simply maintain a note in the 2nd octave. You can blow 1st octave notes much more strongly, but they will be sharp, and you can blow 2nd octave notes far more softly, but they will be flat. Many players prefer this approach because there’s a large pressure differential between the octaves and it makes cleanly hitting notes in the octave you wish easier.

In my experience Burkes and Reyburns are in the middle, just about “right” in my opinion for normal playing.

Tony Dixon is a great guy, a pleasure to work with!

RANT WARNING

But I will say again that the way Irish whistles are supposed to work is for the crossfingered C natural to be spot-on, and the vented Middle D to play right, which makes the open C# around 20 cents flat, easily raised to pitch by “blowing it out”.

A whistle is ruined if that hole is made huge and high so that the open C# is correct to Equal Temperament.

It’s not just me saying this! I have Low Whistles from Michael Burke, Bernard Overton, and MK, three makers who know as much as there is to know about whistle design, and they all are made like that.

And even worse to make the top hole huge, and drill a C natural hole for the thumb, made necessary because the huge top hole has ruined the proper traditional crossfingered C natural. If you want to go that route, take up the Boehm flute or Renaissance Recorder. If you want to play Irish whistle, I urge you to accept the instrument the way it is, which is the way it needs to be to play ITM.

About the halfholed C natural, it’s fine for slow airs and such, but I don’t see the point, because in a properly made whistle the crossfingered C natural is precisely in tune, and the crossfingering with practice is no more difficult that any other note on the whistle; in fact there are many tunes and passages which are difficult or nearly impossible to play at full speed with accurate pitch if attempting to halfhole C natural, but which are quite easy and fluent if using the crossfingered C natural. It’s why most trad players of flute, whistle, and uilleann pipes have long used the crossfingered C natural: it’s easier, better, more precise, and has the traditional sound.

A test tune perhaps is Polly Put the Kettle On. I’d like to hear a video of somebody halfholing C naturals play that tune at full dancing/session speed with all the C naturals clean and precise. (Every other note, almost, in the first part is C natural.)

And playing rolls on C natural, which I do all the time, is one of the greatest benefits of the crossfingered note, and would be very clumsy to attempt with the halfholed C natural.

Re: Low D Whistle tuning problems

In short, I find it strange that someone coming from a background of Boehm flute and Recorder, who is new to whistle, is finding the whistle not to his liking and having a maker redesign it so as to comply with instruments from outside the Irish tradition. Thousands upon thousands, generations, of traditional Irish musicians have made great music on the whistle, and the way whistles are now made conforms to the wants and needs of today’s top traditional players. Learn something from them! There are good musical reasons that these players play the way they do, and want the sort of instrument they want. The top whistle makers strive to put the best instruments into the hands of the best players, and current Low Whistle design is the result of this back-and-forth between top players and top makers.

In my opinion you, as an admitted “newbie”, would be better off getting a high-quality professional Low D whistle by MK, Colin Goldie, Bernard Overton, Michael Burke, Renaldo Reyburn, or what have you, and let the instrument teach you how Low Whistles are supposed to play, rather than try to distort the instrument to match your preconception of how Irish wind instruments are supposed to work. Sorry if it sounds harsh, but I’ve been playing and teaching this music for 35 years and that’s my honest opinion.

On the surface it looks absolutely identical to the old one, not the complete redesign I was expecting, there’s just a slight difference in the top two holes, but the difference in playing is frankly quite astounding. Somehow Tony has managed to get the C# right without losing the C natural. Not only am I playing in tune now but the tone of the instrument seems better than my old one. The highest notes seem easier to reach and the slight buzzing that I was getting on the lowest D & E has gone and he has managed to achieve this without losing the character of the instrument. It still has a warm mellow tone with that hint of breathiness than make a Low D distinctive.

Success all round - maybe I won’t have to smash the piggy bank after all!
I’ll try to post a review on Youtube when I get the time.
