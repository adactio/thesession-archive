Music Information Retrieval

Hi Guys,

You might remember I am working on a PhD to do with modelling wooden flute styles. Well a side project that I have been working has turned out to be very successful in my experiments and I was just wondering what you think of the idea.

Basically, I have developed a Music Information Retrieval (MIR) system for trad tunes. How it works is, you give it a recording of a tune played on either the flute, fliddle or tin whistle and it:

1. Transcribes it to ABC, sometimes with transcription errors
2. “Flattens” the tune to remove certain stylistic elements such as ornaments
3. Compares it against a database of known tunes using an approximate similarity measure, so it is robust to variations, mistakes, breaths and transcription errors.

You can play any part of a tune and it will typically guess the tune correctly. Out of 50 test tunes recorded by 8 different flute and fiddle players in various different recording settings, it guessed 43 correctly and a further 5 were in the top 5 closest matches. It currently has a corpus of about 1300 tunes (reels, jigs and hornpipes) that it can recognise. Once it gueses a tune, it will give you the correctly transcribed ABC, which it can playback so you know its the correct tune.

I have a big server here and I was thinking of making an online version (so you could simply play a tune at a web page and it would give you back the name, tune type, ABC and MIDI) and making it available to the trad world.

I was at the music network gig recently and I was struck by the number of “gan anim”s played!

Firstly, do you know of anything similar? I know the Meldex system and similar, but this system uses ABC, trad instruments and is aimed at trad musicians

Secondly, Do you think this is a good idea?

Thirdly, if it was developed would you use it?

And finally, what sorts of features does the trad community require from a MIR.

All feedback welcomed!

Bryan

Thanks for the feedback.

It currently works very well with flute and whistle and mostly ok with fiddle. It doesnt work at all with pipes and accordion, but it could be made to work for any instrument. Probably even lilting! Its just that I play flute and have modelled the harmonics of the flute in the pitch detector and note onset detector.

Musipedia is a brilliant system and is considered to be a “gold standard” in MIR. It used to be called Meldex back when it was a research project. My system uses some of the same techniques, but is optimised to work especially well for trad tunes. Hence it has special routines to deal with ornaments in the playing and the transcription.

The ABC transcription is not too bad, but it has an “ornamentation eliminator” at the moment, so that it will match a tune whether you play it “simple” or with lots of ornamentation. One of the musicians whose playing it works very well with for example is Eamonn Cotter who uses lots of ornamentation, but the system works equally well with my mate Marie who has a lovely straight up style.

I’ve also looked at those others

Hmmmm.

I think you misunderstand the purpose of my program, or perhaps I misunderstand you. The aim of the program is to identify the names of tunes. In other words, say you have a collection of recordings you made at a workshop and you want to get the names of the tunes and possibly learn about the history of the tunes. You can provide them to the program and it compares them against tunes it knows using a measure of melodic similarity. Where it finds a close match it will annotate the recordings with whatever meta data the previous transcribers have provided.

My aim is to make the program robust to stylistic variations, so that a tune is a tune so to speak. For example, I could play:

~F3AFEEDBABD

And some one else could play:

FGFAFDEDBABD

In both cases, the program would identify the tune as Brendan’ Tonres’s, because they are similar, even though not identical:

https://thesession.org/tunes/451

Humans are very good at recognising similar but not identical patterns. Hence a human sees a face when presented with ;-) Machines on the other hand must be programmed to handle uncertainty. Transcription algorithms are never accurate, so a system such as this has to deal with the combination of stylistic differences plus transcription errors.

MacCruiskeen, in most of papers I’ve ever read that have talked about transmission the majority of the academics are very careful to distinguish between the aural and oral components of the transmission: i.e. the person learns the songs aurally, but the source singer passes on his/her songs orally.

This is particularly relevant with texts that deal with transmission into the 21st century, as there are examples of singers who were recorded in the late 19th century, passing on their song orally to a wax cylinder (there were a couple of maniacs who found it fun to gallivant around the countryside with the gear needed to record onto wax). These recordings were often then transcribed in terms of text, and often music was added at a later date. Due to the advances in technology, many of these recordings are reappearing in places like the Vaughan Williams library etc and being put on CDs / internets. Thus, some people are learning from the paper which, as it was transcribed directly from the singer, may be regarded as oral transmission. However singer who learns from the dots cannot claim to have learnt it aurally, unlike the singer who learnt from the recordings; spawning a bizarre situation where revivalist period singers could learn from a form of (arguably) oral transmission, but the generation later sourced their songs aurally. After this point was brought up (I think it was by Harker in one of his prefaces, but I’m not too sure) failing to ensure your essay distinguished between the two became a bit of an editorial faux pas.
