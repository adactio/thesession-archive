Re: Fiddle Case

I don’t think the material that the bow stick is made of has any real bearing on the tone production. But the weight and spring certainly do, and finding a stick that suits your playing is vital. Carbon bows are made to very tight tolerances - all the bows of a particular model are identical. Coda make (I think) three models. So if you try a whole box of Coda bows, you are really just trying three different bows. With wooden bows there are infinite variations, so the chances of finding one that suits are much greater.

But for me, the real difference between the two is the difference between a porcelain tea cup and a plastic mug. I like porcelain cups and wooden bows. Given the choice, I prefer an object that is both functional and beautiful to one that is purely functional.

Re: Fiddle Case

>>“In other words, using your criteria, an aluminium bow would sound the same as a wood bow? Yes, you missed ”bow resonance."

If you could make an aluminium bow with the same mass distribution and spring as wooden one, it would play exactly the same, yes. But whether that is achievable I don’t know - certainly its dimensions would be different to the wooden bow, and it may well not be possible to achieve due to the different mass/stiffness ratios of the two materials.

But the situation is different with CF - if you design a CF bow with the same stiffness (spring) as a wooden one it will be significantly lighter. So you can then add mass as required to achieve the weight, balance and resonance you require. Again if you add that weight so that the measurable physical properties of the CF bow match those of a wooden one, the two will play the same.

I haven’t missed bow resonance - resonance is a function of mass distribution and elasticity, and if you fix those two properties, then you fix the combined property of resonance too, so there is no need to consider resonance separately.

Re: Fiddle Case

>>“I just don’t think that’s right. If you believe that then you should be able to make a carbon fibre fiddle that sounds like a wooden one.”

The theoretical answer to that is yes, you could make a CF fiddle that sounded like a wooden one IF you could get the mass distribution and elasticity the same.

But in the real world a violin is a very much more complex system than a bow stick. The soundboard is stiffer along the grain than across it, it is made of a series of hard and soft grain lines that differ in both density and elasticity. If you could replicate all that, rather than using a uniform sheet of CF then you probably could make a CF fiddle that was indistinguishable from a wooden one. But in the real world that is not possible.

But how about a violin with just a CF neck and fingerboard? The neck and fingerboard is a system very similar to a bow stick, except it is stressed by the strings instead of bow hair. Do you think it would be possible to make a fiddle with a CF neck that was indistinguishable from a wooden one? I don’t see that as inconceivable at all.

Re: Fiddle Case

>>“All wood is stiffer along the grain than across it. Pernambuco too. It is made of a series of hard and soft grain lines that differ in both density and elasticity. If you could replicate all that, rather than using a uniform sheet of CF then you probably could make a CF bow that sounded like a wood one.”

There are a number of other factors involved that make the difference due to grain direction inconsequential though:

Firstly, the offset of the tip and frog mean that the forces on the stick due to tension and vibration are all in the vertical plane. (I know, there is a small sideways force if you play on the edge of the bow, but I’m happy to ignore that for reasons that are shown below)

Secondly The camber means that when linear compression is applied, the stick bends far more readily in the vertical than the horizontal plane, so minor differences in stiffness in the two planes due to grain direction are of little or no consequence. And if you were concerned, they could easily be modelled in the CF bow by just using a slightly different camber.

Thirdly, the bow stick is an end loaded system. The only thing that matters is the relationship between the tip and frog, where it interfaces with the hair. It doesn’t actually matter whether vibrations pass between the two in the vertical or horizontal plane, in the same way that it doesn’t affect the sound of an instrument if you pluck the string vertically or horizontally.
