Too many songs!

I might be looking for a bit of commiseration here.

One of the local sessions I attend fairly regularly has, for some reason, become the local hangout for aspiring singers who think they can sing Irish songs (sometimes they can, sometimes they can’t, and it’s often things like “Whiskey in the Jar,” not sean nos airs in Irish). I like the occasional song at a session, if the singer knows what they’re doing. After all, song is a part of the tradition. It provides a welcome break from an hour or two of reels, jigs, hornpipes, etc. Then you go back to the tunes for another hour or two. But the issue at this session is that some of the singers take any pause in tunes to belt out a song. You then get tunes-song-tunes-song-song-tunes. The only way to prevent this from happening is to keep the tunes flying and as soon as someone finishes a set, someone else better have one ready to go.

I think our singers don’t realize that a session really isn’t about individual performance. It’s not an open-mike night.

Brilliant metaphor!

It’s very classy that many of our aspiring balladeers arrive armed with pages of lyrics. Literally pages. Along with the pint glasses, whistles, flutes, etc. on the table, there are stacks of eight-by-eleven sheets of paper with song lyrics.

Granted, I can’t memorize lyrics to save my life, but that’s why I don’t start songs in sessions! I doubt if Joe Heaney ever performed staring at a sheet of paper any more than Seamus Ennis did.

One of my theories is that people want to feel like they belong in the “in” group at the session (like they do in any other social setting). They haven’t a clue that a session is mostly about tunes and playing them together, and that while ten verse ballads are Irish, it’s a different sort of tradition. And they probably don’t spend hours perusing thesession.org learning more than they ever wanted to know about session etiquette. So they circle the session like vultures, waiting to perform and be a part of the musical activity.

We’ve had some attempts to do some of the more passive-aggressive things suggested above. Like Nico and I bouncing sets off each other for a while. Two weeks ago, I think one of the balladeers was trying to start a song, but a fiddle player and I started some tunes over him. But it’s not consistent.

Re: Too many songs!

John J has asked me to clarify - so here goes.

Most orchestral music is far too complex to expect a large group of musicians, even at professional level, to play from memory. It isn’t just the notes, it’s all the myriad of little details and changes that a good conductor puts into the rehearsals - for instance, slight changes of tempo or dynamics, phrasing, the balance between the instrument sections, bowing (which affects the tone and attack), whether or not to use vibrato in a particular section, even the occasional cut - none of which appear in the composer’s score. It’s not possible to expect the members of a symphony orchestra to remember all those details, especially when rehearsal time is often limited, so that is why soft lead pencils are very much in evidence during rehearsals.

It’s often not realised that the performer is actually the conductor (or conductor + soloist in a concerto), and the orchestra is his instrument of choice - it’s no accident that many eminent conductors have previously been instrumental soloists. This is one reason why a good conductor can lift a decent amateur orchestra to professional heights, whereas an indifferent stick waver on the podium might result in a professional orchestra going its own way on auto pilot (which an amateur orchestra wouldn’t be able to do - you’d just get a horrible crash).

Of course, many experienced orchestral musicians often have great tracts of the repertoire off by heart, just by mere repetition over the years, but they still need the details of the individual conductor’s interpretation in front of them for a performance. A classical soloist, though, has to put so much time and work into studying a complex and difficult work to paid performance level that it is next to impossible for him not to memorise it completely. The same applies to actors, singers and dancers on stage, and of course to folk and jazz musicians everywhere.

However, you sometimes come across a soloist who may like have the music in front of him. He may perhaps be an older performer who quite rightly wants to avoid the embarrassment of a “senior moment”, or it may be someone who has been asked to stand in at short notice in a performance of an unfamiliar work.

Sometimes you find a professional group of classical players who successfully give concerts entirely from memory. Such groups rarely have much more than a dozen players because eye contact between them is so important. I’ve had the pleasure of seeing the Emerald Players (a group of about 12 string players in the UK) give a 2-hour concert from memory. Not only that, but everyone, except for the two cellists, played standing up in a semicircle facing the audience. I’m sure playing standing up, for fiddles, gives better tone and general all-round performance.
