Re: why did alot/most of the old players play quite out of tune?

Are you referring to old players or old recordings? If you’re wondering why your auntie doesn’t sound as sharp as she used to, the sober truth Arthur is that we are all mortal and if we live long enough, our skills will decline.

If you’re wondering why Kipeen Scanlon or Johnny Doherty sometimes sound out of tune, though: in a addition to the deliberate microtonal intonation and just temperament mentioned above, there are some other reasons why players on old recordings might sound out of tune.
• Some old recordings are of players recorded in their later years when their technical skills were in decline
• Perfect intonation was simply not a primary concern compared to creating lift and lilt for dancing. It just wasn’t considered a requirement for producing exciting music so it was not focused on.
• Some recordings are marred by uneven recording speeds on antique technology, battery-run or even hand-cranked if you listen to very old wax cylinders.
• Some of the great players of the past, who kept the tradition alive and passed it on, did not have the leisure time that we have today to maintain the level expected from modern professional studio recordings
• Some probably recorded drunk, often because…
• Very old recordings were done at a time when recording technology was new, and foreign to the player’s experience. Red-light nerves. A recording may not have captured them at their best. There were no second takes or overdubs to patch imperfect passages.

Edit: Dan just answered most of what I was trying to say, much more succinctly.

Re: why did alot/most of the old players play quite out of tune?

Intonation is not a trivial issue, and as others have mentioned depends a great deal on what someone is accustomed to hearing. In this day and age we are surrounded my music in perfect equal temperament due to auto-tune and digital electronic tuners. Musicians working before these technological advances would have been doing things by ear, and as such may have found tones played ‘non equal temperament’ more ascetically pleasing.

Regarding wind instruments being out of tune, I believe this relates again to technology. In the past for an instrument maker to perfectly tune an instrument, there was no way around having an exceptional sense for pitch. I don’t believe it would be practical for every single instrument maker to have or develop such a skill.

Nowadays this isn’t required, as anyone can tune an instrument with an electronic tuner to perfect equal temperament. Though saying this, there isn’t any shortage of *new* badly tuned wind instruments on the market.

Re: why did alot/most of the old players play quite out of tune?

“Then again the fiddle hasn’t exactly gone through ground breaking evolution”

No, but fiddle strings certainly have evolved, and far beyond what was being used on older recordings.

There is now a huge variety to choose from, including online sources so you’re not restricted to what’s available locally. The general standard of living has been raised as well, so fiddlers can afford to change strings more often. A modern fiddler is probably less likely to leave old, worn-out strings on a fiddle, which always harms intonation.

A similar thing can be heard with recordings of acoustic guitar players in different genres of music from the 1920’s. 30’s, and 40’s. It’s not just the microphones and recording tech. People used to leave guitar strings on their instruments until they snapped from age, far past the point where the intonation was way off.

Re: why did alot/most of the old players play quite out of tune?

This has received a lot of comments. The wooden flutes of the 16th -17th Centuries were tuned to the “Just Scale” or “Ture Scale” and not the “Tempered Scale”. Ireland obtain many of these flutes when the Boehm (tempered scale) system flutes came into style in the mid 1850’s leaving hundreds of wooded “Just Scale” flutes to decay or to be purchased cheap by the poor of the day – this fits Irish history. These Boehm style flutes could be played along with the tempered scale of the day i.e. piano. The intonation difference is hard for those who cannot get away from the “Tempered Scale“ that is so common today. Oh for the good auld days! The ”Just Scale“ is a natural scale to the ear for vocals and fiddlers. Unless a piano is tuned to a specific ”Just Scale“ a ”Tempered Scale" instrument should, in my opinion, not be used for Celtic/Irish traditional music. Throw away the tuner!

Re: why did alot/most of the old players play quite out of tune?

« Yes, Equal Temperament tuning is by definition out of tune, yet that allows all 12 tones of an octave to “blend” with each other, hence the Circle of Fifths. Just Intonation tuning does not have a Circle of Fifths. »

Non-ET systems certainly have do a circle of fifths - it’s just that when you go full circle, the note you end on will not be perfectly in tune with the one you started with.

The gap is known as the “comma” and was identified by Pythagoras. Basically you cannot have both perfect fifths and perfect octaves. God’s mistake!

So what ET does is keep the perfect octave but fudge the fifth (and every other interval). The circle of fifths now works. Whether the notes “blend” with each other as you say is another matter. (Major third on the piano a nice harmonious sound? Not.) However ET does dispense with the need to retune the piano or harpsichord or whatever when you want to play in a key remote from the one you have just been playing in. (ET is not the only system to achieve this - the “well-tempered” clavier that Bach wrote his suite of 48 tunes for was apparently a wee bit different.)

I am learning to play some tunes from YouTube renditions by Martin Byrnes. I love his playing. He is a force of nature. He is sparing of ornaments but gets passion and variation into the tunes through gradations of notes - sharps, naturals - and little unexpected changes of notes in a phrase so that the whole effect is sort of barbaric, primeval, and *almost out of tune*.
