Re: Expensive High D whistles

So Will Evans, you’d suggest my goal of saving up for calliopes in alto and tenor may not be so taxi-friendly?

As for intermediate whistles providing a range of tone qualities, playability and price variance, the range of whistles that have satisfied so many whistle players is solid there, and going higher in price should result in some aspect of the whistle having a premium quality. Tonal quality will vary, but basics like intonation, a full range of notes available without note breakdown, good responsiveness, would seem to be an automatic if you’re spending cash on “premium”.

Durability doesn’t seem so important with whistles as for bamboo flutes/whistles, but I’d be a little worried about paying premium prices for a whistle made of some of the wood types that saliva can break down fairly quickly. First the mouthpiece starts breaking down, then the blade. I’ve played brass, aluminum, rosewood and maple, and rosewood is off my list now, I consider it too vulnerable for whistle mouthpieces. A rosewood body is fine, such as with a Delrin mouthpiece. Lots of that type available. I’ve had no problems with maple.

Tone and playability are a curious thing. I recorded a tune using a Walton’s Guinness whistle, which is the same thing as their Little Black whistle, with a thin aluminum body. The tone was a basic one, but it played clearly. The notes in the higher half of the second octave started sounding more chiffy/windy and breaking down a little, but for the fast notes I was playing and the little time I spent up in that range, that sounded good as riffy expressiveness instead of instrument failure. Those whistles are $el cheapo, so I was happy.

Another thing is wind demand, which could be phrased as how much capacity does the mouthpiece design have to accommodate a range of wind power while having a MINIMUM of clogging. On any particular whistle design, I’d prefer a mouthpiece design that avoids clogging and allows me to use my lungs. A basic way to do that is to focus on the alto range of whistles, such as Bb, A, G, F, where the bore is bigger, the windway and blade bigger. With lower keys that have a larger whistle bore, you can find magnificently playing whistles that are considered “narrow bore”, yet still have windways bigger than D whistles, for less clogging.

If you want to go into the highest range of whistles such as high F and high G, consider using a sopranino recorder instead; the higher pitch is clean, easy, clear, with the added bonus that the instrument is fully chromatic. The sopranino sounds appealing, like a blend of a recorder and a whistle, because the pitch is so high. Try it. Wild birds like it, I’ve played it for them dozens of times. The Yamaha is very reliable, responsive and very inexpensive.

Re: Expensive High D whistles

Seems to me that with Low D Whistles you get what you pay for.

The ones I regard as serious professional instruments are around $300-400.

High D Whistles are completely different.

The background is that when I started playing, in the mid-1970s, everybody played Generation D’s because that’s pretty much what was available. There was no such thing as “an expensive High D Whistle”, the very best whistles on the planet, played by the professionals/top trad players, cost $6.

Which is not to say that all Generation D’s were equal! Oh no, you started out with whatever one you grabbed, or had a good player pick one out for you. In any case you then embarked on a lifelong quest to find a better one, then a better one yet, and so on. They could vary from unplayable to supremely good, in the same batch.

I still believe that no whistle ever made at any price outplays a really, really good vintage Generation.

Generation changed their moulds in 1982 and happily I had acquired my excellent Generations (and an excellent Feadog) by around 1980. The old ones still show up fairly often.

The closest new whistles I’ve played have been by Jon Sindt and Killarney, but Killarneys have varied, my earlier Killarney D is much more Generation-like than my later one.

Copeland whistles are amazing, but they vary too and I’ve come across a really bad one (straight from their shop).

Whistle by only using your lungs and your lips. It’s chromatic, it’s usually much more than two octaves, you can do easy note bends and sliding from note to note any time you want, it’s free, and the clogging issue is no more difficult to deal with than most whistles in the alto to low D range. I suspect that the perception of high variability in quality of low-end whistles may be at least partly a hangover from previous decades. Perceptions tend to lag behind facts, particularly statistically-conditioned ones, since they’re hard to update: people don’t buy a lot of whistles, and getting a good one can just feel like luck. Yes I have old Generation heads which I’ve bound the socket with thread, which I’ve then soaked with Super Glue, making a rock-hard socket that can’t split.

People have mentioned the thing about quality control and to me it’s a bit puzzling why Generation hasn’t been more protective of its reputation.

I’ve told the story before of getting my hands on an unopened straight-from-the-factory box of 24 Generation D whistles. (This would have been around 30 years ago.)

I had been hired to teach a beginning whistle workshop at a festival and the organisers had purchased these whistles for me to hand out to the class.

I played all the whistles and laid them out ranked from best to worst.

One was absolutely superb, one of the best High D’s I’ve ever played, and I grabbed it :)

Another was nearly as good and I grabbed it too. (I later gave both of them away to promising beginners.)

At the bottom end were a few that were just unplayable. They had no 2nd octave at all. Basically they made squeaking noises.

In the middle were whistles that varied from “good” to “pretty good” to “OK” to “meh”.

Seeing as that I had them laid out in a row ranked by quality, I pondered why Generation didn’t draw a line at some point saying “these won’t go out the door”.

They could have drawn the line at the unplayable ones, which would build customer trust, knowing that at very least they’d get a playable thing.

Or they could have drawn the line between the “good” and “pretty good” ones, selling only the top 7 or 8 whistles, and gained a reputation as a higher-end whistle maker, maybe charging $80 or so per whistle, being in the Killarney class if not higher.

Or they could have drawn the line between the top 2 whistles and the other 22, gained a reputation as one of the world’s top whistle makers, and charged $200 or $300 per whistle. Indeed their whistles could have been relied on to be on par with, if not better than, the various names which have been mentioned here. I would have gladly paid $200 for that superb best-of-the-best.

But no, Generation let all out the door, the just and unjust alike, have their whistles priced as toys, and end up gaining a reputation as such among some people.

Re: Expensive High D whistles

Since the reports of wide variability in quality all seem to come from decades back, I suspect that back in the ’80s and before, the standards for manufacturing and quality control just weren’t as good.

I don’t know anything about injection molding, so I can’t say anything about the specifics, but I could guess that the points of failure might have included the material inputs or the machinery - it’s possible that the plastics used to mold the heads deformed a bit after molding, or that tolerances on the molding equipment were looser. Both of those things could lead to large numbers of unplayable whistles going out the door. And if the level of consistency was as bad as reported, then quality control would have been a losing proposition, since the manufacturer would have been throwing out almost all of the stuff they made - so even if they’d got Mary Bergin in, they wouldn’t have been able to afford to follow her advice. Once the rest of the production chain gets tightened up, some sort of QC process - presumably automated - starts to make a lot more sense.

But again, I’m just riffing here, I don’t know anything about Generation or Feadog’s industrial processes. I wonder if we could get one of their people to notice this thread? It would be fun to have a horse’s-mouth perspective!

Re: Expensive High D whistles

I would have thought that if Generation cared to monitor ANY feedback and realized that whistle quality was all over the place decade after decade, that back at the factory they could have stopped the flow of the bad ones, replaced the mouthpieces and sold them, and generally raised the average quality a great deal. But that’s beyond their business model. I’ve never seen a review of Generation whistles that blamed the poor performance on the tube part.

I’ve also never seen a defense of Generation’s lack of quality control that was anything but an insult to all consumers. That they stayed in business at all seems to be based on 1) the market considering them to be “toy” level products in the first place, and 2) on the fact that alternative, superior whistle companies were generally late to get into the game. I think the arrival of the Internet generally ripped the veil off this situation.

Clarke whistles is an example of a whistle maker that made the adjustment and quality control doesn’t seem to be an issue. Their older model, the “Original”, has an ancient mouthpiece design which wastes a lot of air and has trouble with wood swelling/rotting issues. Then the company had a new mouthpiece designed for the “Sweetone”, which solved all that, and still at a low price, rarely if ever gets a bad review.
