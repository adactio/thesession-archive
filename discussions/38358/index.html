Playing for 4 months now and have a problem with trad

Hi guys,

I’m playing for about 4 months now, about an hour every 2 days. Listen to a lot of irish trad and tutorials and getting along quite nicely.

My problem is, I’ve become decent at improvising (percussionist for 10 years, we tend to do that, willingly or unwillingly) and can play fast tunes with other musicians for jamming jazz, funk, pagan, and writing own stuff on low and tin whistle. rolls, crans, tounging and all things I get (not always perfect yet, but understand the ideas and technique)

But with ITM I’m having trouble, I am up to the level of playing the morrison and swallowtail jig at full speed. but struggle a bit with reels and other more intricate jigs. I have the idea that my brain is not used to the flow of trad, and would like to know if there is someone here who can give me advice/tutorials/music I should follow to best get into the mindset of irish rhythms. I know that listening a lot will help, and it does, but I have the idea that my head just doesn’t want to stray from the comfortable european classical mindset, and that most of the time I’m focusing to hard on getting the rhythm that the actual tunes elude me, or that they sound to much of 4/4 if I just follow the melody instinctively .

Any help would be greatly apreciated! (sorry if unclear, will elaborate if anyone has questions)

Re: Playing for 4 months now and have a problem with trad

Sounds to me like you are simply trying to find your own style of playing. There’s more than one “right way” to play ITM, and I get the feeling that, while you want to learn all the basics and are practicing them, you really don’t want to “sound” like everyone else. That’s okay. Keep it up and give it more time and you’ll develop your own style of playing. My own little case history:

I ordered my first hammered dulcimer with dampers, because many of the great HD players, like Ken Kolodner and Joshua Messick, use dampers when they play. But when I started learning and studying traditional tunes, I found myself rarely using dampers because (one) as much as I admire the great players I really didn’t want to sound exactly like them and (two) I probably couldn’t reach their level of expertise anyway. It took time, practice, experimentation, and lots of trial and (t)error. But eventually I began to play “my way,” with my own style. Yes I still listen to all of the great performers and bands playing on many instruments, it gives me ideas about proper phrasing, harmonic structure, ornamentation, transitions and melodic contours. But I still play my tunes “my” way. I want to play my tunes well, and keep it true to the ITM spirit. But the last thing I want is for someone to hear me play and say “wow, he sounds just like so-and-so!”

Hey, it’s YOUR tune when you play it- own it!

David E.

Re: Playing for 4 months now and have a problem with trad

4 months is pretty quick for being “up to speed”… Whose speed? And when you play at that speed, does it sound like the other sources that are playing at that speed that you’re learning from? Have you recorded yourself and listened to it to see what you hear?

Sounding Irish isn’t just about the rhythms. There are a lot of nuances to this music that it took me at least 8-9 years to start to comprehend, and I was listening to and playing nothing but Irish for the majority of that.

As a friend on this board once said - Learning Irish Traditional Music is a bit of a fractal process. You start off and you have the basic shape of the music, and then you zoom in closer, and you find new details. It’s the same shapes that it was before, but you see a lot more of the detail… You get that down to a point, and then you dig a little deeper and discover things that you didn’t hear before, because all you could hear was the basic shape. That process will continue for many years, if you’re open to allowing it.

When you’ve been playing 12 times as long, you’ll still only be 4 years in, and you’ll feel invincible because you’ve learned so much. When your 24 times as far in, you’ll be at 8 years, and you’ll feel overwhelmed by all that you don’t know….

Give it time… Enjoy the journey… There is no destination. There will be no point at which you feel like “I’ve arrived… and I know everything there is to know about this music”, if you stick with it, you will find it to be a lifelong journey of discovery and enjoyment.

Re: Playing for 4 months now and have a problem with trad

“I struggle a bit with reels and other more intricate jigs. I have the idea that my brain is not used to the flow of trad, and would like to know if there is someone here who can give me advice/tutorials/music I should follow to best get into the mindset of Irish rhythms.”

Yes listening is very important, but another important aspect is getting the physical patterns ingrained in your muscle-memory. All the listening in the world won’t do that.

As musicologists who have studied ITM have pointed out, each ITM instrument has its own unique associated patterns. Give the same tune to a boxplayer, a fiddler, a banjo player, a fluteplayer, ask them to spend ten minutes learning it (in separate rooms) and they’ll come back with four slightly different versions. Give them a week to work on it and they’ll come back with four quite different versions, probably. Why? Because the more time you put into a tune the more you make it fit the traditional patterns of your instrument, and also your personal style. (Jazz is precisely like this, with (for example) sax and trumpet each having its own traditionally associated style and patterns.)

I play flute and whistle too, and the form in which I receive tunes is often not very suited to those instruments, both the physical act of rendering the tunes, and the traditional melodic patterns associated with the flute, the two being of course interrelated.

Anyhow four months just isn’t long enough to get all of the flute-patterns into your muscle memory. Once you have the patterns ingrained in your hands, you’ll find that you can learn any new tune very quickly, because the new tune really isn’t new, but rather a collection of familiar patterns strung together in a certain way.

Perhaps many or most people who play ITM don’t think of the structure of the music in these terms, however when they learn 500 reels they’ve played a number of patterns over and over (the 500 reels being formed from a much smaller number of patterns, recombined in 500 different ways).

These patterns exist in analogous families, each family member being equivalent and interchangeable, so that the same tune is generated regardless which family-member you grab on the fly. This is the source of the variation in ITM, which those outwith the music mistake for free-form improvisation.

Re: Playing for 4 months now and have a problem with trad

The whistle voice has less dynamic range than a fiddle or flute, so using decorations is pretty important to get rhythmic pulse. In this sense, I think “decorations” are more usefully thought of as “articulations” or rhythmic elements - not just ornaments.

I found that jigs are easier to get than reels. Something about the steady drive of the triplets makes it easier to add basic rolls and things on jigs.

Reels have more & trickier rhythms that don’t come easy. Playing a reel “straight”, without rolls, taps & cuts is one thing, but getting reels to work WITH articulations is the challenge. Or maybe it is precisely that articulations are necessary in order for the dynamics and rhythms to feel right.

Reels can be rhythmically complex, and it is taking me a lot longer to figure out how to use articulations to express the rhythmic patterns. And there are more articulations options: triplets, short rolls and long rolls and short-time long rolls, and off-beat rolls, and also some rhythmic patterns that are longer. I found “Brother Steve’s” website to be the helpful in understanding rolls, especially his discussion about short & long rolls (which isn’t in his regular index for some reason) : http://www.rogermillington.com/siamsa/brosteve/index.html

Re: Playing for 4 months now and have a problem with trad

My first thought is that if you have been playing other genres you are probably still trying to play ITM the same way: in almost every other form of music the drum/bass/strum engine room take responsibility for the rhythm, and the melody player just lays a solo on top. But in ITM the melody player takes responsibility for the rhythm and the backers just follow. In ITM your solo melody playing has to contain all the pulse and drive that gets people tapping their feet or dancing. If it isn’t there then just adding a rhythm department won’t help and the whole lot will inevitably fall apart.

My second thought is that at just four months in if you think you are getting things ‘up to speed’, you probably don’t yet fully appreciate what you are trying to achieve. Tempo is probably the least important aspect of playing this music well. Jigs and reels have a pulse/lift/sense of excitement that makes people want to dance, even when they are played at very modest BPM. Concentrate on getting that pulse right first (at moderate speed) and over time your tempo will naturally increase, usually to the point where you have to deliberately throttle back. Don’t aim for speed at the expense of rhythmic definition, concentrate on playing well, with plenty of pulse/lift, and let that dictate the tempo.

Re: Playing for 4 months now and have a problem with trad

everything Mark M says is dead right. Without the proper rhythmic foundation your tunes will fly to pieces when you play them “up to speed” and unaccompanied.

I wish I’d read something similar before I started playing ITM for real. It would have saved me a lot of time and I would have been less of a nuisance at sessions.

Listen to a variety of good, steady players and try to work out exactly where they’re putting the stresses and how they’re phrasing the tune. Compare how different players play the same tune. Develop your own taste and style.

There’s no point in wasting your time learning thousands of tunes that you can’t really play yet. Instead, invest the time in mastering a relatively small repetoire then use it as a vehicle to develop your understanding and control of rhythm. When you’ve got that. it will be worth learning more tunes.
