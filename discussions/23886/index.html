Re: Help with the Penny Whistle - awkward triplets

It is easier to change from a low-octave note to a higher note if you interrupt the flow of air with a gentle , brief application of the tongue to the hard palate rather than trying to change the pressure mid-blow, as it were. Although the latter is possible, it is difficult when you are just starting out. The holes must be properly covered, of course; but the pressure of the breath should be as accurate as possible. Try playing the notes you want in the triplet separately, and note the pressure for each. Then when you put them together, change the pressure accordingly and as quickly as the fingers move. It is easier done than said, by the way.

Maybe that’s not such a bad idea…

Ditto on the notes about tongueing, and even if you were to take the several options there, starting the triplet on a ‘t’, or treble tonguing, you have to have control of your tone first and be able to execute a clean triplet with just breath and fingers.

On covering holes, remember it isn’t your finger tips, ever, it is the pad, your fingers should be relaxed and not pressing down on the instrument. If there’s a leak don’t cover it with added pressure, just move your fingers around gently till the soft pad of the finger covers the hole with little effort. Don’t practice tension. As well, take things slow, the speed comes later. Learn and practice the music, and in this case just simple slow tunes that mover along that group of notes ~ c - d - e, both from a C natural and a C sharp.

If you know another player, have them check this problem. Sometimes it can be ones tools that are faulty, and some whistles can add problems, as when the molding of the plastic head is imperfect, such as leaving a little flash, things bits of plastic that add problems. It is likely that your problem is what was mentioned earlier, but sometimes the whistle itself can contribute to problems.

Best of luck… But here, luck comes with practice - not rushed or building on impatience, tension and bad technique…

Hi again!

Another day down under…

I understand and fully appreciate the many comments about tongueing. And I acknowledge deeor’s, llig’s and ceolachan’s comments. They’re all valid.

I mentioned it at the same time because there might be occasions where the triplet needs some articulation. Sometimes this can be to start the triplet, not necessarily the whole triplet, ie triple tongueing eg GGG.

I would very rarely use that tongueing pattern unless a tune specifically required it, eg Trumpet Hornpipe.

Others might need some articulation eg Connachtman’s Rambles. Otherwise how do you get the F#AA DAA second As to sound? Or is this achieved just by a cut?

Catherine, regarding your initial triplet CDE there is a tune called “The Peacock followed the Hen”. It’s a slip jig from the Northumbrian Tradition and it features exactly that triplet, recurring many times. Northumbrian pipes music is often played tight or closed, just as some piping styles in Irish Traditional Music.

So when I’ve been playing in that style, the articulation on the flute or whistle requires a degree of tongueing. Cde | CAA CAA Cde |CAA B2G Cde | CAA CAA etc.

And an Irish Jig which features that triplet and comes to mind is Sonny Brogan’s. That is where I would agree with Michael that playing those tunes is a good way of practising or incorporating those notes within the context of the tune.

Likewise I’ve played with a number of Irish pipers who play the whole range, open and closed and it makes for exciting playing. Exploring what they do and then translating it to the whistle or flute is a wonderful way to explore your own instrument.

Not just playing with pipers has influenced my playing. Fiddlers have different approaches and might play bowed triplets on the same note or other, ie AAA or ABA.

If you play enough with another person you absorb some of their playing and it can be a rewarding experience to play that closely. Certainly I found Northern style fiddlers played a lot more bowed triplets as opposed to flowing or open triplets or rolls for that matter. And I would say that they played with a shorter bow style than long flowing patterns. There’s a lot to be said for having those regional differences.

Alluding to the preferences whether to warm up with some exercises or straight into tunes, then that’s just what I do.

There have been many threads discussing flutes and their scale lengths. With it being a modal instrument there is an amount of scope for flute players, and to a certain extent whistle players, to compensate with embouchure and air pressure to get the intervals sounding right, I suppose like the violin or viola where one can achieve an degree of accuracy if all the notes are fingered as opposed to being played on open strings.

So, when I pick up one of my flutes, it’s just my way of “hearing” that flute’s voice. You might say that I should know that by now, but I just do it because I do. And my whistles all have different voices and I like to get familiar with them in the same way.

I hope that you find some of this useful. In the end it’s all our own choice on how we approach and play tunes, and I hope you have a wonderful time playing Catherine!

To me, there are certain places where the tongue sounds right and certain places where it just doesn’t sound like the music. These places are debatable, of course, because everyones “music” differs. But the point of having a traditional music is that the players of it follow a general consensus.

I just had a quick go at the first part of that common tune The Connachtman’s Rambles. F#AA DAA. On both the fiddle and the whistle I’d be mostly cutting to get the second A. But I could list tons of various other ways I’d do it. However, one thing I don’t think I’d ever do is differentiate between two notes the same with a tongue just because you can differentiate between two notes the same with a tongue. I’d always be using the tongue to accent notes that sound right rhythmically and to accent the third note in these triplets just doesn’t sound jig like. To me, accenting the first of the As with a tongue in each triplet and cutting to get both of the second As makes it sound right. If you accent the third note in the triplet you’ll be making it sound more like a slide than a jig.

I don’t really like things like breaking down one bar in a tune, it’s not the way I work with the music, but to a learner, it may be of some help. It’s impossible to break down completely what little bits of things do to make up the whole of what the music is, not least because it’s constantly varying. I believe the best way to get it is to merely absorb what a jig sounds like, it can’t be done by mathematically taking it apart. However, maybe a little bit here and there might help.

Cde | CAA CAA Cde |CAA B2G Cde | CAA CAA etc. ~ The number of ways that this can be played without tonguing is numerous… But exploring the possibilities, including as Brian has mentioned, trying on the ways of others, is part of the joy of this music, and finding your own way with it… This is also true in deciding what you like and want to emulate, and what isn’t really ‘required’. You don’t have to use rolls, but some folks do, and for others it has become and accepted norm or standardization. Here to there are alternatives, such as bowed or tongued triplets, and other choices, like just playing a series of notes. The roll is one that is famously abused, as can be tonguing. For the latter James Galway’s machine gun way with it is dire, in my opinion…

As has been said previously, warming up with rhythmic parts of a tune, beat, bar or phrase, is a great way to get familiar with those parts and work out the rough bits.

Also mentioned is the differences that can occur between different makes of whistles, and also between two whistles of the same make. For example there are a number of potential cross fingerings for making a C natural, and some work best with one whistle and not at all with another, also true of other woodwinds, and some even allow for a damned good cross fingered G sharp or F natural, which opens up other possible ornaments…

Llig ~ “To me, there are certain places where the tongue sounds right and certain places where it just doesn’t sound ~”

Yes! The joy is when you have the choice to experiment, as Brian mentions, and the understanding to know where it works best, or doesn’t, as Llig infers, which comes with understanding and experience, listening… What needs to be avoided with tonguing is it becoming a crutch, as for many it does, having had to undo that with some that I’ve taught. If you have to tongue, then you’re not in control, you’re not making the choice, it’s a crutch. This often happens in places where a person is struggling, having problems. It is much better to show you can play without any tonguing at all, and still get the rhythm and character of a melody and form clearly across, articulated with clean and percise fingering, and well chosen breathing. Then you can start to experiment, working on what you hear and trying to emulate that, and your understanding will grow and the likelihood is you’ll be using tonguing to add interest, instead of using it to interrupt where you’re unsure or don’t like the usual results. Now, if we really get into it, there are also more than one way to tongue, but that’s outside of the realm of the original post, as is the subject of how breathing can be a choice to add interest, and not just be something a wind player has to do. Yes, you could take up circular breathing, but what I’ve heard of that is damned boring… It’s worth hearing in order to realize how much the need to breath, those silences, add to the life and lift of music…

I wish you were present so we could have a clearer idea of what’s happening, which is why I’ll repeat the suggestion that you get someone to hear and see what’s happening. That is the best solution, direct help. We can only suggest here, based on our own experiences…

Re: Help with the Penny Whistle - awkward triplets

I’ve always disliked the analogy of the skeleton/flesh tune/ornament analogy. It just doesn’t work. It’s not that it’s too simplistic, it’s that it’s specifically misleading. Especially for a beginner.

Get a sharp knife and try to cut through someone’s leg. You may well nick the bone, but the knife will stop. Flesh/bone, it’s different stuff. The only use for the analogy is when you might think about the marrow inside the bone making the red blood cells that carry oxygen to fuel the flesh.

But take a sharp analytical mind to a tune, any tune, any way of playing it, and see if you can really find a hard edge between tune and what you refer to ornamentation. You can’t do it. You simply cannot make a clear differentiation.

Take the example referred to above of two notes the same next to each other. If you differentiate with a cut, is that a decoration? Nonsense. How can you make two notes from the same note without what would be referred to as decoration? It’s not decoration, it’s integral, it’s necessary. It’s articulation.

And what about breathing? You miss out a note or two to take a breath which you skillfully (hopefully) turn into a percussive articulation which enhances the tune. have you replaced a bit of tune there with a bit of flesh? Nonsense. You’ve articulated it.

Re: Help with the Penny Whistle - awkward triplets

What I mean is to get people to realise that a roll, for example, is not really a thing that can be isolated. It is not a thing that is separate from a tune. It is an articulation of a bit of a tune. It is not something you “add” to a tune, it is merely a way of playing that bit of a tune.

Have you ever done that thing where you take a word, a common enough word, any word, and repeat it out loud to yourself over and over? And after a while it starts to sound really odd, weird, stupid even. It starts to lose its meaning and even starts to feel a little awkward to your lips and tongue. If you’ve not done it, try it.

Exactly the same happens when you try to practice articulations in isolation. They lose their meaning.

And when you try to “add” them into a tune they sound like additions. And they sound like additions because that’s exactly what they are. It’s not the way to play.

Context is everything.

Re: Help with the Penny Whistle - awkward triplets

One piece of advice I got that was helpful is to practice a tune at a steady pace, whatever that pace may be. Otherwise, you can develop the habit of slowing down on the tricky bits, which throws everything off. And don’t speed up the pace too fast, or you start fluffing thing, and start practicing making mistakes, rather than practice playing the tune correctly.
Also, depending on the phrase, a D can be played with all holes closed or the top one open, and one way can fit some phrases better than the other way. Beginners tend to keep that top hole open for the middle D, but closing everything can make some phrases easier. Plus, learn the alternate ways of playing the C natural, and also be aware that you can often leave fingers on one or two of the bottom holes without changing the pitch of a note like B or C where lots of holes are open. Some steady the whistle with the pinky when all holes are open, I tend to play C sharp with my finger on the bottom hole.
Most of all, enjoy what you play, and play lots--it all comes in time.

Re: Help with the Penny Whistle - awkward triplets

Yes Al, it’s vital to never play a tune faster than you can play what you regard as the hardest bit.

Some people think a metronome helps here. But the problem with a mechanical device that is not listening to you is that after you have inevitably slowed down a little to get yourself through what you consider the hard bit, the machine will merely make you speed up the next bit to catch up.

The metronome can’t help you play a bit your fingers can’t play. All it does is ruin the next bit that you can play fine without it.
