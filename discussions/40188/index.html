By the way, to pick up on something someone else hinted at above (and at risk of starting a whole other debate!), don’t worry about singing Irish songs in your own accent, Kellie. I sing several Irish songs in my own London English accent, and they work fine. I’ve been thanked by Irish singers in London at folk clubs for singing a song they hadn’t heard in years on several occasions.

I’d feel stupid putting on an Irish accent that wasn’t mine. I’ve never heard a convincing folk singer singing in an accent other than their own. (Just my opinion, no offence intended to anyone reading this who disagrees or who does any different) Yes, you have to alter the occasional word, but surprisingly not very often.

Don’t forget, Luke Kelly of the Dubliners took several English and Scots songs and ‘made them Irish’, to an extent that many regard them now as being core Irish songs. And he always sang them in his own voice and accent.

Re: Know any good Irish songs?

By all means imitate stylistic stuff, technique etc but please don’t imitate accents. To do so is to miss the fundamental point of folk singing. You have to sing it in your own voice: singing is not the same thing as acting. The best folk singers have a voice that is more or less seamless with their speaking voice in terms of general pitch and range. Even if you were to go to a voice coach or something and master the most perfect Irish accent (which region would you go for, by the way? If you emulate each singer you learn from, does that mean you’d be happy to be singing some songs in an Ulster accent, others in a Cork?) you’d still be the guy who introduces songs in a US accent and sings them in an Irish one. Don’t forget that if all the singers you are emulating had taken the same approach as you, they would to state the obvious not have sounded like themselves, and it’s unlikely you would have chosen to emulate them in the first place!

Listening to your recording, Kellie, it sounds like you’re not laying on too thick an Irish accent (thank God!). But if I were you I would work on being yourself, and iron out the still-audible faux-Irishisms on words like “I” and “that”. I would do my utmost to try to sing naturally, as opposed to trying to make them more convincingly Irish-accented. You pronounce words like “mountain” in your own US accent, and that’s you and that’s great. You should aim to be an artist, not an actor.

By all means, make the style and technique of singing as authentic as possible: emulate all you like in that regard. But if you sing in an accent that’s not your own, your artistry will never be as powerful or potent.

Re: Know any good Irish songs?

“…why would I want to use American pronunciation for anything?Some of the vowel sounds in a Northwest Ohio accent are absolutely hideous in my opinion. In fact, in choir we are taught to sing in what I can only describe as a British accent to avoid the thin vowel sounds…”

I’m no singer myself, but I am fairly observant of language. The fact is, speaking and singing are not the same thing; singing involves lengthening or shortening certain syllables, pitch patterns that do not correspond to the natural intonation of speech, sometimes shifting the stress onto the ‘wrong’ syllable and other things that do not normally happen in speech. So certain everyday sounds used in speech might sound awkward in song. Choral singing is a different animal from solo singing - uniformity is key, and teaching everyone to use a particular pronunciation system makes perfect sense. When singing solo, it is simply a case of slightly altering those sounds that seem ‘unsingable’ - you probably do this intuitively, anyway.

> I’m afraid the argument for studiously avoiding accent while preserving pronunciation and syntax is very weak tea, indeed.

It’s a difficult business. I can see where Ewan MacColl was coming from when he banned people from putting on voices, but then everyone puts on a voice when they sing to some extent; there are very few people alive now who developed their singing voices in the tradition before they were surrounded by singing accents from popular music. But, for example, June Tabor singing Scots ballads in an English accent and using Scots dialect where it’s needed to make the rhyme just rings awkward and false.

My advice? Try it in an Irish accent. Try it in your own voice (and really get into the corners of it and find all those sounds that make it your voice). Then just see what comes naturally. Singing in your own accent is pretty unnatural for most of us and needs to be practised in its own right.

Thing about Ewan MacColl is that early in his career he sang in several different accents (including a toe-curlingly awful attempt at an American accent once or twice). He recorded many many albums of Scottish songs with a Scottish accent that many Scots singers at the time were rather bewildered by: despite MacColl having a Scottish mum, his Scottish accent sounds like no other Scottish person I’ve ever heard (that’s the kind way of putting it; I know plenty of people who would use words like “comedy” and “caricature”)

I quite like some of MacColl’s stuff, but - surprise surprise - i think he sounds best when he’s singing in his natural Salford voice. And also when he’s singing quietly - he had a bit of a fixation on singing in a loud, cutting, nasal voice that I think he thought was ‘folky’, a style that unfortunately got copied by a few other singers.

Theodor Bikel sang loads of folk songs of different countries, in the accents of those countries. He had a good ear - he was a trained stage and screen actor - and his accents are generally convincing. But the overall vibe is of a man putting on different hats – none of his songs seem to have an emotional core.

Yes - that’s when you tweak the words. 9 times out of 10 you hardly need to change anything at all. I sing ‘Sheela Nee Iyer’, from the singing of Paddy Tunney, and it has one Irish word in it: speirbhean, which roughly means ‘sweetheart’. Brilliantly, ‘sweetheart’ scans perfectly so it’s an easy, painless switch.

If a song is absolutely packed with dialect, I simply don’t bother trying to sing it. I sing a few Scottish songs, and I’ve changed various words. There’s a song I sing with the chorus “We are not fu‘, we are not fu’” (meaning we are not drunk) and I changed it to “we are not far gone, not far gone”, though I’ve sometimes sang ‘we are not foul’ which has got a laugh. In context it works (it’s a song about lads out on the piss). Although I sometimes like the way some words have different meanings in English: I like “the piper is dozy, the piper is fey” in ‘Farewell ye Banks of Sicily’. Even though “fey” in English means something slightly different to the Scots, I liked the suggestion of a dozy, rather effete piper so I kept it.

Re: Know any good Irish songs?

I once read a discussion about accents in Ireland. I believe the author was some learned man from Trinity. He pointed out that while there were perhaps four ‘accents’ or more properly, dialects, in Gaelic, there seems to be a profusion of accents and dialects of English as spoken by the Irish throughout Ireland. There was a time when no self respecting Kerryman would allow himself to be mistaken for a Clareman. Just as today, almost no one with a good ear would mistake a ‘Norn oirishman’ for a Dubliner.
His thesis was roughly as follows. Over the course of the roughly eight hundred year colonisation/exploitation of Ireland by the English, the English Language itself went through several sea-changes in pronunciation and vocabulary. When an area of Ireland had its Gaelic speakers depleted and ‘pacified’, and the native population ‘got the English’, the form was set by the style and vocabulary of that Century.
He gave an anecdote about an English Clergyman sitting under a tree watching two ploughmen in the old Barony of Forth. When they took a break in the shade near him and heard him reading aloud passages from the Chaucer he was reading, they readily corrected his pronunciation and perfected the rhymes he was having difficulty with in Chaucer’s English. The Barony’s ‘dialect’ went back to to the Geraldine era and was nearly a perfect preservation of Chaucer’s English.

Re: Know any good Irish songs?

Rock music’s different to folk music. Rock places much more value on theatre than traditional music does. Sure, there are plenty of myths about authenticity in rock music - we like to think that the rock star “really means it”. But in terms of delivery, it’s anything goes. Up until punk, it was generally thought that singers sounded daft singing rock‘n’roll and/or other US forms in non-American accents (though Syd Barrett, Nick Drake and to an extent David Bowie managed it). Since the 70s, there’s been loads of British bands singing rock in Brit accents. Just as it took a while for UK hip-hop to find its own non-American voice. When London-accented rappers first came out in the mid-80s, they had the mickey taken out of them. Nowadays, it’s flipped over and no self-respecting British rapper raps in an American accent.

I can think of plenty of British rock singers who sang in American accents - often not all that convincing ones - yet who still made convincing art. But I can’t think of any folksingers at all.
