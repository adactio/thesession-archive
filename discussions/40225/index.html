Re: Is this a good starter flute and Low D?

My $.02… if flute is to be in the equation at all, then don’t get a low D whistle as well. Flute is a demanding enough instrument that it will need your full attention. As mentioned up-thread, get halfway decent on a flute and you can pick up a low D later on, as an easy transition. It may take you a couple of years to get an embouchure you’re happy with, so you need to start NOW and not fool around on a low D whistle.

I recommend a Casey Burns Folk Flute. For the same or less money ($450) you can get polymer flutes, but starting on a Folk Flute means you won’t second-guess the wood vs. plastic question (not that I think wood is “better” at this level, but it’s an emotional/aesthetic thing for some of us). You’ll learn how to care for a wooden flute in case you move to a fancier one later on, and they hold resale value very well compared to a polymer flute if it doesn’t work out. Speaking of which… you might find a used Burns Folk Flute if you keep an eye out, although they don’t tend to last long on sale.

↳ Posted by Michele Sims on January 25th, 2017.

Re: Is this a good starter flute and Low D?

WRT the differences between whistle and flute, embouchure development and maintenance has been mentioned, that’s the big difference. Here are some others:

As general rule, whistle players articulate more with the tongue. Irish flute players generally (not always) prefer finger articulation, with optional throat articulation (glottal stops).

You can get a nice “push” with your breath for a rhythm emphasis on flute, because breathing harder doesn’t change the octave like it does on whistle. Instead you change octave by tightening and slightly shifting the air stream with your lip muscles (embouchure). This ability to alter volume with breath attack means that the flute can be a more dynamic instrument than whistle. A whistle is played more like the pipes with a steady volume.

Compared to a high D whistle, a D flute has large finger holes (especially something like a Pratten style), which can make it easier to half-hole notes like G# and Fnat, and bend into other notes. This may apply to low D whistle too, I’ve never played one.

A flute can exist in keyless or keyed versions. Adding keys to a D flute allows access to tunes that would otherwise require half-holing and cross-fingering, as well as access to other styles of music than just Irish trad. Of course you can collect a bunch of different whistles to solve that problem, but a keyed flute gives you one (expensive) instrument to handle a lot of different situations.

↳ Posted by Conical bore on January 25th, 2017.

Re: Is this a good starter flute and Low D?

Flute and whistle are related, but I would not say they are more than that. Flute requires much more than whistle does, which is why you will find that, whereas most flute players also play whistle, there are many whistle players who do not play flute. Flute requires far more than just a developed embouchure. The entire approach to blowing and playing a tune differs greatly from whistle, where you simply blow. The tongue is used much more for articulation on whistle. It is possible, although not really common, to never use the tongue when playing flute. How you breathe, how you stand, how you position your hands, arms and body are all far more critical in flute playing than in playing whistle. As a player of both for many years, I find flute far more satisfying because I find it a more compelling instrument to listen to and far more expressive. But it ain’t easy.

Re: Is this a good starter flute and Low D?

The thing I don’t like about delrin/polypenco flutes is their weight.

I used to have a c1880 Boosey Pratten flute in Eb made out of hard rubber. It’s a great material because it doesn’t weigh more than wood. It is kinda stinky.

I might have missed it, but to me one of the greatest advantages of the flute over the whistle is that you can play any note at any volume level without affecting its pitch. You can play the low notes booming loud, and play the high notes very softly.

In fact my favourite flute warmup exercise is playing a note as softly as possible, increasing it to as loudly as possible, then back to as softly as possible, all while staying at the same pitch.

On whistle each note is only in tune at one level of blowing predetermined by the maker; blowing softer makes the note more quiet but also more flat, and blowing harder makes the note louder but also sharper.

Also on flute you can vary the timbre of any note at will, making it very nasal at one extreme and very hollow at the other extreme or anywhere in between.

On whistle the timbre of the notes is predetermined by the maker.

In other words on flute any aspect of a note can be varied independently of other aspects, while on whistle they’re fixed and locked together, which is musically limiting.

Anyhow if you’re interested in flute, go for it!

“I might have missed it, but to me one of the greatest advantages of the flute over the whistle is that you can play any note at any volume level without affecting its pitch. You can play the low notes booming loud, and play the high notes very softly. ”

Right, exactly! That’s what I meant by the term dynamics in an earlier post.

Last weekend I was at a concert out here in Washington State by the duo of Cillian Vallely and Kevin Crawford (Lúnasa band members). Front row seat, small venue. It was interesting to watch how Kevin worked his mic, when playing both flute and low F whistle during the show.

With the flute, he maintained a steady distance from the mic and there was a terrific amount of dynamic expression, “pulsing the rhythm” louder or softer with breath control. With the low F whistle, he spent more time moving back and forth to the microphone to get softer or louder in different parts of the tune, because a whistle just doesn’t have the same dynamic range as flute.

P.S. I asked Cillian after the concert about the low F whistle sets, because that goes back a long way with Lúnasa. He said they played those whistles basically to break up the sets so they didn’t sound all the same, and because the fingering was a bit quicker than low D so the technique they had from flute and Uilleann pipes respectively, would translate a bit easier. In a band/performance like that, it doesn’t matter if you’re playing tunes on a low F whistle in a key that isn’t session friendly.

Re: Is this a good starter flute and Low D?

Richard has given the answer to the question about the difference that made me go mainly for flute - being able to change the volume and timbre of a note over a wide range.

I would add that you can hear for yourself the differences in sound between flute and low whistle. Some people favour one over the other because of that.

Less obvious is that the high whistle is a much more ‘nimble’ instrument that the flute or low whistle. You don’t generally hear people playing like Mary Bergin on a low whistle. That is largely a matter of physics - low notes on a wind instrument don’t start as quickly as high ones. I wonder if that is also behind using the low F whistles and flutes for variety - I guess Richard’s ‘great rippling crans’ are enabled by the pitch as well as by the fingering.

So the suggestion above of both high whistle and flute sounds good to me.
