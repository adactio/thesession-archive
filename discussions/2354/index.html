The fork in the road …

Presumably before those of us who participate in these here discussions, who play the music, who muddy the clear waters of the tradition with our own wee tunes (but let’s not open that can of worms again, eh?), etc. got the bug, we were listening to all kinds of music. I mean even we from the bog didn’t always grow up listening to the music from knee-height (I may have heard it, but listening’s another matter), far less actually playing it!

But being part of the music is for many of us a decision - perhaps not a conscious one - but a decision nevertheless. To get to grips with this compelling but exacting, frustrating but rewarding medium requires a diversion of musical effort and energy. For some, that diversion is total (or near as damn it so); for others less than total. But a diversion nevertheless.

I wonder if anyone can remember the point where they came to the fork in the road and made the decision - conscious or otherwise - to take the path along which we’re all travelling …

Well, I can.

A summer of indolence, drunkenness and irresponsibility. I’d just been made redundant from a job which I’d come to detest (with a handsome settlement), my relationship with my girlfriend of long-standing had recently broken up. After a period of fretting, I came to the realisation that I was FREE. For a while at least I had no worries, no ties and I made a pact with myself to get out of London for the summer and have a good oul’ rest in the West.

I didn’t expect to fall under the music’s spell.

I had in mind afternoons spent fishing or walking through the hills. Evenings spent down the pub, consuming unfeasible quantities of Guinness and Black Bush (bit of a busman’s holiday that particular element), maybe the occasional romantic entanglement with some other unburdened, hedonistic soul …

I had the good fortune to arrive late, flustered and unkempt at a guest house where I was (unbeknownst to myself) to stay for the next seven or eight weeks.

“Come in, come in! Tea? Coffee? Beer?” The last option was too good to refuse. “Good man! Grab a seat in the living room and I’ll be with you in a minute.”

A guitar lay propped in the corner and while beer was being got, I noodled around on a few chords.

“There you go! That’ll get the dust out your throat! Cheers!”

A pause while Adam’s apples bobbed up and down in time to the glug, glug of beer being welcomed into the gullets of two men whose gullets rarely wanted for said beverage …

“Do you play the guitar, then?”

“No” says I - hastily returning it to its corner.

“That’s a pity. We could do with a backer for the next couple of nights.”

So, it turns out that my landlord plays whistle and sings with his mate, who plays pipes (and also sings and plays guitar, but not at the same time as piping, obviously) in the local pub. Their normal guitarist is off on some spree somewhere and if I could give them a hand …

As it happened I gave it a lash for a night, one or other yelling chords in my ear prior to each change, but it was dreadful. I find accompanying the music very difficult and when a boyo turned up who could do their playing justice, I was happy to let him step into the breach.

Anyway … the fork in the road …

On two occasions in those weeks my hair literally stood on end with excitement.

The first when I was accompanying the two lads on that first night and they launched into The Kesh/The Lark In The Morning (not a set you’ll hear very often but they managed to pull it off with some aplomb - with a long glide down from the high phrase of the last bar of the Kesh down to the opening A of The Lark). I somehow managed to fumble the chords and together the three of us gelled. The power of their playing blew me away. I didn’t know it at the time, but I was in the presence of two very accomplished, articulate players…

Later. An evening meal in the guest house, downstairs. A lot of invited guests. Dinner being over we took turns going round the table for a song. Yer man, the host, gave us a version of “The Bantry Girls’ Lament” which had my nerve ends zinging with a mix of pleasure and sadness and excitement …

It took a while after coming back to London that I first set out on the journey proper. I’ve taken a few faltering footsteps since. But those two moments, I’ve since come to realise, were the deciders. They made the journey possible and they made the journey inevitable …

Zina …

Here’s the rub.

I went back a few years later and the same fellas were in the same pub, playing (more or less) the same stuff.

Although I had a few tunes in common with them at that stage on the banjo, I didn’t feel sufficiently up to speed to join in with them.

Now, with a few more years under my belt and a bit more command of myself, I’d be raring to give it a lash with the pair of them.

As for their names.

I’m not being coy in not naming them. They’ve become part of my personal mythology. Few people (probably) hold these two guys in as much esteem as I do. I’m sure that for many, they’re just two of many musicians who play in one of many, many pubs.

But they are much more significant than that for me. They’re the boys that lit the fire …

I’ve promised myself a return journey some time next year … no wife or kids … just three or four days to play a few tunes with them and thereby to repay my debt of gratitude!

Re: The fork in the road …

July 1981…..
There wasn’t a lot happening in rural co. Galway for a sensitive non-drinking youth with no car , job or money……

I was getting bored with Rock and Pop music, looking for a way
to express myself, not be part of the mainstream…..and was finishing with Simon&Garfunkel and the Beatles……they had sustained me for a long time……

Me cousin Aido arrived down from Dublin with a tin-whistle and a book on how to play it…… “Radical” I thought, one could play their own music………. That was my first step on the road.

I didn’t know it then but in passing that summer, in the bedroom struggling to cover the finger-holes of the whistle , following the diagrams of the black holes and the uncovered ones…. , under the melody of “Spancil Hill”, I was starting a journey. I might have had had a hunch, a vague, unexpressed feeling there was a world of music waiting to be teased out of those 6 holes. ( I suspect I wasn’t so self-aware… ).

I think I was escaping the tedium of an unfulfilled life to a world of “being”. It must have felt fantastic to hear the rise and fall of those simple melodies, the joy of creating and self-expression…… to play those few simple notes was to really “be”.

The rest of the family watched Wimbledon and Dallas on the TV, and went to dances and discos while I listened to “Sunday Folk ” on the radio ( I recall recording the tune “9 Pints of Roguery” being played on that programme, loving it but not yet understanding it…. then 22 years later I was re-acquainted with the same tune on a Chieftains CD….only now I could understand it.

I laboured for hours that Summer, trying to understand the dots for “Carolans Concerto”, ‘cos it was in the book….. It was a whole 10 years before I managed to learn a proper reel ( Junior Crehans from Liam O’Flynns playing with Planxty. ) , of course in retrospect it was ludicrous for a struggling musical novice, ignorant of any music theory, to attempt “Carolans Concerto”, but it was in the book, and I was familiar with it from an ad it had featured on TV……. in any case it provided me lots of scope for feeling inadequate, and gave lots of opportunity to fail better……
( Perfect for a young Catholic )

Annach-Chuain, Give me your Hand, Vinegar Hill, DonnyBrook Fair, Ballydesmond Polkas……. early stepping-stones on a long path, the fork was behind me and I was already down the road……..

Pat

Re: The fork in the road …

A few years ago, I spent 2 1/2 incredible weeks driving all around Ireland. I was not into Irish music at all at that time. Just thinking I was in Westport and didn’t go to the session at Matt Molloy’s pub!!

As it happened, my boss at that time went to Ireland a few weeks later. He used to play Irish music 20 years ago, and that trip got him back into it. Knowing that I’m musically inclined he suggested I try out the whistle and introduced me to the local session scene. After a few weeks, he invited me to a semi-closed session with what turned out to be some of the best musicians in town. I went with the intention to listen and learn, and sat somewhere on the side. Seeing that I was listening closely to the music, one of the musicians, an older Irish box player, invited me to sit with them. When he heard that I was playing (or rather, attempting to play) the whistle, he made me promise to bring it with me next time.
The 5 or so regulars in that group were extremely encouraging and supportive, even though I played with sheet music all the time (being lazy, I had never previously learned any music by heart; but in a very short time, I was very good at recognizing tunes, matching them with their respective titles, and leafing through my collection at frantic speed). Once in a while, I got some well-meant teasing about my using sheet music, but I also got admiration for my sight-reading skills and occasionally found some sheets pushed in front of me: Let’s try that tune!
I’m sure the two great fiddle players in that session had something to do with me trying out the fiddle. I had never liked classical solo violin, but with Irish fiddle, it was love at first sound byte. After only two weeks on a rental instrument, I went out to buy my own; I knew I was going to stick with it for a long time! And I am pretty sure had I not had the opportunity to experience Irish session music at its best, I wouldn’t have gotten that much involved in it, particularly since the people of the local open session at that time were not overly friendly to newcomers.

This session doesn’t exist anymore in this form. Some people moved away or changed camps (quebequois). But I feel very honored to have been part of this group and I’m sure I learned a lot during these 1 1/2 years. And no, I don’t usually use sheet music anymore and have a fairly large, constantly growing repertoire. But sometimes in the sessions, if someone wants to introduce new tunes to those in the group interested, we do use sheet music. And sometimes someone who thinks him/herself a great musician makes rather snide remarks along the lines of this not being the “correct” way to play Irish music. This makes me think back to the great musicians of my early session experience who didn’t care how the music got into me, just how it came out again!

Re: The fork in the road …

The fork in the road, the spoon in the kitchen - a knife in my heart. Live music - not canned in anyway came into me first on the south side of Chicago. A rent party had some street musicians playing on the back porch of a tenement building with a dance happening in the alley and back yard. Wild stuff for an eight year old kid from the west side.

The group was called the Chicago String Band and they played blues … I listened, studied my piano and got summarily thrown out of class for pounding on a Steinway. I got a five string banjo when I was about 13 - learned what could be done on it - moved onto guitar when I was fifteen or so … but something was lacking in both the spirit and the emotion of the music. It was raw, strong and compelling but not in my soul.

A few years later I heard the Chieftons, then the Bothy Band and a slew of other Celtic players and was baffled by the poly rhythms and relatively simple melodies. I put the theory books down - learned to listen and play along and finally discovered MY roots and my future as an aspiring musician. Mostly I play for my own enjoyment but an odd session or two will happen and I seem to be welcome with either a mandolin or mandola - The Scots tunes really tweak me and a slow march on a mandola or O/M has to be heard to be believed.

It’s now over fifty years since the fork appeared in that slum and what a trip it’s been. I can’t wait to see what happens next.

Thanks Aidan - for reminding me …

This is the sad tale of a man who took the wrong fork…
When I was at school, in the seventies, I got really into Irish music (though not exclusively so), from listening to John Peel and also various Radio 3 trad concerts (one by the Boys of the Loch, where I first heard ‘the Maid Behind The Bar’, particularly sticks in the mind - Cathall made some comment about how it might be called ‘Made Behind The Bar’, so maybe it wasn’t quite such a ubiquitous tune back then). I had a friend who was Polish, but really into Irish music, who would lend me his precious Dolores Keane records (I’m ashamed to say I still have one of them!). And when I left school I got involved with the SWP, and we used to have meetings in rooms above pubs in Holloway, where I first heard sessions (they were rather brilliant sessions, though I didn’t really appreciate that at the time).
I got chatting to a man at one of these sessions, I think at the Victoria, and he seemed quite surprised that given that I seemed to know quite a bit about the music, that I didn’t play anything. I told him I didn’t know the first thing about playing music (the English Comprehensive School system at that time specialised in the cultivation of musical illiteracy, unless you were deemed to be of use to the school orchestra), and he pointed me in the direction of Cecil Sharpe House, where a very whiskery and pony tailed young man sold me the Feadog Stain book and a D whistle.
I found it easy to get started, although the accompanying cassette seemed to be designed to merely cause gloom and depression - the slow and simple versions of the tunes seemed fabulously fast, and the full-speed versions… well! My road forked rather recently when my parents just happened to buy a 3-CD set of the Irish Rovers for me and my siblings for Christmas, about two years ago, when I was 13. Up until then, I had listened to nothing but classical; I was never exposed to Irish trad. I listened to the Irish Rovers about 50 million times and started to develop a liking for Irish music. I eventually started getting more and more recordings and liking it more and more.

Soon, I wanted to learn how to play it. My Dad had an old guitar that he never used, so I took it down, dusted it off, and started teaching myself some chords. I really enjoyed it and learned more and more.

Then, about a year ago now, I bought a whistle and started teaching myself that as well. It took me a while to get started; I didn’t really have a book or anything. I tried screeching out simple tunes I knew and drove my Mom and sisters crazy in the process.

Then, about 7 months ago, I bought a mandolin and started teaching myself that. I also got a fiddle from my parents with the promise of lessons, but that didn’t start till August. A little before then, I bought an old bodhran from a freind of mine and with the help of another freind started playing that as well.

I love Irish music so much now that I have taken six of my freinds, and got them hooked on it as well, and we started playing together in a band. We have a lot of fun together, and we are getting better and learning more all the time.

I passed the fork in the road once many years ago when I was deeply involved in bluegrass music. My fiddler/mentor suggested that my style of playing mandolin was probably better suited to Irish music so I borrowed a coupla albums, one of the early Chieftains, and Mick Moloney. I listened, enjoyed, and attempted to play like that but came back to Jerry, my fiddler/mentor and announced that Irish was just too hard, I was gonna stick to bluegrass.
Jump ahead 20 yrs to 1999. Jerry, still only in his early 60s, was struck down by a cancerous brain tumor. No longer able to play or even get out of bed, he asked me to come by now and then and play my mandolin for him. I did, every week, even after he was moved to the VA hospital and no longer responded to my presence. He eventually left us and I miss him still. The world was a better place with him in it.
After grieving him I decided to have another stab at ITM. Maybe it was because I had become a better musician over the passing years…maybe it was because I had been through a traumatic change and just needed something to really focus on…or maybe I was just finally ready for the fork. Whatever the reason, this time it took, firmly. I know that after studying it intensely for about 2 yrs I am now a MUCH better musician, on many levels. And I know it’s the music for me, it literally speaks to my soul.
And I know Jerry is proud.

Okay, I suppose it’s all died down a bit, but now that I’ve a bit more time…

I didn’t take a fork in the road so much as sort of vaguely saunter off the beaten path. I married an American of Scottish descent, and loved the sound of the bagpipes and taking part in all the geneological stuff his family liked. Even after the marriage ended, I continued to visit the highland festival up in Estes Park. One summer, an Irish stepdance school was doing an audience participation (the festival is now a Scottish-Irish festival, with one of the best feisianna of the year), and they roped me in. It was fun, and they invited me down to their ceili dancing class down in Denver.

Well, that was fun, and I gradually ended up in the stepdancing as well, and then the teacher found out that I’d played the viola in high school. She wanted a fiddler to take round with her to shows and suggested I start, lending me a fiddle and such. Another dancer who played the guitar offered to show me some hints and explained how the music worked.

Of course, I was dreadful, and worked off sheet music, and didn’t have a clue about ornaments, and wondered why it sounded so NOT Irish. Still, everyone was encouraging, so I kept it up, always looking for a teacher and never finding one that I thought sounded really Irish.

Matt and Shannon Heaton moved into town, I met them at a party given by one of the set dancers. I did their website, we exchanged a bunch of favors (and I was so inexperienced at that point -- perhaps “clueless” is a better word -- that I never realized that a fiddler doesn’t have to be taught to play Irish music by another fiddler), and they (their group, Siucra, to be precise) ended up playing at our wedding.

At the potluck ceili we had (inviting the entire dance school, who danced, and anyone else who wanted to come -- we filled up my husband’s club at the time), we had a few musicians join Siucra and they played throughout. At one point, Pete and I were up talking with the musos, and everyone realized that they were all going to be in Ireland around the holidays that year. They knew that we were going to wait to take our honeymoon til December, and everyone laughed and suggested that we take our honeymoon in Ireland in December and join them.

So we did.

We stayed in London for two weeks, then Ireland for another three. Siucra came and stayed with us for a week or two, in our RentAnIrishCottage place in Corofin, and I learned a lot about what I’d been doing wrong.

And I’ve never looked back since. I started taking lessons from Shannon and soaking up what I could whenever I could.

Now…WHY is a different question. My favorite is the inevitable question, “So. How did…(significant pause) YOU end up in all this Irish stuff?” When I can get away with it, I’ll answer “feck all if I know.” When I can’t, I suppose I mumble something about everything somehow just connecting with me. I just kind of fell into it all, the way I fall into just about everything good in my life, so it’s not much worth analyzing, I guess. :)

Zina
