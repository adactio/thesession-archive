Added by AB on December 27th, 2012

Real time improvisation/variation vs. pattern memorisation.

Playing music requires a good memory. You remember how a jig goes in your head & you can sing or play the tune. With muscle memory your fingers know where the notes are and how to play cuts or rolls. But are these only the basic techniques? Is music purely technique; ultimately objective?

Once you have the tune in your head; it’s memorised. So where do the variations come from? To an extent you’re still tapping into memory. But this only takes you so far. It seems in order to make more variations there needs to be the slimmest tether to memory, yet; at the same time, you allow for a more immediate (spontaneous/real time) response to possible variations (improvisation) of rhythm/articulation/melody/phrasing…

I started learning how to improvise way before I picked up Traditional music.

I’ve come to think of improvising as RAM(Random Access Memory).
“Memory that a computer software uses to run programs. Similar to a hard drive except the data is lost when the computer is turned off..” -itrainonline.org

And playing note for note by memory as SAM(Serial Access Memory) “SAM stores data as a series of memory cells that can only be accessed sequentially (like a cassette tape).” -howstuffworks.com

For me, variations are simply alternative patterns. My understanding is that music is very open ended. One phrase was put into place, but other phrases could easily take the place of that phrase without altering the meaning/sentiment behind the music. For example, in poetry…

One could say

“I love you, my dearest.”
or
“My dearest, I love you.”

Saying the same thing, but in a different way. If you listen to story-telling comedians do their bits, you’ll notice that they haven’t memorized them word for word, but that they memorized only the outline of the story. Memorizing the story makes it easier to tell, but neglecting to memorize it word for word leaves room for creativity.

Back to music. A short musical phrase could go,

“dF#A”, “dAf#”, “f#Ad”, “F#dA”, “AdF#”, or “Af#d”.
6 different ways to play a DMajor chord melodically, and that’s only in the root chord lol. That’s only one way to improvise. Being able to improvise music comes from an understanding of how the notes, melodies, chords, rhythms, etc. relate to each other. One thing that use to baffle me was how some people can play anything by sheet, but if you take the sheet music away from them, they can’t play a thing. They are always fascinated by those that can improvise. One of my friends explained it to me this way….

“They don’t understand what to listen for because they play by sight.”

And what do we listen for? Resolve and Tension.
I hope this answers at least one of your questions. :)

Re: Real time improvisation/variation vs. pattern memorisation.

I think it all depends on where you are on the learning curve. In the early stages, where you are still memorizing tunes, and playing by ‘muscle memory’ then yes, the best you can achieve is to learn several versions of a tune and swap them about.

But when you’ve played for years, when you learn tunes without having to deliberately memorize them, when playing your instrument has become so natural and unconscious that the notes you want just come out automatically without any thought or ‘muscle training’. When, if someone plays you a phrase you can play it straight back, like Lonnie in Deliverance, then you can improvise.

The tune runs in your head, and you either play it, or you play something else that goes with it. Very often the improvisation is triggered by someone else - an accompanist plays a strum pattern that you like, so you pick it up and play it next time round. The guitarist plays an interesting chord somewhere, and next time round you play a run on that chord.

But to improvise rather than just playing learned variations you have to be able to keep two balls in the air - the original tune has to keep playing in your head while you play around it on your instrument. That means everything except the creative bit has to be happening at a sub-conscious level, which only comes with long hours of practice.

Re: Real time improvisation/variation vs. pattern memorisation.

I like to think of it like language. The tune is a story, and you know how the story goes, but that doesn’t mean that you have to tell it the same way every time (as if you’d memorized it for a theatre production or “performance” - Gasp, Shock, Horror) ;-)

I don’t think of knowing tunes as memorizing them, I think of it more like knowing the story. Muscle memory comes into play, yes, but I try not to rely on my fingers to remember how a tune goes. That’s an important step in your development process as a musician, but ultimately, it should be much more like typing on a keyboard. Your fingers remember where the notes are, but your brain is telling them what to play.

Once you have developed a certain familiarity with both your instrument and the music, you can strive to be more eloquent in your expression of the stories. For myself, the best (not necessarily the most impressive) variations are the ones that flow out as part of my expression. I guide them purposefully, but they’re not a fully formed “thing that I do here sometimes” kind of thing. I have those too, of course. The variations that I’ve sat and worked out and practiced…

I also think it’s important to remember that variation isn’t just melodic. You are also using variations in timing, emphasis, and the “shape” of notes to form your expression of the musical idea that you’re trying to convey. (By “shape”, I mean how and where the note begins and ends… Do you slide into the note, or play it hard and then cut it off early, etc.)

Re: Real time improvisation/variation vs. pattern memorisation.

I think we need to be clear that we are talking about improvising variations on a melody, not the aimless noodling of lead guitarists and saxophonists. The ‘spirit’ of the original melody needs to remain recognizable in your new variation, which does tend to push you towards fidkid’s ‘micro-variations’ or the ‘building blocks’ others have mentioned. But it can still be a spontaneous process, where you choose a particular building block on the fly, to fit with what others are doing around you. That approach tends to give a much more animated result than if you pre-learn a set of variations, and play them through the same way every time.

In contrast to fidkid’s fiddler, I recorded a piper a few months back. We did five takes of one particular track, but come edit-time there was no way I could patch from one take to another, because in the five takes, one after the other, he hadn’t played the tune the same way twice.

Individual style is usually a mass collection of things that you have been influenced by. So if you’re influenced by pure drop, then your music will have a pure drop aspect. I know this first hand from my own personal piano compositions in other styles of music. When I listened to popular music, my compositions began to develop the structure of those pieces. After a year of studying minimalistic music, my compositions became more simple/minimal. In terms of improvising, it’s difficult not to be influenced by what other musicians do, because it’s a lot more simple than writing your own entire piece.
“I like the double stop that one musician put here.”
or
“I like how that other musician put a triplet there.”
or
“I’m going to learn how that person changed up that entire bar, cause it sounds really cool.”

And after doing this for so long, all of the things you’ve learned from this multitude of musicians accumulate. If the influence is concentrated enough(meaning from a small amount of sources than by a mass amount of sources), and practiced enough, then it becomes obvious where that persons’ style came from.

Re: Real time improvisation/variation vs. pattern memorisation.

I think the analogy with language is an apt one. When fluent in a language, you can effortlessly play about with words, syntax, but still be understood, but when learning one, you arduously put words together into sentences and you fit them into the grammatical structures that you have carefully memorized. I think playing variations in tunes is a little bit like that. For experienced players, it’s almost effortless to play about with the phrases, while keeping the tune itself perfectly recognizable. Novices, conversely, get very fixed ways of playing a tune and if they play one phrase differently, they bugger up the next one.

That said, I think when you’re learning, you can pick up little tricks, i.e. substituting rolls with triplets or with playing an adjacent note. That sort of thing is a good place to start.
