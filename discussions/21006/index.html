Starek,

Look at the time signature something like 4/4 or 3/4 or 6/8 at the beginning of the piece of music.

The top number is the number of beats per measure, a mesure is the “block” with notes in it on the sheet music.

To start try to find a waltz with one chord above the measure. A waltz is in 3/4 time so each block will get a 1-2-3 strum. If there are measures with no chords over them stay on the same chord with the 1-2-3 until it changes again. You can begin there, your sturmming/picking will evolve as you listen and get more familiar with the different kinds of strumming/picking.
Hope I haven’t confused you.

Good Luck

Re: Guitar Chords and music……

I’d have to warn any beginning guitarists away from thinking that you can just look at the sheet music and transform the notes into chords by some sort of mechanical process. That tends to lead to all sorts of odd and not quite right stuff. Quite apart from the fact that the melody players can phrase things in ways that turn the implied chords on their heads - and the better the player, the more likely this is - and the fact that there is no such thing as a “phrase” on the page, the phrases only happen when someone plays the tune, there is also the simpler objection that there is a lot of ambiguity on the page that is resolved when the tune is played. For example, in the Wind the Shakes the Barley, you’ve got:
A2AB AFED | B2 BA Bcde |….

Is that second bar telling you B minor or G? If you listen, it’s pretty obvious, but if you’re trying to be clever, it’s not.

Or in Red Haired Boy, the strong high g that opens the second part - is that telling you to lean on a G chord? Or maybe an E minor? Or is it telling you to stay on the A and let the melody make the seventh? Or should you go completely mad and play a C? If you try to work from the paper, you have no real basis for knowing that the A7 sounds the best.

This is not to say that you shouldn’t try to work out what the chords MIGHT be by looking at the music, but you shouldn’t think that you know anything for sure until you listen to the tune and see what works. Your ear is ultimately the only guide, it will tell you what the harmonies are if you learn to listen to it.

Re: Guitar Chords and music……

“a chord is just the notes of a melody all stacked up, and a melody is just the notes of a chord spread out.”

True in part - but I would take out the word *just*. If you were to stack up *all* the notes in a melody, the result would probably be rather mushy and discordant - you need to learn to identify which notes are essential to the harmony and which are not (‘passing notes’). Often, the most effective approach is to play only the bare minimum of notes required to support the melody.

“I’d have to warn any beginning guitarists away from thinking that you can just look at the sheet music and transform the notes into chords by some sort of mechanical process.”

Yes. Conventional music theory certainly applies much of the time, but if you consistently follow the theoretical route, sooner or later you’ll come up with a chord that just won’t sound right - Ultimately, you have to rely on your ears and ‘feel’.

Major Seventh Chords

Major seventh chords are never needed for backing tunes in these modes:

1) Aolian (or natural minor)
2) Dorian
3) Mixolydian

Major seventh chords can sometimes be used for backing trad tunes that are set in major keys, but even then, they should be used sparingly.

Re: Guitar Chords and music……

TDM raises an interesting point. I think it depends on where you are in accompanying the music and what you bring to it. A beginner familiar with the language of modes will naturally assume that “dorian” means “avoid the flat six in all cases”, where a beginner coming from a background of simply accompanying songs, without a theoretical framework, will more likely think “minor means 1 and 7” and either use 6 chords or not, based on what their ear tells them and how well they listen. As either player learns the language of harmony specific to Irish tunes (ie, the habits of Irish melody) they’ll develop a more intuitive sense of where and when a 6 chord will fit, and whether it should be the flat or the natural.

The peculiar flexibilities of Irish tunes mean that referring to a tune as “dorian” or “minor” is often approximate - the same tune can be found with flat or natural sixths, on different recordings, or even in the same setting. For example, different settings of The Tap Room or Rolling in the Barrel will have c or c# or both, according to the melody player’s understanding of the tune and also according to their whim.
(Another reason to steer clear of the temptation to try to accompany a live tune based on a static sheet of paper)
