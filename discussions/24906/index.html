Re: Dots.

Now, let’s have a sensible answer:

On guitars, fret markings are typically found at the 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, and 12th fret positions.

The 12th fret has a special significance in that is is the half-way point between the nut and the bridge - the point at which you would obtain the first harmonic of the harmonic series of a given string. Which (on the 12th fret) would be one octave above the fundemental note of the string in question.

Re: Dots.

The provision of fret markers is obviously intended to provide a visual cue for the player, to divide their visual search space and make it easier to locate any particular fret more quickly by visual inspection. Human eyes can offset by small amounts without appreciable processing time - an offset of about 3 is easy, but an offset of 6 typically requires counting (exceptions are made after experience is gained, for example carpenters can spot lengths on a measuring tape very quickly). So if you break the twelve-fret span into clumps of no more than 3 frets, you’ve pretty much ensured that a player will be able to eyeball the correct spot on the neck without much delay.
If you’ve got a marker at the 7th fret and another at the 12th, this more or less requires something at the 9th or the 10th fret, and the choice is pretty arbitrary. I suspect that it’s convention. You have a choice of mimicking the pattern at the bottom end of the fretboard or mirroring it, and it really makes no functional difference. Guitars mirror it, mandolins mimic it.

The trouble I’m having with trying to relate the fret marker position to an absolute scale position is that the 9th fret is not “the major sixth” of anything in particular, only of the open note of the string you’re playing on at the moment. When you’re up at the 9th fret, hopefully you’re not calculating your note values relative to the open string! So the 9th fret might mark any note of the scale, depending on where your home note is and which string you’re referring to. Trying to tie it to one particular scale degree of one particular scale doesn’t relate at all to the way I think about playing guitar or mandolin.
Your mileage may vary, though.

@all *serious* contributors to this discussion!

I’ve done a little more research since yesterday - here’s a summary of my current thinking ….

1) On fretted instruments, the harmonic nodes occur at the 4th, 5th,7th,9th and 12th fret positions.

2) Some fretted instruments are not marked at all, but (on those that are marked) the 5th, 7th and 12th fret positions are always marked.

3) On most marked guitars, the 9th fret is marked (which corresponds to a harmonic node)

4) On most marked mandolins and tenor banjos, the 10th fret is marked (although this does NOT correspond to a harmonic node).

5) The 4th fret is NOT marked on marked guitars, mandolins or tenor banjos even though it DOES correspond with a harmonic node.

6) The 1st fret is marked on marked guitars, mandolins and tenor banjos even though it does NOT correspond with a harmonic node.

Do you all agree that I’ ve got my facts right?

- If I have NOT, please tell me where I’m wrong.

- If I have, a little more research to try to explain the anomolies might be interesting ….

Talking of harmonic modes……..
…..you can play an octave by lightly damping the string at the 12th fret ( this divides the string into 2 equal vibrating parts ) .
You can play an octave and 5th by lightly damping at the 7th fret ( this divides the string into 3 equal vibrating parts ).
You can play a double octave by damping the string at the 5th fret ( dividing into 4 parts ).
You can play 2 octaves and a 3rd by damping at the 4th fret OR the 10th fret ( dividing into 5 equal parts ).
Hence people tuning their guitars by octave harmonics, lower string at the 5th fret, higher string at the 7th fret, except 3rd to 2nd string,when you need to play 4th fret harmonic on the G string,and 5th fret harmonic on the B string.
