Alternating between whistles of different keys

I’m suffering from Instrument Acquisition Disorder (IAD). My first whistle was a Clarke Original high D last October. I enjoyed it so much that I bought a Syn Low D in November, and this month, January, I just purchased a new Goldie in Low F so I could play songs written in some flat keys. I love them all. The problem arises when reading sheet music and alternating between the Low D and Low F: the fingering for a “G” on the Low D is a “Bb” on the Low F; a “D” on the Low F is a “B” on the Low D, etc. It’s not a problem when playing by ear, but it is very confusing when reading music on two different whistles. Are there any recommendations on getting used to this more quickly, or does it just get better with practice? Any help from others who have overcome “IAD” is appreciated.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

Hello! As you say, it is reading music that causes the confusion. Most of us just treat every whistle as though it were in D. :) See this answer to a similar question:

http://www.rogermillington.com/siamsa/brosteve/ask.html#different

And because the whistle is basically a diatonic instrument, in many cases different keys of whistle are not capable of playing the same music. There isn’t much overlap of the easily accessible keys between an F whistle and a D whistle, and there would be even less between an Eb whistle and a D whistle.

So, if you must read music on different whistles, the easiest thing might be to transpose the music. By which I mean, for example, if you had to play in F major on your F whistle, transpose the music to D major - pick up your F whistle, disregard the fact that it’s not actually in D, and away you go.

Of course if you happened to have absolute pitch this would also drive you mad…

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

For Irish trad it would likely be better for you to just use a D whistle for playing with others, and learn your tunes using that fingering (except the odd tune which would benefit from a C whistle). When you play whistles in other keys, you can use the exact same fingering, and it will automatically transpose to a new key, but the tune will come out right.

You can play most Irish tunes on a G or A whistle in the “proper” key (the same as you’d play on a D whistle), albeit with different fingering. However, at this point it might be better to just use D whistle fingering on all of them.

What I’m saying won’t apply if you are playing in a group or ensemble that does not play Irish traditional music and you want to play in the same key as the other players, but otherwise, I wouldn’t worry too much about it, and just treat them as D whistles if you are mainly playing them solo or with other instruments in flatter/sharper pitches than normal.

Also, if you are playing ITM, it would be better to focus on ear-learning, and avoid written music when possible.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

Yes it’s customary for whistleplayers to read as if they’re playing a D whistle.

But this is a problem if you’re doing “legit” gigs (studio gigs, Church gigs, orchestra gigs, etc) where they’re sticking sheet music in front of you in various keys. If you can only read in the “D” position, you have to write out transpositions for yourself.

It’s an enormous advantage if you can sight transpose and therefore read sheet music in any key while playing a whistle in any key and have the notes all come out “concert”. I know people who can do that- I envy them.

I can only sight transpose a few keys. For one thing, transposing up or down a third or a fifth (jumping one or two lines, or spaces) isn’t that hard.

The other thing is that, due to me playing Highland pipes, I have a built-in transposer in that

xxx xxx can equally be D or A.

When I was playing Northumbrian pipes and Spanish pipes regularly I had more transpositions to hand, namely
xxx xxx could be G (NSP) or C (Gaita Gallega)
but now I’m too rusty on those transpositions.

So, at “legit” gigs I’m usually having to write out some of my parts.

When I saw the title of this thread I thought it was going to be about playability, about how, when switching between various whistles at a gig, the way the different whistles behave differently can bite you in the butt, if you don’t play them all often enough to have a good feel for the unique peccadillos of each one.

It’s why so many “pro” players will have a matching set in all the keys by the same maker, usually Goldie or Burke. You can just grab any whistle out of your roll and it will behave like all the other ones.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

As noted above, if you think in intervals it isn’t difficult. Because the whistle is effectively diatonic you can pretty much ignore the key signature. Look for the ‘key’ note of the whistle on the stave, which is all fingers down. Form there if the note moves up one position (line/space or vice versa) you simply lift one finger. Line to line or space to space, lift two fingers and so on.

I learned to do this being a player of the italian ocarina. As ocarinas have such a limited range being able to play them in a whole bunch of keys is required to play with others, and reading by interval makes it trivial to do.

This method works on any instrument, though it’s easier on those with a liner fingering.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

Yes, I think everyone who has answered understands the question, but you maybe haven’t grasped what they are telling you.

If you take your sheet music and transpose it up a 6th (or down a 3rd) and write it out, you now have the tune that was in F written in D. If you now take your F whistle and pretend it is a D whistle, and play the tune as you have written it out in D, what comes out of the whistle will be in F.

i.e. a D on the original score becomes a B on your transposed score, you play it xooooo, which gives you a D on the F whistle.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

“Transposing beforehand works, but it eliminates the possibility of spontaneous performance”

Well it sure eliminates the possibility of a spontaneous train-wreck, which will mean that the next time there’s a gig, they’ll call somebody else.

Having sheet music in front of you only effects spontaneity if you so choose. I’ve done hundreds of gigs where I’m improvising, with the sheet music right there on the stand. What the sheet music does is allow you to play the part as written when you choose. You can also choose to play anything else any time you want… the sheet music has no mystical powers over you. Who does have power over you is the person paying you- and he might want you to stick to your part. Or not! I’ve sat in the orchestra/band and have the conductor simply point to me and say “blow” (which in jazz-speak means to improvise something). Or just point at you, which means take a solo.

The best musicians I’ve worked with are fantastic sightreaders AND fantastic ear-players AND fantastic improvisers. Being good at one doesn’t diminish the others.

BTW one problem with using transposed parts, if you don’t have Perfect Pitch (which I certainly don’t), is that you might not be aware at all times of the Concert Pitch key you’re playing in. Many has been the time where I’ve had to glance at the music stand of the guy next to me to see the Concert key we’re in, due to me having a transposed part. Why does this matter? In rehearsals it’s because the conductor might say “OK let’s go to where it modulates to F” and on your transposition F is written as G (so you can play a C whistle). Or they might have handed you a part that’s in the wrong key, either because of the whistle you’re playing, or simply because the part is wrong- I’ve seen that on professionally published parts. Or the conductor might signal modulating up, and you might want to know from what key you’re modulating (though the easiest way is just to grab the next-higher whistle in your bag and keep playing just as you were).

Over the years, I have learned to read sheet music in different keys, transposing in my head on the fly. Playing concert pitch sheet music on a Bflat trumpet, that sort of thing. But that gets tricky, and makes you prone to mistakes. I would suggest, rather than doing that, you learn to transpose music, and write it out so that the same notes always trigger you to use the same fingering. For example, Bflat fife music is written so that the fingering is the same as with a D whistle. But when they play a tune that looks like it is written in D, they are playing in Bflat.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

I have whistles in most keys from Low D up to High G. At sessions and Comhaltas D, G, A and C are the useful ones (in that order). My selection of whistle is based on any challenges in the music:

a) low Cs or frequent F naturals can be played with C and G whistles

b) music that goes below the staff (low G/A/B/C) usually fits the Low G whistle - High D whistles play it up an octave which often means they are loud/dominate changing the timbre/tone which doesn’t always suit (NB slower pieces).

c) when too many high D whistles are being played I get out the Low D whistle to soften the whistle timbre and help my ear to hear what I’m playing amidst the whistling throng.

I use abc notation and an excellent app on my iPad (Tunebook) to organise, edit, transpose and publish music for myself and our Comhaltas group. I take it with me each week and when I can’t find the tune in my sheet music it will search and find it on the iPad.

I find that many tune settings can be improved (from a whistle player’s perspective) by moving the key to one where the fingering progressions flow more easily and then using an appropriate whistle to preserve the actual sounding pitch. This is what happens most easily with the G, A and C whistles.

Every so often a tune will be set in a key that the D whistle players can’t touch (perhaps E flat, A flat, B etc) and they sit out or play another instrument. This doesn’t happen often but is a chance to look at whistles in other pitches.

Generally my other whistles (B, B flat, E, E, E flat, F, C sharp) are for playing on their own or with guitar, keyboard, accordion accompaniment.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

“some of the Scottish tunes I find are written in flat keys. I’d like to be able to keep them in the original key.”

You’ll find that there’s no concept of that in traditional music, really. No such thing as “the original key”. (Traditional tunes have no known composer, and thus no way of establishing what the original key might have been.)

So if your Scottish tune is a Highland pipe tune, the “original key” yes would be three flats, Scottish pipes being (in effect) in E flat Major (or B flat Mixolydian if you prefer).

But if any Scottish piper happens to play the same tunes on Scottish Smallpipes in A, then the tunes will be in two sharps, in effect in D major (or A Mixolydian if you prefer). Or that same piper could play the tunes on Scottish Smallpipes in D, which play in one sharp, in effect G Major (or D Mixolydian if you prefer).

Any of those keys, flat keys or sharp keys, are the “original keys” and on whistle you can play those tunes with equal “correctness” in any of those keys, or in any key whatsoever.

I too do a lot of Church gigs and I’ve ended up with whistles in every key, because you never know what key something will be in. Also, each whistle plays two Major keys, and you might find you like one over the other due to how the tune “sits” on the whistle. So a particular tune in D Major might be playable both on a D and an A whistle, but you might like it better on the A. Tunes in F often work best on a C whistle, and so it goes.

Re: Alternating between whistles of different keys

I have played a lot of different instruments, my background is classical but I have played a lot of trad, jazz etc. I’ve played whistle for a few years now. Reading in D/G/etc. on a D whistle is the easiest for me, or reading in D and using the same fingering on a different whistle (I don’t have perfect pitch so it doesn’t bother me.) That’s because its the whistle I learned on and the key I read in most often. I don’t have a problem reading in other keys on other instruments but on the whistle my fingers remember where a certain note is and it can lead to playing a wrong note. (Eg. reading in Eb and you see a G, you might be likely to play an Ab as that’s where that note would be on a D whistle.) While trying to use the intervals as your main guide is a good tactic, I think it has limitations and dangers. It is of course a totally normal part of sight-reading but if you want total security I think there are better ways/extra securities you can give yourself.

For example if you are playing something that contains a lot of leaps or notes in isolation your interval recognition could let you down. Or if you are reading something that has accidentals. For me the best tactic is to remove the association of actual pitch from the holes of the whistle and instead use tonic solfa; doh, re, mee, etc or degrees of the scale. Then you can orientate yourself by the key signature. You know that G is the third degree of the scale of Eb so you play your Eb whistle with the bottom 2 holes open and you get a G.

While it can take a bit of getting used to, I think it is the best way. Using the interval method with trad tunes or simple melodies will probably serve you fine for the most part. Problems like what Richard said about not knowing what key you’re in wouldn’t be nice though.

The most obvious comparison would be to the recorder, as they come in different keys and the players would have the same potential issues. The recorder family is non-transposing, which means that sheet music for recorder is nearly always written in the key in which it is played. A written C in the score actually sounds as a C. The recorder has a more notation based tradition though with more chromatic music but I still think the example is the best way to go about it if you want to get really good a reading in different keys on tin whistle.
