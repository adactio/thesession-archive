Nature vs. Nurture.

I’ve wanted to have this discussion for quite some time, due to several different reasons. One of those reasons is because it raises a very important question to me, as someone who lives to help others pursue musical and artistic development. Note: I will be sharing very personal information related to my own skills and abilities. This is not intended to be a boast, but rather, is relevant to how I relate to the question and the artists that I mentor and support. It is not my intention to insult, offend, or belittle anyone. With that being said…

…A couple of years ago, I took an Ancestry DNA test with 23andMe. While learning that I am over 80% Sub-Saharan African(mostly Nigerian), I was delightfully surprised to discover that I am also estimated to be over 14% Irish/British! Now, sure you have to take things like this with an entire tablespoon of salt, but conveniently for me, I have an uncle who also took the DNA test. His results were consistent with mine, his estimated Irish/British % being virtually double! It goes without saying that I was absolutely ecstatic when learning this.

I’m looking through my profile, and one of the first things 23andMe shows me is one of my genetic traits.

“Ability to Match Musical Pitch: Jerone, based on your genetics and other factors, you are more likely to be able to match a musical pitch.”. Now, at this point, I’m totally flabbergasted, but keep reading…. “If you hear a musical note, can you sing it back? While matching pitch comes naturally for some, it’s almost impossible for others. Either way, genetics plays a role in the ability to match a musical pitch.”. “Of people with genetic results like yours: 60% can match musical pitch. 40% cannot match musical pitch.”

Now, I’ve been a musician my entire life. There’s always been this hypothesis that things like this are somehow genetic, but with no clear scientific evidence. I mean, how do you even go about studying something like this? Well, apparently 23andMe has figured it out, because judging from their report, they’re not just making this up for our entertainment.

“For this analysis, more than 660,000 23andMe research participants of European descent contributed their genetic data and survey responses on the ability to match musical pitch.

We identified 529 genetic markers that were associated with the ability to match musical pitch. We used these markers together with non-genetic factors, specifically age and sex, to create a statistical model that predicts the chances of having the trait. The full statistical model has an AUC value of 0.58.

The statistical model predicts the chance of being able to match a musical pitch to be between 34% and 65%, depending on your genetics, age, and sex. The average 23andMe research participant has a 50% chance.”

For me, this raises a lot of questions about the general conversation of “Nature and Nurture”. We want to tell people that they can accomplish anything if they work hard, try, practice, believe in themselves, etc. but how much of that is really true? In the context of learning music, the simple ability to match musical pitch is the lowest-common-denominator or learning music by ear. It’s the foundation of all of ear training. An entire set of invaluable skills are developed from this one ability. And you’re telling me that I was born with it, and some people aren’t?

I mean sure, this doesn’t mean that I didn’t have to work. It certainly doesn’t mean that I didn’t have to practice. It doesn’t mean that everything has been easy. It doesn’t mean that I haven’t struggled. It took me over 11 years of study and practice, with all of the nurturing in the world, to even reach my first musical goal. But now, it would be dishonest to say that it’s not easier for me than it is for others. Even further, if it’s been difficult for me, how much more difficult must it be for those who aren’t advantaged? Those people who claim that music is difficult, that it doesn’t make sense, that they have no talent for it; This really could be a reality for them, or at the least, they feel this way. Who am I to tell them that it’s not?

My main question is, how do we support these people, in a world where we may actually be limited by our own make-up? I’ve met countless people who say that they’re bad at music. That no matter how hard they tried, they never got from 0-1. Some have said they have practiced for years, only to reach an unsatisfactory standard. In the end, it seems I could be encouraging someone to waste their life over something I believe in. That’s not something I’d ever want to do.

There is no doubt that nurturing has played an equally important role in my own development, but even still, all of that nurturing was a response to the natural ability. My grandmother bought me a piano because something told her I was musical. My school teachers would let me play their pianos in class, and as I got older that turned into free books, free music programs, access to special events and learning opportunities. I’ve been given an assortment of instruments, even as recently as just a couple of years ago. All in all, I consider myself unspeakably lucky, and I have an unending-gratitude for every last bit of it. But none of that helps someone who either is, or feels like, they are incapable.

I guess what I’m trying to say is, I’ve always played music to share the joys of music with others. I’ve always hoped to make music accessible for everyone. Is this something that I’m going to have to let go? For myself, the conversation of desire to play music always comes up. Some days it feels like just about everyone has at least tried at an instrument, and either regrets giving it up, or laments being incapable. Where do we take the conversation from here?

Re: Nature vs. Nurture.

I would say that two fundamental questions are:
(a) how much time and effort are you willing to put into the education of others who are less able than yourself, bearing in mind that this effort may well take away from your own musical development?
(b) If you hope to assist those who are musically challenged, do they actually have the motivation and willpower to profit from your help?

Let me give some examples from my own experience. As a teenager I could sing reasonably well and accompany myself on guitar, and could hum or whistle a lot of traditional tunes. I was interested in learning the fiddle, but everyone I talked to said it was too late and I should have started a decade previously. However, I did manage to teach myself tin whistle, and around the age of 30 got a chance to learn the fiddle thanks to the kindness of someone who lent me an instrument and show me the basics of fingering. It was a long struggle with months of no apparent progress, but eventually I was able to play well enough to join in sessions and even earn some money from the occasional gig; I went on to learn the pipes. My conclusion is that I had a reasonable aptitude (which may have been helped by the fact that two grandparents had played the fiddle) as well as motivation. The determination I had as a late learner more than made up for tuition that I could have had as a kid, but at the time probably would not have appreciated. I’ll never be a brilliant fiddler, but I can play to a standard that gives me pleasure.

By contrast, I have two acquaintances who enjoy singing, but their performance suffers from the fact that they have a poor sense of pitch (I suppose they are what might be known as tone-deaf). I’ve tried to accompany each of them on guitar, with little success. One of them cannot sing intervals correctly in a way that most people would recognise as the tune: the other is a bit better, but tends to drop the key by a semitone with each new verse of a song. They may want to improve their singing - and I suspect that learning a melody instrument might help - but I don’t have the time, inclination or skill to deal with their particular musical problem.

Re: Nature vs. Nurture.

My $.02 (and Happy New Year everyone!) First, I would suggest delving into the traditional music of India or China, if you think having Irish/British ancestry confers some special ability to match pitch that’s lacking in other genetic lines. Most humans have this ability, it’s not regional.

Second, I believe that what we call “musical talent” varies far more within genetic populations than between different ones, and likely on something like a bell curve. Most of us are in the fat middle part of the curve, we can learn to play music if we put in some effort. A few child prodigy musicians are on one extreme end of the curve, who may or may not develop that ability further, but they have to work less than the rest of us when they do.

And then there are a few poor souls who can’t hear pitch differences or keep time on the other end of the distribution curve. What we commonly call “tone deafness,” or referred to as “amusia” in the neurological literature. You might want to read up on that, it’s fascinating. I’ve met one or two people like this, who have struggled all their lives to play an instrument or song, working hard at it with outside instruction, and they just can’t do it.

Anyway, I’ve never seen any evidence that either amusia or prodigy-level musicians are more common in one world population vs. another.

Re: Nature vs. Nurture.

Jerone, let me put on my retired genetics professor hat for a moment.

“Markers” are small changes in DNA sequence that are mostly not in the parts of your DNA that carry genetic information. Because of this, any association of markers with a trait like musical ability is correlation, not causation. In other words, your markers also say that there is a 35% chance that you aren’t musically talented. From a DNA sequence point of view, there are no significant differences between the races. The markers from people of a given race are just as different from each other as they are from people of different races. So markers by themselves don’t say anything about origin.

Nature/nurture discussions are complicated that because most things are both nature and nurture. Someone can have the inborn aptitude for something but never find out because they are never in a situation to discover the aptitude. A given population can be good at hearing microtones not because they share similar markers, but because they speak a microtonal language

Showing my age, but all students who wanted to play in the band when I was in elementary school were administered a test by the band teacher. There were three parts, in which one had to say if two tones, two rhythms, or two melodies were the same or different. After scoring the tests, the band teacher then looked at each student and recommended an instrument, presumably so that the person with short arms didn’t play the trombone, or the tiny person play the tuba, but mostly so that he didn’t end up with a band with 20 flutes. When he got to my brother he said “for you, I recommend art”. Not very inclusive to be sure, but the wisdom of the time clearly recommended weeding out those without basic aptitude. On the plus side, it probably made for better bands and prevented frustration for students with no aptitude, but it would select against those who weren’t used to listening to music. Oh yes, that brother ended up getting in to the Music School at the best university near our parents’ home-he was good at music but was terrible at taking tests….

As a professor, every year at the end of term after the final exam, someone would come to my office. Typically, if they were a good student, they complained about their grade and hoped that I would raise it. If they were an average student who barely passed, but passed, they were the ones who thanked me profusely for not giving up on them, for helping them and putting in the time with them out of class. So that makes me sympathetic to giving folks a chance even if they have limited ability.

On the other hand, I went into biology because I was no good at math. I nevertheless had to take an advanced calculus course that I failed by 2 points. I went along to the professor at the end of term basically to beg, and he said “I will pass you on one condition, namely that you promise never to take another math course”. Naturally I promised I would never darken the halls of the Math Department ever again. So there are many ways of being kind…..

End of day, I let anyone into my class who wanted to give it a try, whether they had the prerequisites or not, or what their grades were. Some succeeded, some didn’t, but I gave them a chance.

One more true story. Towards the end of my career, I got an email saying something like “Hi Dr Brock, you probably don’t remember me but I was in your Biology xxx class in 1987. I don’t remember anything I learned in your class, but I did learn one thing-that whatever I do, I need to be as passionate about it as you were about genetics. PS, I am now a professor at Harvard”. So you never know what people are learning, which I think is another reason to give people a chance.

That said I had no patience with those who wouldn’t put in any effort, but if that were doing their best, I gave them my best as well.

This is a fascinating topic and discussion. I did 23 and me, and they told me I was a descendent of an Irish king, Niall of the Nine Hostages. Well, I thought, that will explain the Irish music obsession despite no immediate Irish ancestry. Strange thing was it was via my Dad, who is 100% Spanish.

Unfortuantely the 23andme statistics, dsepite what they say, they are designed more to entertain, the power of the models is often very very weak - in my opinion some of it is a bit like astrology in that they are relying on confirmation bias. In my sample they got a lot right, but also a lot wrong!

>We identified 529 genetic markers that were associated with the ability to match musical pitch. We used these markers together with non-genetic factors, specifically age and sex, to create a statistical model that predicts the chances of having the trait. The full statistical model has an AUC value of 0.58.

An AUC (area under curve) of 0.58 is very weak, meaning that the strength of the model is about as good as a coin toss. Also, I can think of a few reasons why there could also be underlying factors unrelated to anything specifically musical driving this too, the model does not seem to control for stuff like general intelligence . Weak models like these can often be driven by unseen factors and ‘non linearities’. If you have 660,000 people and just 1% of them have various genetic conditions that basically prevents them from doing many things, music pitch ability being just one of them, the model will be significant, but basically nothing actually to do with music. For the other 99% of people it could be just random - and in this case I don’t believe they control for the vast variety of phenotypes that could bias or influence music ability, so I would place a sizable bet that the model is a case of ‘fooled by randomness’. Neverthless I would have felt a bit sad if it had told me that I didn’t have good pitch ability, and was happy that it told me I did :)

I personally don’t think that there is currently any convincing evidence that the genetics of a ‘normal’ person will be able to significantly prevent the chance to gain a ‘normal’ decent level of musical ability given the right upbringing. I suspect that the language comparison is very apt, and evidence is there that if you have no musical training or exposure as a chid then it will be ‘as if’ you don’t have the ability genetically, in that you can’t get back to the same potential you had as a child.

I’m not sure how to answer the moral question of how to encourage someone who you suspect is never going to be ‘good’ at something. Honesty is great, but in this case, being honest about what you think of their aspiration is risky as you could turn out to be wrong! But the other side of the coin has consequences too - I have a close friend who did 2 years full time course in singing after college, but cannot sing in tune at all (really), and despite it being his dream to be a singer, it was a lot of time he could have spent studying something else. I still don’t understand why a teacher never sat him down and said ‘look there is a really big issue with your intonation, before we move further, we have to get it right’. So perhaps this is the key, to focus on the next most important problem to solve. Which for most people I know seems to be being able to sit still and practice for more than 5 minutes before giving up :’)

I did the 23andMe thing too. It was interesting, but as commenters have said, the headline ‘percentage likelihoods’ don’t have much statistical significance. Two things struck me about the ‘musical aptitude’ features, whether from this source or from more targeted and replicable testing of individuals:

Rudall: “1. If this measure is about ability to recognise the absolute pitch of a note, does this matter much once you’ve started a tune? In other words, don’t we learn and play tunes as a set of intervals from the original note, and if it’s all intervals, do we need an ability to recognise absolute pitch?”

I would adamantly argue that this is a very emphatic yes! The basic ability to recognize musical pitch means recognizing any individual pitch, no matter where it’s place is in the music; Even if that place is in a greater harmony/melody line. The ability to actually follow a melody line is a secondary skill, which is the ability to recognize whether or melody is moving up or down; followed by the ability to recognize how far up or down the pitch has traveled. This is a trial and error process and can not be performed without the original skill. Yes, it is true that we learn music in phrases, and that a core technique is using the first note as a reference. However, if you can’t recognize the notes that follow the first note, you cancel out the trial and error process because you have no way of knowing if you’re following the music correctly.

Re: Nature vs. Nurture.

I’m no geneticist, but I do know enough about statistics to be very careful when looking at large datasets.

What you have here is a sort of machine, into which you shovel five hundred items of information about your genetics, and turn the handle, and the machine tells you a prediction about you.

Now each on of those five hundred items can take on different values, so the total number of possible outcomes is vast: the number of atoms in the universe is tiny in comparison. So how can the model make a good prediction for a particular set of inputs? The answer is, it can’t. All it’s actually doing is reproducing the data it was trained on, a process called “overfitting”.

This is a common problem and there are all sorts of solutions, and I’ve simplified the process quite a bit, but at the end of the day 23andme have no incentive to *actually* get this right: all they want to do is give you something that sounds sensible and plausible. How will you ever know otherwise?

‘trad music in your mother’s belly, etc etc………………..’ what!!! My mother liked a bit of light classical music and played a wheezy electric organ very, very badly - my dad didn’t have a note of music in him. Hardly an auspicious start but somehow I got from Lonnie Donegan to Leadbelly, then from country blues to old timey Appalachian [with diversions into the Beatles, Stones and Kinks along the way] and then somehow to Martin Carthy and Dave Swarbrick, the first Irish trad I heard on the radio was Cathal McConnell and Robin Morton.No I didn’t think it was shite it was what I’d been waiting all my life for [probably about 14 years at the time]
