Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

Hey! A question I’m somewhat capable of answering. Not because I have great intonation, but because I have struggled mightily with it. To put it in context, I’m an adult learning, having played for three years now.

Some things have come easy to me (bow), some took work but focus and simple steps led to improvement (rhythm), but intonation has been a multi-headed dragon of challenge.

The one caveat I’ll say is that every body is different, including ears and mind. So something that works for one person may not work for another, and some may disagree with something I say and neither of us could be wrong (or right).

One thing I’ll say about intonation on fiddle is that it’s a really multi-faceted musical challenge. It’s as much about your ears (being able to tell when your sharp of flat) as your body (mechanically hitting the right spot) and is one of the first things to go when playing faster than you’re used to or learning new tunes.

One of the first things I tried was your thought having my tuner up on my fiddle. It would help when I was using it, I’d slowly start to correct myself but it never stuck. It really did nothing for me. I’ve read more since, and basically one of the problems is that it’s not helping your ears or bodies learn intonation, it’s reinforcing yet a new (and relatively unimportant to the music) sense, your eyes.

I’ve recently gained huge momentum on this over the past month and for the first time feel hopeful and confident that it won’t be a giant musical anchor drowning me in a musical sea of self loathing.

First, your ear. To have good intonation, I think requires you to being able to tell when you’re in tune and out of tune by ear. My ear was hyper-crap. Like, crappest of crap. I came into fiddle with zero musical experience, rarely listened to music, and have been accused of being tone-deaf.

Things to try: ear training, either intervallic or functional.

Intervallic ear training is doing exercises to be able to recognize the difference in pitch distances between two notes, so like a unison (same note) versus a major third (four half steps). Four half steps would be A and C#. As you learn to recognize these pieces, it becomes easier to hear proper intonation.

Functional is a little different (and I think generally recommended), where you learn the distance notes are from the root of a scale. So It’ll play a chord progression, and then a note, and you have to learn where that note is on a major scale.

I did a lot of ear training and it didn’t help me much as I struggled to progress. I even created a VR ear training app with a friend hoping that would help.

Perhaps I never stuck with it long enough or with enough consistency. Many people have found great success with them so it’s worth giving them a try as it’s worked for many people.

What did help me:

I switched last summer (so two years in) to learning tunes by ear. It was hellaciously slow going. Loop sections. I’d slow tunes down, try to sing with them. That’s mostly how I learn tunes. It’s helped my ear more than anything else I’ve done and while it takes a lot longer to “learn a tune” it is a lot faster to “playing a tune” for me.

That alone wasn’t enough, and while my ear is better, it’s going to take a long journey to get to great. That said, I feel confident by learning tunes by ear my ear will get better over time, and not stagnate or lock in mediocrity.

So here’s the next problem: body mechanics.

Someone recently posted the comment that musicians are athletes for small muscles, and it’s true. Playing music isn’t just a question of ear, but also body and subsequent body mechanics. And in this respect, the fiddle is not intuitive. There’s a lot of weird angles at play.

I started to pay attention to when my intonation started getting off and that led me to read more about hand frame. Hand frame is essentially how your left hand (or if you’re left handed your fingers on the strings hand) is held in relation to the fiddle. I watched loads of videos and realized that I had some big hand framing issues.

My hand was all over the place, and this means when I did certain things (play the high A on the E string) my whole hand would get off, and then some notes (G on the E string) would get sharp and stay that way or it would shift everything to be a bit too sharp or flat.

After watching loads of videos and making no progress, I met with a local violin teacher. She gave me some good pointers for my body, and some exercises. The pointers were basic stuff (have the pinky pad face you, rotate the left elbow towards your body). These helped a lot, especially around muscle efficiency, and when I was consistent I was a bit more consistent, but I would still get off like before.

Then I was recently in Ireland and noticed how many people played without a shoulder rest. I started asking questions, watched some videos on how to do it, and decided to give it a go. Removing the shoulder rest took some effort, and there’s some risks around holding tension, but it’s made a HUGE difference for me. I don’t think it alone would’ve done it, but alongside all the other stuff was sort of the final breakthrough.

The quick summary of playing without a shoulder rest is you hold the instrument on your collarbone, and then use the neck rest as merely a… rest for your neck, You also have to hold the fiddle up with your left hand, kind of letting it sit a bit up on the V between your thumb and index finger.

Playing without a shoulder rest did two positive things for me (and one negative).

One, it significantly helps your ear, as you feel the vibrations through your body. And if your fiddle is tuned (always tune your fiddle after picking it up) you’ll feel the difference when you’re playing with good intonation as there’s a lot of vibration that’s helping you.

Two, it gives your hand two stable points of contact with the fiddle, regardless of what your fingers are doing. This aids significantly in proprioception (that is knowing where your body is in space) in relation to the fiddle. Sort of like a homerow on a keyboard.

The risk is that you can easily add too much tension to your left hand, but quite honestly, that’s always a challenge with instruments. If you are careful and watch for tension, you’ll find it points to other issues (elbow, wrist, finger angle) that if you work to stop, you’ll end up stronger. Like I found that my G on the E string caused me to pinch my thumb and index finger base together to make it work, and the act of figuring out how to not do that created a change that decreased tension, and thus helped with intonation.

The last two things that I did to help was using a mobile app called Intonia. It lets you record yourself and shows pitch. I don’t look at it when I’m playing, but will record myself and play it back, making note of when I was sharp or flat, and then making mental adjustments / awareness to make improvements. The last additional thing is I try to keep my eyes closed when practicing intonation. This removes that sense and makes it an aural experience.

I now have added things like practicing first finger whole steps, or working on third finger double stops (so the D on the A and the open D) etc. It’s finally becoming a fun thing to practice, and the tunes all sound better!

Your mileage may vary on all of this but hopefully there’s nuggets of help. I’m excited to see what other people say. Learning music is very personal so what works for one may not work for others.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

Let me be the first to point out the obvious problem: If you try to improve your intonation by playing with one eye on the electronic tuner, you’re going to run into big problems, because the intonation that sounds correct to your ear will often be flagged as “way off” by the tuner. The problem is the equal-tempered scale. As a fiddler we want the intervals to sound “sweet” and to “ring” correctly, and that means we sometimes need to deviate from equal temperament.

There is an iOS app called Music Wrench which will do exactly what you are suggesting: you play a tune, and it shows you a piano-roll version of what you played, with each note color-coded according to whether you were sharp or flat. But I don’t use that function, because equal temperament makes it misleading. In theory you could go into the settings and tell the app to use a specific just-tempered scale, but I haven’t really tried doing that.

I do use tuners for tuning up/periodic retuning (Music Wrench and Elegantune are both good apps for that), even though in my early lessons, I was trained to tune by ear to a reference pitch. I’m trying to get the open strings to be precisely in tune within the equal-tempered scale (so that they are equally close to being “in tune” for all keys), and that’s hard to do by ear alone.

I also use a lot of drones or reference pitches when I practice (especially when warming up). The drone can be provided externally through a keyboard or something, or it can consist of an open string (make sure your fiddle is really well-tuned first, though!) I feel like that helps.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

I prefer to tune my open strings in just temperament (in fifths, by ear)—the fiddle is more resonant tuned this way than if tuned to equal temperament. When playing with an equal tempered instrument (piano, say), it’s standard practice to adjust your fingering as needed.

Most digital tuners are set to equal temperament, so they’re “off” of just temperament. (I don’t use a tuner, so have no idea if some digital tuners can be set to just temperament; if so, that would be more useful for fiddlers.)

Regardless, on the fly, you have to use your *ears* to play in tune, so it only makes sense to train your ears to hear intonation accurately. The sooner you start, the sooner you’ll be able to do this.

You can hone your intonation by checking finger placement against open strings. Or you can use a YouTube video of a drone note to play against (for the whole tune, or more specifically for the particular phrase you’re practicing). Far better than watching a tuner react to every note, using a drone note encourages you to use your ears to hear in-tune intervals. Developing accurate intonation is all about learning to listen more closely to pitch, intervals, overtones, and “beats” (or absence of beats) to hear when a note is really in tune.

A good demonstration of beats (starting at 1:15): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUHWAy0_K6Y

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

This is such a rabbit-hole, and everyone’s journey through it is different. On using a tuner, I think if you’re struggling to recognise and name notes you play, using a tuner as a crutch to find the right place could be helpful, but it’s definitely not a long term practice solution for all the many reasons already rehearsed.

Every note that we play has a role and if our ears don’t understand that role, we can never place it correctly. For that reason, I think the very first thing to do is to gain confidence in what is called audiation, the ability to hear a pitch before sounding it, or to recognise it in context. Vechy mentioned the concept of functional ear training. Functional ear training does the same thing that learning to sing confidently does, from different directions. I’m a fan of an app called Functional Ear Trainer, but I’d also recommend getting confident with the basics of singing in old-fashioned solfège. A book of old hymn tunes and a pencil and a toy keyboard will gradually get you going in the right direction, along with 5-10 minutes on the app a day.

On the violin itself, learn to measure the first and third fingers: third finger same as the string below, first finger forming a strong chord with the string below. Most notes can be checked against the string above or below but these two will ensure your hand is in the right place and are the easiest to hear. You can also use your fourth finger against the string above. Not all fiddlers use that fourth finger but it can be mighty handy here and there, and it’s essential in some styles.

Spend some time playing the simple one octave scales of D, G, and A against drones - the website dronetonetool.com is really handy for this. When you’re confident with those, extend them out to two octave scales, and also play the arpeggios, all with drones. Also try a few simple slow tunes with drones as well. Every note has a “centre”; try and find it on every single note and make it sing.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

Have I got this right: you are all hoping that the tuner will teach you where to put your fingers down? If so, well…

Sure, once you are proficient on the fiddle, you do know pretty well where to put your fingers down. But your ear is constantly telling you when adjustments are needed - and they often are, e.g. if a string is slightly out of tune, or if you want to blend with the uilleann piper sitting next to you (as fiddlers we can leave string quartets out of our reckoning), or if you are playing someone else’s fiddle which has a tiny difference in scale length. In other words, yes there is muscle memory, but that must always be subservient to the ear, every second that you are playing. If you can play in tune on your own fiddle, you should be able to pick up a big viola or a child’s ¼-size fiddle and play in tune within a few seconds.

This, one hundred times this.

The fiddle is a glorious, wonderful instrument. But if two are gathered together, and one can’t adjust to the other, they will be out of tune. Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

Very good posts from Calum and Stiamh.

Milo, yes, tuning a fiddle to a reference note (e.g., A=440) and then adjacent strings in fifths really does let the fiddle sing.

FWIW, at most sessions I attend, I tune to the dominant fixed pitch instrument (if there is one). Sometimes that’s a concertina, sometimes a button box. Even if that instrument is tuned to equal temperament, I’ll tune just my A string (or sometimes my D string) to their A (or D), and then tune my other strings to fifths. My fingers then make all other adjustments as needed.

I sometimes play with a box player who’s D is slightly sharp, even taking ET into account. So I tweak my open D string slightly north to make it less discordant. We laughingly refer to this as collective wet tuning. ;-)

Another point (alluded to by Stiamh) that has been mentioned on many other threads—the notes on fiddle are not fixed in place. They land in slightly different spots when playing in different keys. For instance, your first finger B on the A string is in one place for G major and slightly sharper when playing in E major. Your finger must land a hair up the string to be in tune in E major, but it’s your ears (and the auditory centers in your brain) that tell it where to go. As Stiamh says, all this is part of the learning curve for playing fiddle, but in time it becomes second nature.

Fiddles (and flutes and pipes) are a challenging animal. You may not make music right away on concertina, box, banjo, or mandolin, but given a properly set up instrument you should be able to get good intonation and genuinely musical tone on the very first day. The same will likely take months or even years (to attain consistency) on fiddle, even for “natural” musicians.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

“Tune the A to 440hz and then play open string double stops and adjust as necessary?”

Yes. Teach your ear to hear that perfect fifth. When they’re truly in tune, the two strings together won’t have any discordance. But more than that, they’ll produce an “extra” resonance, the sum being greater than the parts. In large part that’s because their overtones overlap—for example, an A note is a strong overtone of the tonic D. So when the A and D strings are spot on in tune, the A perfectly fits into the D’s overtone A vibrations. Each pair of open strings works this way.

↳ Posted by gimpy on June 26th, 2023.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

I’m sure Calum will get back to you, but the gist of it is this:

Place your ring finger on the A note on the E string. When it’s in precisely the right spot, it will be an octave above the open A string below. Play the two strings simultaneously and they’ll sing together.

The same holds true when your ring finger holds the A string D note (and play open D string with it) or the D string G note (and play open G string with it).

You’ll hear a rich harmony if you do the same exercise with your first finger on one string, playing it and the string below together. If your first finger is slightly flat or sharp, it will sound discordant or jarring. When it’s precisely in the right place, the two strings sound pleasing together.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

“How do those of you who tune by ear do so? Tune the A to 440hz and then play open string double stops and adjust as necessary?”

Yes, just that. Tune the A string, then detune the D string slightly, and slowly bring it up to pitch, while playing together with the the A string. Commonly known as a “rolling 5th”. Then repeat for the other strings.

So far you’re hearing and aiming for the “perfect” 5th. Another check is to play the open A along with the D string at the first harmonic (12th fret), so you hear a 4th. I think of it as an “insurance” :) Then repeat with the other strings.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

The tuner can be helpful, especially if you’re in a place where there’s extraneous noise, like a session. But your ear is the most important thing because that directs your intonation - literally note for note. When I’m playing I like to check at certain points to see if I’m OK. For instance, If I’m playing a G note, I listen for a ringing of the fiddle that corresponds with the open G string. Same for a D note, which will cause the D string to resonate.

Same goes for the A and E strings, although these are less likely to show up in typically Irish tunes. But once you start listening, you kind of automatically hear the ringing, which tells you that whatever you’re playing is pretty much in tune.

So, again, your ear is the most important thing. Tuners are good for noisy situations, but your ear is key for really fine intonation.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

Seperatio, yes, there are fretted violins, sometimes used to mimic (and exceed) the sounds an electric guitar can produce, useful for rock and jazz.

Most experienced fiddlers feel that being fretless is an advantage, not an impediment. It certainly allows for great freedom of expression—lovely little smears, big swoops and slides, and microtonal use of intonation.

Some beginners use decals (sold for this purpose) to mark “frets” on the fingerboard to guide finger placement. In my experience as a mentor, those decals actually slow progress for most people. I’ve helped players as young as six and as old as sixty who quickly developed decent, workable intonation without any such crutches.

Fiddles have a very short scale length. Newcomers to fiddle should bear in mind that this means (1) finger placement of just an eyelash north or south means being off pitch, and (2) to get on pitch often means adjusting your finger by just an eyelash up or down. Often, you can get spot on just by barely rolling the tip of your finger forward or backward (rather than sliding up or down at all).

Ultimately, playing in tune is much more about using your ears. Your fingers will follow.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

Aldon, the point is that concert violinists (and any decent fiddler) can readily land accurately on pitch without needing to slide into it. This certainly holds true for first position (which is where 99.9 percent of trad tunes land).

Here a violin teacher explains aiming for 100 percent accuracy (and the bad habit of sliding to correct pitch): https://violinist.com/blog/laurie/20188/27424/

That said, even the most precise players do sometimes make micro-adjustments. Often all that’s needed is not a slide but an infinitesimal rocking of the finger tip forward or backward. And large shifts into other positions are often done by sliding into the note.

But neither of these applies much to playing jigs and reels at dance tempos. At the pace we typically play, you’re either “on” the desired pitch for a millisecond or you’re not, and you’re immediately on to the next pitch.

I find myself making micro-adjustments in two particular situations: to match another instrument’s tuning (often pipes, whistle, or flute, but also when playing with an ET-tuned piano), and when high humidity leads to sticky fingering.

One of the big advances towards the violin, historically, was the ditching of frets. At one time they were bits of string or wire tied onto your instrument, but players soon discovered they had to keep moving them around to play in tune in different keys, and at some point some frustrated proto-fiddler ripped the whole lot off and trusted his fingers instead.

The thing about using frets is that they might help a struggling player, marginally, but at the expense of giving them the opportunity to train their intonation.

Certainly you can, but once you can do this consistently, the aim is to hit the note exactly without sliding. Then you can choose to use it as an ornament, rather than having no control of it.

Question about tuning fiddles using beats and tuning compared to other instruments, both equal temperament and those, like Uilleann pipes which will use just intonation based on a root of D for concert pitched instruments.

If you tune from the G-string up using a zero beat system, then you’ll be 2 cents sharp of equal temperament on each string (just intonation fifth compared to equal temperament fifth). The offset would be additive, so by the time you get to the E-string it would be something like 6 cents sharp of equal temperament.

So, to blend well with other instruments, do fiddlers, if they are using a tuner, generally at least tune their A to equal temperament, and then tune the other strings to be zero-beat consonant?

Just curious. In real life my experience is that some fiddlers use a on-body tuner for all the strings and as a result tune to equal temperament, and others who get a solid reference A from a concertina or pipes, and then do the rest by ear, with all kinds of variations in-between.

In my experience, fiddlers who are “pipes aware” even if tuned in equal temperament, will shade their pitch as required to better blend with the pipes in just intonation, most evident on the major 3rds and 6th intervals.

Re: Using a tuner to help with intonation on the fiddle

banjo76543, hearing the beats does take some practice and perseverance. Part of the challenge is that the beats are fast (think “wawawawawa”) when the notes are well out of tune, but the beats get a lot slower (waaaahwaaaahwaaaahwaaaah) as you bring one string closer in tune to the other. Most people have to train their ears to hear really slow beats.

When the strings are truly in tune, the beats stop—there’s just a wonderfully smooth, even tone. After you learn to hear the beats, over time, you won’t lose that ability, but you’ll likely end up listening mostly for that smooth, even sound, and then you know you’re in tune.

@Flinty «Just this morning I was frustrated as I was sure i had the high D singing nicely with the open D on the 3rd string, but my tuner said it was slightly too sharp. Would this be because of temperament and perfect 5ths not matching exactly? I need to learn to trust my ear!»

This is a bit of a puzzle. First thing to note is that in equal temperament, while fifths are not a pure interval, octaves are. So whether you are concerned about playing in ET or not, your octaves have to be spot on - 2:1 frequency ratio, no beats.

But an ET fifth is a slightly narrower interval than a pure fifth. So, if you tuned your A string to 440 Hz and then tuned your D string to it, using the time-honoured non-tuner method of perfect fifths with no beats, theoretically your ET tuner ought to indicate that your D string was ever-so-slightly _flat_, if anything, not sharp. So if you got your d on the A string perfectly in tune with your open D string, that note ought to be flat of ET too.

If on the other hand you tuned your D string to your ET tuner, and got your d on the A string perfectly in tune with it, your tuner should be happy.

I’m not sure what the practical lesson to draw from this theoretical analysis is (assuming my theory is correct). I’ve only ever tuned my strings to perfect fifths and I have never checked my intonation with a tuner. (And yes I (generally) play very nicely in tune with myself and other instruments, fixed-pitch or otherwise.) So for me, ditching the tuner and trusting your ears would be the way to go, but it’s up to you!

No reason not to have fine tuners on every string - except preference. If you don’t have them, your pegs need to be working very well. If they are working well - turn easily and stay put - then tuning the way a classical player would is very fast and accurate. This is a matter of bowing the strings and turning the pegs at the same time, listening to the beats. Less easy to do with fine tuners. But if, like those of 95% of fiddlers, your pegs are not working properly, tuning that way with the pegs is a big pain. My advice is to get your pegs fitted by a skilled luthier, maintain them as needed with various preparations to aid either slip or grip, and learn to wind the strings properly so that they butt up against the inside of the peg box, aiding grip.

@DChammers: There are many ways to play along to a drone. The basic idea is this:

- Find a drone on an app or on some online source. I tend to look for a clip on YouTube like one of these:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IhdTpG4KNfQ

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ygo0ZimLfsQ

And then you play your tune (say, a reel in D Major or D Dorian), paying attention to how your intonation melds with the drone. Obviously, notes of the same pitch or an octave should blend right in. 3rds and 5ths will make a nice dyad, while some notes will sound a bit jazzy against the drone. But if you think about the discordance as “tension,” even dissonant notes will have a specific relationship to the drone that sounds “right.” For example, a major 7th against the drone (e.g., a C sharp against a D drone) may sound jarring until you understand that it creates a very particular sense of tension that wants to resolve to the tonic drone.
