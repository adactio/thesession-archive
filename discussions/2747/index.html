Vibrato in Traditional Flute.

Opinions, I suspect, will be split on this one.

I’ve *read* recently that it says loudly you’re not a traditional player if you use diaphragm-pulsed vibrato, as it is derived from the classical idiom.

Yet I’ve *heard* some traditional players use it to great effect, not only on slow airs, etc., but on reels and other dance tunes. It can give real woody, emotional feel to a tune.

I personally have never really developed the knack, beyond basic dynamics alterations, but that doesn’t mean I don’t respect its exponents.

Any views?

Danny.

Re: Vibrato in Traditional Flute.

Danny’s subject is pretty clear - diaphragm-pulsed vibrato (AKA breath vibrato). Just a bit of reasoning here and then some examples. Don’t believe everything you read. I would love to get my hands on whatever it is that you read. I think I would find it rather amusing. Since the wooden flutes themselves were adapted from the so called classical idiom, don’t you think some techniques were as well? I’ve heard recordings of some of the older players (1950’s) using breath vibrato and finger vibrato. Matt Molloy uses breath vibrato often in his slow airs. Just listen to them. A trained ear knows the difference between breath vibrato and finger vibrato. Laurence Nugent, Gary Shannon, Grey Larsen, Cathal McConnell, Liam Kelly and Jimmy Noonan all use breath vibrato to name a few. These players also use finger vibrato. They are also generally labeled “traditional”. Remember that alot of the older players used flutes other than the simple system variety. Radcliffes, Clintons, and modified Boehms were used extensively if not recorded. Some of these flute systems are easier to execute breath vibrato rather than finger vibrato on. I would submit that the notion that using breath vibrato is not traditional, is just plain foolish and uneducated. If anything, it actually takes away from the traditions versatility as a complex folk medium by removing a valid and widely used technique for self-expression.

Re: Vibrato in Traditional Flute.

I’m not sure about flute players, but i know that some whistlers use breath vibrato. I think flute players do to. The difference is in the way it’s used. Modern flute players use vibrato all the time, it’s part of their “sound”. In traditional music, you use vibrato as an ornament, instead of leaving it on all the time. It takes a while to learn to play without constant vibrato, because it requires you to be much more accurate in your tuning, but i think it’s worth it. I use vibrato sparingly even when i play “classical” music on the modern flute.

The difference is much more stark with string instruments, because the vibrato has become so much part of the string sound. Some of the more contemporary groups, like the Chronos quartet, play without vibrato, and it sounds very stark.

My humble opinion is that the use of “always on” vibrato is a way to fudge intonation problems. It’s probably a necessity in a large orchestra, but not for solo or small group playing.
