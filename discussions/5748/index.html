What’s the problem?

I am from Baton Rouge Louisiana. I have been playing Celtic music for a while now. I have done some accompanying on organ for Kirking of the Tartans with the local pipe band here.

However, I cannot find anywhere to play with other musicians. I know that to find a session I am supposed to look in the Sessions section this website or look up other members.

However, I already looked at the sessions page and there aren’t any in my area. Three fourths of the members listings have no city, state, or country listed. In other words, their profiles are blank. I have yet to come across any from the Baton Rouge area.

I have mostly been playing along with recordings and that kind of thing. It just simply cannot replace playing in a live session with other musicians. My main instruments are the keyboard, guitar, Irish flute and pennywhistle, plus spoons and bones. However, I have recently picked up the English and Anglo concertina.

I had heard of two celtic groups who play in my area. However, I have contacted them about a zillion times and they don’t seem to be too interested in getting any new players. In other words, they are almost totally closed to anyone new coming in.

I finally went down to New Orleans for two different sessions held at various pubs there. It was even worse than Baton Rouge. There is an arrogance there that I find very distasteful. I am not a beginner. I am very well-trained on my main instruments and I am rapidly starting to master both English and Anglo concertina.

Am I asking in the wrong way? The main thing I want to do is just listen at the sessions and get an idea of tempos, etiquette, and that sort of thing, then perhaps join in. My hope is to form a band of my own.

I hate to say this, but I am finding that for the most part, the people I have met in my area are of the mindset that they are just fine the way they are and don’t want anyone or anything to interfere. They just don’t seem very open or very welcoming for that part.

I am also finding that this seems to be the state of many sessions in the U.S. What is it? I talked with a friend of mine from California who went over to Ireland for some workshops and he said the sessions are very welcoming for the most part and love to help musicians “learn the ropes”, so to speak.

Could someone enlighten me on this phenomenon, which I am starting to realize seems to be occurring mostly in the States and not over in the British Isles.

Re: What’s the problem?

It’s a funny thing, Stefan, this whole Irish session, what-d’you-call-it kind of thing. I remember talking to a rather famous Irish player once for a bit, early on when I first started playing this stuff, and he practically exploded when I asked about session etiquette. “There’s no such thing!” he cried. “That kind of thing is just to sell books and such!”

However, in talking with other players at my own level and other folks who are well experienced in Irish music and the culture (one person, when I told him the above story, stared at me incredulously, then started laughing and yelled, “bull sh*t! There’s nowhere half so concerned about etiquette as a session, not even a wedding!”), i started realizing that there IS an etiquette to sessions -- it’s just that it’s the etiquette and culture of Ireland, and so many Irish people don’t believe that there’s anything special to the things at all, because it’s just the way Ireland is, speaking in over-generalizations.

Added on top of that, of course, is the fact that sessions are not only often bits of Irish culture stuck on top of wherever the session is taking place, but every session is exactly what the inhabitants want to make of it, so it gets even more complicated. No wonder some people just give up on sessions!

So you went to two *different* sessions once each and decided the musicians in your area are arrogant, closed and unwelcoming? And all you had your heart set on doing was listening?

You might want to be more honest with yourself (and us) about what your expectations were. It sounds a little more like you were expecting to be greeted like family right off the bat based on your abilities (otherwise you’d have given each session more than one chance before deciding they’re a waste of your time).

I’ve said this before and I’ll say it again: You have to do your time. The people in the sessions you went to have probably been playing together for ages. They probably have dinner at each others houses and watch each others kids. A huge part of their purpose for being there has nothing whatsoever to do with music, so relying solely on your musicianship to win them over (and giving them only one chance to be so impressed with it they swarm around you with open-ness and warmth) isn’t going to get you very far.

The point is, you might be very, very good, but they probably don’t care. Would you pull up a chair at just anybody’s table in just any pub anywhere and make yourself at home because you heard them telling jokes and you happen to know some yourself? No matter how funny your jokes are or how well delivered, they’re still going to be looking at you kind of funny for the way you just kinda… you know… plopped yourself down.

Then again, if you plop yourself down with those same people often enough, eventually they’ll get used to you and stop looking at you funny. Maybe even start to enjoy having you around. But these things take time.

Be patient.

Re: What’s the problem?

I do find that playing for Scottish country dancing and playing Irish traditional music aren’t the same thing. There are folks who are members of this site who do both, and some of us who play/have played for SCD who don’t begin to have the musical skills to actually do it in Scottish stylings, and have zero skills in playing Irish traditional tunes in Irish traditional ways, but find the site helpful in many ways. But the sessions listed here are heavily, and intended to be so, Irish traditional sessions. There are some that are not that focused, but it may be there are sessions or groups in the Baton Rouge area that are not listed here where you will find musicians interested in playing for SCD.

You might try your local contra dances if there are any, because at least in my area there is some overlap there, and it is certainly true that many members of this site play for contra dances. You might look for old-time or bluegrass musicians who might find it fun to play for dancing and learn the protocols and, as we call them, “road maps”, of playing for Scottish dances. But be aware that they, too, may not play tunes the way that, say, Alasdair Fraser plays them, who is, in my opinion, the quintessial SCD fiddler, tho they can play sets of tunes the right number of times thru and in a way that makes folks want to dance. It is also true that the tunes played in a SCD setting are not the same tunes played in an Irish or bluegrass or contra setting, tho again there is overlap. How much your SCD group is willing to be happy for the band to play some tunes they don’t recognize, and how much your musicians are willing to learn tunes outside their current musical culture, and how much those two attitudes overlap, will affect how successful your efforts can be at putting together live music for SCD.

The SCD groups here have had some live music for over twenty-five years, and the core musicians are dancers who play for the fun of it. That seems a good place to start, tho of course they will also want to dance. But it’s that group that expanded into more musicians who aren’t dancers and are happy to “just” provide music (an equally fun thing to do).

So, good luck on your search - but playing for SCD is a subsidiary interest for many musicians - but it only takes two or three! or one, if you’re Alasdair Fraser.

CJ

Isaiah wrote: “I have mostly been playing along with recordings and that kind of thing. It just simply cannot replace playing in a live session with other musicians. My main instruments are the keyboard, guitar, Irish flute and pennywhistle, plus spoons and bones. However, I have recently picked up the English and Anglo concertina.”

If you walked into our session with all these instruments and told us you’ve been playing along with recordings etc. -- it would probably scare us. If you came in without all those things and sat nearby listening because you wanted to learn the music -- we’d be as helpful as we could.

You also wrote: “I am also finding that this seems to be the state of many sessions in the U.S. What is it? I talked with a friend of mine from California who went over to Ireland for some workshops and he said the sessions are very welcoming for the most part and love to help musicians ”learn the ropes“, so to speak.”

I’m from California too, and I’ve traveled to Ireland many times. I think if you’re going to a festival with workshops and that sort of thing -- learning “the ropes” is part of the experience. If you go in the off-season to regular on-going sessions many times you’ll find that the folks playing are there to have a good time and enjoy playing tunes together -- and they wouldn’t be interested in changing their session into a learning experience when beginners show up. You have to realize there are different types of sessions anywhere you go, and you need to find the right one for you. If you’re a beginner, then a “slow session” is probably your best bet. But don’t go to any session and expect it to become a “slow session” when you show up.

I don’t want to discourage you, but you have to go into this without assumptions that might alienate you from the people who already play the music.

Slowplayer, I hear you saying that a session should change its pace to accommodate every newcomer who pulls up a chair. But some newcomers will want to slow down, while others might want to speed up. What then?

I’m usually the first to invite a newcomer to start a tune at my own sesh, and I’ll play along at whatever tempo they start. So I agree it would’ve been nice of the Mulligan’s crew to pause and ask you for a tune. But it’s not their obligation to do so.

What’s “fast” to one player may be “moderate” to someone else, and “racing impossibly” to yet another. When I visit an unfamiliar session, I don’t expect to be invited in, no more than I would expect the locals at a strange pub to stop their conversation the minute I walk in the door and make me the center of attention. Regular sessions exist for the pleasure of the regular players, not to meet some visitor’s needs and expectations.

Of course, festival sessions and other impromptu sessions play by different “rules,” and a big part of the fun is feeling the group dynamic change and build as strangers migrate in and out, insinuating their various styles and preferences into the mix.

Re: What’s the problem?

Part of the issue here is the inherent tension between being “inclusive” and having “fun.” People have differing definitions for these words, different places to draw the line. What’s “fun” for one person may be another’s nightmare.

Most of the sessions I’ve been to seem to think of themselves as inclusive. But this means different things at different sessions.

One inclusive session might welcome any level of musical ability, as long as no one strays from playing strictly Irish trad tunes.

Another is inclusive because they play lots of other “Celtic” music, and even a bit of French Canadian or American old timey. But the players expect a high level of musicianship.

Another session plays tunes from all genres and welcomes beginners on up. But light up a smoke and you’ll be shown the door.

For some, “fun” is mainly the social interaction, the craic, or flrting with the saucy fluter across the table.

For others, “fun” is ricocheting through tunes non-stop, finding music inside you that you didn’t know you had.

For some, “fun” is meeting new people over a few pints. For others, “fun” is relaxing in the company of old, familiar friends without distractions.

Obviously, if you mix these definitions up in certain ways, some senses of inclusivity can hamper some senses of fun, and vice versa.

It’s not unusual for session players to come across as snobby simply because they want to play in a way that allows (1) a few people to play a common repertoire (Irish trad tunes), (2) at danceable speeds (no dirge tempos), (3) with players who can add nuance and lift and life to the music, and (4) be able to hear it. For many of us, this is a big part (not the only part, but a critical element nonetheless) of what session “fun” is. Because we’re having fun, and as social animals we’d like to share that fun, we try to be inclusive. But when being inclusive leads to (1) a downpour of tunes from other genres, (2) reels that crawl, or a glut of unimaginatively played slow airs, (3) bland noise from inexperienced players, or (4) a wall of sound from too many backers, inapropriately loud instruments, or the sheer number of players overall, then either the session will want to grow less inclusive, change it’s definition of fun, or suffer.

Isaiah, you need to read the story of Bodhidharma and Huike . Irish culture is Buddhist, and this colors everything they do. There are rules to how the game is played, and, I’m afraid, as a noncitizen visting Rome it is you who must learn to act like a Roman if you hope to attain citizenship.

You can’t just wander down to the county clerk’s office and fill out a membership card.

Unlike the jazz or American traditional “jam” where any old axe murderer who happens by is welcome if they have some chops (or sometimes even if they don’t) the Irish session is an issue of *kinship.* Desiring to join a session is like desiring to become a friend of the family, or, in some cases, an actual family member, or maybe a disciple of one. It’s about culture, not music. Music just happens to be part of the culture. You might well be the best musician in the world and be rejected by punters, or the worst musician in the world and be accepted by the best if you’re a good hang. The “T” in “ITM” has meaning.

So you may well just need to put in your time sitting outside the gate, becoming a familiar sight to the neighborhood, before someone, someday, looks at you and says “Hey, you look a bit cold and tired, wanna come in and have a bit of porridge by the fire?”

And you’ll have gone from ignored bit of rubble by the roadside to family member is one swell foop, just because that’s the way it works in that culture.

Of course you’ll still be the *new* family member for awhile.

I find your comment on groups interesting though. Groups aren’t typically something you solicit to join. They solicit you. All you can do is position yourself to be among those solicited the next time they lose a flute player. Until then, well, “I told him we already got one.” This has nothing to do with ITM. This about groups.

If you found your role as an organist by soliciting and being accepted I’d hazard a guess that at the time they *didn’t have one,* but found the idea inherently desirable.

I’ll second that your distinction between musicians and players is “rather strange.” A player is someone who plays, and a musician is someone who has put a considerable amount of time into studying the art, craft and theory of music. A Real Musician (tm) is someone so at home with their instrument, art , craft and theory that music, generically, in any style, is so much an innate part of them that it’s as natural as taking a p*ss.

I’m just a player. I’m working on becoming a musician. I despair of ever becoming a Real Musician (tm).

What you describe is the difference between a “jerk” and a “nice guy.” Plenty of musicians are jerks. Plenty of players are nice guys. Then there’s Mark O’Connor.

All of the above said I’ve never had any trouble with being ignored at sessions, or even at concert settings. Much the reverse. I’ve been pulled out of the audience to participate (by strangers) when all I wanted to do was to go out and have a quiet little listen. The fact that I am *reticent* to do so only seems to make them push all the harder to get me to join in. I think there might be a clue buried in that for you. After you read up on Huike you might want to check out Newton’s Laws of Motion.

And I’ve never met a player/musician yet you couldn’t get some sort of a chat out of, sometimes even while they’re on stage, if you have something legitimate to chat with them about, and do so with appropriate timing.

In the manner of such books as “How to Pick Up Girls” (I’ve always found that “Hi” is a dandy opening line. Some people feel “Hi, I’m (your name here) is better, but I always just get funny looks with that one. Maybe it’s the way I pronounce ”parenthesis") I’ll offer this suggestion, position yourself as an obviously appreciative *audience* member, and when there’s some sort of apppopriate break in the proceeding aim at your target and say:

“Man, I really like your style, and the tone of the thing is fantastic, what is it?” With feeling, like you just squashed a cop.

At the very *least* you’ll find out what “it” is.

And will have ceased being a complete nonentity.

Here’s another suggestion. Instead of complaining that you can’t get invited in because they aren’t inviting, be inviting yourself. Why are you so “hep” to join someone else’s family and feeling rejected when you can’t, when it’s perfectly possible to just start a family of your own?

It seems to me that if you yourself were as inviting toward others as you wish others would be toward you you’d already be the “Grand Old Man” of your own session and you wouldn’t be here looking for a place to participate in the first place.

Find a pub in your own neighborhood. Ask them if they mind if you just sit in the bacl corner and play quietly for bit. Things should progress nicely from there, if you’re a “musician.”

If I were in Baton Rouge I would likely be happy to join you if you asked when I gave you my “opening line,” but I’d fully expect *you* to do the asking.
