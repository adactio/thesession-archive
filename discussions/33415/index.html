Re: Good guitars for playing trad music

Joe fidkid asked some very pertinent questions.
I would ask another; do you think having a particular brand name on the headstock means you have a good guitar ? My answer would be; not always. The brand is a guide, but not a guarantee, to the qualities of any instrument.
I am lucky enough to own two old American guitars; the smaller is good for old-timey sessions, fingerpicking, song accompaniment; the larger is good for plenty of bottom/mid in sessions and band work, without decrying its treble-end response.
I would suggest taking someone more experienced with you when you start looking; play the instruments yourself, to see how they feel and sound to you, but always listen to how they sound being played by someone else, i.e. how the audience will hear them. You might find, after trying the top names, that there is an instrument that really appeals to you further down the price range. While there is the argument that you should always aim for the best, it is personal satisfaction that is the most important thing in the end.
Good luck and keep searching !

There is not a specifically “good guitar” for Irish music. I would go for an all round instrument suitable for plectrum and fingerstyle playing.

Re: Good guitars for playing trad music

“nice brands of guitars made preferable for playing irish music.”
You are approaching the problem from the wrong end - the nationality of the music is irrelevant, it is your personal playing style that counts. And the name on the label isn’t a guide to anything - all makers offer a range of guitar styles, some of which will suit you, some won’t.

The first thing to consider is the body size/shape. Over the years different guitar shapes have developed to suit specific styles of playing. Obviously these aren’t clear cut rules, there is a lot of overlap and blurring of boundaries, but it should point you in the right direction.

Jumbo - designed for Country clawhammer with fingerpicks - too ‘boomy’ for most other styles.

Dreadnaught - designed for flatpicking and strumming - great for playing hard, but lacks clarity when played quietly. Pretty much standard issue for pop/rock and strumming singer-songwriters.

OM - designed for fingerstyle. Great projection, clarity and dynamic range. This is a good all rounder, and the choice of many (but by no means all) traditional musicians.

Parlour guitars - the original style that all the others evolved from. This is a real “player’s guitar”. The small body makes it very comfortable to play and it has a bright, clean sound. Almost forgotten for 50 years because it can’t compete with other styles on volume, but it has recently made a comeback because volume doesn’t matter if you are using amplification, and it is less prone to feedback than larger guitars.

Next consideration is the soundboard.

Cedar has great attack, and is exceptionally clear at low volume, which is why it is widely used on classical guitars. But it overloads and sounds muddy if you try to play too loud. Don’t even think about cedar if you use any sort of pick. It is also very soft - even a stray fingernail will leave a dent.

Sitka Spruce. 90% of (non-plywood) guitars made today have Sitka soundboards. It handles loud playing very well, but is not quite so responsive to gentle playing. It is also physically stronger and less prone to splitting than other woods.

Engelmann or German Spruce (depending on whether the guitar is made in the US or Europe). Different species, but the acoustic properties are almost the same for both. These give very good attack and clarity, and have a wide dynamic range - not quite as good as Sitka at the loud end, but much better for the quiet stuff.

Mahogany. The only reason Martin made mahogany fronted guitars was to give cowboys something robust enough to throw over their shoulders while they rode across the prairies. It has no acoustic virtues, and behaves very much like plywood due to its interlocked grain. Mahogany has made a bit of a comeback recently, because when you play through a pickup the acoustics of the instrument don’t matter much, but the only reason for choosing mahogany is because you like the look.

When it comes to the back and sides the wood does make a difference, but not nearly as much as most people think. With solid wood back and sides, hard woods like rosewood give a brighter, crisper sound, softer woods like mahogany give a warmer, fluffier sound. But most volume manufacturers these days use laminated backs and sides, even on quite high spec instruments. There is nothing wrong with that, just go for the one that looks nice.

All that is just rough guidance, and should only be used to decide which guitars to try first. As everyone else has said, you need to play lots and just pick the one you fall in love with. Good luck!

Re: Good guitars for playing trad music

You were originally looking for guidance on good guitars for playing Irish music on. A loaded subject as you are discovering based on the replies here!

Obviously your playing style will be the major discriminant, but the instrument you play will have a major impact as well. Lowdens are highly-prized guitars but quality varies and they are quite expensive, they are very “clear” so you can hear each string. Not everyone’s cup of tea either - Avalon guitars are based on Lowdens and are a bit cheaper, certainly used ones are, and any I’ve played have been very nice indeed.

Martin guitars are also worth considering, they offer a wide range of styles within a number of price ranges, you could pick up a decent Martin dreadnought anywhere between €1500-2000.

Loads of other makes - Yamaha, Maton, Takamine, Gibson, Guild…etc. etc.

My personal favourite at the moment is a Gibson J45 with rosewood back and sides, it sings to Irish music and is not as boomy as a dreadnought. A nice Martin OM size might be worth considering?

Basically if I were you, I would descend on my local music store, try out loads of guitars and try to identify “the one” by the process of elimination.
