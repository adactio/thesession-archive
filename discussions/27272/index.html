phrasing example

Hi guys

Following up on the discussion about phrasing from the other day (and I think there is a bit of a semantic muddle up because “phrase” and “phrasing” refer to different things, in that phrasing often deliberately disregards phrase boundaries), here is an example that I remember as sticking out very clearly as an amazingly simple way to transform a tune into more than the sum of its phrases.

Take the first few bars of the high reel:

a2 fa eA ~A2|cAeA fAeA|a2 fa eA ~A2|Bcdc BG G2|

Two 2-bar (or 4x1-bar) phrases which echo each other by their identical beginning and different endings (with appropriate Amaj resolution for the first and Gmaj for the second - as is typical of mixolydian tunes). But anyway, played back to back, these two phrases are nothing special - and really cut the tune up into regular bar-boundaried parts. But listening in a session a few years ago, I heard:

(a2 fa eA ~A2|cAeAfA) (fg|az fa eA ~A2|Bcdc BG G2|)

Such a small difference and the tune is suddenly really different. Just think of the countless opportunities there are in every tune we play!

Re: phrasing example

Ah ha gam, I was struggling to articulate what you might be missing, but it seems a lot clearer now.

“Phrasing doesn’t disregard phrase boundaries, it defines them.”

Good phrasing does so so much more than define a phrase’s boundaries. It can toy with boundaries, it can disguise them, it can and frequently does disregard them, it can be sarcastic with them, it can take the plss out of them, it can do what ever it wants with them.

But more important than this, good phrasing in diddley music is no more about the boundaries of diddley phrases than phrasing in language is about where you’d place a comma.

Phrasing is about caressing phrases, the content of phrases and how the phrases in a tune relate to each other, not merely their lengths. It’s not about defining phrases, it’s about giving definition to the tune as a whole. Good phrasing is an elegant set of curves and percussive interest, not the mere mechanics of stop start. Good phrasing is the artistry of playing tunes.

Re: phrasing example

Oh good, an argument! :-P

The distinction some are trying to make here between phrases and phrasing seems to work only if you think in terms of conventionally notated phrases, based in large part on how the dots are organized into measures. If you have no knowledge of that, or can disregard it, then the phrases are much more free to stretch and shrink and stop short and sail on. Which is what good phrasing does in this music--mix it up, change the feel of the tune by re-interpreting the phrases every time around.

It’s not about bar lines or even A and B parts, etc. It’s just a matter of hearing phrasing lots of different ways.

Re: phrasing example

Ok, Will, I’ll bite ;)

I would argue that the vast majority of tunes can be described (and may have been written) to contain melodic phrases which repeat (and are thus bounded) by barlines (or rather, bar lines not existing in irish music at all, they are bounded by regular numbers of beats).

Some tunes, hornpipes in particular, may find this boundary displaced to the anacrusis - but in the relationship to the dance, the boundary is in the same place - where you typically start a new dance phrase.

Other tunes may find themselves ambiguous as to where the repeat actually starts (particularly as phrase conclusions are often repeated).

The above is for what I would call “phrases”. I fail to see how you could argue against their existence, any more than you could argue against most tunes being structured in an A and a B part. If these phrases of 2/4 measures do not exist, show me the tunes where the repeating melodic structure has the phrases fall elsewhere.

As for phrasing, I think I completely agree with what llig and you are both saying, which is to take these phrases (whether you believe they are intrinsically there or are only there because we put the tunes in the straightjacket of notation) and mix them up.

I have a similar argument about “figures” in dance (imagined as set patterns, to a certain number of beats). I believe that in the vast majority of cases, these “figures” simply do not exist and are inventions/simplifications used for teaching/notation. In particular, when expert dancers “make up” dances (with the exception of called dances), I don’t believe that they think in terms of N-bar figures. So although dance can be described as figures, I think these figures are a figment of our analysis (and an unfortunate carry-over of our teaching methods).

“phrases” in music however, appear to me to be there, both as underlying structure, and as an implicit rule in composition.

Speaking for myself: As I come upon 20 years of playing trad music--having come in from the outside--I’ve come to be of the opinion that if you want to talk about Irish traditional music, go ahead… But if you actually want to understand and internalize how to play it, your best bet is to stop trying to translate it into spoken language as an intermediate step, and do your best to try to learn it first (by copying) and optionally describe it afterwards.

Mind you, I’m a pretty technical guy. I write software for a living and read programming manuals for fun… But sometimes the task of understanding is not made easier by describing them in English (or French, or German, or even Irish) first.

As a side note, I don’t think that ‘figures’ in dancing are at all artificial. With the exception of solo sean-nos dancing, there’s very little, aside from individual footsteps, being made up on the spot at all, and in any kind of group dancing, the concept of a “figure” is nearly critical to the group of 8 people being able to dance together at all (“called dances” is a misnomer here, since Irish sets are rarely called--everybody is generally expected to know the figures and their order, so you only get a “caller” when sets are being taught).

Re: phrasing example

Tirno, I suppose my previous post--that it’s not about bar lines-- comes from inside the music. In this music, the person who first puts a tune together isn’t likely thinking about bar lines, or whether a phrase is 2 beats, 4 beats, or 8 beats long. Many tunes were assembled by people who didn’t bother with notation at all. In most cases, the phrases/phrasings at the heart of a tune appear to be based on the amount of time needed for a figure in the dancing.

I’d say your example in the OP does a good job of showing how phrases (not just phrasing the way you seem to want to separate the two) readily, eagerly, ignore bar lines. If how you play a tune brings it to life, and that approach seems contrary to the bar lines, then forget about the bar lines. (Hint: they don’t exist when you close your eyes. :- )

Donough, if you’re playing for dancers, then you keep the beat steady no matter what you do with the phrasing. Experienced dancers hear the lift that good varied phrasing creates and they respond to it.

violamike, the pick up notes are only the most obvious place where genuine musical phrasing differs from the notated organization of beats. In this music, good players routinely run phrases beyond 2 or 4 or longer beat counts, or stop short, or knit chunks together in ways that someone unfamiliar with this music would never think of doing.

Bluegrass and western swing music do this a lot, too, often improvising long runs that overwhelm any previous stated sense of phrasing.

Comparing good diddley music with bad diddley music is like comparing Brunelleschi’s dome with Tower Bridge in London - a hideous monstrosity of Victorian gothic kitch stonework built to disguise a perfectly functioning and very clever cast iron machine.

Thanks for the answers, all. My original post was only tangentially about the difference between phrases and phrasing - and the people who have taken part, unlike in the last discussion on the subject, overwhelmingly agree on the fact that, whether my “phrases” exist or not, phrasing either plays with them or ignores them completely. And people who can’t play (irish) music yet (or approach it from the apparent structure given by the dots) make the mistake of using the “phrases” to define their “phrasing”.

As to the question of whether it can help or not… I’m not convinced. Of course analysing isn’t the silver bullet. But surely the whole point of teaching something (any skill in which some people have a “native” competency, having learnt through immmersion) is to achieve some kind of shortcut, either to speed up the process which normally happens through immersion, or to tide people over so as not to be impatient while immersion (or the slower equivalent via weekly sessions and a lot of music listening) happens. I remember several teacher distinctly mentioning the Q/A structure of tunes and saying that hearing that was an essential component of understanding the organisation (because for complete beginners, irish music does sound rather like a huge jumble of notes). What is that then? A waste of time (given how ridiculously impatient many people are with learning new things, I could completely buy that it’s something teachers are required to do, to “pretend” that there are shortcuts - it’s certainly what happens, in my opinion, in the teaching of dancing).

(of course, even if analysing and dissecting were useful, the value of doing it without instruments and one on one interaction is dubious, particularly amongst people who don’t know each other’s playing)

Several of you have argued that I’m completely wrong in believing that these “phrases” (or whatever you want to call them - I’m happy to call them whatever) exist outside of the rigors of notation. Llig either panders to my distinction or accepts it when he says

“Good phrasing does so so much more than define a phrase’s boundaries. It can toy with boundaries, it can disguise them, it can and frequently does disregard them, it can be sarcastic with them, it can take the plss out of them, it can do what ever it wants with them.”

I think that makes sense, both from an “internal” and an “external” point of view. The delight of good phrasing (seen from the inside) is that it’s both familiar and “right” and unexpected at the same time, like a good Ogden Nash poem. It deliberately tries to be subtle, shaping the tune and throwing elements of it into relief. Seen from the outside, if a beginner is taught “phrase by phrase”, the most important misconception they now have is that the tune is placing these phrases end to end. It isn’t, from those “phrases”, the tune has to be reconstructed, so that the boundaries no longer exist (similar to repairing a crack in an instrument, come to think of it).

Now these “phrases” might be completely artificial or abitrarily set at around 2 bars, for the purpose of teaching, but taking a look at say gallagher’s frolics (coincidentally submitted by Will, many years ago)

D|:~E3 GFE|~B3 dBA|BdB BAB|GBG AFD|
|~E3 GFE| BAB dBA|BAG FAF GEE E2 D:|

There is a repeated melodic chunk: ~E3 GFE|~B3 dBA, which might arguably be D|:~E3 GFE|~B3 dBA|B. Would you claim that this chunk (which I would call a phrase) does not actually exist in the tune and is just a figment of written notation and pedagogical deconstruction? And what of the “complements” of these chunks: BdB BAB|GBG AFD and BAG FAF| GEE E2?

Re: phrasing example

Hmmm. I teach music every day, and I’ve never thought of it as a short cut, or just filling the passing time to keep an impatient student preoccupied. If that’s all it were, I’d quit today.

To me, teaching is mindful immersion. The only way it might speed up the process is by helping a student avoid red herrings and bad habits.

As for how Tirno presents the phrases or chunks of Gallagher’s Frolics, that’s a great example of one of the frustrations of teaching these tunes. When someone doesn’t know how the tune goes, you give them neat little chunks, to make the tune easier to comprehend. But playing that way stifles the tune by erasing Llig’s “allora” notes and by setting the chunks at a specific (often symmetrical) length. Those tidy, specific, symmetrical chunks are only one among many ways to phrase the tune. To have them as your foundational sense of the tune strikes me as something of a straightjacket.

Which is one of the more important reasons for learning to pick up tunes, by ear, at tempo, from well-immersed players.
