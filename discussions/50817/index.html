There are some real “how long is a piece of string?” questions there - the answer to all of them is “depends”.
What’s your budget what do you intend to do with this album? Is it just for your own satisfaction, to sell at gigs, to use as a calling card for concert/festival promoters…?

Must admit, I’m not sure where you’re headed with employing guest musicians… typically with this sort of music you’d be playing with people you are regularly playing with, who you have a rapport with, right?

Recording studio costs can be anything from £200 per day to £2000 per day depending whether you’re booking a box room in an industrial complex to Abbey Road. But I think you can find a good studio within the £200-£400 per day region.

Then there’s the cost of mixing - at least another day at that same rate but it depends on the project, the number of instruments, songs and how complicated it all is.

You could perhaps use the same mix engineer to master too - a lot of studios offer that service now - but traditionally you’d pay for separate mastering. Again, how long is a piece of string…

[Cross-posting with Matt:]
For example, what do you mean by “guest musicians”?
If they’re your friends (professional or not), then some nominal daily rate might be appropriate, say, £150 upwards.
If you actually mean session musicians, then you’re looking at Musician Union rates, which will be a lot more.

In the UK, studio costs start, I guess, at around £50 an hour. The bigger studios, with better equipment will cost a LOT more. That rate might or might not include an engineer.
Also, bear in mind that you’ll spend just as much time (if not more) in mixing as you will do in recording.

There’s something to be said for Edgar Bolton’s single take approach for a warts-and-all recording. We’ve become so used to hearing the ubiquitous auto-tuned, quantised pop stuff on the airwaves that it’s easy to think that’s what music should sound like. But you’re never going to achieve that sound in the studio without using “aid”, so having a wee bit of humanity in the recording is an alternative.

If (as Matt Milton suggests) you are recruiting musicians from within your circle, they might be kind enough to do it as a favour – although, unless you are totally strapped for cash, it would be the decent thing to offer them what you can afford. Re: ITM - Creating an Album

I’m currently in the middle of working on an album and I expect it will work out at around £3000 for 300 CDs. I think we got mates rates on the studio so I won’t give details, and we don’t have guest musicians. But as others have said, the cost depends on the studio and on the musicians. If you plan to record each of the musicians separately this will obviously take a lot longer than all playing together, and time is money, but it gives cleaner tracks for the engineer to work with. However some (myself included) find it harder to generate the same energy this way.

Recording gear and software is now relatively cheap, but do you have the skills to mix and master it? That may depend on what standards you are trying to achieve. A half-way solution is to record the tracks yourself and send them to a professional to finish off.

There are plenty of CD and vinyl manufacturers who show prices on their websites. I’ve not looked into vinyl prices, but you may find the minimum run is larger than for CDs. Mastering for vinyl is a separate exercise from mastering for CDs.

You’ll need to obtain ISRC numbers for the tracks from your local licencing body (PPL in the UK), which shouldn’t cost anything, and if you aim to sell through retail shops you’ll need a barcode, which will.

You may need to pay someone to do the graphic design (most CD manufacturers will do this, for an extra fee) and if you’re using someone else’s images you may have to pay royalties for those. If you pay a photographer or pay for copyright images make sure you obtain all the rights in the images you require eg for online use as well as the actual album artwork.

Don’t assume all “trad” tunes are non-copyright - do your research, and don’t just rely on comments on The Session or what other artists put on their CD sleeves. (In the UK) you’ll need to submit details to MCPS even if all the tracks are out of copyright. If a tune is copyright but not administered by MCPS you’ll have to try to agree a fee with the composer, or take a chance and go ahead without their consent. If you’re not in the UK there will be local copyright organisations to deal with.

Assuming you’re self-publishing, you’ll own copyright in the recording itself and the musicians own the copyright in their own performances. Register the album with PPL (in the UK) and you may get royalties if any tracks are broadcast.

Digital aggregators such as CD Baby charge a small fee to upload the album which they then distribute to Spotify, Amazon, Apple Music etc.

It all adds up, and all the costs are up-front, but for this sort of music after the initial burst of sales you can then reasonably expect to receive payments back over quite a long period. I’m still getting occasional small streaming payments for an album I made nearly 40 years ago.

Re: ITM - Creating an Album

Both my albums cost me about the same (2016 and 2019). I recorded both in my home studio, and I am not including the cost of building the studio, most of which I had in place before recording, although I did buy some new microphones for the second album for about $1800 (the first album was recorded primarily on SM-57 mics). I recorded in ProTools, of which I have a perpetual license, but I’m not including that cost either. I did have both albums professionally mixed and mastered at a studio in San Francisco, but I am not including travel costs for that… The albums had 12 and 11 tracks. Prices are likely a bit higher, since it has been 7 years since my last release (I have another project coming along -- very slowly).

Ballpark figures (in USD):

Mechanical Licenses for the tunes: ~$235
Mixing: ~$1200
Mastering: ~$220
CD Production (300 units): ~$1150
Musicians: (all album guests were gratis, but I did pay Charlie, my accompanist, by buying him a new instrument, and I gave multiple copies to anyone who appeared on the albums.)

So call it a ballpark of about $2800 per album, give or take. Between the two albums, I have about $2500 worth of sales. Mostly at festivals and camps, but I still have a trickle of online sales too. Nowadays, a lot of people don’t really care about owning a CD (or have any way to play one anymore). But recording the albums has opened a lot of doors for me for both performing and teaching, so I think of my albums more like really expensive business cards…

Re: ITM - Creating an Album

Know beforehand what you want the songs to sound like. Find other songs on albums that have a tone or quality that you want. Then you can aim for something specific. Costs vary. If you can do the basic track recording with a fine mic(s) at home or some kind of small studio space, obviously costs drop way down and you won’t feel pressued to be quick. You’ll want to have time to try different things. You always discover things in the studio, so you should have time to accept and try ideas, riffs, effects, whatever. Mixing and/or mastering should not be a mystery to you, even if you have someone else do it. If you DO hand over mixing or mastering to anyone, be absolutely sure they appreciate your style of music and don’t just give it a routine quick processing and leave you with a stale product, and money wasted. There are zillions of wannabe software geeks with audio software, but that doesn’t mean even 5% of them can do a reliable, capable, quality job. Be prepared to say NO to friends and friends of friends. Audio processing is not a pizza party.

What do you prefer for: EQ, clarity of each instrument and voice, compression and dynamics, tone colours included, doubling or tripling of tracks to make them feel more solid, what effects to use (reverb, phasing, layers for harmonies, percussion, any extra tonal colour for each instrument), those sorts of things. Give each instrument and voice it’s frequency range isolated in the mix, for clarity and dynamic range. That’s a very common mistake in recorded music. Avoid it. Get each instrument and voice sounding clear because they aren’t swamped by other things. Bring in vocals last, so you always understand the freaking words. In the studio, a singer might sound just fine, but in the final mix 20% of the words are unintelligible because the voice got swamped by other sounds and busy stuff. Avoid that! FM radio is loaded with examples of that crap.

Re: ITM - Creating an Album

When it comes to recording and getting your hopes up, one technique I’d strongly recommend is that after you’ve practiced the tune and think you know what it should sound like recorded, make a fast, simple and cheap demo recording of it, for the basics, so you sit and listen to it a few more times with your band to consider if it’s 100% final or do you hear a few good possibilities and spaces where something could be done great in terms of what it should sound like in a final recording.

A simple Zoom recorder or the band going through one or two mics, is all you need. Forget the audio quality, it’s about musical composition concerns at that point, not audio quality. This lets you get it out of your head and be more objective and experienced with it, rather than letting a proper studio effort be the first time you’ve heard it recorded. You’ll save lots of studio time and costs by doing that!

As a side note, remember that playing in a recording studio is not the same as playing live or at sessions.
Playing with a tempo track, with no audience, and no glitches at all is its own skill. Some musicians are quite good at it, some others are not used, as they never do it.
This can increment greatly the required studio time.
So if you’re a musician and you never recorded, consider you’ll be in a differente environment; if you’re a producer, look for musicians who are used to studio settings.

Whatever you spend on the production, make that sure that all the information is correct regarding copyright, mechanical rights etc. Composers (living or dead), for every single tune - forget about writing ”trad. arr” or even ”arranged by …” (putting a set together and adding a few chords is not an ”arrangement” by any stretch ), ISRC codes for all tracks, correct information about who does what, on which instrument, on which track. Such things shouldn’t be forgotten for any recording, whether you print 100 copies or 10,000 copies.
