Hey there, I’ve been playing The Music for about 6 years on the tenor banjo, a drummer for a lot longer before that. I play a good amount of jigs and reels, and to a lesser extent other styles in the tradition (except slides for some reason). Just when I thought I was starting to get an understanding of the situation I get hit with airs and Ceòl Mòr. From my understanding this tradition is more formal than our traditional session repertoire, but I’d love to understand this concept in better detail. How does one learn these tunes? Is it feasible to attempt them on a banjo? I know there is a pibroch fiddle tradition so it doesn’t seem far fetched. Any information on form, history, modes, etc. is much appreciated. I have a background in ethnomusicology so literature any anecdotes are of great interest to me.

Re: Ceòl Mòr/Pibroch

Welcome to the Session!

I’m certainly no expert on slow airs or Pibroch, and this may sound discouraging, but I gave up on trying to play that stuff on mandolin which is not dissimilar to banjo in available technique.

It’s not that it’s a more formal tradition, but that the heart of the expression in these tunes is based on long sustained notes, with ornaments breaking the sustain. That just doesn’t exist on stringed instruments with frets played with a plectrum. Not unless you go “Italian” with tremolo, which has a long history on mandolin but never sounds right for me in this music. Our plucked notes just don’t last long enough.

It’s why I’ve been transferring more of my Irish/Scottish repertoire from mandolin to flute in recent years. Not all that successfully when it comes to the really serious stuff like airs and Pibroch, which takes a lifetime to master, but at least it’s a sustaining instrument. I hope this doesn’t sound too discouraging, and maybe some others here can chime in with more input.

Re: Ceòl Mòr/Pibroch

Well, in general getting stuck into a bit of ceol mor down the local session is not guaranteed to endear you to all present; even among pipers appreciation varies, shall we say.

As a musical form it is definitely difficult to present on an instrument without sustain, and there is a school of thought that it is so closely bound up with the GHB that one should not attempt to unbind it. I wouldn’t go that far but I do think you need to know your way around it before experimenting.

Some resources that might be useful…

Books
The Highland Bagpipe & its Music, Roderick Cannon
A piobaireachd tutor, either by the Piping Centre or College of Piping are good
General Principles of Piobaireachd, by Andrew Wright
Piobaireachd and its Interpretation, MacNeill & Richardson

These would all be more or less essential reading. For historical/cultural background I’d also recommend

The Highland Pipe & Scottish Society 1750-1950
Traditional Gaelic Bagpiping - John Gibson - has less to do with piobaireachd but some important history.

Scores

For a non-piper I might recommend
Sight Readable Ceol Mor
Binneas is Borreraig (the complete volume)

The latter uses a slightly odd notation but it’s easy enough to get to grips with, and if it really drives you potty a few moments work with a ruler can fix it.

The mainstream tradition tends to work from two publications, the “Kilberry” book, and the Piobaireachd Society Collection of sixteen books of around a dozen tunes each. Both the scores and letterpress of these books require some caution in interpreting them.

Recordings

There are not many recordings purely of piobaireachd out there in print. I’d recommend though

Fhuair mi pog - Allan MacDonald & Margaret Stewart
Colla mo run - Allan MacDonald & Margaret Stewart
Dastirum - Allan MacDonald
A Living Legend - Donald MacPherson
Ceol na pioba - various - CDTRAX5009

There are two major instructional series, both on Spotify. One is called “Masters of Piobaireachd” and features the teaching of the “Bobs” of Balmoral, two of the main conduits of piobaireachd tradition, and the other is the “Classic Collection of Piobaireachd Tutorials” by Donald MacLeod.

It is worth making the point that a lot of scholarship has been done on this form of music in recent years and much of it has not been crystallised in people’s common knowledge, so even in the sources above there will be wrong and misleading history in places. Retain an open mind.

Keep in mind, as well, that like many trad. music disciplines, one could spend a lifetime in pibroch, and many have and do. It is a deep, deep well, and not ventured into lightly.

Re: Ceòl Mòr/Pibroch

"Ceòl Mòr…from my understanding this tradition is more formal than our traditional session repertoire, but I’d love to understand this concept in better detail. How does one learn these tunes?

Any information on form, history, modes, etc. is much appreciated. I have a background in ethnomusicology so literature any anecdotes are of great interest to me."

About ceol mor being more formal, yes, it’s art music, though the repertoire that’s come down to us has pieces that were functional such as the “gathering” pieces.

About it being originally harp music, I think that’s obvious. From what we can piece together the music was being played in Ireland (and later, Gaelic Scotland) long before bagpipes arrived. We have to keep in mind that the bagpipe was a recent arrival. The fringe of western Europe not only is the last bastion of old things, it’s also the last place to get cool new stuff (which is what bagpipes were). Ceol Mor seems (to me and others) related to the traditional art music of northern India; in other words, it’s Indo-European and not specifically Goidelic.

I think it’s also clear that when the pipers arrived they learned not only the repertoire from the harpers but also mimicked harp performance practices. Note that traditional ceol-mor based Highland piping uses clear pure tones. If you spend time listening to other piping traditions from across Europe west to east, then listen to a Highland piper playing piobaireachd, what jumps out is that the Highland piper isn’t utilising expressive devices the other pipers are: bending notes and doing finger vibrato. Rather piobaireachd in a very un-bagpipe-like way insists on absolutely clear bell-like tones from the chanter, as similar as possible to the tones of a harp.

All this aside, ceol mor is not as complicated as some people say. It’s theme-and-variation, familiar to us in Baroque music and jazz.

The “urlar” (ground) is the theme. Some believe that ceol mor was originally improvised, as Bach would do, and as is standard in jazz. So the harper or piper would play the theme (possibly a song-air already familiar to the listeners) and then progress though a series of stereotyped variations.

The problem with modern piobaireachd is that modern players slavishly play the manuscripts that happen to have been notated and preserved. This is a problem that people in Irish traditional music are all to familiar with: from a rich tradition of numerous players generating innumerable variants of a tune (indeed no two performances being identical) a random outsider will happen to hear and notate one particular performance, which gets published, then held up to later generations as the one-and-only way to play the tune.

In piobaireachd the art of generating the music has been lost, and instead the same few manuscripts are endlessly learnt and played. It would be like jazz dying out and 200 years from now players learning the performances off the surviving jazz recordings, not realising that the performance recorded was unique.

Listen to Barnaby Brown, who is doing much to re-create the original performance practices in ceol mor.

About the modes, many of the ular are in gap scales, pentatonic and hexatonic. As with all Highland pipe genres a few urlar are in the Lydian mode. Most are in an ordinary Major key (though not a full Major scale). Of course there are no accidentals, which by the way are quite easy to do on the pipes but impossible on the harp, another example of the pipers mimicking harping performance practices.

Anyhow here are some scales seen in piobaireachd urlars that lack the pipe’s Major 3rd (C#)

Low G, Low A, B, D, E, High A (very common)

Low G, Low, A, B, D, E, F#, High A

Low G, Low A, B, D, E, High G, High A

Low G, Low A, B, D, E, F#, High G, High A

Many urlars feel like ordinary Major tunes, though pentatonic

Low A, B, C#, E, F#, High A

or lacking only the 7th

Low A, B, C#, D, E, F#, High A

Re: Ceòl Mòr/Pibroch

> piobaireachd the word itself is the clue

Ah, well, now. More or less, piob = pipe (cognate with English), -air = someone who does (piobair = piper), -eachd = the act of doing, so the literal translation of piobaireachd is piping or perhaps pipering. The word has become attached to the genre in English, but in Gaelic it refers to any act of piping, big or small. Gaels speak of ceòl mór or ceòl beag, but these are just descriptive of length.

In any case, I think the music itself is quite capable of telling us what it needs, and we do not require the Pibroch Police to be going around confiscating banjos and other overly frivolous instruments.
