Re: Tuning Drones for Scottish Smallpipes - in D

You can buy a bagpipe tuner, but I don’t see much point unless you play in a band. You tune the drones to the chanter. I don’t know about the Shepherd sets -- are they mouth blown? If they are, allow at least ten minutes of playing time before tuning them precisely, because they’ll drift around as they absorb moisture.
Tune one of your drones to the bottom D on the chanter -- listen for the ‘beats’ as the two notes coincide. They should be an octave apart (or two if it’s the bass drone). While maintaining constant pressure, play high D on the chanter -- the drone should still be in tune. Getting the two notes of the chanter to coincide involves pushing the reed further into the chanter or drawing it out a little, depending on whether the higher note is flatter or sharper respectively. A tiny movement of the reed makes a huge difference, so take it easy if you need to adjust it.
Once you have one drone in tune, it is a matter of tuning another drone to that one (and the chanter if you like -- some people stop the chanter by squeezing the neck). If you have a harmony drone on your set, it will be tuned to A on the chanter, and should harmonize nicely with the D drones, and, if the chanter is set up properly, every other note as well.
There is quite a bit of information about the pipes on Youtube; but not much about tuning the drones, probably because it becomes almost second nature after a while. Just listen for the beats, and adjust the slides up or down until the beats slow down and disappear.

The bottom D is an octave below the thumb-hole at the back, which is top D. The hole covered by your right pinky is a low C natural, an octave below the top hole on the front of the chanter. The drones are tune to D (bass) and A, or sometimes G, although the set-up varies between makers. You’re doing well so far.

Re: Tuning Drones for Scottish Smallpipes - in D

The most common way of tuning the drones is like this, although systems vary. I suggest you set the drone slides to the place where the hemp binding stops and the plain wood starts, and find which note on the chanter they are nearest to -- there isn’t much leeway.

0 <— D (Top thumb hole)
o <— C
o <—-B
o <—-A (Tune the baritone drone to this OPEN hole)
o <—-G
o <—-F
o <—-E
o <—-D (Tune bass and treble drones this hole CLOSED )
o <— Low C (when closed, i.e, air coming from bottom of chanter)

This is the standard fingering. Think of the scale as being from the SECOND hole from the bottom, which is the bell note (all fingers closed except the pinky), and the hole beneath that as an add-on.

C X X X X X X X X (thumb)
D O X X X X X X X
E O O X X X X X X
F X O O X X X X X
G X O O OX X X X
A O X X X O X X X
B O X X X O O X X
C O X X X O O O X
D O X X X X O O O

The ‘haunting melody’ has been removed by the user. Is there another version you can link to?
If you are going to follow Will’s advice and not play the drones you might instead benefit from playing against an electronically-generated drone such as that made by a computer or keyboard. I also went a year on the goose -- but they were GHB, and a LOT harder to blow. I believe that your playing would be better if you had at least one drone going, as it would give you a reference for your chanter.

Half your speed -- you are getting lots of ‘crossing noises’, which means, for example, catching the bottom D when changing from G to A, or an A when changing from B to G. When you play slowly, that does not mean just slowing everything down -- it means holding each melody note for longer while concentrating on making the transitions between notes as clean and crisp as possible. There should be no audible transition between notes, including gracenotes.
Which leads me to the next point. Unlike other genres, Scottish piping has over the centuries developed a highly-refined system of gracing. You would do well to learn these gracenotes and where they are played, as opposed to making up your own on the fly. In order to do this, you will have to learn to read pipe music. You could get a teacher, but he or she will make you read music in order to teach you, so you might as well do it yourself. It isn’t difficult, and it will make life much easier, as well as improving your playing no end.
There are plenty of on-line resources such as these --
http://www.teachyourselfbagpipes.co.uk/rhythmandreadingmusic.htm
https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCb5PF4lUXL6ZcLAiLhYfVIQ
But most of them are for GHB, which are in a different key. The fingering, however, is the same. Whatever you decide, pipe music or ‘whistle’ music -- you will benefit no end from learning to read it. If you get stuck, send me a message and I will give you a hand.

I’ve played a set of Shepherd D’s on and off for a dozen years now and this is my two bits re. the reeds. The drone reeds they supply aren’t too bad but Pipe Dreams eezedrones are better and Ronnie does sell them already set up for Shepherd D pipes. If yours are sounding strong enough and you’ve not broken anything when inexpertly tinkering (as we all do with our new toys!) then stick with what you’ve got until you can keep the pipes steady and tune them properly.

With regards to the chanter reed, Shepherd’s GHB reed used to be reckoned (and still is by some) as among the very best. The plastic reeds needed for mouth blown smallpipes are a compromise but can sound quite sweet, the only way to really improve on it is too convert the pipe to bellows and switch to a cane reed. Having done this myself I can tell you that’s quite a job and relies on a lot of work rebalancing the instrument. I even ended up having the chanter carved to retune some of the notes. It’s not a process I’d recommend for a beginner at all.

Over the pond there in particular beware the snake oil sellers who will sell you all kinds of modifications to “improve” your pipe set up, most of them are just cashing in on the fact that people are often learning with little or no tuition and are looking for fixes to problems of technique. There’s always some bit of new kit that someone can be persuaded to buy when they are desperate to try and master something new. Shepherd make good pipes and their D smallpipe is a fine example of a mouthblown smallpipe that really doesn’t need much tweaking.

For what it’s worth I agree with Gam regarding getting the drones going. The smallpipe, unlike the GHB, takes next to no training to get the breath pressure for. The main problem is keeping it steady and not overblowing rather than training to achieve a higher pressure. For someone who has not been playing long and from the little bit of the clips I have watched of you, your fingering technique, although basic, is competent enough to stand the distraction of playing to the drones. You will also benefit from a steadier chanter reed with the drones operational as well as train your ear for purpose of tuning.

Ps. When it comes to tuning don’t get too hung up on having the chanter bang on tune for now. Tune bass and tenor to your top D and the baritone to A. I find it easier to tune the baritone first as I often knock it tuning the others, make sure the joints aren’t sloppy too. Go with a drone at a time while you are getting your ear in and technique going but fire them all up together regularly and get the feel for the whole instrument. When you start getting the drones nailed in on top D and A then start thinking about what you are going to do with getting the bottom hand in tune too. All being well you won’t be too far out. It’s really not worth worrying about the fine art of chanter tuning until you’ve got the basics of technique sorted out though.

Re: Tuning Drones for Scottish Smallpipes - in D

Please Robert, post your clips without the drones. They are just a distraction at this stage of your development and make it un listenable for me . Once the chanter is in tune according to the tuners ive linked for you then, and only then will drones be a welcome addition and will you have a chance of actually tuning them . Untill the chanter is in tune you have no hope of tuning the drones to it…… Im happy enough to help out but only if you take the advice on board .
Without drones, the tuning of the chanter is not so important and you can develop your ear/brain feedback mechanism with time, patience and practice. BUT with the drones going, constantly out of tune , the chanter not tuned etc then your ear will not develop and in fact will become so used to playing out of tune you wont even notice , Its counter productive.
Its a typical beginners mistake on any instrument and any genre to attempt to shortcut the learning experience ,especially in this age of commercial pressure where we are lead to believe that by buying this gadget or paying this teacher or by watching a Tv, that somehow it will all just happen without sustained concentrated effort over a number of years. Slow down. Its not something you can attain in weeks or months, its a lifelong process .If you conscientiously practice, follow standard operating procedure you can achieve your primary aims in the shortest possible time, if you deviate and take short cuts you may never get there…This is exactly the same advice I would give a fiddler or guitar player or any instrument for that matter. Enjoy and be happy with small rewards.8-)
