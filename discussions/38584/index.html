Re: Curious as to what this symbol is and called.

Turn symbol. Means play the written note, the note above, the note again, the note below, and back to the note again. All in the time of the note to which the turn applies.

The natural sign indicates that the note below the written note that is included in the turn should be A nat, rather than the Ab indicated by the key signature.

Copyists and publishers of that era used the correct symbol to indicate a musical direction. A turn symbol is the closest equivalent of the traditional roll. Suggesting a mordent might have been implied is just wrong. In the same way as suggesting a banana might have been implied.

Ah well then, there’s the rub. “Real” music notation programs would likely never have these sort of problems and yes, ABC has some severe limitations when called upon to address some of the issues I’ve asked the forum for assistance on. Never mind that since this is one man’s transcription, he may have thrown in some “personal” notations that align with nothing in the real world. There are certainly some interesting things on the scans that can leave one in a state of consternation. Joe Buchanan, the gentleman that went to what I regard as an extraordinary effort to write all of the tome by hand, consistently spells “field” as “feild” and does so for all “ie” pairings. Try bouncing that off of the various ABC search sites when you’re doing research on the tune! In fact, submitting the tunes in the tome to the The Session will require more spell checking to avoid unnecessary replications than I care to get into at present. So far, the most severe problem I’ve encountered is actually the straight line slur (above the notes) and whatever the heck is the straight line under the notes in this particular score:
http://www.thisoldtractor.com/guzzi007/Celtic/IMG_0414-sm.pdf
Perhaps they’re straight line slurs as well and that’s just Joe Buchanan’s method of noting them. Unless someone has the name for them, then maybe it’s safe to assume that’s what the author meant. And what we’ll disregard in an effort to at least document the tune.

ABC notation also does not support putting a crescendo above the notes which is where it appears to be in the pdf noted above. It is possible I suppose, that the crescendo is on the line before the last line but that makes little sense to me in the musical sense.

If someone wants to tell me what that straight line under the notes is - fine. I can use the info, but I’m just going to use the standard slur for those in ABC notation. I did try using the %%slurheight .1 command to - sort of - convert the slurs to (almost) straight lines but it couldn’t be turned on and off to allow normal slurs so that particular experiment, although interesting, died an honorable death.

As far as the turn/natural doohickey goes, disregarding it’s actual intent but instead concentrating on getting ABC notation to correctly display it (vice playing it correctly - it’s text, you’d have to use the = sign in front of the note to actually get the correct playback, but then, it won’t look right and I suspect that most anyone playing a roll will get it right as a matter of familiarity with the technique), the following line does the work:
“@-6,18♮”!turn! y
The “@-6,18♮” will always display the ♮ directly under the !turn! and (sometimes) put the pairing of the two half-way to the next note when the “y” is invoked with the natural smack on the top line. Without the “y”, it would have to be individually encoded for each separate instance. Discovering the “y” modifier saves a lot of effort. Definitely a case of RTMS (read the manual stupid - and I did). The “y” is just a method of spacing in ABC, has no effect on the playback (not that I care in this particular instance) and is adjustable although the default is 30 pixels.

In the end, it’s an effort at ABC because The Session doesn’t support anything else - and I’m too cheap, too old and too ornery to learn yet another software package. It is interesting though, to find out just how far ABC notation can be pushed when you read the manual(s). "Making Music with ABC 2
A practical guide to modern ABC notation by Guido Gonzato" is by far the best reference I’ve run across to date.
