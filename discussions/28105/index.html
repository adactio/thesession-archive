Re: My ITM Progress - Jigs and Reels

One thing that sometimes helps me is to “play the tunes” in my head. Since I don’t have to think about the operation of this machine, I can really concentrate on the tune a lot more, and since I don’t have the acoustical feedback in my head, I really have to focus more on the tune and not just the act of playing then tune. Since the only way I hear the right note is by thinking it, I really have to think the right note - this is as opposed to the muscular training that you do when you make the right movement of your hands and the right note comes out, and you don’t necessarily have to have the note in your head at all - you can do it all with the hands, which is quite wrong, of course.

So the idea is to be able to imagine everything that you do in playing the tune, and to hear, in your head, exactly what all of that sounds like. If it helps, imagine the Kevin Burke recording and imagine playing along with it, but it’s best when the tune itself is what brings you along.

Another thing to think about when you’re playing, either in your head or in the flesh, is breaking up the tune into phrases. A tune is not a bunch of notes in a row, it’s a sequence of interlocking phrases. If you start hearing where those start and stop, and how they connect to each other, you’ll start hearing the tune more clearly, and I bet you’ll start playing it more clearly as well.

Re: My ITM Progress - Jigs and Reels

You should still learn tunes from recordings but you don’t have to (and shouldn’t) play it at the same speed as said recordings. One things I do when practicing is dick about with the timing and phrasing. Play the tune driving and fast once or twice, then play it slow and swingy another time through. Stuff like that. Listening to different recordings of the same tune can help develop a sense of varied ways to play it. The best way, of course, is to play frequently with live musicians and then you have to learn how to be adjustable in your playing.

Slowing down has the added benefit of clarifying the phrasing. Trad players all over the world commonly play at speeds beyond their ability and plow through phrasing (and we’ve all been guilty). When you ease off the speed, you can better hear how phrases interact with one another.

Re: My ITM Progress - Jigs and Reels

Bear in mind that this is a phase most musicians go through--having the mechanical ability to crank out a tune, but not yet having an appropriate musical *feel.*

So don’t stress too much over it. But also don’t linger here any longer than need be.

A couple of thoughts:

- Speed does not create lift and pulse and phrasing. You want to be able to play with lift and pulse and phrasing at any tempo.

- If you can’t create lift and pulse and phrasing at a slow, easy (for you) tempo, then you won’t do any better at a faster tempo. Likely worse (which is what I hear happening in your videos above).

- On fiddle, 99.999999% of this is in the bow hand. You’ll want to be able to swell and ebb a note, or from one note to the next, with subtle changes in bow speed and weight. And you’ll want to be able to start and stop the bow with complete control, so you can leave gaps--silences, breaths--when the phrasing begs for it. The whole trick to getting an expressive bow hand is to s….l….o….w… waaaaaaaaaaay dowwwwwwwwwwnnnnn. Even s………l………o………w………..e……….r………. And play a tune or part of a tune or just a lovely phrase and see how many different ways you can make that piece breathe and sing.

It sounds to me like you already have a fair idea of where the pulse needs to happen. Now you need to get that into your bow hand (and every other cell in your body). Don’t be in a hurry. Go so slow (as Itzahk Perlman recommends) “that nothing escapes your notice.”

Re: My ITM Progress - Jigs and Reels

It’s not too fast, it’s just you are losing control of the bow at the speeds you are playing.

Other than slowing down for the time being I think you should try some bowing exercises on your jigs. First try to slur every note in a phrase and when you can slur no longer, switch direction but try to make that switch imperceptible to the ear. In classical music this is called legato. Now try the same jig bit switch direction on every note. This will get you comfortable playing the notes in sequence but break your reliance on bow patterns and you’ll be able to be more expressive with your bowing on the fly.

This should help you get to a point where you can throw in legato to smooth out a phrase or put out a rhythmic bark with the switch in bow direction.

Also, I think you should practice rolls with all fingers but start with rolls on the first finger, then the second, in as many keys as you can stand. Then take any reel, and practice the notes before the roll, the roll itself, and the notes after, then repeat until you can play the phrase with no hesitation in bowing. Do the same for all your bowed triplets, but really try to get your wrist to play the triplet, soon you won’t be thinking about them anymore.

Re: My ITM Progress - Jigs and Reels

you’re sawstroking your jigs, for one thing. At this stage, that makes everything sound really choppy (in a few years, when you have a better feel for bowing, you’ll be able to get away with it, but not now).

First thing to do is try slurring over the barlines. Find a nice slow fiddler on YouTube (not Martin Hayes, because he single-strokes his jigs a lot) and try to follow their bowing. Do this with several different fiddlers, so you get an idea of how different players do it.

I would also by Matt Cranitch’s book. He has lots of great tips in there, lots of tunes and techniques that build on themselves. If you follow what he says, you’ll be going in the right direction. Normally I would say get a teacher, but since there doesn’t seem to be one in your area, Matt’s book is a good place to devote some attention.

Earl, lowering the action on a fiddle doesn’t mean flattening the arc of the bridge. Usually you just lower the string height at the nut, and perhaps also with the bridge--lower, not flatter.

Lower action may make fingering a little quicker, but you also lose volume, and also some of the pop of cuts and rolls. Too high an action and it can get tricky to finger cleanly without extra unwanted fingering noise or intonation issues.
