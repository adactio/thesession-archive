As someone who has published quite a bit of workshop material and books over the years there are some points raised in this discussion that I wish to take issue with, nothing personal against the poster/s.

1)‘’Aside from that, if this is a first printing, some amount of typos will inevitably be encountered.‘’ I believe if you’re publishing a tune book, but especially if you’re selling it, you need to ensure that it is accurate and proofread a number of times through. My first book was a 2,100 Guitar chord dictionary. I read it through about 10 times finding a number of errors, maybe 25 in all, and fixed them. On the 11th occasion I could find no errors. I let it sit for about 2 weeks then went through it again and found no errors, and thus I published. It was then I could recall those immortal words of our late great Prime Minister Gough Whitlam who said of his own writings‘’ Not only am I an author, but a published one‘’! But seriously a lot of time and energy should go into proof reading something as important as a book. And I’d be the first to point out that sometimes there are minor grammatical typos in some of my workshops, but I sincerely hope not in my transcriptions. Re: Tune Books With Chords

An F chord in an Am tune is often fine, Matt, like in Tam Lin. The trouble is that most Am sounding tunes are actually dorian, with a smattering of F# notes. In those tunes, surely D/F# would be a better (and safer) substitute for Am, if a substitute is desired.

As you say, “if it sounds good, then it works”, which to some extent, I agree with. The problem is that what one person thinks “sounds good” may not sound good or right to another person. Some people are a lot more conservative than others when it comes to what is acceptable in terms of harmonic accompaniment. I tend to think that tune books, if they have accompanying chords should, in CMO’s words, indicate “the basic harmonic structure of the tune”. That way, it should at least sound “right” to almost everyone.

The modern fashion in trad seems to be to impose a preconceived harmonic structure on a tune. A common one, for example, is I-I-IV-IV-I-I-IV-IV over 8 bars of a major key reel or jig, whether or not the notes suggest that. There is also this idea that because “accompaniment is improvised” you can just groove through your favourite progressions and you don’t really need to listen to the tune.

My usual starting point is to listen to the tune and listen for the changes. So, to start, stay on the root chord until the tune tells you you need to move on. But I have to say that it worries me that I’m often not sure if people can tell the difference between the IV and the V chords.

Re: Tune Books With Chords

Re “F chord in Am tunes are not traditional”.

I always played Dorian-key tunes with the “extra” F, C, etc chords. I got into this music in the New York area in the mid-1990ies, and that’s how it was done back then.

But now I check the Fiddler’s Fakebook, and i.e. the Star of Munster only has the Am and G chords, that’s it. no F chords. Tunes in this book are mostly amalgamated transcriptions of commercial records, Star of Munster is from Kevin Burke’s “if the cap fits”. This takes us back to 1978 (LP) and 1982 (fakebook).

Regarding the random C referred to earlier, it’s obviously a hint at at mixolydian just to alter the mood a little. I don’t like it that much but a closely related Em7 chord is much nicer and gives more lift IMO.

And the poster who said don’t use chords that aren’t diatonic with trad tunes is generally right but don’t forget that doesn’t confine us to 3 or 4. You can happily use 6th or 7th chords built on any of the first 6 notes of the scale for variety and also different inversions - for much more variety.
