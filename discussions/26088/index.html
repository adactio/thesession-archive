Re: Dedication and practice versus genes

Erm, and if that isn’t what you’re looking for, then maybe this is a better response.

A few musical savants are born into every generation. They too have to put in the hours and hours of playing to fulfill their potential. But they typically get there faster and so may go further in virtuosity than the rest of us.

On the other hand, musical ability isn’t some final destination. As Yo-Yo Ma says, playing music is about expression, not perfection. So your mindset and attitude toward playing music can go a long way toward greasing the skids to becoming the musician you want to be.

If you engage in it as “play” rather than “work,” you may enjoy it more and this may in turn help you improve with less frustration.

If you set small, achievable, short-term goals (learning and improving in steps), then you’ll more likely stay focused on those specifics and improve toward those goals. Set priorities on what you most want to improve on, and go for it. Then find new priorities. (For this music at the session level, the key elements are solid rhythm and being able to play smoothly. Next come timing and phrasing. Everything else is icing.)

Re: Dedication and practice versus genes

full measure, I think one of the most important goals for a fiddler is to learn to hear (and then be able to play) micro variations in timing, down to how you start, carry, and end a note. We’re making music with a hank of horsetail tied to a stick! There are no indicators inked onto the hair for where to start, stop, swell, bounce, ease, or ebb a note--that’s all up to our abilities to hear where and when to do it and get nerve synapses and muscle fibers to respond appropriately.

The bow hand is soooo important.

So I have an ongoing short-term (is that an oxymoron?) goal of getting ever more nuanced in how the start, middle, and end of each note sounds. I pay attention to this whenever I pick up the bow, and I know of specific spots in certain phrases and tunes where I want to really focus and improve on this.

Also, you can take goals like “get cleaner, crisper bowed triplets,” and break that out into:

1. Learn to play light, clean triplets, not just crunchy ones.
2. Consistency: make all three notes of the triplet distinct (not mushy or blurred) 99 times out of 100, at typical session or dance tempos. (And if you can’t do it at 112 bpm, then aim to do it at 80 bpm, and then at 94 bpm, etc.)
3. Be able to emphasize either the downbeat of a triplet, or the end of it.
4. Be able to come out of the triplet cleanly, bowing the next note in the same direction as the last bow of the triplet.
5. Be able to come out of the triplet cleanly, bowing the next note in the opposite direction as the last bow of the triplet.
6. Learn to do bowed triplets in either direction (DUD and UDU).
….and so on.

Sometimes this breaks out along mechanical lines. For example, if you want your rolls to sound better, start by improving your ring-finger and pinky cuts. Get those firing clean and crisp, and timed the way they sound best, and your rolls will sound better right away. Then work on the fingering for the rest of the roll, all the while paying attention to the start-carry-end aspects cited above.

Re: Dedication and practice versus genes

full measure - there’s a nice little essay “On Practicing” by Ricardo Iznaola, published by Mel Bay. It’s mostly aimed at the classical guitarist, but mostly it’s good advice for anyone. Worth considering, although at $7 for 24 printed pages it’s something that I only bought because I was a teacher. If you’re in Boston some time, let me know and I’ll lend it to you - I don’t consult it much these days.

In the meantime, here’s some thoughts, some of them derived from Iznaola, others from other sources:

I distinguish between ‘practicing’ and ‘playing’. Playing is what you do when you pick up your instrument and make music. Practicing is what you do when you make a small step on a long and well-mapped road towards some particular improvement in your skills. You can learn from both, but they’re two very different activities. An hour with your fiddle in your hand is not necessarily an hour of fiddle practice - in fact, it’s almost certainly not.

Playing you can do any time, but if you’re going to practice, you should do it at a regular time every day, for a particular stretch of time, and you should put up your fiddle when you’re done. You can play later, or you can play before practice, but there should be a definite interval of some other activity between the two, and you should always know which you’re doing.

Practice should short, and focused. You might devote an hour a day to it if you’re completely insane and hyper-focused, but I start my students on fifteen-minute blocks and only allow them to increase those slowly, over time. When you play, you sometimes find that you’ve “gone away” for a moment or more - you carry on playing, but your mind wanders. When you practice, your mind should always be on what you’re doing. That’s really hard for most people to do for long periods of time, so I feel you should keep it short.

Practice is problem-solving. You have an obstacle, you identify the obstacle, and you come up with a way over it, around it, or through it.

Practice can address many things, and it can address several things at once, but there should be one thing at the center of it. If you’re a guitarist learning to flatpick, you might work on tone production or volume, and in the process be paying attention to your posture and your breathing. Or you might do a complicated flatpicking exercise that you know pretty well, simply to think about how you’re breathing when you do it. The two exercises look the same from the outside, but the mental attitude of the student is quite different.

So when you’re thinking about “small achievable short-term goals”, I’d have you thinking in terms of things that you can address in a series of fifteen-minute exercises between lessons. The important thing is that you know when you wake up in the morning what you’re going to work on today, when you’re going to work on it, and what you intend to get from it. And at the end of the week, you can consider the problem afresh and decide whether that particular exercise is getting you somewhere, or if you need to address the problem in a different way, or what you need to move on to next.

Maybe that gives you something to think about as regards practice. Take the bits that make sense and grind over them for a while, and use whatever remains.

Re: Dedication and practice versus genes

Bad news, it’s talent. Good news, we all have it, or the latent ability to succeed in an artistic pursuit given the environment where it can be allowed to flourish.
First, you need access to it. If you’re lucky enough to be born into circumstances where music is present ‘talent’ can begin to bloom early. If not, it’s not too late to create a musical environment at any time in life. As has been mentioned previously…surround yourself with the goal, in this case music. Listen, and be immersed in the music you want.
Know that allowing latent ‘talent’ to develop means spending time in the activity. It may look like work to someone from the outside, but if you can find a way to come to it from a place of desire and curiosity, it won’t feel like work.
How to develop as a musician? Allow the desire to improve to arise naturally through exposure to your objective. Listen to music that you love, get access to a musician who inspires you, and listen intently. If you are constantly being reminded of your objective then you can’t help but be inspired to look for it in your own playing.
For example, if you’re hearing beautiful intonation constantly, you can be guided to listen more intently to your own playing for better intonation. If you’re listening with curiosity and interest to your own intonation then you can’t help but become absorbed in the process of discovering the physical movements that lead to better intonation, and you’ll find yourself drilling those movements until they become a naturalized part of your playing. The very same drill, or exercise, in different circumstances could seem like a punishment, or medicine without the interest or involvement.

I’ve seen people go from playing horribly out of tune to well in tune in one lesson, once they understood what to listen for. I’ve had students I wanted to give up on suddenly take off. And I’ve seen “naturals” stymied by a lack of desire or an unwillingness to focus.

Talent alone doesn’t presage accomplishment. And awkwardness in on aspect doesn’t count out fluency in many others.

As someone who initially pursued a career in music, left it, and has now rediscovered the joy of playing, I have a somewhat different perspective on this question. I believe anyone can rise to a minimal level of competence at any activity given enough work and dedication. But, there is a point in all endeavors where it is natural ability combined with hard work that get people over the “hump” from a good to a great player. Up to a year ago, the defining moment (and the end) of my musical pursuits was when my college professor told me I would never be a professional musician because, while I was very “technically proficient,” I did not have enough of a “natural gift” or “ear” for music. In retrospect, at that time in my life, he was probably correct. I used to spend 10+ hours a day in the music center sight-singing, ear training, and practicing my clarinet, but no matter how hard I worked, I could not play back anything by ear. I could play virtually anything written on a page, but I could not identify intervals and I could not sing a note unless one was being played for me to match. I could not transpose music without consciously thinking in mathematical terms. I could not improvise..period. Without my lines and dots, I was naked and unable to function.

10 years later, after following different non-musical pursuits and dabbling with piano, and guitar, and now discovering ITM, something just “clicked” in my brain. All of a sudden, I could hear in my head what a harmony to a song was suppossed to sound like; I could complete melodies without having heard them before. The “music” started to ‘make sense.’ I can now repeat phrases and melodies by ear--albeit with a lot of plunking and noodling. I find myself able to transpose from my C to D whistle and vice versa. While it takes conscious thought…so much of it just “comes to me” “naturally” noiw. My fingers move without a conscious thought of “move this finger, tighten embachure, play two steps up.” I can’t explain it, but it is as if a whole part of my brain has suddenly been turned on.

I think part of it was probably that in college, I was just too “serious” about music. I approached everything very analytically, and stopped enjoying it. I lacked “passion.” All I saw was lines and dots and could not “hear” the connections, even though I could decribe what those connections were supposed to sound like. For some unknown reason, that quality has now manifested itself.

Call it “talent,” “natural ability,” “spirit” or “passion” or “ear for music”--but there is definately some intangible aspect to hearing and playing music that cannot be quantified, taught, examined, and replicated on command by everyone.
