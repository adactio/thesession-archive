Eclectic traditions

I hear comments about playing traditional music which are probably appropriate if the general topic is folk music. However, if the purpose is to understand why tradition is important to the music, then I think the comments are made out of ignorance.
What do you think? Here are 2 examples which I have heard in the past week.
~ “Irish, appalachian, & bluegrass music are so intertwined that if you are playing in one genre you can play the same tune in another genre. I love when someone wants to argue this with me. Red Haired Boy for instance . . .”

~ Every time a band forms, if they want to continue performing, eventually they figure out they need songs. That is what audiences want. Diddley by itself isn’t enough."

What is the current state of the traditional Irish dance tunes in your opinion? Personally, the sessions and one on one tradition is what is most important to me. However, I wonder how much tradition is there in musical forms which strive to be more eclectic. Is there a tradition of combining styles or is there a pure drop at the heart of traditon?

Re: Eclectic traditions

All traditions evolve. Why does that make the word tradition meaningless? Your rant makes no sense. Sure traditions get marginalised as they are supplanted by the “main stream”. But many traditions keep going and continue to evolve, all be it from the sidelines of general popularity. And their ebb and flow in and out of main stream conciseness with forays such as riverdance or Finbar Fury singing with his five string banjo on top of the pops are not things to wince about. They are mere aberrations which have little effect on the “traditional” music.

Sure it’s difficult to define traditions, not least because of their tendency to trigger irascible feelings when challenged. Sure, their may be a number of almost conflicting definitions of the word, but to say the word is meaningless is to deny the relevance of past generations’ contribution.

In fact, the one common element that binds any and all definitions of tradition is, as I said earlier, respecting past generations’ choices above your own. i.e. the very opposite of eclecticism.

Re: Eclectic traditions

I know it sounds like a contradiction and in a way it is. The music is full of contradiction however and we learn to be pragmatic about it. It’s to do with what you say about there can be no such thing as pure tradition. In fact, I’m not so sure there can be anything that is pure. Maybe pure maths? But that’s about it.

But to have the concept that the music is contaminated by such things as “the resurgence of interest in the uillean pipes” is not really the way I’d look at it.

I think that if broadly speaking, you can try to imagine that you consider the feelings of past generations before introducing innovations, then you would be on the right track. But the best thing to do when you do bring innovation is to never discard anything from the past. Build on the past without smothering or discarding it.

Matt Molloy is a good example of a great innovator. He transformed flute music, but discarded nothing that the tradition gave to him.

I think that strumming in general has been a positive innovation. It adds layers of harmony without (largely) smothering the melody (I say largely, because there are tunes in the tradition with deliberately ambiguous scales and tonal centres. Most strumming to such tunes removes this ambiguity, which is a backwards step. It discards)

The introduction of fretted string instruments playing tunes, however, is certainly a backwards step. The amount of articulation that is discarded is depressing. But at least it’s not lost. Lost on the pluckers, yes, but not lost to the tradition.

Re: Eclectic traditions

Yes, there is a trace of Irish and Scottish heritage in bluegrass and Appalachian old-time, but the biggest influences come from other directions. If you could somehow remove all the Irish and Scottish genetic material from bluegrass music, most bluegrass fans would never notice the difference. And the core of Appalachian old-time is largely (not completely, of course) independent of what we know as Irish diddley, their common ancestry being mostly from three hundred years ago, with a lot of time in the American melting pot meanwhile.

The handful of common diddley tunes, like The Red Haired Boy, that worked their way into some corners of the Appalachian tradition are really, I think, more exception than rule. Anybody have any information on the provenance of that tune? I don’t think I’ve ever heard it played by diddley players.

I think the influence of African-American music in both bluegrass and old-time is nearly invisible to most Americans because that influence is so pervasive in the history of our popular music.

All of those things combined I decided to consider tradition anew. Each of you may or may not have your tradition in a particular genre & a particular style of music. Either way ~ that’s grand!.
I’m not part of a traditon at all though I would like to honor the diddley music I choose to play.I use the phrase with the utmost respect. The way to do this, more than anything, is through tradition. Passing tunes along person to person, learning the phrasing, the articulation. Those traditions have endured over time.
