Re: I Reelly don’t understand Hornpipes.

One of the tricky things about hornpipes is that there are a lot of ways to play them right, and of course infinitely more ways to play them wrong. Listening, of course, is essential, but you knew that already. If you use some sort of mp3-playing device or system, you might go through your recordings and gather up all of the hornpipes you’ve got on vatrious recordings, and play them on a mix for a while. You’ll probably notice two common features. One is a much more pronounced accent on the one and three, and the other is an inclination towards more varied rhythmic patterns in between the beats - triplets, long notes, quarter notes, and so forth - than you’ll find in reels. This goes a long way to giving them the “bumpy” feel that’s usually characteristic of the tune, which is resolved in the typical hornpipe ending, with three strong quarter notes and a pickup to the next section or the next downbeat.

I think if you listen for those things, you might get a start at answering your question.

Re: I Reelly don’t understand Hornpipes.

Not all hornpipes are played ‘humpty dumpty’, so the ‘swing’ can’t really be used to distinguish between a hornpipe and a reel, The main difference is in the tune itself, with the hornpipe usually having lots of triplets (three notes played in the time of two), and a weak fourth beat -- think of the Sailor’s Hornpipe: diddle| UM DUM DUM diddle IDDLE DIDDLE DUM diddle. As with other forms of music, for example the tango or the samba, it’s hard to describe but you know it when you hear it.

Re: I Reelly don’t understand Hornpipes.

@jWalkert - Notwithstanding the very limited relevance of sheet music to the playing of Irish (or other) traditional music, it is possible to explain hornpipes using standard musical terminology.

Firstly, hornpipes have some similarity to reels in that both tune-types are scored in 4/4 time and consist mainly of quavers and crotchets, with a predominance of quavers.

When playing a pair of quavers in a reel, each quaver is played with an equal time value.

By contrast, when playing a hornpipe, the time-value of the first quaver is increased slightly, whereas the the time-value of the second quaver is shortened by the same amount. This is commonly known as “swing”

The amount of “swing” imparted is dependant on many factors - e.g a musician’s personal playing style or regional playing style etc. - but is likely to be probably somewhere between 20% and 30%.

In sheet music terms, sometimes hornpipes are scored with the first quaver of the pair dotted, and the second quaver scored as a semiquaver. However this is certainly not an accurate representation, as this would equate to a 50" swing, and hornpipes are never played like that..

More commonly, hornpipes are written “undotted”, like reels. You just need to understand that it’s a hornpipe, and conclude that some degree of swing is required. Of course, the term “hornpipe” may not actually feature in the tune title, so how do you know? In many hornpipes, both the “A” part and the “B” part finish with three consecutive crotchets, which can give you the clue that it’s a hornpipe.

A final complication is that sometimes reels are played with a small degree of swing, and sometimes hornpipes are played with no swing at all!

Hope this helps! :-)

Re: I Reelly don’t understand Hornpipes.

The swung vs. unswung issue is only a half truth; as llig says, a reel can be swung and a hornpipe can be unswung, but that doesn’t make a reel a hornpipe or a hornpipe a reel. The most important distinction, I think, is one of emphasis.

Take a bar of 4/4, divided up into 8 quavers:

In a reel, these are divided up into two groups of four. There is a strong emphasis on the first quaver in the first group, and a secondary emphasis on the first in the second group. The remaining quavers are of roughly equal weight (the 3rd of the group is generally a little stronger than the 2nd and 4th, but there is some room for flexibility here). For this reason, some would argue that a reel should properly be notated in 2/2 or “cut time”, since there are two principal beats in the bar - 2/4 is used in some older tune collections, with semiquavers instead of quavers.

In a hornpipe, the bar can be divided into 4 groups of two quavers. Again, the strongest emphasis is on the 1st and 5th quavers of the bar (the 1st quaver in the 1st and 3rd groups of 2). But the emphasis on the first quaver in groups 2 and 4 is only slightly weaker, resulting in four clearly defined beats in each bar.

Using this principle, it is, theory, possible to play any reel as a hornpipe or hornpipe as a reel - and there are certainly some tunes that could serve equally well as either. But most tunes are, to a greater or lesser degree, structured according to this rhythmic emphasis and there are certain features that typify each type of tune. The most obvious example of this is the characteristic three-crotchet ending to many hornpipes; another common feature is triplets that are intrinsic to the melody (as opposed to triplets in reels which are usually more of an embellishment), sometimes occurring in runs of 4 or more - The Rights of Man, The Belfast and The Harvest Home are all good examples.

I remember reading somewhere a few years ago that the ‘swung’ style of hornpipe originated in the Northeast of England in the 18th Century; as it gained popularity elsewhere in the British Isles, that type of tune became known as a “Newcastle Hornpipe” (also the name of a specific tune). Triplet runs in a tune would suggest that it was originally intended to be played with ‘swung’ rhythm, since this type of rhythm is, in a sense, triplet based. Yet it is perfectly possible to play such a tune with ‘straight’ quavers, only playing the triplets themselves in ‘triplet time’, so to speak (or realising the triplets with uneven note values - e.g. two semiquavers and a quaver).
