Pros and cons of learning Irish music using software tools

As many people here will know, I’ve developed a tune practice tool which takes tunes in abc notation and breaks them into phrases. I wanted to reflect on the pros / cons of using such tools for learning.


# Pros of learning using ‘MIDI-based’ tools:

- There is less complexity in the sound. Human musicians add a lot to their playing beyond the ‘bones’ of a tune. Tools that just play ABC lack this detail, which could help beginners to hear what is happening.

- You can easily break tunes into small chunks for learning. My tool was designed to automatically split tunes into short phrases and allow you to practice them one phrase at a time. Playing or learning a whole tune at once could be too much to handle.

- You can change the tempo arbitrarily without the music sounding terrible. Because of how MIDI works, you can change the tempo freely, and my tool allows the tempo to be changed while the tool is playing.


# Pros of learning from imitating human musicians:

- The main advantage of learning from human players is that there is much more detail in the performance. You can learn phrasing and ornamental details just by imitating what you are hearing. Humans also don’t play rhythms ‘mathematically’ as notation literally implies.

Those details cannot be learned from MIDI based tools that just play ABC ‘as is’, because they are not represented in the notation. There’s a lot of stuff in human playing that can’t practically be represented in any notation.

Playing from human players can be easier by either having a teacher who can play something slower, or by taking a recording into a digital audio workstation, and slowing it down. This however sounds bad if something is slowed down too much.

Using either MIDI based tools or a DAW it is possible to slow things down and gradually work up the speed.

Does anyone else have any other thoughts on these topics?

Re: Pros and cons of learning Irish music using software tools

Like you say, there’s so much more to the music when played by a human than the simple sequence of notes. MIDI playback has no feeling in it. Personally I don’t want to spend any of my listening or playing time listening to or playing sounds without feeling. I feel like any time I could spend practicing with MIDI sound would nudge my playing in a direction I don’t care for. Perhaps even more so than sheet music, because at least with sheet music you have the freedom to imagine whatever feeling-infused sound you want, but with MIDI you just have the one sterile sound.

So for anyone who’s not sure and is thinking about how to go about learning the music, I would urge them to listen to lots of music and when a particular playing or recording of a tune captivates you and you *must play it*, then learn that tune from that recording. And for practicing tunes, just play the tune that you want to play in the moment - the tune that you feel in that moment. This is 95% of how I’ve spent my solo time with this music.

That said - I haven’t tried your tool, and I imagine there are folks who value different aspects of the music than I do and that’s all good, and I appreciate your making it and remain open minded that perhaps I’ll find it useful someday. But you did ask for thoughts so there’s my tuppence. :)

Re: Pros and cons of learning Irish music using software tools

@Will Evans The tool is here: https://pureocarinas.com/phrase-by-phrase-abc-tune-tool

Personally I see ‘the bones of a tune’ and ‘the playing style’ as two separate things - learning a tune, be it from notation or by ear, is learning a sequence of notes, then playing style is a personal thing one can layer on top of that.

learning things by ear comes more naturally to me, but I also have almost no short term memory. Because of this, just listening to music in and of itself has no learning value. To be able to learn anything from human players requires either taking a recording into a DAW, splitting it into chunks, looping each chunk one at a time, and repeatedly imitating it so that becomes muscle memory, or alternately working with someone in person who can do the same thing.

Software tools do not have to be MIDI based, and having an easier way of taking human performances and cutting them into phrases would be very helpful.

It is important to be aware that people are not the same, and a learning approach that works for one person may be completely ineffective for another.

Some of the arguments against using software arise from the assumption people carry on using it, don’t they? Which isn’t necessarily the case.

I use it to help me get the bones of new tunes under my fingers and iron out trip-ups. I often learn them in sets of two or three, so it starts me thinking about transitions; when I’m juddering over the cattle grid between two tunes that don’t work together, it also prompts ideas for alternatives. I sometimes return to the tool when I’m speeding tricky tunes up, but not always.

After that it’s, finding my own way with the tunes and playing them with others. The software does the same thing my gran did when I was eight: keeps repeating the tune till I’ve got it. Just less prone to getting tetchy when my attention wanders.

Re: Pros and cons of learning Irish music using software tools

As a form of code, ABC notation is certainly a handy storage system, and it has enabled the invention of any number of conversion and playback tools. Their utility and convenience are clear enough—they make it easier to share tunes and to woodshed on them based on each person’s preferred approach (e.g., ABCs, standard notation, tablature, etc., and aural playback, phrases or whole tune, transposition, tempo, etc.).

For me, the primary downside is that you end up learning your tunes from an app, not a person. Maybe that balances out when you take your rake of tunes to a session and find human connection there. All well and good. But personally, I would not trade for anything my experiences of learning tunes, and absorbing the music and craic, face to face with people. That personal interplay is where my understanding of a tune, its emotional weight—its heart—comes from.

It’s also a simple question of how you want to spend your days. Clearly, many of us today spend countless hours on a screen, interacting with a machine. Some people seem to prefer that over direct person-to-person interaction. In contrast, I see learning a tune as an opportunity to get away from the screen, to meet up with another musician in person. Over years and decades, this builds a heartfull of wonderful memories—sensory rich associations and influences—that shape who I am and how I play this music. I don’t know how you’d get that from learning your tunes from an app, the screen as your only companion.

Re: Pros and cons of learning Irish music using software tools

I have to say: I have no idea how memory works, like how short-term becomes long-term, or whether there’s even such a thing as long-term memory in the absence of short-term memory.

I started as a singer, learning songs by ear. “The Eentsy, Weentsy Spider” might be the first song I learned from listening as a kid. Before I was ten I had memorized the sound tracks to several Broadway musicals, including Fiddler on the Roof, West Side Story, and Paint Your Wagon. I memorized Harry Belafonte’s entire 90-minute, 1959 concert at Carnegie Hall.

I like to transcribe tunes I’m learning into ABC notation because it forces me to listen intently to the music being played. The ABC-to-MIDI pathway gives me a way to confirm I haven’t internalized a note sequence that’s more convenient or familiar than correct.

But I was taught Irish Traditional Music aurally, with my teacher playing whole tunes for repeat listening at home, but also teaching me phrases in call-and-response during my lessons. It isn’t just the notes that matter. It’s also in the phrasing, which includes dynamics.

Dynamics vary from tune to tune. It would be awfully tedious to try to program attack, sustain, delay, and release for every note. Personally, I haven’t been able to get what I need from an ABC transcript.

Re: Pros and cons of learning Irish music using software tools

@Jimi lampert - There are a few ways of viewing that:

- Learning a tune as an experienced player without the ‘musicality’ aspects may be desirable because they can then add their own playing style onto it without influence.

- Learning something without ‘musicality’ aspects may be helpful for a complete beginner, as the complexity of the tune as an experienced player would perform it may be overwhelming.

- If a player were to exclusively learn tunes from notation or midi for their whole live, that would be bad because it ensures that a player cannot develop a more expressive playing style.
