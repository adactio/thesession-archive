I know what it feels like to be a numpty (ie). Frustrating because in my alternative universe as a wise and long respected purveyor of liturgical folderol, I was on the ‘other side’. Then I started learning B/C box with basses and found out what being a numpty(ie) feels like!

Here’s the follow up challenge because it is my latest hill to climb now that I have hopefully moved out of general numptiness. Like someone said, we were all there once.

It’s one thing to practrise and woodshed, playing nice and low like Llig, SWFL, JK and my teach JW have drummed into me. But -particularly if you don’t have a regular session to go to (or one where the regulars have any tolerance for someone trying to ‘grab on’ at session speed and hold on, or where one might not have someone to play along with if one lives in the backwoods of the Balkans or waste lands of Tinley Park) but quite another developing the skill to play along with others at speed. If one is past getting the tune down stage, the next challenge is to develop the confidence to play along.

How did other folks here do that? For me it’s very different than playing along with the classically trained lot I cross every Sunday with well coordinated spots in front of us and the Liturginazi waving her baton in some semblence of rythym.

Playing along with CD’s, or now the folks who have too much time on their hand and fill YouTube? Sit in front of the train station or in the park and play along with whoever shows up?

Seriously. If you have been in a position where you only get to play along with someone really good once every couple of weeks (or less), how would you develop the confidence so you can work up to session speed and not be a numpty (ie)?

I’m not a real professor, but I play one on the Numpty tv channel….

Zippy, the condition you describe is *inadequacy,* not numptiness. By definition, if you are aware of your inadequacy and seek to remedy it, you cannot be a numpty.

Numpties are people whose credo goes something like this:

“When you earnestly believe you can compensate for a lack of skill by doubling your efforts, there’s no end to what you can’t do.”

(thanks to http://www.despair.com)

I went through about a decade of low-on-the-learning-curve with this music without anyone to play with. I don’t know if the following will help, but it’s what got me over the hump and into sessions (when I finally lived someplace with a session).

1. Play along to recordings, at full speed.
2. Play along to Thistle and Shamrock every week, on every tune you know that comes on. Try to learn a tune or two by ear, as it flies by on the radio, no recording allowed.
3. Woodshed. At least once a week I’d choose a single tune and play it for 2 or 3 hours, focusing on an aspect of my playing that needed improvement. Of course, I’d work on that aspect every other day of the week, too, but the burst of concentration seemed to help.
4. Never sacrifice a steady beat. Learn to play with an unshakeable rock solid rhythm before you move on to improving other aspects of your playing.
5. Once you’ve achieved #4 above, spend some woodshed time playing toward session speed. Just above your comfort zone. You have to practice playing fast, just like any other skill. Even if a lot of your playing time is slow to become more smooth and nyah-ful.
6. Don’t stop.

Ooops, forgot to say: find or start your own tune learning session. Not a “slow” session so much as a circle of musicians willing to learn tunes together at a reasonable pace for everyone. Where you also gradually work on bringing tunes up to speed.

A few years ago, my teacher took me aside before a session he led and said, in approximately so many words, “Listen. You need to follow me.”

And you know what? That’s all it took. After the next session, I played with more attentiveness to his playing and later asked him, “Was that better?” He told me, yes, there was no issue anymore. A year or two later I thanked him for that lesson, certainly among the most valuable he’d taught me, as by then I’d had some experience playing with folks who’d been playing 20, 30 years or more and still didn’t seem to realize they had to follow the person who had started the tune.

But I’ve seen “advice” given to numpties with considerably less success. I’m hesitant to give it, myself, as I feel I lack the authority to do so: I have much less skill and playing experience than some highly experienced and skilled players who simply don’t listen, and so I’m near the bottom of the pecking order at my local sessions. I did hear of a fellow musician who got frustrated with one of the more regular offenders, and told him that he sucked, and should stop coming to that session, and probably stop playing music altogether. Which failed on two counts: the offender was devastated, and when he did return, he was playing as insensitively as ever. Another confrontation, which I semi-witnessed (I saw the session leader approach the offender at the bar, but didn’t hear) resulted in an apologetic numpty who declared that he wasn’t going to return. Which was fine with the rest of us. But I’ve also seen overzealous musicians take non-numpties to task for utterly inoffensive behaviour that the former just happen not to like.

There’ve been threads on this board about whether sessions need leaders, and having been to many with and without, I’m coming down pretty firmly on the side of “yes”. Having an appropriate person specifically tasked with the role of maintaining an atmosphere that is respectful of the music is essential to maintaining the integrity of a session. In any case, it would cut down on the “Some eedjit at my last session [brought a digeridoo]|[accompanied every tune in C# minor on his 12-string guitar]|[bashed away on a full drum kit all night]!” threads.

Re: Numpties- Follow up discussion

Overcoming or minimizing one’s numptiness is mostly a matter of being socially aware and adept. Music adds another dimension to that, because you have to be able to socialize musically as well as all the other ways (verbally, body language, etc.). Which is where the dictate to “listen, listen, listen” comes in.

What helps me recover from my own numpty moments (some would call them prolonged interludes) is a reminder to *actively, accurately* listen to the specific music someone else is playing--rhythm, timing, pace, melody, harmonies (stated or implied)--and to play responsively to that. So it’s not just listening. To really play music *with* someone else, your listening has to be:

Active
Accurate
Specific
and
Responsive

Which does *not* spell ARSE, so when I catch myself drifting in a session, I think, “Don’t make an ARSE out of yerself” and that reminds me to be an active, accurate, specific, and responsive listener.
