↳ Posted by JimmyGibs on September 1st, 2019.

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood?

Depends … :-) different woods have different tones as the surface material has different reflective properties . Delrin being very hard reflective and impervious has a hard edge to it that would be absent on a boxwood flute for example . Also the surface on Delrin will be machined to leave a very polished surface so polished ABW which is also hard and reflective will sound similar but more mellow . If the surface is left rough and unpolished then the tonal qualities will be more complex , both as it’s not so reflective but also due to the turbulence created by the uneven surface .
Also relevant is whether tone holes are undercut or not , again due to turbulence .
Then of course every flute maker will make a different flute !!!!
I think it’s worth experimenting but would not suggest changing simply because the material is different ! Saying that I have an old ABW seery flute I could part with :-)

↳ Posted by Will Evans on September 1st, 2019.

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood? -- YES!

There have been studies concluding that the material is not a significant factor in the sound and playability of a flute. But my experience over forty years doesn’t bear that out. I haven’t ever played a Delrin flute that I thought was equal to the best wooden flutes, although I haven’t ever played a Delrin flute made by a major maker. It might be that makers don’t spend as much time hand-finishing a Delrin flute as they do on a wooden flute (polishing the bore, tuning, cutting the mouth hole). This is understandable. Delrin, by all accounts, is a nasty material to work with.

Most makers prefer to work with wood rather than with Delrin. Delrin turns differently and smells worse than wood as it’s being worked. The difference in price between a block of “raw” Delrin and a piece of wood is insignificant in terms of the final price. Would people pay many thousands of dollars for a used Delrin six-key flute made by a major maker? Would an Olwell or a Wilkes flute with silver keys and fancy silver bands fetch as high a price as a Wilkes or Olwell wooden flute? Would a player wait seven years for an Olwell Delrin flute, or 17 years for a Wilkes Delrin flute - even if the maker would agree to make a plastic flute?

I don’t know if the forgoing is relevant to your question regarding an upgrade to a wooden flute. But your use of “upgrade” indicates that you in fact consider wood to be a better material for a flute than plastic. And I would agree.

↳ Posted by Michael Eskin on September 1st, 2019.

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood?

I suggest making the decision on emotional rather than technical grounds, because you’ll find conflicting answers on synthetic vs. wood, and about any tonal differences between types of wood. I’ve heard beautiful music played on Delrin flutes as well as wood, but I play a wooden flute for largely emotional reasons. I’ve played wooden musical instruments all my life, and I like the look and the feel of wood under my fingers. I like the historical link to the past as well.

You’ll have a small amount of maintenance compared to Delrin, swabbing it out after every playing session. And you’ll have to be careful about humidity control. A small number of people have skin sensitivity to certain species of wood, and you won’t know about that until you try it.

One major advantage of wooden “Irish” flutes is that there is a much wider selection in both the new and used market compared to Delrin or other synthetics, especially if you decide to get a flute with keys. I bought my current keyed flute secondhand.

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood?

Please note that anything I say is only my opinion based on the idea that no one has shown me any compelling science. It is my belief that the material a flute is made of just doesn’t make all that much difference. I think the architecture, design, size and shape of the holes, the conical bore, the quality, precision and care of manufacture do. Of course my mind is always open to the science. The makers of fine flutes seem to choose wood, exotic woods at that, as the medium for their art. Others may choose wood because it’s easier to work with (so I’ve been told). Flute players are endlessly variable with the tones they can achieve. Some years ago as a brand new flute player I was told the tone I had on my delrin flute “needed improvement” and that playing my fine wooden flute made no difference. Turns out I sounded just as bad on a friends wonderful Grinter, another’s Copeland, another’s Le Hart, and yet another’s Hamilton. As far as I can tell flutes made by just about anyone would sound mostly the same regardless of the material they chose. The player along with the maker’s skill makes the difference. Does anybody believe that Matt, Conal, Kevin, Hammy, Grey, and a host of others would less than stellar because the were playing plastic? Would an Olwell in Blackwood sound the same as another, less skilled maker’s, flute also in Blackwood? So yeah, I think finer flutes sound better, not because they’re wood, but the artists who make them choose wood as their medium.

Something else to consider (in my opinion) is the notion that in anywhere there is more than one of anything, paint colors, flavors of ice-cream or single-malt scotch) and yes the sound a flute makes, will find arguments about which one is “better”. I applaud you Jimmy for feeling that a better flute is in the cards for you and ,at the same time recognizing you aren’t in a place where you could definitively tell what “better” is. Be patent and maybe re-visit the issue when you can play better than your flute can. Then choose an instrument based on the way it feels in your hand and the sound YOU think is best. I’ve always there are 2 reasons for buying (or doing) anything, the real reason and the reason you think best justifies your choice when you explain it to others. Stay in the reality lane. It’s likely that you have more to do with the sound you make than the material.

↳ Posted by Chuck Cochrane on September 2nd, 2019.

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood?

If you’ve been playing for a couple of years then it may well be time to think about your next flute. IMHO the first flute is usually chosen before the player has enough experience to know and judge what they really want from a flute. I have a couple of superb flutes that I don’t play (and should really sell…) which have been pushed aside in favour of the flute that I now mainly play. I just like it better…

Like the question; “What is the best malt whisky?”, there is more than one answer to the search for the best flute and indeed it is more a matter of “How can I find _my_ flute?”. Start by trying every flute you can - be shameless about asking other players if you can try theirs. At worst, they can say no. Start by setting your target as building up your experience on as many different flutes as possible - find out which you like and why. Keep notes if it helps you. Flute events often have a lot of flutes that can be tried - my favourite was one of a bunch of flutes made by Windward sitting on a table at Boxwood (in 2007?) and I played around with all of them and really liked that one. My current flute is the keyed descendant of that flute - requested from the same maker to be as similar to the keyless as possible. I probably spent about 50 minutes playing that flute alone both in a cupboard and outside, and probably at least an hour on all the others on the table together. Very nice of Forbes and Yola to let me.

At some time in this process you will stumble across a flute that is available and that you immediately like. Buy it. Whatever it is made of.

↳ Posted by Will Evans on September 2nd, 2019.

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood?

>The science absolutely supports a different tone from different materials.

It certainly doesn’t. One can make reasonable arguments based on physics for there being a difference and there not being a difference. The only way to know is to do the experiments and as far as I know none done so far have shown any such difference.

Of course makers and players do *believe* there is a difference and it would be foolish to discount their intimate experience with such instruments. But again, science supplies us with plausible reasons why players and makers might be fooled into believing differences exist when they do not.

The experiments that have been done have been criticised - perhaps rightly, I don’t know - and more work could be done. Will it really help though?

At this point in time all we can really say is that the difference is not large, but most good makers and players think that wooden flutes are superior.

↳ Posted by Calum on September 7th, 2019.

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood?

“Vent hole height matters, so they’d have to redesign.”

That seems to be one of the major reasons why Delrin flutes aren’t thinner to reduce weight. The 19th Century “Irish” wooden flute has a tone hole placement and undercutting for intonation that was carefully worked out over a period of time. A Delrin flute made with a thinner wall to reduce weight couldn’t use undercutting, so the tone holes might be a different sizes or spacing. Since the weight difference isn’t *that* significant between Delrin and wood, it’s just easier to base the design on wooden flutes where this has all been worked out a century and a half ago. It also makes it easier for a beginner to start on a Delrin flute and transition to a wooden flute later, if the tone holes aren’t too different.

There was a discussion about this recently in the C&F forum: “What happens if you decrease the wall thickness?” http://forums.chiffandfipple.com/viewtopic.php?f=2&t=108921

Re: Should I upgrade from a delrin flute to wood?

Just have to posit as someone who dabbles with a little qualitative research in her regular life that sometimes quantitative research just cannot adequately cover or delineate certain topics. This I would have to say would be one of them.

Sound and sound quality are NOT, I repeat not fully objective phenomenon, but rather based first in the psychology of hearing and perception, and also in the ever more psychologically difficult to research quantitatively areas of music production and preference. Qualitative research is being used more and more because science is starting to catch up with the limits of quantitative research and starting to understand that people actually can be very good reporters of their own experiences if you give them the chance.

All that preamble is to say that yes, Wood makes a difference. I’ve played classical flute since I was like ten, and I can guarantee you that you cannot put the cut and build of a professional level Muramatsu or Powell on a “nickel silver” and get the same sound that you can on a “solid silver” And wood does make a difference, to be fair to carbon, there are some models that come really really close, but I still get the best sound on wood. And though, alas, it is only an anecdote, a session member switching between Delrin and Wood had a better tone on the wood as well.

Also, a wonderful question to ask any bit of research done, is, who funded it and why? I don’t know in this case with these studies and perhaps they were totally un-financially biased. It is just such an interesting finding that goes so against the grain of so many people’s individual experiences trying instruments with different materials.
