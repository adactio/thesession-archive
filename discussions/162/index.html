Re: Is comhaltas a good thing or not?

I think -- was it Joe? or Will? -- had a very good point to make in another thread on a similar subject. And that was that both ends of the tradition vs. innovation spectrum provide very necessary balance.

People and organizations such as Comhaltas serve a very important function. They slow the speed of changes to the traditional form, because, let’s face it, as long as it’s a living form, it’s going to change, but to stay traditional it has to be a slow change. They preserve the knowledge of the old way of doing things. Especially since there are now Irish traditional musicians who have no inbred (as it were) knowledge of the culture from which it sprang (and I include myself in that number, although I’m constantly learning), this function becomes very very important. Irish music is almost inseparable from the Irish culture. As it widens out into a global form of music, it runs the risk of becoming less and less Irish. Whether or not that’s a good thing is a matter of opinion. But it is important to know back history and what came before you in order to effectively change the tradition.

Michael Flatley is a great example of that. For those of us who teach stepdancing, Riverdance and all of the following shows has been a great boon and also a pain in the feckin’ arse -- students want to dance Irish, which is great, and dance just like in the shows, which is not so great, because most of them want to start with hardshoe, and often don’t understand why we won’t let them.

Music that is not traditional but is based off the traditional tunes or formats are NOT bad. They are simply not traditional. There’s nothing wrong with that. There’s nothing wrong with hybrids. There’s nothing wrong with jazz or bluegrass or country western (and boy howdy, if you want to hear arguments that far eclipse any of the ones we’ve ever gotten into on this subject, try getting a mainstream jazz musician to accept someone like Kenny G. as a jazz musician). One of my favorite groups at the moment is Lunasa. All of those musicians are excellent players, and they are making wonderful music that is not traditional. I listen to them and I love them, but I am also aware that it’s not strictly traditional. There’s nothing bad about that. But I know the difference.

I am a Chinese American woman. There’s people out there who think it’s wrong for me to be a stepdancer -- not just weird, but WRONG. I don’t pay much attention to them unless I have to. My rule of thumb is: know what came before you and be aware of when you’re changing things to make your own music -- but always make your music your own, traditional or not. I suspect folks like Eileen Ivers and Liz Carroll feel the exact same way.

Heh. Well, I feel that I should make it clear, though -- right now, I am necessarily focused on the traditional way of doing things. As someone who was not surrounded by the stuff, steeped in it as it were, from an early age, I have to do it that way. (Some Irish people have to do this as well, actually.) I have to become a dinosaur, because I have no basis on which to build a new Irish tradition upon -- I don’t have the old one down yet! I can’t go changing the tradition in any valuable way yet -- I don’t know the old ways well enough yet. And I may never, but we’ll give it the old college try.

It’s a subject with a LOT of ins, outs, and contradictions, much like the Irish themselves. For instance, the Michael Flatley thing is incredibly complicated. It’s sometimes very difficult to tell a student that, no, they can’t do THAT or THIS while they dance, because, well, Michael Flatley did it, so it should be correct, right? Well, as a matter of fact, no, it’s not, unless you happen to be Michael Flatley dancing in Lord of the Dance or whatever. If you’re a student under a TCRG, you have to dance it THIS way or THAT way, not some hybrid of American tap or jazz with Irish stepdance.

So, I think Comhaltas is a very good thing, but as Backer says, not the ultimate authority. Just *one* ultimate authority. *grin*

Niamh, you have touched on some difficult questions.
Limiting/defining the tradition: Comholtas resists playing up the neck… I have a recording of Eugene O’Donnell playing fiddle in harmony and up the neck. I think he is very traditional, and further he won the all Ireland dance 6 times. He knows the tradition. What tradition is Comhaltas trying to protect? Whose interests does it serve?
There are traditions much older than comhaltas, and a tremendous variation in the styles of Irish fiddlers, yet these styles and traditions have been defined quite narrowly. For me, the question is whether they are trying to preserve history, or rewrite history, whether they are trying to revive the music, or trying to recreate the music.

From another perspective, here in Ontario, Canada, the fiddle contests demand classical technique, vibrato, position playing. Without it, a player cannot win! So here, people are saying the opposite to what you say, "why do we need such skills, it isn’t part of our tradition? It all comes down to trying to find the perfect balance, and there is no perfect balance. Everyone has different values, everyone wants something different. Those who arrange the contests must feel as if they are caught in a tug-of-war between the two camps on this question, and on every other question as well.

In putting the question to ‘The Session’, I suspect that you have already made some decisions. In a sense, we all have. I think of my self as a traditionalist. I often see something that doesn’t sit right with my experience, and I ask, “whose tradition,… that isn’t *my* tradition!” My tradition encompasses all those who play the music, because for 300 years people have taken different approaches to the music. Our heroes are people who were DIFFERENT. Being different has been part of the tradition for the past 300 years, and will be part of the tradition for the next 300 years.

And for anyone who doesn’t agree with this, you have every right to be wrong! :O)

Re: Is comhaltas a good thing or not?

You know, rigid parameters have never been conducive to learning, for me, personally. I was classically trained in piano for years, then in singing - Royal Conservatory. Grades, contests, exams, recitals… For me it was stifling and discouraging. Frustrating, boring, you name it. As for the contests, it was never as satisfying to win as it was disappointing and discouraging not to, and it did always come down to some crusty old bag’s personal taste in the end. I used to dread the contests as an excercise in public failure as a kid. I am no less baffled now about why anyone would want to compete or be evaluated than I ever was. I would never do it again, and I wouldn’t recommend it to anyone.

There are, however, many who would beg to differ. For every skill, you’ll find a contest if you look. (Even just for being pretty!) And you’ll find a hundred eager contenders to be the ________est _______er in the group. Good for them. It obviously gives them some satisfaction or they wouldn’t do it, n‘est pas? Leave ’em to it. I prefer musical co-operation and non-discriminatory support (despite discriminating tastes). Just because it’s not for me doesn’t make it a bad thing. It’s just one more club in life I choose not to join. I do believe it’s widely understood that comholtas teaches with very rigid and somewhat formal parameters, and that’s where you go if you want to learn how to play Irish music exactly as Comoltas has decided is the “traditional” and “authentic” way to play it. If that’s not for you, don’t bother with it. Why wait to be “kicked out”?

Maybe this all sound obvious and trite. I’m no more willing to evaluate whether Comholtas is a “good” thing or a “bad” thing than I am to let them evaluate me. Later I’m going to go have a pint and play some tunes with some friends for fun. While I’m doing that, someone else will be doing technique drills at home, boning up for a contest. They exist, I exist, they have a musical community, I have a musical community - to say my way is “better” plants me firmly in theirs. Isn’t that ironic?

Calling old Bach on the rules - now THAT takes big b***s! LOL!

Speaking of Bach, there are musical purists today who say that Bach’s music should be played without dynamic variations because that’s how it was done in his day. As far as I know, none of these foks were there at the time. Yes, the harpsichord and clavinet were incapable of dynamics, but not so the organ, violin, and many other instruments. I’d be willing to bet that the Chaconne was played with great feeling and dynamic response. Also, when the first true pianofortes - with the ability to be played dynamically -appeared near the end of Bach’s life, he was fascinated by them.

All of which is a long-winded way of saying that musical progress - and perhaps all human growth and evolution - has been achieved by a handful of geniuses struggling against a vast tide of self-proclaimed “authorities” trying with all their might to prevent all change in the interest of maintaining their position of power. Galileo comes to mind…

Some upstart once challenged Beethoven on a series of parallel fifths in one of his pieces - a major no-no in Western classical music. Ludwig Van responded, “I allow them.” I’m no Beethoven, but I like that answer! (Of course, he also knew “the rules” backwards and forwards!)

I’m interested too, Dceol. Please do comment, as Will has asked!

Long story to share on the subject:

The competition issue is a very interesting and oft-discussed subject around our dance school. We often remind the kids (and adults!) that when they’re competing at a feis, it’s important to have the mindset that you are NOT competing with the other dancers -- you ARE competing with yourself. We ask them to try to have the mindset that it’s not winning the medals that’s important, although that’s fun, because your strong points may not be what an adjudicator wants to see (if you’re a lyrical, graceful dancer, and the judge likes athletic power, you’re not going to place). What’s important is that you endeavor to dance the best you’ve ever danced at each feis. It hasn’t much to do with a “pecking order” of dancers.

One of my most frustrating but most rewarding students ever was an adult who has real problems with physical coordination -- she told me once that she was almost two years old before she managed to start walking. She is highly intelligent, with several degrees and works as an engineer, but has a terrible time getting her body to do what she wants it to do -- the mind knows what it wants, but the body has a disconnect somewhere.

I sat with that student in a class largely made up of only herself for over a year, going over the same six steps, over and over and over again, an hour each week, then two hours each week. Though she got very frustrated with her body, she never took it out on herself or me or any other student -- she got discouraged once or twice, but we told her that the day *we* gave up on her, then she was allowed to give up on herself, and that we never would.

The day she passed into Beginner Two was so rewarding for both herself and me. I’ll never treasure the memory of a champion dancer placing a medal the way I do the day she happily tested out and passed into the next level. She is currently working her way through the Beginner Two curriculum, again, up to ten or twelve times slower than other students.

Insofar as I’m concerned, this dancer is as much a champion as any girl of 16 competing at World’s.

To reply to Zina about what you have to play. At senior level, Over 18, you must play four contrasting tunes, usually a jig, hornpipe, set dance and reel. The jig can be a double, single or slip jig, the reel can also be a polka. You cannot as far as I Know play more than one jig or reel. Emphasis is placed on rhythm and melodic variations. You must play the tune twice over. Wills story is not uncommon and is always enjoyed as many technically good musicians might not fair out too well when asked to a session.

Re: An Ordinary Life

Zina, this is _folk_ music afterall, and not too many folk can earn a living as professional musicians (because demand is higher for plumbers). This is changing now that it’s relatively easy and cheap to record and market ourselves, so more plumbers and computer programmers and dentists are indulging their dreams of musical fame and acutally pocketing some income from it. I’d wager, though that the musical hacks--who also happen to be excellent plumbers, computer programmers, and dentists--will always outnumber the truly talented players.

I count myself among the hacks because I know what I sound like, and I wouldn’t pay good money for a cd that featured my playing. Which is not to say that I can’t fit right in at most sessions, or enjoy performing local gigs, or even that I don’t have something to contribute, musically. If I have any talents, though, they lie in teaching and encouraging others, and carrying the tunes--at last week’s local session, several people were struggling to remember tunes we haven’t played in years, and I managed to get them started each time. One of the other players smiled and slagged me about how they keep me around as their beer-fueled portable tune storage system…. :-).

To bring this back to Comhaltas and competitions….there’s a difference between playing the notes and playing music that moves people. (My music usually moves people toward the exit, *wink, wink*, though once or twice a year I pull out a genuinely fun ride for people within earshot of the fiddle.) I think the emotive aspect tends to get repressed when people compete and focus too much on maintaining certain standards, but some of those same people can play with real fire and heart when the pressure’s not so high. I prefer listening to _them_ play.
