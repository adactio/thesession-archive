Is our hunger for the “sensational” in ITM making us forget the traditional?

All,
I found this interesting quote in the New Yorker over the weekend. It’s by Adam Gopnik writing on Magic, not Irish music. But I thought it was apropos…and if you substitute “music” for the word “magic” [although maybe they are one and the same] you come out with a disturbing portrait of our modern times and taste, especially where it concerns the whole idea of tarting up Irish music in more and more silly “arrangements” played at warp speeds. He obviously feels that to defend tradition [in the world of ‘old style’ magic] is to be classed as an old fart. Could not the same be said of the world of ITM in the last few decades?

His quote:

…“something big and vital is passing from the world, and yet [the feeling is] that to defend it is to be immediately classed as retrograde…..the way forward…lies not in mastering the tricks but in mastering the mind of the modern age with its relentless appetite for speed and for the sensational-dressed-as-the-real….[And I had] the urge to say what all of us would like to say -- that tradtions are not just encumbrances, that a novel is not news, that an essay is a diffferent thing from an Internet rant, that techniques are the probity and the ethic of magic, the real work.”

I’ve asked the same question…maybe in a more esoteric form.

Regrouping from previous discussions, the sense I got was as far as the popular stuff (what I have previously referred to as ‘faux Irish’) its gonna happen, it does draw uninformed people to the traditional music form (a more philisophical or cynical sort might start drawing paralells to using specific materials to attract flies, in an attempt to increase the popularity of Dressage), and somebody out there must love it otherwise they wouldn’t send so much money to PBS for all those DVDs, CDs, tote bags and coffee mugs. There have also been arguments made that this somehow jogs the memory and sentimentality of many who have immigrated and have few other ITM options. The follow up comment is that those of us playing seriously, should hold our noses when this stuff is played during Public TV pledge breaks and Irishesque cruises to the Bahamas, and keep playing our music the way it should be played.

I second ceolachen, but accept the reality that dancers dressed like Druids, acompanied by over reverbed drums being played by pony-tailed guys dressed in skin tight leather pants and white strap tees (in my neighborhood when I was growing up they were called ’Dago-Tees"-information aside, not that I would ever use the phrase given it being very disrespectful of a nation with superb accordion craftspeople), while some poor lady fiddle player is forced to jump about while playing something at a 240 to 320 metronome setting…..Sells DVDs and CDs that can be shamelessly hawked by Public televison.
