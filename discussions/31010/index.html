Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

I also played the highland pipes before learning uilleann pipes and whistle. The best advice I could give you, I think, is to treat the whistle as a different instrument, and not to try to adapt your piping to the whistle. Dig out those Clarkes, or buy another one, and set about learning one or two Irish tunes.
A low D is a big stretch compared to a pipe chanter. You could maybe get a Bb whistle, which is about the same as the pipes, and see how you get on. If you can manage a few tunes, then maybe think about a low D if you still want to.

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

Welcome, Andrew!

“Why the key of D?”
D is the most common in Irish traditional music (ITM) -- if you want to play with others -- in a session, for example -- you won’t much use a Bb whistle. The most common uilleann pipes are in the key of D, the most common whistles are D, & the majority of Irish tunes you’ll run across can be played on a D whistle.

“An earlier post recommended a Bb, since the finger spacing was similar to what I’m used to on the bagpipes”
Yes, the finger spacing on a Bb or an A whistle would be closer to what you’re used to… but you won’t use these as much playing with others, & if you want to learn tunes from recordings or sheet music or ABC notation, you’re get further faster starting on a D whistle.

So, it partly depends on how you plan to learn most of your tunes…. & partly on what you want to do with the music once you have it….

Where are you located? If you’re in the States, take a look at Jerry Freeman’s “tweaked” whistles, which just means he takes inexpensive, mass-produced whistles & makes changes that result in a consistently better sound. With a regular inexpensive whistle, it can be hit or miss whether you get a good one. Yet with a tweaked whistle, you’re not out lots of cash like you are with a high-end whistle.
http://stores.ebay.com/freemanwhistles

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

Just a couple of points:- you will be used to playing -- or rather, learning -- tunes from pipe music; so you will have to re-learn your staff. Once you realize that your bottom note is D not A (this applies to all whistle music, regardless of the key of the whistle} you are halfway there; but you have a bit of un-learning to do.
Another, perhaps more important, thing is that Irish sheet music does not contain the information necessary to play the tune. The best sheet music is that which contains the least information necessary to convey the general melody, and the only way to make it sound right is by listening to what other players do. The sheet music is a rough guide at best, and is often rather misleading, so be very careful when using it. Good luck, and enjoy your new-found freedom!

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

Andrew, I’m afraid I’m going to be a contrarian and suggest that you go straight for the low whistle if that’s where your interest lies. The breath and hold requirements between a low and high D are so different that I’m not persuaded you’re really going to get any benefit by taking the intermediate step. I suggest you: a) take a look at Chiff & Fipple (which is always carrying discussions about taking up the low whistle), b) choose a couple of whistle makers that catch your eye, c) give them a call, see whose advice resonates and buy accordingly, d) once you get it use C&F again for advice on holds, and e) take any tune you know by heart and try to play it.

During the Eighties, when there wasn’t anything like the range of low whistles and teaching materials around nowadays, most low whistle players (including plenty that weren’t high whistle players to start with) learnt by winding and rewinding Moving Hearts tapes and copying Davy. You could do worse. Good luck.

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

This is what Cath Shaw (from Shaw whistles) once wrote me explaining the tuning of their tin whistles, especially concerning the C natural:

"The whistles were always conceived as a diatonic instrument operating within their own pitch.
This means that they have a Just temperament tuning rather than equal temperament, as befits a folk instrument.
This means that the third and the sixth are a little flat of equal temperament.
The sharp seventh is a little flat to enable a reasonable natural seventh.
Were the metal about five times thicker this wouldn’t be necessary as there would be an adequate mass of air in the holes to control the cross fingering.
A whistle is a compromise instrument and the c natural on a d whistle perfectly demonstrates the extent of the compromise."

Hope this helps.

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

Agree with most of what has been said above - so just to expand.

Q. Why a D whistle?

Clearly, you will be able to play in D-Major (two sharps) - but you will also be able to play in the E-Dorian and A-Mixolydian scales, as these modes also have two sharps.

C-natural is obtainable on a D whistle by using cross-fingering , so you will be able play in G-Major (one sharp). But you will also be able to play in the A-Dorian and D-Mixolydian scales, as these modes also have one sharp.

That gives you six keys. Not you absolutely all that you need to play ITM, but it will cover you for most of what normally crops up in ITM sessions.

You can also play in A-Major (and the related modal keys) on a D whistle by half-holing to obtain the necessary G-Sharp - but that is more difficult, especially at speed. Also, quite a few ITM tunes are hexatonic (with a missing seventh) - so if they happen to be in A, you won’t need a G-Sharp anyway.

Bb whistles (and whistles in other keys) are mostly used for solo work

Q. Why high D?

(a) Easier to learn on (b) much cheaper. When you are sure that you are ready, you can move to a low D.

Q. Why a cheap high D?

It’s normally bad advice to suggest that a beginner should buy a cheap instrument, but that’s not really true for whistles. The difference between a cheap one and a really expensive one is relatively small - evidenced by the fact that quite a few professional players use cheap instruments, as had been stated above.

But do check the pitch when you buy one - they do vary quite a lot within brand. If the shop won’t let you try before you buy, go to a different shop that will!

Good luck!

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

One point I should make Andrew is that you say you’re not really interested in the whistle for playing at Irish sessions, and this site is in fact geared towards people to whom playing at Irish sessions is their main pursuit.

Another site, Chiff & Fipple, has a larger proportion of people who play whistle for other purposes, and people who are interested in the whistle for its own sake.

In any case, just as when you started on the Highland pipes, getting some instruction on whistle could only do you good. For one thing an experienced player could check out your whistles and discover which are OK as they are and which need either to be “tweaked” or be tossed. A “newbie” whistle player who goes out and buys a couple inexpensive whistles at random is like the person wants to get into Highland pipes and buys a set of pipes at random and takes them out of the box and tries to play them without any guidance; they’re not going to get very far, but an experience piper could get their pipes “set up” in short order.

There are many tricks to “tweak” or fix a whistle which isn’t playing well, as many tricks as there are to getting a Highland chanter playing right.

Anyhow a lot of the advice above is good. I’ve been playing Highland pipes for nearly 40 years and Irish whistle and uilleann pipes for nearly as long. One thing: on the small whistles most players use the endjoints of their fingers for all holes, while on the big whistles such as Low D the middle joint is used on both hands for the index and middle fingers, the endjoint for the ring fingers. Thus the finger posture is different from the Highland pipes.

As people have hinted at, unlike Highland piping there’s really no available sheet music which gives Irish tunes arranged exactly for the various specific instruments whistle, uilleann pipes, flute, fiddle, accordion, etc. Rather, Irish music collections give generic versions which players of any instrument can use to learn the “bare bones” of the tunes, and the individual musician is expected to flesh out the tunes to suit his own instrument and personal style.

Unlike Highland pipes there’s no “one correct way” to play any tune, or ornament, and likewise there’s no “one correct fingering” for many of the notes on whistle, flute, and uilleann pipes. Traditional players use a variety of ornaments and styles and fingerings.

If you want to go for the Low D, I say go for it! Perhaps the easiest-playing inexpensive Low D is the moulded plastic conical-body Tony Dixon. I have one and it plays very sweetly. It’s also more ergonomic than most Low Ds, due to the conical bore.

I myself play MK Low Ds for their complex tone and big volume.

About cracked mouthpieces on Generations and other “cheapies”, when they have a split where the brass body goes, I wrap the plastic with a binding of strong thread, then soak the thread in superglue to make a solid strong mass. They can’t split any more!

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

Be aware that there’s a difference in the Irish Traditional Music (ITM) world between having a variety of choices and “anything goes”.

There’s a wide variety in traditional styles, but they’re still all traditional styles… if a person from a GHB background or a Classical Flute/Recorder background shows up an an Irish session, and attempts to play Irish whistle by transferring their style from their previous instrument, they will stick out like a sore thumb, because of their unidiomatic approach.

Same with the GHB, when you have “Sax guy” pick up the pipes and transfer his sax fingerings and style onto the pipes… he won’t fool any piper for a moment!

The finger you use to cut isn’t very important. What matters is that your articulation is crisp and clean. You’re looking for a quick pop or chirp that breaks the notes apart without sounding like another note. In a workshop, Brian Finnegan told us he really likes the sound of oxx|xxx cuts because they’re poppy. I happen to use the same fingerings as AlBrown. Using the finger directly above the note sounded is acceptable (I do it for B and G) but IMO a bit harder to master than cutting with a hole higher on the whistle because finger-above cuts tend to sound like notes if you’re not careful.

Are you Andy Ross the reedmaker, here in California? Because if you are, I would gladly trade whistle lessons for a few reeds!! I played in a band with Steve Megarity for a couple years.

Anyhow about specifics about whistle gracenotes and so forth, I can tell you what I do, which is what I picked up from Irish traditional players when I started the whistle back in the 1970s. Perhaps the style has changed since then.

Anyhow what they did, mostly, back then was use the upperhand ringfinger to “cut” all the lowerhand notes; it’s the gracenote which on the GHB would be an “E gracenote”. So, this gracenote is more or less the “default” gracenote (as opposed to the GHB’s upperhand indexfinger gracenote, High G, which is their default or go-to gracenote).

So, Bottom D, E, F#, and G are all cut, usually, with that A gracenote.
A can be cut with either of the two remaining fingers, and there’s only one choice for cutting B!

(BTW Irish players use the term “cut” where GHB players would say “upper gracenote”.)

The staple Irish articulation is the “roll” which is a melody note repeated three times, first plain, then cut, then patted. (A “pat” is what GHB players would call a “lower gracenote”.)

The style is thus very different from GHB style.

Say you’re playing Paddy’s Leather Breeches. On the GHB you have three melody notes which are articulated with the normal GDE gracenote pattern. On an Irish instrument you would play the first note WITHOUT any gracenote, and in fact would often “bend” or “glide” up to that first note for a nice smooth bluesy attack; then the second melody note is created with a cut, the third with a pat: note CUT note PAT note.

(On the GHB it would be in effect CUT note CUT note CUT note.)

In many reels and jigs there are few if any “ornaments” per se, but rather the music uses rolls to propel itself forward, just as GHB reels and jigs often use the GDE pattern to propel itself forward.

Re: Beginner whistle player … where to start?

“This is where I am getting a bit confused, since there doesn’t appear to be any true structure or rules to how the music is played.” -- There is true structure and rules about how Irish music SOUNDS, not exactly which finger you cut with to get there. There is also a lot of freedom within the structure for individual expression, making the so-called rules of Irish music a bit nebulous. There is a basic understanding among Irish players as to what’s pure drop and what isn’t, though Irish music is a living tradition so its definition is constantly shifting and changing.

“What’s stopping a GHB player from integrating traditional Highland gracenoting into whistle technique?” I don’t know names of GHB techniques, but the ones with clumps of grace notes right on the beat aren’t used at all in Irish music. I don’t think the rhythm would fit. Seems like I’ve heard GHB techniques that have the rhythm of crans or double-cut rolls and those could work in an Irish context. Traditional Highland gracenoting also uses gracenotes to separate everything by necessity, while on whistle you should be mixing in tonguing/glottals with the finger articulations to get your rhythm.

A lot of the grace-notes you play on GHB simply don’t work on the whistle. ‘g’ grace-notes, for example. Obviously anything using low ‘g’ such as a taorluath or a birl is impossible. g-d-e sometimes works depending on the whistle (‘g’ often simply doesn’t sound) but you rarely if ever hear it in Irish trad.
What I did when I started was just to try everything, listening very carefully to the result, and gradually eliminate the things that didn’t work -- which was most of it :( .
Your ‘e’ doubling transferred to the right hand becomes a cran, so you might spend a few years developing that (only joking -- a few months might do). Other than that, just try various cuts and taps with all possible combinations, and then use the ones that sound best.
I think the reason for the lack of standardisation is that the instruments are all different; but you will find a refreshing freedom once you get your GHB corset off.

I haven’t seen Grey Larsen’s book, but I did attend a workshop he gave, many years ago, and he made it clear that his approach to cuts and pats and rolls were not things he picked up from older traditional players, but rather things of his own devising, based on logic.

In my quest to learn traditional Irish music back in the 1970s I always took the (as one might call it) the ethnomusicological approach, in other words I would listen to what the old Irish traditional players did and try to copy it as best I could, without questioning “why” they played the way they did.

However I would, from time to time, run into American players who took what I call the “Yankee ingenuity” or “American know-how” approach, that old ingrained Yankee mindset of rejecting what was done “over there” in the old days and using clearheaded Yankee thinking and logic to devise a “better way” to do things.

So, Grey told us that he had created a system of cuts based on logic. It so happened that his system was different from the way I had seen every traditional player I had encountered do it, so I rejected his system and stuck to the old ways.

Anyhow about the term “pats”, let’s see…

“Pats are gracenotes intended to sound below a melody note; in nearly all cases the pat is produced by ”patting“ one finger on its hole, thus sounding the note just below the melody note.”

Dennis Brooks, The Irish Union Pipes Tutor

In one uilleann piping journal, the feature which contains blurbs of criticism or praise is called “Cuts and Pats”.

I have heard “tap” for a lower gracenote but amongst pipers “pat” is the more common term.

Anyhow Andrew very few GHB embellishments transfer to the whistle. It’s better, in my opinion, to get away from that sort of approach and rather approach the whistle as an entirely new instrument with its very own style. One of the very few GHB bits of fingering that I do on whistle and uilleann pipes is what GHB players call “the bubbly note”, which is a lowerhand indexfinger cut followed by a lowerhand middlefinger cut. I’ve found this to be a nice, useful ornament on E, the lowerhand ringfinger and littlefinger being held open the whole time (not closed as for the “bubbly note”).
