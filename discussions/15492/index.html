Re: enjoyment for all?

Well, the question really is: What does the pub owner envision for this session? After all, he/she is the one who’s granting space for it -- and perhaps even paying money -- so he/she has the ultimate say as to what form the event takes. Ideally, the owner should communicate his/her desire to the musicians, paid or not, and then it’s the latter’s responsibility to fulfill it as best as possible.

Does the owner just want a “quiet,” atmospheric music session that doesn’t take over the room?
If that’s the case, he/she probably would like to make it a “closed” session, i.e., confined to whoever is hired to play. In this instance, the customer’s interest in, and enjoyment of, the music is regarded as incidental: If they don’t appear to hear it while they’re there, fine; if they pay attention and like it, that’s a bonus. Which means it’s incumbent upon the musicians to decide whether they can expand the session at all to include other players and still retain the character desired by the owner.

Does the owner want a music session that is truly customer-friendly, where anyone -- musician, vocalist, dancer -- can join in as they see fit, and are even encouraged to do so?
If so, the owner likely doesn’t care so much about the quality of the music as the degree of participation for all present. That, therefore, will/should be the governing philosophy for the musicians who anchor the session: OK, maybe we play pretty much the most well-known tunes and songs, and we don’t worry about whether we’re all in tune, or playing in unison; everyone plays, everyone wins.

Now, there are certainly degrees and variants to these scenarios, and in some cases the owner may not really care that much about what kind of a session it is, as long as hosting it doesn’t prove to be a detriment to the establishment.
But it seems to me that maybe 90 percent, maybe more, of the answer to bodhran bliss’s question is found in the pub owner.

Lollypolly, have you played in Irish sessions much?

One of the benefits of people coming together around one genre of music (in which everyone plays the same tunes together, in unison, not swapping solos), is that it helps focus the (musical, at least) expectations a bit.

The best sessions are when you can break down the barriers between listener and player, when everyone joins in on the choruses, where the music stays on track without anyone feeling like they have to take things in a particular direction, when people can join in or drop out without any adverse effect--folks playing music with folks and enjoying themselves. Of course, it doesn’t always head that way of its own accord, and a little steering is required from time to time. Re: enjoyment for all?

I am on the fence on this debate, to some extent. By their very nature, and Irish session is “cliquey”. As llig recently stated:

“Me and my mates have a peculiar hobby. We like to play music from a very tightly defined sub-genre. We go out to the pub and indulge our hobby. And we are not interested in people who are not familiar with this tightly defined sub-genre. And if anyone tries to invade our evening with something out with our self-penned remit, well, after a few civil and polite warnings, we may well be rude to them.”

Yep… There is a picture of that in the dictionary next to “clique” - and it is a well justified clique, IMO. There’s a fine line between being open and welcoming to musicians, and having a free-for-all that wouldn’t be fun (for many of us, at least).

So in that regard, the relationship between musicians and punters is even more open than with other musicians. The punters are part of the equation of a pub session, whether you like it or not. And while I don’t CARE whether there are punters there, whether they’re paying attention, or whether they like what we’re doing, they’re still part of the equation.

And it makes for a more enjoyable session if that particular part of the equation happens to be enjoying the music (as opposed to yelling at the rugby match on the tube so loud that we can’t hear each other, for instance). You can be open and welcoming to the punters without “performing” (I know… that’s a can of worms kind of statement that may come back to bite me), and I personally think that’s a pretty good policy. If you help make the punters feel welcome, you’re helping the establishment, and as sts mentioned, the pub and their wishes are part of the equation too.

Re: enjoyment for all?

I said this many times before: a session is whatever the people who established it want it to be -- even if they change what they want it to be. The simple point being that visitors and participants shouldn’t be enforcing their own preconceptions or misconceptions on it.

I’m big into trad, and sessions I’m hosting feature primarily trad tunes and songs, but I’m flexible too. If I ask someone for a song and they want to sing a Dylan song -- I have no problem, but if someone comes along and starts singing Dylan songs one after another uninvited -- I ask them to stop. If they want to sing Dylan songs informally at a pub, I have no problem with that -- but they should start their own session on another night.

It’s very simple -- sus out what the session is -- join in if you have something to add -- stay away if what you want to add will detract. It’s common courtesy and has nothing to do with cliques. I would consider it a clique if people were exclusive for personal reasons that have nothing to do with the music. If people have closed sessions -- that might be a “clique” because it would even exclude people who are capable of adding to what the session is. You can have a clique within and open session if people are ignoring anyone outside of an elite group. But this is subtler and again has little to do with the music.
