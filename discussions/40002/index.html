The Nature of Sessions

Fiddle Aunt’s thread on competitiveness in sessions follows in a line of threads that address various aspects of what can ruin a session. I thought it might be of interest to start a thread on the nature of sessions, or put another way, how Irish sessions differ from what is loosely called jamming in other genres of music.

Most jamming includes the following:

Solo breaks
accompaniment
improvisation
harmony

Jamming is largely about taking a melody as a jumping-off point to express musical ideas that favor the instrument being played, the creativity of the individuals and the embellishment of what the singer and/or instrumentalist who called the song/tune is doing.

Absolutely NONE of the foregoing exists in an Irish session (except for the kind of accompaniment supplied by a guitar, bodhran, etc.). In a session, the tune is the be-all and end-all. The expectation of the players is to

Know the tune
Be able to play the tune at a reasonable tempo
Keep redundancy of instrumentation in check by either not playing every tune or playing softer
Be capable of starting a set
Create or choose sets wherein tunes segue well
Be able to keep up with the ability level of the group at large
Continually be learning new tunes
Knowing when to sit out

While there is general agreement (I think) to these factors, their existence can sometimes lead to individuals at a given session to have a bit of a chip on their shoulder. As an example (and I did a thread on this), I was recently admonished by a visitor to our session that I should limit playing tunes that are in minor keys. I don’t know where he got that, and the members here were pretty much appalled, but there you are. Some people get a notion and feel that their ability and experience gives them a license to either say or do things that insult or intimidate others in the session. It is by no means widespread in terms of the numbers, but it only takes one, especially if they are otherwise a good player, to place a cloud over the proceedings.

I think this is pretty much the nature of the beast. My goal here is not to offer a solution or look to others to do so. What I believe would be helpful is to simply acknowledge that sessions pretty much stand alone in terms of the factors I have outlined. In my opinion, if you understand what goes on, finding a way of working with it or withdrawing gracefully becomes easier. Whatever unpleasantness you may encounter, it probably isn’t about you, although it could be. Examine the situation as best you can and it will probably become clear what you should do.

As a person who plays a multitude of genres, I say without reservation that sessions are tougher to play and feel comfortable in than in any jamming situation I have ever been in. Even in very welcoming situations and at my level of playing, which is pretty good, there tends to be a tension I feel nowhere else.

Re: The Nature of Sessions

I’m going to throw a spanner into the works here.

I don’t agree at all that there is one type of session. It think that’s hugely prescriptive and an extremely narrow definition of what a session is, or can be. I personally think there are several different kinds of session, each of which has implied rules and conventions. I’ll try and go through as many as I can:

Firstly, there’s the ‘regular’ session, i.e. one that occurs on a fixed date at a fixed time with ‘regulars’ who always go and pretty much play the same tunes all the time. In these circumstances, I can absolutely agree that what Ailin and MichaelEskin are suggesting is valid - that there a set of rules particular to those sessions that are pretty much inviolable. Certain people will lead, others will follow, nobody does anything likely to upset anyone as we’ll all be meeting back here in a week’s time to do it all over again.

On the one hand, this kind of session is great for a learner to go to. They can regularly hear the repertoire, go home and learn the tunes, absorb the way they are played, come back to the session refreshed and ready to have another go. I’ve been to many a session like this, and often found them very enjoyable. These sessions often thrive on the occasional visitor, the irregular drop-in, the ‘new blood’ that brings something fresh to them, and that can be great.

On the other hand, there is that chance that things can become too formal. If nobody can break free of the ‘rules’ from time to time, and nobody joins afresh, then doesn’t it run the risk of becoming… crystallized? I don’t personally think this is necessarily a good thing.

The second kind of session could be called a… development session. One where new tunes are de rigueur… where players are constantly bringing their newest tune and almost submitting it for workshopping. This is the sort of thing we used to find after class at the Comhaltas session I was involved with for a while, or at summer schools across in Ireland - a shared space where the latest tunes could be brought to the group and hashed out in real time. Of course, this is ideal for learners, but not exactly conducive to group playing. Hard work, and not particularly accessible to outsiders. Nevertheless, it also gave a kind of feedback that is otherwise very hard to find in ITM sessions.

The third kind of session occurs in an ad-hoc setting, at a festival or a fleadh, for example. Players who don’t necessarily know each other commune and try to find common musical ground. These are the most unpredictable of all session, and can be tricky. They are also probably the place you’d most likely find bad etiquette (as discussed in the other post referred to in the OP). Anonymity is a powerful incentive for bad behaviour in my experience, and these sessions can often be chaotic as a result. As a basic rule, the bigger this type of session is to start with, the less reward there is for sitting in on it. They can also be magic, filled with unexpected musical connections, and all the better for meeting new friends.

The fourth kind of session is one that has a foundation based on people that you know and trust, but does not necessarily take place in a regular setting. Let’s call these ‘guerilla’ sessions. In and out, a few tunes before anyone’s noticed. These obviously rely on prearrangement, and on being able to call in a circle of friends who might fancy a tune. On the plus side, this has all the feedback benefits of a ‘development’ session, and all the comfort of knowing beforehand what the shared repertoire is likely to be, but without being utterly formal. In fact, you can kind of relax. You’re among friends. (the best ‘regular’ sessions can be a lot like this, though there’s less chance of a ‘guerilla’ session ending up stale unless you’re there for days at a time) On the other hand, unless you advertise, these sessions are going to be pretty exclusive, as they are generally invitation only.

These are all entirely different scenarios, but I would class them all as ‘sessions’ in one way or another. The best of it is, any one of theses could morph over the course of a few hours into one of the other types, and it wouldn’t matter at all. I don’t personally think imposing rules on a bunch of friends who sit down to play music is either helpful or desirable. Perhaps that’s just me.

Re: The Nature of Sessions

I haven’t put a whole lot of thought into this, but I’d probably distill my thinking into the following points:

1. Only play when you know the tune
If you don’t know the tune, sit it out. If you know it, play it if you like. There’s probably leeway on this if it’s a slow/beginner-type session, but at a high-standard session playing along anyway despite not knowing the tune is a major breach of session etiquette. There are also a few seriously fast learners who might figure out a tune by ear by the third time through, but those are few and far between!

2. Follow the leader
Whoever starts a set picks the speed and level of ‘swing’. Don’t try to speed it up, slow it down, or flatten out a ‘swingy’ tune, or swing a straighter tune. If you desperately want to iron all trace of swing out of some hornpipes, or play a set of jigs like Martin Hayes, wait until you get to start a set.

3. Play in tune
For instruments that do not have a fixed pitch, at least do your best to keep/play your instrument in tune. The tolerance for tuning probably depends on the overall level of playing at the session.

4. Don’t be a knob-end
People won’t like you. In my own experience, the really good musicians tend not to act like w@nkers towards their session mates. It could just be the sessions I have attended, but poor-to-mediocre musicians seem to be the worst offenders in this regard for some reason. Be nice to your regular session mates, visitors and n00bz. I owe a lot to more experienced musicians who encouraged me to come back week after week and start sets etc. when I was just starting out.

Re: The Nature of Sessions

I have not done a survey of “Irish” sessions to determine which “types” are most common. I do think the main idea of an Irish session is as a forum for paying Irish music. But there are many different kinds of such things, and they span a continuum from things that border on gigs to others that are much closer to jams. I think the idea of a session police to rule that a session is a proper Irish session or not is counter-productive.

It does come down to what the organizers or leaders want. I find some sessions are ALL about the music, and there is absolutely no sense of using it to train a musician to to learn tunes, start sets, play at speed, etc. If you cannot add to the quality of the music, you would be told not to come back. Other sessions are very social events, where players are encouraged to participate, providing whatever they can to the proceedings, and if needed, being gently nudged in the “right” directions. Plenty of sessions are quite eclectic, and encourage different kinds of music, and even things like harmonizing and solo breaks. I would not say that this disqualifies them as Irish sessions. The mix of songs to tunes can vary from session to session. Some sessions similarly have a good mix of Irish airs, often done solo.

Maybe the issue OP is trying to address is how to deal with people who do not conform to the purpose the leaders have for the session. It is up to the leaders to decide what they want their session to be, and how strongly to enforce their vision. If a session is invaded by Bluegrass players who want to change the format, the leaders need to decide how much of that they want, if any.

I like Matt’s taxonomy, but I think even that is overly constraining. There are no clear lines between the different types, and plenty of sessions mix two or more objectives in their session. There are as many types of Irish sessions as there are people organizing them.

That said, it is useful for people who visit a session for the first time to come in expecting to be playing Irish music. But I know there are sessions that are happy to welcome complete novices with absolutely no experience with the music, but a willingness to try.

“Solo breaks, improvisation, harmony”

So, some people have said the ITM sessions they attend have these things. It’s hard for me to imagine.

“Solo breaks” implies “breaks” in the arranging sense, the arrangement going full band>break>full band, which implies that the session is an arranged piece of music. This is a different thing from what happens at an ITM session where one person will start a tune, and others might join, or might not. Playing a tune solo that way isn’t a “break”.

“Improvisation” implies people not playing the melody, but noodling/improvising based on the chord progression in a jazz manner. I’ve heard that once at a session and it was quite annoying, a session-breaker: a guy showed up with a Renaissance Mute Cornett (look it up) which is the size of a pennywhistle but the volume of a trumpet, and improvised arpeggios etc which more or less followed the chords of the tunes we were playing. He appeared to not know a thing about ITM.

“Harmony” also implies people not playing the melody, but playing a harmony part. That’s something I hear with arranged ITM (The Chieftains etc) but not at sessions. (Moments of it perhaps, sometimes due to players knowing different versions of the same tune which happen to harmonise in places, less often players intentionally throwing in a bit of harmony. These are different from having players dedicated to harmony parts as their primary role in the music.)
