Place, culture and ITM?

Hello everyone,

I am a relative newcomer on the pilgrimmage to ITM and curious what the collective wisdom and sentiments are (especially for those with the experience) regarding the following:

In this modern, transient and globalized world, is immersion in the landscape, culture, and history of a place STILL important to truly learn (in your ears, and in your heart) the music of said place?

Eg, Ireland and ITM. We recently have all sorts of recordings, and tools for curating and listening to large repertoires from excellent musicians from distant times, and we have all sorts of excellent ITM musicians and communities persisting all over the map. What then remains “Irish” about the music? Is ‘place’ and culture still an integral part of learning and retaining the feel of the music, as we look forward in years to come?

A friend argued that yes, it is critical simply from a language perspective. The exposure and immersian to gaelic language and intonation is the component most needed to really get at the phrasing and feel of the tunes, and this is why place and culture will always be critical to ITM, lest you develop a New York accent!

Thoughts?

Re: Place, culture and ITM?

More seriously, I don’t think you need to be in Ireland (or know anything about Irish history) to learn how to play well, but it exponentially helps to develop the right accent and phrasing if you’re around other people who play well. But you find that all over the world. Sure, New York, Boston (Massachusetts, people, Massachusetts!), and London, which took in huge waves of Irish immigration during the 19th and early 20th centuries, have well established scenes, but you can find great Irish trad musicians in lots of places.

If you’re in a corner of the world where there are no good local players, then it’s more of an uphill battle to learn to play well. But that’s not because they lack Irish-ness. It’s because you unconsciously pick up a lot of your understanding of how tunes go being dragged along by musicians better than you are.

↳ Posted by DrSilverSpear on February 3rd, 2019.

Re: Place, culture and ITM?

I think the language connection is important and often overlooked. Once you get to the stage where you pick up your instrument and just play, without thinking about where accents and ornaments ought to go, then the rhythms and cadences of your playing naturally reflect your spoken language, it’s what your ears are used to. The identifying features of local dialects are very often clearly identifiable in the local instrumental style.

But that said, there is no reason why you shouldn’t pick up the tunes and play them in your own accent. I’d far rather hear a Scot sing an American song in their own accent than putting on a fake American accent, and I’d rather hear an American fiddler play an Irish tune in their own way that trying to slavishly imitate an Irish fiddler.

The tradition exits in the present moment, where ever one is, playing tunes. Guinness, mountains, the smell of peat burning, flat caps, braces, recordings and Gaeilge are irrelevant. I know a band who specialise in loud amplified Irish music. They always get much adulation but they can intrinsically be a bit shoddy at times (to say the least!) But the Irish sporty boys crowd in the pub, for instance, love ‘em not because the music is good, they love them because it uncritically feeds their mawkish sense of Irish nationalism.

Re: Place, culture and ITM?

With regard to the modern/ transient/ globalized world - it is critical for individuals and societies to establish bulwarks against the disposable and blended trends and to treasure skills slowly acquired and perfected over time which link to a shared heritage. ITM is a spiders web of centuries deep connections to a distant past. It is one of many excellent candidates for a dedicated and prolonged study and which will reward the student with a lifelong gift in terms of learning opportunities. The well is deep.

I’d be sceptical that there’s a causation link between the spoken language and the music intonation - but can agree correlation. The strong and complementary ORAL traditions in terms of song, poetry and story telling as well as the music suggest a people who valued the ability to commit to memory and share via performance a large communal body of works.

ITM was born of a place and time - but its strength is in its continued evolution and vibrancy not that it was crystallised in 1650, 1750, 1850 or 1950 as a canon. Its ability to absorb external influences while remaining an authentic and readily identifiable ethnic musical sub-group in global terms is testament to the underlying magic of its foundations. ITM has absorbed tunes, tune types, instruments, playing styles, melodic and harmonic innovations and even rhythm from across the globe without losing its distinct identity.

ITM and Irish-ness aren’t synonymous - it is a welcoming church. There are wonderful musicians from all parts of the world that have been drawn to the genre both as their original musical foundation and as cross-over from other genres/ traditions. It is again testament to the richness of the corpus that it has the ability to cast a wide net and attract talented musicians from diverse backgrounds.

Anybody, anywhere who is attracted to the sound of ITM can get up and running. Especially nowadays with all the recordings and materials to which you refer. After a certain point - proficiency and style will be strongly influenced by being able to play and advance through tuition and playing with stronger players and especially as part of a group or a session. Those opportunities are now available almost globally for somebody who is dedicated to the ITM cause. Wishing you every success with your personal ITM pilgrimage - newcomers always welcome on board.

Re: Place, culture and ITM?

A look across the boards on this web site reveals time and time again the difference between folk music, in this case mostly ITM, as a culture and folk music as a genre. There are folk musicians galore of all abilities who are not rooted in the cultural heritage of a particular place but have adopted the nuance and expression of a musical culture as their own. Of these numbers few engage to the degree whereby their efforts and ability is enough to bring them entirely within the fold, though it can and does happen.

Every time I visit Scotland I am aware that a lifetime spent studying and playing the music I love is but nothing compared to the immersion that musicians, similarly engaged, enjoy simply by being a native within a vibrant, local culture. I can bluff it out, entertain and enjoy what I do but try all my days I will never have that immediacy and connection that creates a culture rather than mimics.
