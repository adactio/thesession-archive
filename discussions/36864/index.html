Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

Hello! I am an American composer and harpist currently playing in a trio with two flutists (classical flutes, not Irish flutes.)

I have been doing arrangements of traditional tunes for us to play for some time now, but the more I listen to recordings, and read the discussions on this site, the more I realize I may be doing dumb things you might expect a newbie to the world of traditional music might do. Things like not repeating the ‘A’ section before going on to the ‘B’. I realize that sounds very basic, but I did a few arrangements early on where I didn’t, and now that I am listening to more and more recordings I realize how awkward it sounds.

I was hoping to hear any basic advice you might care to give, and I do mean basic. If you can think of anything you’ve heard an inexperienced composer do which made you roll your eyes because it wasn’t quite ‘right,’ and you have a moment to share it with me, I would be very grateful!

Thanks!

Re: Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

Most of the dance tunes are played AABB with each of those parts being 8 bars in length. Muck around with that structure, and you create something that doesn’t sound Irish. Sometimes you get single reels played ABAB, but not nearly as often as that 32 bar pattern.
Irish music is modal. If you try to impose classical harmonies, and especially classical minor harmonies on a dorian tune, it often tends not to work well. The tunes are most often played in unison. Too much harmony, and you turn it into something that is no longer traditional Irish music. One book I have found that has some nice clean harmonies is The Fifer’s Delightful Companion, by Ralph Sweet, that has harmonies to American fife and contradance tunes, many of which come from the Irish tradition. But use those harmonies sparingly.
There are rhythmic patterns to Irish music that are different from what you would hear in the classical world. Listen to a lot of it from the original sources (CCE recordings being a good start). I have heard too many musicians trying to play Irish music without the ‘lift’ and pulse, and it ends up sounding alien.
If you want it to sound Irish, the cleaner the better. Have both flutes, and even the harp, play in unison. Or some sparingly applied harmonies from the harp here and there.

Re: Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

I agree with Al Brown on harmonies and especially on lift and pulse. Piece’s advice about the fiddle may be tongue in check, but do listen to some good fiddlers to hear how they can develop secondary rhythms with their bowings. If you can appreciate that, you may be able to hear your own music differently.

As an example with harmonies, you might consider doing a harmony on one part of a tune, but not on all parts. That is, take a three-part tune; go around once without harmonies -- AA, BB, CC -- then do a harmony only on the BB parts the second and third times.

Another thing to consider is using drones, which go nicely in Irish trad, although they can be overdone like anything else. If you’re not doing this already, I’d suggest you listen to some Irish pipers for how they approach drones and playing in general. For instance, they don’t always go to the “I” chord (or diad, as it were) even though the melody is going there -- they might delay a beat and stay in the V, for example.

And finally, my take is that once you understand the AA/BB aspects of most all tunes, feel free to break that or any other rules and do what you think sounds best.

Re: Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

Agree with all the above. The harp in particular sounds better with more sparing arrangements - sometimes just a note in the bass, or (of course) just the melody on its own. My own experience with playing the music of Carolan over quite a few years, is that the more I play, the simpler I make my arrangements.

So you could choose to do one complete run of the tune as just a melody and add sparing harmony notes, as well as contrasting with something more “full”. But rather than being tempted with beautiful, lush harmonies, I think a good guiding principle is always “what can I leave out” - and keep that process going until you have just the barest elements which work for you.

Just to add emphasis, Alix:

It seems to me, too, that the overall “sound” of traditional ceili music
played by a group is the sound of the melody played in unison.
That is about as basic as it gets.

As already indicated by the other comments, the further you drift from that fundamental quality,
the less you will reflect much of the traditional sound I believe you are shooting for. Being sparing
with any rhythmic accompaniment, or any harmonizing, is definitely central to what you are asking about.
(That has been said already - it bears saying again. :-))

Air playing and certain solo pieces are exceptions, especially those based upon traditional Irish songs where there may be a bit of playing around with the meter and structure to be evocative or whatever. But when you go into those branches of the music, you are in an area of musical creativity which did not require the same values (stricter adherence to certain structures, rhythms, tempo, etc.) as the dance music.

Therefore, I might continue to focus on understanding the dance music,
as perhaps tougher to “get it right”.

Alix, it looks like you live in the Washington DC area. There are many harpists in the area who are knowledgeable in playing and arranging Irish traditional music. Send me a message and I’ll put you in contact with some of them. There are also good workshops available.

Some quick harp arranging advice: If you aren’t learning tunes by ear rather than sheet music, now is the time to start. This applies to your flutists, too. Do not rely on notation for ornaments; it’s not standardized as it is with classical music, and the ornamentation is difficult to capture in notation anyway. Inverted chords are your friends. Triads are not. Use thirds sparingly. Listen to the music. Listen to other instruments. Have fun.

Re: Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

The other thing I would say about “arranging” ITM is to try to get the flautists out of tonguing or breathing every note, even though it may go against all their training. Irish music is played ( mainly ) as a continuous stream, taking the breaths at an unstressed note immediately before a significant one, rather than the classical style of separating every note. Get your flautists to listen to a few masters like Matt Molloy and Cathal O’Connel, to name just a couple. Other contributors could suggest others to get some idea of the correct style from.

Re: Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

One of my arrangement pet peeves is sheet music that shows three sharps in the key signature (to help people understand that A is the tonal center) when there aren’t any G-sharps or even C-sharps in the tune. So you then see ‘accidentals’ within the body of the music any time a G or C note comes up. If following conventional sheet music guidelines, I would rather see this notated with only one sharp. One sharp doesn’t automatically mean the key of Gmajor. In this example it’s A-dorian.

When I’m transcribing I have my own cliff notes/chicken scratch way of doing it. My preference is to not put ANY sharps or flats in the key signature. Then I add footnotes such as “all C’s to be played as C#”. Or I’ll write in the sharp, flat or natural symbols as accidentals where necessary (slightly breaking my pet peeve above). I automatically assume that any F note is F# and not Fnatural, unless otherwise noted.

Re: Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

About the OP’s question, I don’t know if there’s really any such thing as an arranging “mistake”.

It’s your arrangement, and however you do it, it’s your creation.

Are we talking about a group of “legit” (as the jazz world calls them) or “classical” (as the trad world calls them) players? In other words ordinary orchestral-style Boehm flutists, not folk musicians?

If so, you’re going to sound like what you are, a group of orchestral players playing arrangements of traditional folk music. So the “rules” such as they are that guide traditional Irish ensembles don’t apply to you, and you might as well have fun with creating arrangements that sound good to you.

I know this sounds like heresy to some!

Yes the Chieftains are a great example of how to arrange traditional music in un-traditional ways, such as writing countermelodies, doing jazzlike improvisation, and deconstructing tunes into individual phrases.

For a more traditional approach, where the tunes are left alone to speak for themselves, you can listen to any traditional group. In traditional groups the only “arranging” is picking tunes which sound nice played together, usually tunes of the same time, usually three tunes, each tune played thrice. Doing special intros, segues, or ‘outros’ is considered gimmicky. Ditto having somebody play harmony.

An example (from my band’s setlist) is this set of three jigs

The Banner x3
Sport x2
Hag with the Money x3

(Sport has three parts, the other tunes two, so the duration of each tune is the same.)

I would, myself, go with the “classical” approach and have harmony parts etc. Having two flutes play in unison is a waste of a perfectly good fluteplayer!

BTW Yhallhouse, I do play The Butterfly in 15/8 over the backup rhythm and chord progression of Take 5. Works great!

Re: Help me avoid common arranging mistakes (please!)

Have a look at www.rudemex.co.uk where there are a number of tunes set for two or three parts. We don’t claim to have any special expertise in writing arrangements, but a quick go at some of these might give you an idea about how someone else has gone about it, and might spark an idea or two - if only how not to make the same mistakes!

Basically, I work out a chord sequence for the tune, and generally use that to construct a bottom line (bass or similar) then add a middle line to fill in the harmonies and/or work as a counter-melody. Sometimes I do it the other way round, and write the counter-melody/harmony first. Some seem to work better than others, and someone with a good knowledge of musical theory would no doubt make a better job of it. You’re welcome to get in touch if there’s anything there that suggest that would be useful to share thoughts.
