Re: Kane Sisters and fiddle intonation

I’ve listened *a lot* to both of the Kane’s cds, and I’d agree that there are plenty of moments where Liz and Yvonne aren’t always playing the same pitch, or even the same timing on certain notes and articulations. Some of these differences are obvious (to my ear) and bordering on “oops, they should’ve re-done that take.” In particular, I notice that one sister’s high b on the E string sometimes runs a little sharp. There are low notes on the G string that sometimes wander a bit too.

But to criticize them for that would be missing the spirit of the music and its overwhleming excellent other qualities. First off, their unison playing is wonderful--full of tiny, endearing shared nuances as well as individual characteristics that shine through and give us two fiddles interpreting the same tunes. Most of the time, they’re playing tight, intricate lines well enough to sound like a single fiddler with a chorus effect. But thank god for the differences, which make it sound so much more alive and personal!

To me, the overall effect is one of lift and wildness, and the players giving themselves over to the tune (not trying to insert their egos or play off each other), harkening back to an older (non-commercial) sound we rarely hear on recordings these days. Reminds me of Bobby Casey and Junior Crehan and Martin Rochford. The kind of playing that comes only from long, wee hour explores together into the music. How many of us take the time to do that with another person these days?

You’ll hear some of the same wandering intonation in lots of great players--Kevin Burke’s earlier recordings are famous for it. Listen to his tracks on Portland, which he overdubbed with a second, “identical” fiddle line, and pay particular attention to the high first string notes--not always spot on. And better for it.

In this age of digital recording and professional careers, we’re at risk of forgetting that timing and tone matter more in this music than intonation. And that a wide (as opposed to narrow) sense of intonation can enrich the music.

Re: Kane Sisters and fiddle intonation

Yes, I think Kevin is an excellent example of someone who in many ways plays in a modern idom, but manages, at the same time, to fully maintain an old style flavor to it.

Pay particular attention to the way he works the E string, compared to the D string. He’s generally pretty solidly in tune on the low notes, which he uses as an anchor for the tune, so that he can dance around on the intonation of the high notes and make them wail and set your nerves a bit on edge. Now that’s Irish music.

The low notes are the sound of the sea wind, and the high notes are the cry of the Banshee that it carries. Either one alone is just a sound. Both of them together is an *Irish* sound.

To get that sound:

a) Stop thinking of notes as some fixed thing in a fixed position on the fingerboard. They’re not. Think in terms of a voice, which has an infinate number of tones “between” the classical notes. Listen to some eastern vocal music to get a feel for this, then play like that sound.

b) Do exactly what every violinist is taught never, ever to do. . . go sharp. That’s where the “skin crawly” feel of Irish music comes from and why classical musicians are taught not to do it. They don’t want that. We do. That’s the wail.

There’s an additional ramification to this. Between a violinist who is ten cents off and a fiddler who is ten cents off, there is actually going to be an interval of twenty cents.

Re: Kane Sisters and fiddle intonation

Fiddlers would go sharp on some notes, flat on others. You could scratch up a doctoral thesis of some sort working out whether they’re adhering to mean-tone temperament (pre Equal Temp tuning system). Maybe some of these really wild notes are equivalent to wolf tones!
Pete Cooper talks about this in his tutor - blunt notes is Packie Byrne’s term for them. The Pipe on the Hob (in “DMix”) is the example he gives, the F# in the first part. Listen to Bobby Casey play that, or the Ballinakill Ceili Band.
The chanter (and thus regulators) of the pipes are tuned to beatless intervals with the drones - as sweet and rolling as possible, what they call a Just Intonation system, although pipemakers in the past just called it being in tune. It doesn’t line up very well with the equal Temp F# and C, especially (against D drones). Perhaps these are what the fiddlers are hitting, but where they’re getting a drone from is the mystery.

Re: Kane Sisters and fiddle intonation

I haven’t heard the CDs so I can’t pass an opinion. However, good points about things being (technically) out-of-tune. Some intonation rules I adhere to myself if anyone’s interested (just talking fiddle here).

Imagine a scale in D on a properly tuned piano - the notes I’d play would be D, E, F# G, A, B C# D. All would match the piano’s notes except F# and C#, which I’d play sightly flat in comparison.

Forget the piano now. On fiddle :

Playing C# + E together - the C# will be slightly flat.
Playing Cnat + E together - the Cnat will be slightly sharp.
Playing a fourth (eg B and E above it) - B will be fractionally sharp, being the leading note.
D + D octave - no variation allowed! (Same for any other octave combos). I think that’s the most ear-jarring
combination of a two-note chord being out of tune.

That’s talking about playing solo. Playing with other melody and accomp. instruments will obviously require some variation on these “rules” - usually done ‘on the spot’!

Getting back to the original complaint, even with good spirit in the music, poor intonation usually annoys me (but only if it’s persistent throughout).

“I would have thought that trad fiddle players make much more use of partially flattened notes than sharp with the exception of the supernatural third.”

For every complex problem there is an answer that is clear, simple, and wrong. -- H. L. Mencken

And I was well aware that I was positing a simple answer for complex problem, and thus in many respects was wrong.

But the problem I was addressing was only partially related to the one you address. When one speaks of going sharp or flat one must ask sharp or flat compared to what. The concept is relative. I wasn’t really speaking relative to the equal tempered scale, but relative to whatever tone is to be played. Although it may be considered the ideal no human being can actually hit every note they intend to perfectly every time. What actually happens is that you play a note, it’s off by some amount, you listen to it and make an adjustment to bring it to where you want it to be. Someone being taught to play the violin “properly” will be instructed to always err on the side of being flat and sharpen to the intended tone, because being flat may be off, but it isn’t jarring. Being sharp is. Listeners will notice that you were sharp even though you flatten to the “correct” tone in an instant.

What I was saying was try to play the tone you intend, whatever that may be, but then adjust it sharp to the point where it noticably jars.

Of course you don’t do that on every note. Where do you do it? Well, to listen a lot of older style players, but the highest pitched leading tone in the tune is a likely candidate, and to a lesser extant the highest pitched root tone. It isn’t so much dependant on the structure of the scale as it is on the structure of the tune. The notes on the sheet music are not only just the shell of the tune, they are the shell of the notes themselves and proper intonation will actually be different in different relative octaves. Can’t do that on a piano even by tuning it to a different temperment.

Notice I say tone, not note. Every tone, no matter its pitch, is a perfect note. They’re only “right” or “wrong” compared to something else. When I am playing against some standard, say a piano, I make my tones conform to that, as I must, which is one of the reasons I don’t do that very much. When I am playing solo I haven’t a frickin‘ clue what notes I’m playing; and don’t give a frickin’ damn. I play intervals. In closed positions I’m perfectly happy playing in the key of A#+20. What possible difference does it make what the root tone is?

Try this. Pick up your fiddle with your eyes closed and place a finger down on a string completely at random. It’s very unlikely that you’ll be on a note that the piano can play. Now think of a song you can hum, but haven’t played on the fiddle before. Row, row, row your boat, Cockles and Mussels, something like that.

You should be able to play it, by ear, from that random starting point fairly easily, unless you’ve gotten locked into the idea that music has something to do with playing Ds and F#s or something. It doesn’t. It has something to do with the interval between two tones.

Re: Kane Sisters and fiddle intonation

“Do trad Irish fiddlers, as a species, play sharp rather than flat (compared to equal temperament . . .”

No, they tend to just intervals because they are consonant, however, they tend to play discordant intervals. The net effect is to sound out of tune from either a Just or Equal Tempered point of view. In the case of the major third it will be flat compared to Equal, but still sharp compared to Just. The real issue isn’t really being sharp or flat, but discordant and the music is colored by varying the degree of discordance.

Of course it is not unheard of for Irish music to deal with the problems of thirds by simply deleting them, using either a pentatonic or hexatonic scale. . . and you don’t want some damned guitar player shoving it in anyway.

Irish music is not “out of tune,” it is discordant music by its nature, from the nature of the people who play it. It lives in the spaces left by other forms of music with its own intonation. It accepts modern concepts and forms, but not wholly and remains essentially ancient. It favors instruments with chiff, rasp, odd harmonics. Instruments that can be played “out of tune,” but on in odd ways. That’s why fiddle. . .and pipes, and whistles. That’s why when all the world was stringing harps with gut and plucking them sweetly the Irish strung theirs with wire and struck them percussively.

The music is often stunningly beautiful. . .

But it is not “pretty” or “sweet.”

It’ll make you want to dance with vigor, sob uncontrollably, or hit somebody, but it won’t make you want to sniff posies. It’s adrenaline rush, not endorphine high.

I just read a review of Kevin Crehan’s CD whose only criticism of it was for the airs. . .because he couldn’t hold a note on pitch in them. Well of course he isn’t holding pitch, because he’s playing them the way they are supposed to be played. Would you criticise a wailing mother whose only son has just been sent to the gallows for not wailing on an even pitch?

The music is not out of tune. It is on edge. By intention.

KFG

Anyway, to get back to the matters in hand. Most experienced orchestral players know instinctively what the pitch of A440 is, but that is not the same as having “absolute pitch”. If you have – I should say “suffer from” – that phenomenon then you have a hard-wired memory of a definite pitch for each note, so if an accompanying instrument is tuned only a few cents off what’s hard-wired in your brain then you’ll have a worrying time trying to play or sing along with it. The orchestral player’s “knowing” A440 is no more than a simple memory of a specific pitch acquired by long exposure to it, and anyone can acquire it in time. As most people know, an orchestra normally tunes to an “A” played by the principal oboe. Many is the time when the string players have noticed that the oboe’s A is slightly flat because they know that as that instrument warms up so it will rise up to A440, which is irritating when they’ve all had to tune to the oboe’s initial A437 or whatever.
