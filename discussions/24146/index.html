I would just point out to be cautious with this advice, particularly with the key of Am. There are not many tunes that are properly in Am (ie with Fnats rather than F#s). Most of the tunes that people say are in Am are actually in A Dorian and in those instances it is highly unlikely that an F major chord will sound anything other than awful.

I think that people are sounding cautious on here because so many of the tunes have little quirks or turns within them that you might not expect and a set of standard chords will not really help. Sure, a lot of the tunes will fit into some fairly standard progressions but you really need to hear the changes to do it properly and it is not something you can just busk without knowing the tunes in your head. Re: Guitar chord progressions for ITM…

the third and the fifth, because they are an A minor chord. Everyone who wants to know, the dorian mode is almost the only “minor sounding” mode in Irish Traditional music. which is most commonly backed with the minor chord built from the starting note…

(, which most of the time is played without any thirds anyway, so I would rather call it an ambiguous chord, but yet, that a Major third is definitely not optional, except in Gravel walk which I like to play with descending chromatics starting from C# on the downbeat, but only as a variation the second or third time through, so nobody is paying attention who might tell me they hate it. )

… and the Major chord built from the note a whole step below the starting note, and mostly no other chords. so Em D, Am G, Dm C, Gm F, and all the other chords which you might use when the melody asks you to are intermediary. Sometimes though they can be important. The more modern the music, generally the more chords the music might pass through, because modern tune writers deliberately write things to have more harmonic interest than ancient tune writers did. Your flute playing friend should know what the chords are, because they are playing them when they play the tunes. At least the flute player I play with ( who knows nothing but wind and rudimentary piano) knows the chords If I ask her to think about it.

Re: Guitar chord progressions for ITM…

I’d have to say though, in all my experience as a melody player, I’ve never come across a non ITM immersed guitarist who could pick up the changes, as simple as they may be in all reality, they have a rhythm and drive that the guitar player has to be ready for. prepare yourself for 3 and 3/4 measures of an Em chord ( which sounds best with the third removed and other notes included in the top of the chord to match or harmonize with the melody for breif periods of time ) and then for a beat you move to a D major chord, then, downbeat of the next measure (the 5th measure, you are back to Em, but only to move quickly to a G major on the third beat (there’s that minor third you were omitting earlier, showing up now to distinguish the second phrase from the first,). The second phrase might generally have more chord changes than the first leading up to the false cadence at the end of the part (because we are in the dorian mode) The B minor chord can also be useful in this key, and and the next tune might logically be in B dorian, A dorian, G major, or D major. but the transition to G major would be the same as A dorian, not E dorian because they both have one sharp in the key signature.

What is interesting with some ITM minory modal tunes is that some often change mode between parts or during parts without changing tonal center. Im thinking here also of some players who may “customise” their own version of a particular tune to their own ends. Often this means including an interval that may not be obvious to you or you may not be expecting (until you hear it but by then the moment has past) as this interval would be absent in a more regular rendition.

An example; Last night I was sat playing Jenny’s welcome to Charlie and thinking about these very issues. One of the ways I was playing the 4th part I included both C C# (common enough in ITM) this would beg for a different chord an A or maybe an f#m in the progression, even if it’s just to show your listening, which could also be achieved with just an open note at the right moment or incorporated in a bass line. But in reality a simple full blooded D including the F# (maj 3rd, on the bottom for added effect) expresses this wonderfully.

As has been mentioned the ears have it and listening is the key Mr/Mrs Gnome, god’s speed with the practice.

In the right context, specifically band or performance work, audience interest, then sometimes well worked arrangements can enhance the music.

But I’m sure you would agree that a banjo, fiddle, flute, pipes or whistle played well and in full flight needs no more.

Again I think it goes back to the fact that a tune played well solo or an unaccompanied song will always catch the ear.

I’ve heard some “great” traditional players give the best solo concerts, not needing intricate arrangements, and for me this has enhanced their prformance and the intimacy imparted to the listener.

So your comment “when it’s a gig where the audience expects it.” carries as much weight. Thank you.
