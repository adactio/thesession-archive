Technically Difficult.

I discovered the learning power of etudes a while back and there’s no doubt that I learned a lot from Chopins’ Op. 10, No. 3. But, it took 2 years. It’s only four pages and a little over 4 minutes long. My question is, is it worth while to learn a technically difficult piece of music? Are the skills and the knowledge you gain worth the time and discipline it takes to learn these masterfully written pieces of music?

We’ve all looked for short-cuts in terms of learning new things, but we soon find that there are no ways to get around the learning process. My logic tells me that the closest thing i’ll get to a short cut in music, is diligence and discipline, which aren’t really short cuts, but just ways to be more efficient right?

I’m beginning to think that harder pieces don’t necessarilly give more back than easier pieces. What do you guys think?

(The rest is ramble)
I remember when I first started playing this music, The Mooncoin Jig and The Trip to Nenagh were two of the first tunes I wanted to learn, but my goodness they are HARD. I set them down for a while, but I picked the Mooncoin Jig back up about two months ago, which was nine months after I first tried learning it. In those nine months I learned a lot of easier tunes, and even got to experience the “falls under your fingers” thing with a few of them. Now I can play The Mooncoin Jig really well. Not just that one, but I finally like Gravel Walks, cause I don’t play it crappy anymore.

Even though I started working on The Mooncoin Jig and The Trip to Nenagh at the same time, worked on them to about the same extent, and set them down for the same amount of time, I still play Trip to Nenagh crappy, which kind of confuses me. I’m still struggling with Dogs Among the Bushes. The Graf Spee still isn’t fun to play but i’m not giving up on that one, that’s my tune lol.

There are a handful of others like Master McDermott’s reel, The Victory, O‘ Mahoney’s reel, Sheila Coyles, Andy McGann’s(The one Kevin Burke plays) but I can have a good time with that one on piano. And still, there are other tunes that i’m better at after setting them down, like McFinns’ reel, The Red-Haired Lass, The Eavesdropper. Some tunes are just harder than others I guess.

Re: Technically Difficult.

I’ve lost count of the number of tunes I’ve struggled with only to come back later and find them comparatively easy, but I’m sure that work I put in to a difficult tune will pay dividends elsewhere, perhaps much later, even if I still struggle with that particular tune. And I would never give up on it unless I don’t actually like it much, in which case I have better things to do with my life.
Spend some time trying to isolate the exact series of notes or movements that is causing the problem, slow it right down until you can play it to your satisfaction, before increasing the speed very very slightly. Bear in mind that it might be something before or after the tricky bit, maybe bow travelling in the wrong direction, or fingers in the wrong place, or an isolated phrase that you approach differently everywhere else in the tune.
It might be months or years to get it right, or it might only take a few minutes once you have realised what the difficulty is and found a way to play it.
One thing I would never do is change the bit I’m finding difficult to something easier. Of all the tunes I play, there is only one pair of notes that I am beginning to think are impossible to play on the fiddle (by me that is) but I haven’t admitted defeat yet.

Yes, if you play classical music, since pushing the boundaries of technique is a prevalent trend in that music; whilst the classical music canon contains many works that are composed with aesthetic beatuy as their sole aim, there are also many that are designed to show off technical brilliance. Re: Technically Difficult.

Crossing a string was mentioned earlier on, ie crossing to a non-adjacent string. There can be an alternative way, sometimes.

In the “One-Horned Sheep” [ https://thesession.org/tunes/3644 ] the 1st two notes are G and G, that being G on the 1st string, then cross to G on the 3rd string.

I came up with an alternative to crossing over 2 strings :

Play the 1st G as a harmonic on the 4th string - as if you play a C with 3rd finger, but just touch the string, don’t press it down. That should be the pitch of the G on the 1st string.

You now only need to cross to the 3rd string to sound the G an octave lower.

Re: Technically Difficult.

One of the challenges of crossing multiple strings is coordinating the left and right hand. If I have to jump over a string I almost always have a finger down well before I need to change stings. It can be a bit of a stretch, but it helps me focus more on the right hand, rather than both at the same time.

Don’t go out to learn a difficult tune just to see if you can do it. Don’t learn a difficult tune just to try and impress people with your skill.

Do, try to find more difficult tunes if you don’t feel challenged by the tunes you already know and are working on. Do try to find a really really challenging tune to work on if you want to make other less difficult but still challenging tunes a touch less daunting. Do learn a challenging tune if you like it so much that you just *have* to learn it.

Just remember. It is easy to play a really difficult tune badly. It is difficult to play an easy tune and make it sound real good.

Re: Technically Difficult.

@gam and Fiddlelearner:
Tricky string crossings are usually easier when using the bow’s middle by balance as a pivot point. This is why a preceding upbow works: it gets your bow’s middle by balance over the strings you will play.

The bow may have a small hop to avoid the A str, but in the middle by balance you shdn’t need to lift the bow or create a hole in the sound. Just roll the wrist without much arm (elbow) movement (I use none).

The aGd can be played in order with one finger when the finger does not touch the strings to the fingerboard. Touch E + A strs for the *a*, then bump the finger over the A str to D + A strs for the *G* and *d* notes.

Or, quite seriously, for some circumstances, a finger will play both E and A strs *a* and *d*, and the thumb will play the *G* on the D str. Again, no need to push strings to the fingerboard.

Practice = Discovery. It doesn’t matter what notes you play. After you learn a few notes in a row, the rest of your life is spent discovering how to communicate with them. So, if you want to learn an aspect of playing (such as pp), choose some notes that you know how to play (open strings are often good) and try out - discover - how to make the desired sound in the most straightforward, efficient way. Pay attention, and you will learn what works, and what doesn’t. Use a few scale notes, arpeggio (chord), or a simple tune, so you can play them easily and are inside the music during discovery. Reading etudes is an outside approach, a detour.

Mary Lou Williams (jazz piano great) said: “Practice what you’re gonna play on the gig.”

Learn what you need to play, when you need to play it, and you will always be interested and improving.

Of course MLW assumes you have a gig for which to practice. And of course you do, even if it’s your first gig way in the future.

Best wishes, vlnplyr

[My question is, is it worth while to learn a technically difficult piece of music? Are the skills and the knowledge you gain worth the time and discipline it takes to learn these masterfully written pieces of music?]

Yes. It is like the way athletes over-train in order to be able to do their target feat without breaking a sweat. If you want to be a really good blues, r&b, and rock guitar player, if you tackle jazz chords and theory, it will help your blues and rock chops bigtime, provided you don’t wreck your shake-and-shimmy cylinders by over-juillarding.

However…..NO, if you tackle said difficult piece before you are motor-skills ready and conceptually ready and brain-pathways ready to do so. Then, it is a waste of time and may actually harm your development. This is rampant in Irish Trad-Land. The Chris Langan quote offered in an earlier comment says it all. You must not tackle the chosen difficult piece until you can play a piece the next rung lower on the difficulty ladder not just accurately, but fluidly and fluently. With ease, grace, swing, and emotional dynamics all gliding along effortlessly. Then on to the next-difficult one.
