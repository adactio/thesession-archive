“Your old road is rapidly aging…”

There was a time when the “album” was an experience that needed the whole package to be complete - artwork, notes on the back cover, the full set of songs or tunes.

Even if someone was copying an LP to cassette (not that we would ever have done anything like that, of course - we weren’t at all like the immoral young punks of today with their electronic mp3 bootleg swapping) it was considered uncool to leave out any of the tracks, even ones you didn’t like. Having the complete album experience was the thing.

Of course, now if you want background information on the production or photographs of the band all you have to do is Google them. And you can pick and choose songs or tunes from a given CD, or even make up a mongrel CD from various albums giving the artist no say in how the tracks are ordered.

It’s just the way it is. I’m sure the coming years will make even bigger changes in the way we buy recorded music. You can see the end of the movie rental store coming with “on-demand” movies available from cable TV (at least in our area.) I don’t imagine the music CD store is going to make it much past the end of the decade.

All in all, I’d say it was a good thing - much greater accesibility, maybe even more control over marketing and profts by the musicians themselves.

Re: Downloading Music

Kerri,

My parents are professional (non-irish in the ‘professional’ capacity) musicians who have been working in the music industry for 40 years or so. Through that I got into doing live sound and studio work at an early age. This is not to say I’m an expert or an insider, but I do know a great deal about how these things work.

This is actually a fairly complicated discussion with a lot of tangents, so it’s going to be hard to refine a response to the ‘core’ of the matter, but I’ll try.

First, taking a cue from the lawyers who run the music industry, let’s get the parties straightened out. We will bundle the labels, their studios, and the lawyer groups under the name of “TWMM” (Those who make money) henceforth.

TWMM have been very careful to make sure that people see things their way, which is why people make arguments like volume production argument you made above. And to some limited degree, it’s true-- But only because that’s how TWMM made the system look.

Let me address Jode’s point about CD profit first, since that’s the easiest. Let’s say you sit down to a contract with a label. The first thing you’re going to see is ‘percent of royalty’, which will be typically around 10-12% of CD sales.

Yay, so you make $1.50 on each CD sale. That adds up quick, right? Well, sort of. See, you, the artist, pay for packaging out of a percentage of your royalties (Note: This is not an ‘part-in share’ -- In other words, your percentage contribution (Usually, say, 30% of your royalty percentage) is the total cost for packaging. After that, you generally have to pay the publisher a percentage of your royalties, which is usually about 4% These deductions are not sequential, by the way. So now you’re down to about 8% of the sale of a CD to be paid in royalties. In the end, after all the little details, you make about $0.90 per CD.

Still, sell a thousand CDs, get $900 bucks! Not bad, hmm?
Well, except the fact that then comes recoupment. The ‘costs’ of producing you, including advance (if any advance exists above and beyond ‘studio costs’, which are usually paid by the artist-- see below), ‘advertising’ fees (Usually a set cost, which the label uses to advertise its top 10%, but which is paid by everone), etc. are calculated such that almost no groups ever reach the point where they’re paid anything.

Let’s take some numbers from this: CD sells for $15. Artist ‘earns’ $1.50. Packaging (CD, jewel case, printed material) costs $0.30 of that, another $0.08 or so goes to the publisher for distribution network costs. All these costs come out of that initial $1.50 in ‘royalties’. Those accrue towards the ‘debt’ a group incurs in recording (Usually they record in the label’s own studio, often at a heinously expensive ‘rate’- Often, especially in Nashville, they end up using the label’s own ‘backup artists’ (One fellow my parents knew plays most of the slide guitar you hear on all those heinous Nashville country albums), whose time is also billed to the SWMM at much higher than the rate they earn themselves.)

If you’ve been reading carefully, you’ll have noticed that the meager $1.50 the band ‘earns’ in royalties pays for the entire cost of the album, including studio costs that are calculated with a ‘profit margin’ for the label sufficient to keep the label at break-even point-- just on the ‘royalties’ of the artist.

At any rate, if you don’t believe any of the above, please-- don’t take my word for it. I invite you to go stick your nose into the dirty little world of music business contracts, with the above in mind. In many ways, it’s kinda like the old ‘company store’ miner dilemma. Especially with ‘cross collateralization’, which is a cute little term the label uses to say, “You can’t earn any money as a group until your ‘debt’ to us is paid in full, which means we take any proceeds from concerts or appearances.”

Now, much smaller labels are slightly different, and there are a ‘few’ out there who are actually in it for the artist and the music.

There are also a lot of artists who release their music themselves (And frankly, if I like them, I’m happy to pay $10-$15 for a CD produced by the band themselves-- and know exactly to whom it’s going). There are some who release their music via mp3 download sites, and again, I’m happy to patronize them (heh).

Now, I realize that the preceding was full of numbers and percentages and stuff, but all I really did was present the facts of standard ‘recording contracts’. The truly evil things TWMM do I will leave for another discussion, save for to say they hurt musicians and music as a whole in the name of the almighty dollar, and then convince them that it’s the only way to be. It’s an industry matched in sleaze only by ambulance chasers and used car salesmen.

I hope that answers Jode’s question about “… but where is your return?”, and Zina’s “Pennies per disc” argument. I have no problem paying more (Than $2.00 - $5.00 ;) for a CD from a small label with integrity, or for a self-produced CD, but the only reason we’re paying $15-$20 a CD today is because of the corruption of the industry. The reason the little labels have to charge that much is because the big guys make it just about impossible for them to get their foot in the door. There are almost as many ‘starving labels’ out there as there are ‘starving artists’, but the big ones certainly ain’t.

Respectfully,

-Kysh

Selling CDs is a nice way to cover the expenses of producing a CD. Maybe it even gives you a little cash to cover a nice dinner at a concert. But there aren’t very many folk musicians without day jobs (Well.. except for the unemployed! :), and CD sales aren’t exactly going to take off just by selling stuff at concerts.

The approach I favour is, as long as you can handle the bandwidth, just giving people mp3s. There aren’t many people who are going to come up to you after a concert and say “Man, I loved your stuff! I’d buy your CD but I’ve already got it all on mp3.” .. So for ‘small time’ musicians, it’s hardly an either-or.

The effect it does have is increased interest in you, which translates to more bookings, more shows, more music and more money to support ‘the life’.

(Another silly benefit is that you get people on your ‘mailing list’, which is often a requirement for application to play at a concert or regional event.)

Anyway, I’ve gone on enough about this. :> I hope I’ve made my statements a little more clear.

-Kysh
