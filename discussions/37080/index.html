Conciously incompetent

I heard a great description of the 4 stages of the process of learning.

1. Unconsciously incompetent.
2. Consciously incompetent.
3. Consciously competent.
4. Unconsciously competent.

I am generally at stage 2 on the low/high whistles, with occasional forays into stage 3 on particular tunes. I think this principle applies to learning to play the instrument, and also to playing any given tune.

How long, and how many hours of listening & practice & sessions, should it take to reach stage 4 on a wide enough range of tunes to play most of the tunes in a session? I would like to have an idea when I can reach my ‘Desiderata’ state of flawless, unthinking, skill at level 4. I suspect it will be a long time,

Let me know your experience. I am impatient to get to the top, well aware that there is still a hill to climb.

Re: Conciously incompetent

Personally I think you’re looking at the process of learning in a negative way. Miles Davis didn’t see each note as an obstacle, but an opportunity. So it is with each stage of learning (skeptical as I am of categorising the highly subjective process into four distinct boxes…).

I think you already know the sort of answers you’re going to get to this question. If you want someone to say ‘7 years’ then you’re in for disappointment. Learning an instrument is one of the most rewarding, mind expanding transferable skills you are ever likely to undertake, so to feel impatience to ‘level up’(!) undermines the whole process.

If you are indeed consciously incompetent then you should be able to determine in which areas you’re incompetent and work on them in isolation. Bad timing? Play slowly to a metronome and gradually speed it up. Poor intonation? Practice slow scales in front of an electric tuner. I’ve taken apart my playing and put it back together more times than I can count. I Might play like sh*t for a few days but I come out of it better than I was.

But this isn’t about me! As I touched on before, learning is a subjective process and you need to find what’s the right way for you. I genuinely wish you all the best, and hope your music takes you where you want it to.

Three comments regarding your 4 stages - and I am unconsciously competent in a nonmusical arena:
1. Your road to being unconsciously competent is longer than you hope, and will outlast your initial wave of enthusiasm.

Jerone, I think the rough translation when applied to learning music is:

1. You’re a beginner, you’re not good at your particular instrument, and you have no idea how bad it really is.
2. You’ve played for a bit, you’re still not good, but at least you can tell the difference between that instrument being played well and not well.
3. You’re good when you try to be.
4. You’re good whether you are trying or not, and you don’t always realize how good you really are.

Not saying I agree completely, but I can see some truth to the idea. Certainly with stages 1-3. Not sure I understand what 4 is supposed to mean, honestly, so if someone has a different interpretation, I’d read it.

Re: Conciously incompetent

I use a metronome only to get a feeling for the tempo of a piece, and then I turn it off, for two reasons. One, there is an old saying, that practice, practice, practice should NEVER make perfect! But playing against a metronome forces a compliance to a formal timeframe, which in essence take the life out of music. Two, the best strategy I have found to learn a piece is by starting deliberately slowly, s-l-o-w-l-y, while paying attention to all of what I am doing. Sure, a metronome could be set to very slow speeds but it then encourages me to keep up with it, yet so often I feel a need to stop and repeat a phrase, which I then do, while a metronome otherwise would expect me to have continued. Therefore, as I was saying, once a metronome has given me the tempo I then turn it off and in my own space and time I then get on with my study, metronome free!

Oh yeah, I eventually do speed it up but only when I am ready to do so.

Re: Conciously incompetent

I agree O’. I may be relatively newish to flute, but I’ve been a bassist all my life (well at least for the last 55 years or so). As such it has always been my job to set tempo, rhythm, and dynamics on stage in any group (never played in an orchestra) I’ve ever played in. That is not to say I’ve been the leader. It is the job of the bass to create a platform on which other player stand. A bass is not a metronome, but the bass always knows where the beat is. That said, I know something about tempo and rhythm. May I add to what you just said?

I believe that the metronome is the most valuable tool any musician can have. When I hear some say they never use one or can’t play with one , the more sure I am that they need one. Of course music is about creating and resolving tension. That’s true about every part of a tune or a song. So…if you don’t know with absolute certainty where the beat is you can’t “play” with it to create any kind if dynamic effect. You’re just flailing. I’ve observed that people who can’t play with a metronome can’t play in time with other musicians either. One way to use the metronome is to leave it on and stay with it…effective in a clocklike approach and a good way to start out. Another is to leave it on and listen to the effect you create as you move away from and resolve to, the strict “click, click, click”. I suggest that this way helps one understand the conflict/resolution dynamic. Yet another method is, as nicely suggested, to listen to the metronome until you “get a lock” on the rhythm then turn it off leaving yourself free to play what you feel. This where you want to be. Still no matter how much I wish it were different, I’ve found that you can’t start anything at the end. It’s a process. Whenever I find myself faltering in any way with a tune the first thing I do is reach for the metronome. The problem is usually somewhere in matching melody to rhythm. Foot tapping is no substitute. Foot follows fingers and not the other way around.

Oh and there’s one other thing a metronome does for you. It keeps you from playing alone. It’s one other guy in the room that you must listen and relate to…not a great melody player…but he can teach you a thing or two about playing with others.

Madoc, but the point is that any natural irregularities to a tune are more naturally found and more naturally modified during the process of learning a tune without a rigid tempo. Sure, you could first learn a tune by a disciplined tempo, but then any deviation from that template becomes more of an artificial construct, not so natural. Again, playing against a metronome tends to ingrain a strict definition of a melody into a player’s mind, but beginning with a tune as a ball of soft modeling clay allows artistic impulses to be freer and more spontaneous, and also allows for abandoning those same artistic impulses and going on to another interpretation.

Re: Conciously incompetent

Sorry Mark, but I completely disagree with your post. Metronomes point out problem areas, and if you chose to repeatedly flub your way through them that’s on you, not the metronome. Also, slowing down in the middle of a tune for the problem areas is an awful way of dealing with them. Don’t set your tempo at the speed that you can handle the easy parts. You don’t want to practice rhythmic irregularities or anything else that you don’t want to end up in your playing. You are much better practicing the problem areas in isolation until you get them up to speed and then going back to playing the entire tune, or else playing the whole tune at a tempo that you can manage the trouble areas at. What you do to scales in the privacy of your own home is between you and your priest.

Re: Conciously incompetent

Here’s a thought about something that’s axiomatic about just everything we do. When the rubber hits the road we don’t do what we know…we do what we’ve practiced. If we practice a thing poorly, we do it poorly. If we practice a tune, slowing down for the more difficult parts, back to tempo or even glossing over the easy figures, that’s how we play it. So…if we practice/learn a tune without a solid rhythmic foundation we play it like that. It can likely sound intricately wonderful that way…as long as we aren’t playing with anyone else. Multiple players, even a group of two, each with his/her own ideas of rhythmic structure is, well let’s just say it sounds like a phrase beginning with “cluster”. Of course…of course…it’s the variations in rhythm and tempo that create the tension/resolution that makes a tune interesting. Who wants to play with a drum machine? But to make those variations work in a group requires either a lot of rehearsal (as in a show band) or the ability and willingness to listen carefully to what other players are doing and adapt ( that works both ways). That’s what a metronome can teach you, to listen and adapt. After all, Metro Gnome is a rather unimaginative player, but he’s rock steady on the timing and that frees you to learn to be as creative as you wish. Yeah, if you practice duplicating his timing you’ll play just like him, technically solid but without imagination but if you don’t know where the beat is, you’re not creating, you’re just drifting. As a bassist I play around with the beat all the time, but I always, I’ll say it again, ALWAYS, have to know where the beat is. The rest of the band and the audience depend on it.

Frankly, I’ve never met a player without no sense of timing who owned a metronome. Some of us are gifted with near perfect rhythm, some have to work at it…some more than others…and some are in a state of disbelief about their timing. Bottom line, get a metronome and learn to use it. How you use it is up to you, there is more than one way to make it work (personally I like the way suggested by O’M and his reasoning). At the very least it will keep you honest about your rhythm. Don’t give up and throw it in the corner if it’s hard to do. The harder it is, the more you need it and the better you’ll be for it.

Re: Conciously incompetent

Ross, I agree with your posts on metronome use. You did say ‘Who wants to play with a drum machine?’ and I do agree with your sentiment. However - I think a decent drum track can be quite useful to practise with, and you can get a little bit of sense of interaction too. By ‘decent’, I mean a track that’s measured (lasts the length of the tune or set), has three or four drum sounds, is quantised (humanised) and has a few turns and fills. These days, that’s an easily affordable option and easy-to-make too. I’ve made some for myself.

Back to the metronome - I do agree with the value it has in helping keep time. To me, it’s on a few levels. If you’re at the early learning stages on an instrument, you may be mis-timing without realising, when playing an awkward phrase or unusual pattern, etc.

If you’re a competent player, it’s still very easy to mis-time (albeit that it may not be *too* bad), so the metro or drum track is a good way of keeping you right. You know, you phrase, you push and pull the time, but sometimes you get ahead of the click or fall behind it, without correction.

There have been discussions on here before about metronomes, and some just don’t believe in them at all. Fine - I can’t really debate on that level. All the same, I don’t see how a lone player can know with certainty that his/her timing is good, without using something as an external reference.

One last point - the reference to ‘you’ is of course the reference to the generic ‘one’ :)
