Re: 1/2 vs 1st position (fiddle)

Nonsense, I live in the gutter, I merely lie here looking at the stars.

I’m not in the least offended,it’s just that the technical skills that are required for playing Irish music well and for playing other types of music well are not at all the same. If you need to practise arpeggios and scales, maybe you’re missing the point that they are part of the tunes (as your teacher pointed out) therefore all you need to do is play the tunes.

Take a tune you know well and can play in tune, then play it in a different key, and make sure the intervals sound the same. There’s no need to make this stuff complex. All you have to do is 1) love it passionately
2) play it endlessly
3) listen, listen, listen

That means hearing how the tune changes when you can’t make a run of notes without crossing strings, how a triplet fits differently when it uses different notes, how the fiddle responds differently to different strings, how the rhythmic emphasis may or may not change depending on the availability of open strings to hit certain notes hard. In short, play the tunes you know in different keys until those keys become familiar. It’s a lot more fun, and more effective. That’s speaking from experience. I thought scales would help when I wanted to start playing tunes in F (one of the nicest fiddle keys) but the only thing that helped me play tunes in F was to play tunes in F. At first it sounded sh it, but now it sounds ok. Now the pattern my fingers make when I’m playing tunes in F is as familiar as the pattern they make when I’m playing in other keys. The tunes are all made up of the same twisty-turny stuff, regardless of the key, and it’s the twisty-turny stuff that gives the tunes the life and lift that is entirely absent from a scale of notes (unless you’re a mathemetician I guess), so you can’t learn the life and lift in F just by playing a scale in F. Surely that’s easy to understand? It’s not snarky, it’s not elitist, it’s not insulting, it’s just the benefit of my experience.

I may have been dismissive in my first post, I certainly wasn’t offended in my second, I hope I’m not being obnoxious in my third, but it’s just beside the point to use scales. I can play a scale over and over again until it’s in tune, then I can play a tune using the same scale and it sounds sh it. That is because the relationship between the notes of the tune isn’t the same as that between the notes of the scale - the twists and turns of even the simplest tune are unique to this music - and playing those twists and turns at speed, and in tune, can’t be achieved by mechanistically repeating ascending and descending scales. Irish music doesn’t follow the scales like that - the twists and turns ARE the music - they’re not just a part of it.

It’s like exams - they only test how good you are at sitting exams. Scales only determine how good you are at playing scales. How good you are at playing scales doesn’t make a fiddler’s fu ck of a bit of difference to how well you play the tunes.

Is it possible that your practice routine of playing scales and arpeggios doesn’t particularly prepare you well for playing the tunes, and *that* is why your playing “goes all to the dogs” when you play the tunes out? All I’m suggesting is that if this routine isn’t producing the results you want, maybe it’s worth considering changing your routine.

I have nothing against isolating and woodshedding on scales and arpeggios, but personally, I only do them when they’re part of tunes that I want to play. That way, I can woodshed on them separately *and* in the context of the tune, so I learn the transitions in and out of those phrases, and how they fit the feel of the tune. This allows me to play a scale run in a tune without making it sound like a scale--it will sound like a musical phrase instead of a scale exercise.

And if I train wreck on a tune at the local session one night, I’ll spend some time playing it super slow the next day, really getting inside the tune and sussing out any hiccups. Such slow playing is invaluable. But I spend *all* of it playing the actual tunes.

YMMV. Apologies if you’ve already tried this route and it didn’t work, either. Just hoping to help you troubleshoot your “going to the dogs” issue. :-)

Re: 1/2 vs 1st position (fiddle)

Yeah, I know it’s an old thread, but it was referenced in another recent discussion, so ….

Ab, Eb, Bb are all in 1st position, assuming you play the semitone above the open strings G, D, A, starting on 1st finger.

If you want to know what is playable without changing hand position :

for (high) Db, on 2nd string : only 2nd position (1st note is Db, 1st finger)

for (low) Db, Gb and B : these can all be played in either 1st or 2nd position.

as for Brad’s comment : [* … the more mastery one has on an instrument the better of an all around player one will become. Going through the scales is A #1 way of whipping intonation into shape - which is a problem I have & am working on. *] … I couldn’t agree more.

Intonation problems are ‘root level’ problems, and are completely genre-independent.

Brad’s practice above is a tried and tested method of improving intonation, regardless of what music you play.

The scales in the flat keys he mentioned (although not used much in Irish music) are still beneficial. If you only ever played scales in the ‘open’ keys, you could tend to subconsciously rely on the sympathetic ringing tones of the adjacent strings to help keep your intonation true. If you practice in the flat keys, you are forced to recognise only the intervals between the notes you play, with no other reference.

It can also help to prevent pattern-locking, depending on the individual.

It’s down to personal preference and individual players, really. Some will avoid any exercise, and just play ‘the tunes’. Sometime that works, but other times the root intonation problem simply gets propagated to the other 72,000 tunes, the ear gets used to it, and … :)
