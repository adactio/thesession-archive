Music to grow up to

Rather than hijack another very good thread, I’m starting this one. There have been discussions here about people’s musical influences, but not about what they actually heard in the home growing up. Have you come from a musical family? Are you one of the lucky ones whose parents and aunts and uncles all played in ceilidh bands and let you absorb Irish music like breathing air when you were a kid? Or were you like me, forced to listen to “easy listening” orchestral “music” on the kitchen radio because your mother thought it was “relaxing”? Did you *like* the music you heard at home?

All stories welcomed!

Re: Music to grow up to

My mom didn’t put on much music, it was only after my dad died that I learned she liked Patsy Cline and other early country music. My dad was a trumpet player, who was quite good when he was young (sat in with Jack Teagarden’s band at the 39 World’s Fair in NYC, for example). He was a big jazz fan, and when I was young he was going through a N’Awlins phase, so I heard a lot of Dukes of Dixieland, Al Hirt and Pete Fountain. We also had lots of records from Broadway musicals like The Music Man, My Fair Lady, etc.
Myself, I was into the rock bands that mixed in jazz like Chicago and Blood, Sweat and Tears. I was also big on Carole King and James Taylor, and other folkies.
In fact, over time, James Taylor became the one artist that everyone in my family, my parents, brothers, me and my wife, and my son, and all the rest, could all agree upon that we liked.
The traditional stuff is something I discovered as I followed my liking for folk music in different directions over the years.

Re: Music to grow up to - Gilbert and Sullivan: the latter had a gift for tunes.

I didn’t grow up playing anything tho’ my sisters went through the piano grades. We had a primitive gramophone and a load of unused 78’s, plus a few 1960’s favourites - not pop, but Flanders and Swann, Peter Sellars (who famously stuffed English, Scottish and Irish folk music on equal terms on one of his records!.), Mary Poppins, The Sound Of Music, Fiddler On The Roof, some Elgar / Tchaikovsky / Schubert / other classics. And just the odd Scottish dance music E.P. Carols round the piano at Christmas.

At eleven though I got hooked on Gilbert and Sullivan through some of their songs being taught in group singing classes at the school I went to. Sullivan (who saw the G&S operas as potboilers, and really wanted to be recognised for his more formal work) was a great tunesmith, and the sheer catchiness of his tunes and overtures, among other things, is something I recognised when I came across ITM later. This need not surprise - his father was an Irish bandleader working in England (his mother having an Italian, possibly Jewish, background).

I wonder if any trad band has taken on G&S numbers or overtures, in the UK or Ireland or anywhere else. The early ’80’s De Danann line-up with Mary Black et al. would certainly have been able to do so, if they’d felt so inclined, what with their being able to do “The Entry Of The Queen Of Sheba” and those music hall songs. I think Sullivan can fairly be claimed by Ireland as one of the great composers and musicians to have come from that land, even if he did not live, study or work there.

Re: Music to grow up to

My first exposyre to music was probably The Archers theme tune (n.b. a single jig). In addition to Britain’s longest running soap opera, my mother has always listened to classical music, with a particular fondness for Mozart - I think ‘Eine Kleine Nachtmusik’ has been fairly deeply ingrained in me(As for the ‘Mozart effect’, I think that could be a fallacy). Also, a Jewish upringing has meant that I was infused with the music of the Synagogue from an early age. Neither of my parents had much interest in contemporary pop music, although, rummaging through their 45 collection as a child, I remember finding and listening to such gems as Lena Horne, Millie, The Seekers - and Scott Joplin’s Rags played by Joshua Rifkin (an LP, not a 45).

Unfortunately, growing up in the ’80s, for much of my childhood, I was also exposed to the terribly cheesy chart music that was around at the time. Although, thinking about it, there always was, and still is, cheese around - I just happened to like it. Also, I should not underestimate the influence of Sesame Street.

My early teenage years were dominated by an obsession with Queen. Then The Beatles, Pink Floyd, Led Zeppelin, Traffic, Love, The Byrds… A cassette of Hungarian zither music, that I bought on holiday with my parents, aged 17, then drew me into some hazy notion of something called ‘folk music’. I went on to explore the world of mediaeval and renaissance music, but ultimately, I think, found ‘living’ music more attractive. Aged 19-20, I started to familiarize myself with the folk and traditional music of the British Isles, among other places, and began to play tunes on the mandolin. Meeting an Irish beginner box/whistle/bodhran player at university and forming a band started to steer my interest toward Irish music.

And this is the music I’ve been growing up to ever since.

My mother, may she rest in peace, loved Broadway shows, and
she bought many recordings of them when I was a child in the ’60’s. My favorites were Hair and Jesus Christ Superstar.
I must have played each record hundreds of times and I learned all the lyrics to these shows at the same time I was learning “You’re a Grand Old Flag” in school.
At the same time, she was also kind of folky and she listened to a program called “Woody’s Children” every Saturday night from 6 to 7 PM on a NY radio station. This show played anything that would have made Woody Guthrie smile, so the producers believed.
These influences, alongside AM radio singles, are my primary musical memories of my childhood.
My only childhood memory of “Irish” music, despite my mostly Irish heritage is hearing songs like “When Irish Eyes Are Smiling”, just the first verse always, sung acapella on or about St. Patrick’s Day, by someone who had to much to drink. I then somehow found my way to ITM on my own self-led journey that began with rock guitar, and passed through CBGB (Country BlueGrass & Blues). Now I work on banjo triplets with the same intensity that helped me to learn show lyrics 40 or so years ago.

Re: Music to grow up to

The feature event in my Mum’s life was when she played a piano concert in my home town as a kid. She still has the program, and until recently she would occasionally play the organ, mostly hymns that she would come back home singing when she went to church. My Dad’s claim to fame was his ability to ape some common Scottish reels on the voice pipes. He didn’t play an instrument, but he should have. In later life he collected music of the musak kind of stuff, for playing in the car when Mum and Dad were travelling around Oz. He loved big band and would sing along with anything. Dad would organise trips in the car into great fun singing sessions. Neither had any interest or connection with Irish Traditional Music. Why would they play Irish as they were third generation Australians of mixed Scottish/Irish/British ancestory?

As a baby boomer, I was exposed to Peter Paul and Mary and Pete Seeger through my older brother who had a paper run and saved up his earnings to buy our first grammaphone. I was facinated by the tune “Super ski-er” - “Well they called him super ski-er as he sat there on the sundeck, for he swore that he would never take a spill, but when they finally brought him down they had to use three toboggans to carry all the pieces down the hill…….”. Del Shannon’s “Runaway, a-run-run-run-run-run away”.

Because no-one in our house could afford records for a while, Del Shannon got played over and over and over. Loved it. I was eight when my Grandpa went to England and brough back a new English bride who had been an air-raid warden in the blitz. She gave us her old wartime 78’s from London, so we sang along with those too. Aussie Bandstand, the early Bee Gees, Simon and Garfunkel, The Beatles were always there in the background, but I wasn’t into buying records then. I guess I was just waiting for music to happen for me - and it did, in the 70’s with the big revival and my discovery of ITM.
