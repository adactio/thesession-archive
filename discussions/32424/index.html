By hand, CreadurMawnOrganig? Are you mad? You’re made of sterner stuff than I.

One aspect of horseradish preparation that I find really interesting is that the heat doesn’t really kick in until you cut into the root --when various enzymes in the cellular structure start to mix, it creates a chemical reaction. You can stop the reaction by adding vinegar. Anyone who has developed pre-digital photography will be familiar with the ‘fixer’ that stops the silver from continuing to darken on the paper--that’s what vinegar does to grated/pureed horseradish, it ‘fixes’ it. You can get the level of heat that you want by letting the horseradish sit for a shorter or longer time before adding the vinegar. Wow, man. Chemistry you can eat.

I think with the reskinning of the yella board, its more of a horseradish theme than mustard these days. Yet somehow with a milder flavour.

“Horseradish and beetroot sauce sounds intriguing. I’ve not had it. Typically served with.. what? Pierogi, brisket or kielbasa?”

If you’re Jewish, it’s probably most often served with gefillte fish or cold sliced meat. If, like me, you don’t eat either of the former, then it goes nicely with most veggie things, so long as they don’t have a strong enough taste to conflict with it, I suppose. It’s nice just spread on a slice of matzah (roughly speaking, that’s water biscuits/plain crackers, for the uninitiated).

I see you’re now in Dublin, Joe. You probably won’t find many Jewish shops there (although there is a small Jewish community), but there are at least a couple of Polish grocers there that might well have it http://www.yelp.ie/search?find_desc=Polish+Food+Shop&find_loc=Dublin.

Alternatively, you could make it yourself. Having studied the labels of a few different brands/varieties, the essential ingredients are beetroot, horseradish, vinegar and sugar - trial, error, taste and commonsense should allow you to arrive at the right proportions. I suspect that the beetroot is probably boiled first, but raw might work as well.

Back on the subject of mustard, I did a week-long house-sit in July, for a neighbour with two cats. On my first evening there, having served he cats their favourite dish of fish-flavoued biscuits, tinned meat in jelly and worming tablets, I set about preparing a quick meal for myself. I searched the kitchen for a pepper mill or ready-ground black pepper, but all I could find were whole peppercorns and a stone pestle and mortar; no matter - it would do the job admirably. I reached up to take the pestle and mortar down from the shelf but, underestimating their weight, I failed to support them adequately and allowed them to tip slightly. This was enough to cause the pestle to precede the mortar off the shelf, crashing down onto a small, delicate, glazed mustard pot. Needless to say, the mustard pot came off the worse of the two. I managed a reasonable job of gluing it back together, although the lid handle, a large part of which was made up of thick glaze, will never quite be the same again.

So, that’s the story of the mustard pot. Continuing on a sinapic theme, a couple of days later, a close friend of the owner of the house (and a friend of mine) came round for dinner. Being a much more frequent visitor to the house than me and knowing the kitchen intimately (a bit of a foodie as well), he revealed that there was a huge plastic tub of wholegrain mustard in the cupboard. He also let me into the secret of how well it accompaies broad beans. (Dang! I’ve spilled the beans now).

It’s such a privilege to have this opportunity to tell stories about mustard. I wonder how long it will last before it is deemed subversive.

Smoked Mackerel Paté ~ with a bit of mustard ;-)

A good dollop of mustard for this one, in the most recent case, today, it was a home made one with horseradish and herbs, pretty potent, and by dollop it was a heaping spoon’s worth.

around 250 grams of smoked mackerel, and you can use any of the seasoned varieties if you like for added interest, I skinned them and then fried the skin up into a crisp, a kind of fish bacon, which I enjoy munching on, or on toast or like a bacon butty.

200 - 300 grams of cream cheese - flavoured or not

Maybe about 150 grams of finely grated strong cheddar, or whatever cheese you like.

several cloves of garlic, and I can get carried away here, but I only used three large cloves this time.

Your choice of pepper ground - we tend to use a three colours pepper mix ~ green, pink and black, sometimes white too but not today.

Whiz it all together for a mackerel paté. We particularly like this with oatcakes, a nice tartar sauce and cucumber…

Missing ~ I usually add zest of a lemon or lime and some juice from the same, but I didn’t this time, but might add and mix it in later after I have a taste and think about it… ;-)
