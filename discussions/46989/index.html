Re: What do whistlers do for tunes they can’t play?

Personal view from one whistle player: If maybe you are talking about tunes that drop below low D (plenty of lovely reels do: Farewell to Erin, Cedars of Lebanon etc) then this whistler at least will simply fake the note by playing it in the lowest octave available (so for the A below low D I will simply play the A above low D). It changes the tune but not in a way that is out of tune with the rest of the session. I’ve heard other whistle players who will change whistles for the tunes that go low (ie change to a low D) but most ones I know are not going to avoid playing a lovely tune just for the lack of a note or two!

For tunes that use accidentals not easily available in the standard D whistle, well you can change to a different key of whistle if you really want or like myself, switch over to the flute if it’s still possible. Some whistlers are very proficient at half-holing a note but I’ve never found it easy to do at speed. And if it is in B flat I will most likely just sit it out and continue with my pint while listening.

Re: What do whistlers do for tunes they can’t play?

Why wouldn’t a whistle player be able to play a tune on whistle? Is it because whistles are keyed? Also, isn’t it common practice for whistle players to have whistles in multiple keys?

Concertinas are keyed also(My Anglo is a C/G in a Wheatstone layout), and even with the accidental row, things get really weird when you start getting into keys with more than 2 sharps/flats. For example, the Wheatstone layout only has one C#/D# button, so if I want to play tunes in the key of AMaj, I only have one path for phrases that use notes Bnat and C# consecutively in either direction, if I want to stay in technique and maintain flow of the tune. Things get even weirder in the key of EMaj because of the removal of the Dnat note found in the keys of C and G. This isn’t to say that these tunes can’t be played on the concertina, but it does make for some interesting navigation work that you don’t encounter on chromatic instruments like the piano. On piano, navigation is only about finger-crossing and pivoting technique, but the path is always the same. There are finger crossing techniques for the concertina, but there is the added challenge of finding the appropriate path, because there are multiple paths any given phrase could take.

Re: What do whistlers do for tunes they can’t play?

If you want to learn a tune that is outside of the standard scale of whistle notes, or has accidentals, or has ornaments that are difficult, the best thing to do is find a tune that exposes your weaknesses and practice it over and over for weeks, or months, until you’re comfortable with it.

Here’s one to start with: https://thesession.org/tunes/16070#setting30274

If a tune has an unworkable fingering on a D whistle (or keyless flute for that matter), like, say, a roll on Bb, then adapt to a different figure which suggests the notes without seeming that you’re making a mistake.

Always exude an air of confidence and very few will question your choices.

↳ Posted by Michael Eskin on April 3rd, 2022.

Re: What do whistlers do for tunes they can’t play?

My whistler friends can play most tunes that come up at trad sessions. They use a D whistle for a lot of tunes, with occasional half-holing to get accidentals, or folding to get notes below the low D. For tunes in Gdor and Ddor (which fiddlers seem drawn to), they keep a C whistle handy. An A whistle can be useful, particularly for tunes in D that go below D. A Bb whistle rounds out the quiver.

These four whistles put most session tunes within easy reach.

Some whistle players take up flute and get a keyed flute to cover all the notes.

Some learn a completely different instrument that can play a wide range of tunes—fiddle, tenor banjo, concertina, etc. Then they bring their whistles and fiddle or whatever to the session.

As a fiddler, primarily, when asked, I’ve played particular tunes in a more whistle-friendly key to accommodate a whistle or keyless flute player.

Re: What do whistlers do for tunes they can’t play?

The difference is that generally a un-keyed flute player has one flute, and generally that flute is in D (as would be defined for a whistle) and has the same challenges as a D whistle for keys that require half-holing or cross-fingerings.

A flute player isn’t, at least in my experience, going to show up with a pile of unkeyed flutes in different keys, while it’s fairly standard for a whistle player to bring one in D and one in C to cover the vast majority of the tunes.

Instead, a un-keyed flute player will use the amazing power of money to add jewelry to the flute they have in the form of shiny new keys, or upgrade to a 4-key or more flute to more easily play those more challenging tunes and leave the un-keyed flute at home.

Many fluters also play whistle and keep a C, A, and Bb in their kit.

That said, and to xabeba’s point, some tunes do fall more naturally on a given instrument than on others. Likely this stems from certain tunes originating on a specific instrument—many of Paddy Fahey’s tunes make perfect sense on fiddle, for example, but they’re not the most whistle friendly.

In a session, if the fiddles all play a run of Cm and other flat-key tunes, I’m likely to start or suggest a set that favors the wind players so they can get some circulation back in their fingers. It’s a welcome courtesy to pay attention to who’s sitting out the sets and come up with tunes suited to their instrument, ability, and repertoire (if you know it).

For sure there are a lot of tunes, maybe most tunes, that can be played and cross fingering/half holing are viable, if imperfect, solution. Keys do not a better player make. The F, Ab, Bb, C, and even the little Eb can be played with half hole, cross fingers, slide through, heavy lip or keys. My point is that our 8 keyed flutes are capable of playing anything (even if I am not). It is a choice to use or not use them. We all make choices different from each other. For instance I use the long F almost exclusively, the C much more often, the Ab and Bb are preferred but there are plenty of times I’ll alternate them. Other players just as successfully make other choices. No harm, no foul. I have flutes in C, F, and Eb (a Bb and keyed F are ordered) and rarely use them. When I do it’s because I lack the talent. As for the weight thing, my heaviest is an 8 keyed, post mounted, Blackwood at 15 ounces. The lightest is a 4 keyed, short foot rosewood at 9 ounces. At 76 years old I can still handle the extra mass. (In fact I tether the rosewood to my shirt to keep it from floating off😉).

In short even if I use them less often keys give me a better tone, more useable range, and more flexibility and that makes them worth every penny.

↳ Posted by ross faison on April 5th, 2022.

Re: What do whistlers do for tunes they can’t play?

“The broader issue is this idea that some people have that if they’re at a session they have to be playing on everything - if you don’t know the tune, don’t play it. That goes for any instrument.”

Well said, Nico.

A big part of what makes a given session “friendly” is if someone (or ideally everyone) is paying attention to who’s being left out by the chosen tunes, pace, keys/modes, etc. A session isn’t *required* to accommodate all the players present, of course, but if the core players harbor any sense at all of wanting to be welcoming and inclusive, it helps to think about tune choices, keys/modes, pace and so on so that you’re not inadvertently limiting someone’s participation. (I also understand what might drive some sessions to opt for Eb instruments or an hour straight of Gm reels if they’re trying to discourage certain disruptive elements.)

A good session anchor will be mindful of who’s been sitting on their hands and play something appropriate to get them playing again. Being conscious and considerate about this can go a long way toward preventing people from (1) feeling left out or (2) trying to play on every tune even if they can’t. Folks are generally happier to sit out a set when they know they can join in on the next one.
