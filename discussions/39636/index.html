I think the headline is a intentionally triggering, like its often the case with online articles, for generating more clicks…
Isn’t it a quiet obvious, that talent has a distinct impact ? In the article, they pointed out, that the working memory of very good pianists is above the average working memory of other people. So, other people are just natural gifted and will get better results an various activities than others. That seems to be a matter of fact.

But on the other hand, there is NO ONE who is really good or professional at playing an instrument, for example, who doesn’t also practice very much. A line of the article made me laugh
“In sports—one of the areas in which deliberate practice seems to make the most difference—it turned out that the more advanced the athlete, the less of a role practice plays.”
Otherwise, if there was a linear improvement with the practice, in sports for example, after enough years, an Olympic athlete should be able to compete on the speed of light,or jump 10 meters in the air…a much more interesting question in the article would have been, what happens with an olympic athlete when he stops practicing ? For me, the conclusion is, there are more gifted people, but neither they or the average people can be very good, without practice. Becoming better is only determined by practice (the time you invest, but of course also how you practice) not by your talent (for the not 1 percent).

Re: Practice Doesn’t Make Perfect

Right you are, David. Practice is essential no matter what one’s skill level or innate ability.

There’s no denying talent exists, but whether you have it or not, it’s bad work ethic simply to fall back on it whenever you feel lazy or lack motivation . . . eventually, that way, your “talent” will fail you anyway, because you haven’t really honed it. At the best, you won’t improve. Which stinks.

The last paragraph mentioned, “Saying that training is everything may be tempting, but it’s wrong. ‘One of the criticisms people direct at us is that we’re killing people’s dreams,’ Hambrick says.” What I believe to be the true murderer of dreams, however, is the very phrase, “Practice makes perfect.” It’s the sort of thing fledglings (like myself) get told, but it just makes a fellow feel cheated when he practices at something for four years and is still nowhere near perfection. Also makes him feel like something’s the matter with himself because the classic adage (presumably classic because it is timelessly and universally applicable) doesn’t work on him.

I don’t believe that practice makes perfect. And I don’t believe it needs to. (What is “perfect,” anyway? It’s totally subjective.) I have learned to be--and learned that it is much better to be--satisfied with, “Practice makes better.” Because true motivation must come from within, not without. You can’t compete with your image of “perfect”--that’s far beyond you, whatever your level. But you can compete with what you are; that’s tangible; that’s achievable. You can try to get better, little by little, and then after a long and patient age, look back and see how much you have improved.

I don’t know if what I have said has made much sense to anyone else. So many of my thoughts, I find, are so easy to understand, yet so difficult to explain . . . Do you folks ever feel the same . . . ?

Re: Practice Doesn’t Make Perfect

The book “The Talent Code” by Daniel Coyle is worth reading on this subject and totally convinced me. A very interesting book, he concludes that truly gifted sportmen, musicians etc are rarity and that practice is the thing but not just any practice, but what he calls “deep practice” at the very edge of our ability. I can’t do justice to what he says in a couple of sentences but the evidence he produces from the research he’s done convinces me of his argument. Basically it’s about a brain tissue called myelin that wraps itself around the “circuits” we create in our brain to carry out a particular task or movement. The more myelin layers a circuit has the better and more efficiently it performs. Age reflects the ability to produce myelin which is why children learn so much faster . . . but myelination is also dependent on not just practice, but the WAY we practice and he’s amassed loads of evidence to support his findings. You might not agree with what he says but on the basis of this book, I’ve totally changed the way I practice the low whistle.

“How would you train another Mozart or Shankar or Martyn Bennett?” I think the book address just that question. Read it. It totally convinced me.

Yes I so often feel that way. I’ve been a professional artist my whole life, well except for a few years when I was an art teacher. And I never believed in ‘talent’. There’s a great art teacher Dr Betty Edwards who got AFAIK 100% of her students drawing well (she’s retired from teaching now AFAIK). She wrote an influential book Drawing On The Right Side Of The Brain. I’ve taught using that book and it’s very successful.

Now I’m a manager of an art department and I hire artists. I look through their portfolios and I (and anyone) can immediately tell if they’ve had formal training or not. There are fundamental mistakes that all untrained artists make that no trained artists make. (I tell people they beat all those errors out of you in art school…)

I have to laugh when people ask “did you go to art school or are you just naturally talented?” To me this question comes from an erroneous set of assumptions. People don’t pop out of the womb with a knowledge of human anatomy, the structure of the skull, to name a couple critical things people have to know to be able to draw people well.

So to me it’s absurd how musicians, for example, are often referred to as “talent” in the business. Yep they just were born knowing everything about music theory, how to play their instruments at the highest level, and so forth. It’s ridiculous. I professional jazz musician told me once “there is no talent. It’s study, and practice.”

So I didn’t believe in ‘talent’ at all, until I started teaching music. One example will suffice: I had two students (separate lessons) who just happened to be at the same point in learning the pipes one particular day. At each lesson I demonstrated a particular ornament. One student played it exactly right the first time he tried it, and every other time afterwards. He never had to study the ornament, never had to practice it. He just heard it once and immediately could play it perfectly every time.

The other student struggled to play it. I had to break it down into discrete steps, which we labouriously worked through for the rest of that day’s lesson. At next week’s lesson he was still having a hard time with it, though he had practiced it every day. A year later, though he had meticulously practiced it nearly daily, when asked to play it 10 times in a row he would play it correctly maybe 7 out of 10 times.

Re: Practice Doesn’t Make Perfect

I think the “one-size fits all” idea of how to practice is often counterproductive. Once you find a process that works for you, stick with it. Students will ask me, “How long should I practice each day and what should I practice?” I can tell them what works for me, but with the caveat that what works for me, may or may not work for them. I figured out years ago that the way for me to learn tunes and develop new skills was not to spend hours in the chair beating a tune or technique to death, but rather shorter very precise practice sessions spaced out over the course of the day. 10 minutes before breakfast, 10 minutes after. 10 minutes here and there during the day (I work from home a lot). People tell me practicing is hard, or it’s a lot of effort to learn tunes. With my method I have found that I can learn tunes and techniques with nearly zero effort. Any “effort” that occurs is something I bring to the process, either by trying to play too quickly too soon, or not following the process to let my brain do what it needs to do to assimilate the information and build the muscle memory. I suggest everyone find their own process and then stick to it rather than blindly following anyone else’s advice on how to learn, including mine!

It is, therefore, very unlikely that any individual will have every natural advantage which would help them to play a particular instrument because all their natural advantages have evolved to enable them to do quite different things and it is only by chance that they also contribute to their ability to wield a bow, pound a bellows or blow into a tube and make a particular kind of noise. Because there are so many different advantages and talents which an individual might or might not have, it is likely that most people start with some of them and no-one has all of them. The person who finds it hard to memorise a tune, for instance, might have an advantage on rhythm. The person with over-small hands might have an advantage of dexterity. It’s pretty meaningless to talk about “talent” as if it was one thing. That’s why it matters what you practise. Everyone needs to enhance their own particular talents and compensate for their own particular deficiencies.

Re: Practice Doesn’t Make Perfect

What I find really interesting is not the discussion of what makes the best-of-the-best, but what the average person is capable of - if they let go of preconceptions about ‘having no natural talent’.

My username reflects the fact that I did not take up music until my mid-thirties - precisely because I believed I wasn’t born with the natural talent that musicians ‘should’ have. Music didn’t magically flow out of my fingers when I had a few piano lessons as a child. Every aspect of sheet music was a baffling mystery, basic timekeeping seemed beyond me. Nobody in my family played an instrument, I knew no musicians and (crucially) we had no piano at home, so I did no practice. I thought that you had to be good at something the first time you tried it, otherwise you just weren’t ‘talented’.

Incidentally, as a child I was labelled a ‘talented’ artist and went on to enjoy drawing for many years but in retrospect, I’m convinced that this was more to do with the availability of pencils and paper and the encouragement of my family, in contrast to the absolute unavailability of access to any musical instruments or any musical instruction.

Clearly innate ability has SOMETHING to do with it - I work with people with severe physical and intellectual disabilities who will never master sitting or walking unaided, never mind the complex physical demands of playing a musical instrument. But I don’t believe it when someone with average capabilities says they’re ‘just not musical’ or ‘just not artistic’. With sufficient interest, guidance and practice, most people can achieve a good, even high, level of competence!

Re: Practice Doesn’t Make Perfect

For me the motivation is core to this. I have been able to excel in several areas through my life, but only when I felt it worth the effort. Part of that motivation seems to hinge on their being a worthwhile reward which would merit the effort involved. For me that never is financial (just how I roll) but normally involves some form of excitement or social engagement or inclusion. If I see those as being open possibilities than I can begin to be motivated to make the effort required.
Without that I wouldn’t even begin to be motivated to expend the effort.

On a related issue, the best teachers I have had have been those who can read this & create the vision of the end reality for me as a student. Other very successful teachers I have observed ignore this and only deal with already motivated students who bring that with them, leaving the rest to drop out to be replaced by more talented students on their waiting lists. One approach mines all the talent the other filters it. There are merits in both approaches, but until I chose to immerse myself in music, I have always needed the image painting motivators to inspire me. With music. This has changed to needing little or no external motive. I think those who find this early are seen as talented because they are hungry for anything that feeds that vision. I wish I had found it early in life, but I sure won’t waste it now.

As to practice there seems to be a wider confusion between exercising and practicing which goes way beyond this thread. I think it is a linguistic confusion where the words really mean the same thing but people try to use practicing to mean training. Many of us don’t want to train or achieve their best in this area, they want to derive enjoyment from it. Noone else can impose their idea of enjoyment or reward on another. There is no need to be the best at this or even to be the best you can be. This can simply be an act of pleasure and should be treated as such if you have no other vision of anything else you wish to get from it. It paralells very well with other areas of pleasure where we may exercise lots and have many successful experiences with long term results, but that doesn’t mean we want to train to be the best or become a professional at it. :)
