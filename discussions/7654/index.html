How to get the joy back?

Hi anyone else who hosts a session,

Have ever just gotten completely fed up with all the B.S. that seems to come with hosting a session? How did you get back the joy?

Anyway, the end result is that I show up on Tuesday nights a joyless wreck watching the clock until its time to go home.

Anyone else gotten to this point and found a way back to the joy that was present when you started a session? I don’t want to stop hosting, we’ve got a nice thing going, but really could use some advice…

Thanks,

Michael

Re: How to get the joy back?

When faced with the usual session issues, we tried several strategies short of moving the session. I’ll list them here just in case someone might find a useful idea in them.

(1) We started a monthly slow session to help newcomers learn tunes--and perhaps more importantly, to help them understand how our regular weekly session works.

(2) We included a brief explanation of session etiquette in the front matter of our three volume set of books of tunes for the local session.

(3) We had periodic face-to-face discussions amongs all the session regulars about how to resolve problems.

(4) We also had periodic email conversations between sessions among all the session regulars about how to address certain problems.

(5) We hosted a “session lesson,” asking a highly respected visiting musician to teach a group workshop in how a session works.

(6) We took a break during summers, switching to house sessions and impromptu musical potlucks.

Ultimately, I decided to quit running the session and gladly handed the thing over to a husband and wife team who are doing a great job as we swing into fall. They’ve set up a schedule where we play at a different pub every week, rotating among four pubs a month. One is our old stomping ground, and another is a new pub just opening. The new place will accommodate only 6 - 8 players in the space set aside for us, so this will change our usual session dynamics, hopefully for the better. We’ll see.

After 8 years as an unpaid session facilitator, I’m really happy to be free of the weekly obligation (as I understood it) to show up, or find someone else who will commit to showing up and carrying the tunes. In fact, it’s rewarding to see it continue, under someone else’s guidance.

Re: How to get the joy back?

Yep. I always took time off each year, and other core players were usually happy to step in for a month or three. Not only does this give you a break, but it also helps the other players (who try to fill your shoes) understand and appreciate how much work it can be. This alone can improve group behavior and dynamics--the temporary hosts will “get it,” and they’ll then be more likely to help explain things to newbies.

At times, it also helped me to remember that a session is a community, like a neighborhood. You don’t have to be best friends with all your neighbors, and you may not even like some of them. But it’s in everybody’s best interests if you can at least get along civily. And when someone’s behavior really crosses the line, you’re well within your rights to set them straight--or exclude them--to protect the collective peace.

Re: How to get the joy back?

As far as I can tell, all groups are cyclical, including families, folkdancers, musicians, Elks clubs, . . . some times they click, sometimes they squabble. The real value, as far as I can tell again, is that they go on, and mostly they will go on even if we think they will stop if we, in particular, quit. Life isn’t quite that fair - and aren’t we glad it’s not? Where we would be if anything stopped just because we quit?

So, as a life-long “sparkplug” who is continually learning other ways of doing things, as apparently many folks here are, and many folks here already know other ways, here’s a couple of points I’ve learned over the years:

Life (and sessions) go on - tho fortunately the first time I dropped out of a group I was aware that was true, so it wasn’t really a shocker

When they go on, most of the time it’s not better or worse than it was before, it’s just different - and since no way of being can encompass everything, the new way will have its own charms, even tho you miss the old way’s charms

My friends and colleagues may go on with the group - it’s their right, and responsibility for that matter - I try to learn a little something from them about how to see the stuff from the viewpoint of someone with a different temperament and different political skills

If you’re indispensable we don’t need you - :>D - the best groups are co-ops, with many people contributing in lots of different ways - the group has lots more resiliance that way, and a better chance of absorbing/deflecting/allowing trouble makers to self-unselect attendance. And folks don’t burn out, or do so less often or less dramatically. This one is an opinion, which I like to think is fact :>)

If you want to win a tug of war - let go

There are some groups I just don’t belong in. They ain’t gonna be co-ops, and the people in them are content that way, and the system is set up to promote non co-op. I find something else to do, or another way to do what I love.

Yeah, the joy is a hard thing to lose, and sometimes it’s hard to find another way to have it. But you know, you’re cyclical, too. Sometimes one can sit back and watch a group go thru the chaos and find it really interesting how it regroups, and who plays a part in it doing so. It helps, if a group really likes being a co-op, to do some explicit things to reinforce the co-op process - many of the things Will listed are processes we use to give us some of the resilience and life.

But given all that, sometimes a group just goes in a direction that takes the fun out of it for you, and you no longer fit that particular “corporate culture”, as we used to say in organizational theory classes. Or it keeps going in the direction it’s always gone, but you change. And then you go lick your wounds and find a group in which you do fit. It may not be an ITM session, but something else you love that you haven’t done for awhile. It may be, as some folks have suggested, a kitchen session instead of a pub. It may take a while - and in the mean time you’ll change, too - and that’s a good thing. Some day you’ll come back to the original thing with a fresh start, and you’ll make a different kind of difference - who wants to make, at, oh, say, 60, the same kind of contribution they made at 18? Let the 18-year-olds do the stuff they’re uniquely set up to do. And they can be great fun. But you can contribute lots at 60, too.

And isn’t it fun when this place turns into a philosophy session?

Sessions are difficult animals since their success is dependent on cultural social norms as well as on human personality and interests. These things in combination are volitile and lead to a subjective status quo within the session itself. Ideals are as individual to people as snowflakes are to each other. If sessions are to be ‘open’ then you might expect the ‘rule structure’ and leadership to naturally shift over time since policing is generally looked down upon and people are supossed to ‘learn the ropes’ over time. Again, some peoples‘ ideas about ’the ropes‘ are going to be different and thus perhaps dissonant. There are likely many ways to combat this, I’m sure if you search thesession.org you’ll find a lot of ways! But ultimately I think a good way is to accept the fact that change will occur… so… find ways to enjoy the natural ebb and flow as it will create an interesting texture and history over time. If that kind of thing *isn’t for you* or has gotten you down, for whatever reason, you could try having a closed session every once in a while and control it as you and your mates like. You can balance it with the more ’volitile‘, for lack of better words, public session because let’s be honest, they have their benefits too! This is what my friends and I do. The ’closed’ session can be controlled better and believe it or not, can even grow. And in those times where your session sanity is up for question, it is a nice break.

Anyways, *if* you have that option, it’s worth considering since then you and your friends can get that control back. For our little group, it also gives us the opportunity to play other types of music, and instruments with good humour and no expectations.

This ‘session’ , rather ‘the yellow board’ survives despite the real world, I think, because text can be read and re-read and misunderstandings more easily sorted out. In the real world, words and actions are fleeting and therefore more difficult to sort and the sores last for a long time as a result. Too bad really…entropy. I think that when a session is in it’s infancy it is important to have at least a ‘creedo’ or ‘code of ethics’ that can be adhered to. If anything just to have something to lean on and keep things enjoyable for everybody. It’s something that is fluid and can flex or change over time but exists to give just enough structure to an otherwise freeflowing event… a cup to hold the water instead of a sandwhich bag so to speak. I really like the idea of having something humourous on the wall or something like a song for newcomers to read that tells them in a friendly way what the dynamic of the group is. Saves everyone the guesswork and allows pure justification for getting the idiots out if there are any :-)

To quote tulloch: “People seem to get all romantic about the violin”

Yeah, same thing about the accordion…. almost…. OK, not even close. :-)

Seriously, though, I’ve always felt that the music was “the music” and it leads an interesting life of it’s own, growing and changing, weaving it’s way into lives and cultures in ways you may not always expect or understand. Still, however, it’s not MY music, nor YOUR music, it’s simply the music. I can enjoy it in my house in the evenings alone after the wife and kids have gone to bed, turning tunes and weaving them together into a stream that carries my thoughts away…. And I can share them with other musicians, each applying our own interpretation of tunes and yet melding them together to make something special.

In my neighborhood, if I sat on the front porch to play, I’d likely as not get a visit from the neighbors or even police to knock off the racket, it’s a quiet neighborhood and noises of any kind outside the houses aren’t looked upon kindly. Strange, almost like a cemetary with the graves above ground, but still, ’tis how it is. No wide-eyed wonder, no amazement, just disdain I think, for ruining their nice quiet afternoon.

Oh, and by the way, my iPod is oft used and loaded with trad goodness, so the iPodders aren’t alwayas off into your worst imaginings of what “music” may be. :-)
