Each time you listen to a particular recording of ‘this’ music, you are going to reinforce on your nemory the way it was played on that occasion. This includes ornamentation, tempo, any implied harmony from backing, box basses, double stopping, regulators et cetera and the overall ‘shape’ of the tune. Only ever hearing tunes live, in the room, means you hear it newly revealed every time; you have no fixed expectation of how it will unfold until you play it. And tge same for the people you are playing it with, so you can all mess with the tune brand new every time: mimic each other, swoop & jiggle & stretch the tunes, fleetingly catch each other’s eyes with a wry smile. But that time only, because once it is played, it’s gone, waiting to be reinvented the next time. And not remember ing how it sounded on a record.

Re: the tradition is in the here & now…

Another point is that the ‘pure tradition’ may well be nothing of the sort. In ballad study, early twentieth century scholars came up with the romantic notion that songs were generated spontaneously by ‘the singing dancing throng’ - then that minstrels passed them down from generation to generation as a sort of sacred trust - but more recent research shows that the ballad tradition has been affected by broadside & chapbook hawking from the seventeenth century onwards. As soon as the likes of Michael Coleman became popular, regional styles of Irish fiddling started being affected by the Sligo style, as groups of people would club up to buy Coleman’s records and play them for friends & neighbours. Imagine someone hearing a tune played by an Irish fiddler who swears he got it from his grandaddy - but what we didn’t know is that the revered ancestor actually picked it up from a little-known recording of Coleman’s. He then modified it, which is what we still can do today with recordings; there’s no need to follow a disc slavishly & there’s also the possibility of following a ‘real musician’ slavishly. In the real world categories are blurred.

↳ Posted by Gobby on January 18th, 2014.

Re: the tradition is in the here & now…

The tradition is not oral, unless you’re talking about sean nos singing or traditional storytelling. You could make a case that it is oral in the sense that people talk about the music and about technique, but for instruments it’s better to use “aural,” as it describes how one picks up the music (by hearing it). Musicians talk about “aural skills” all the time- in fact, that’s often the name of the solfege/sight-singing/eurhythmics course in a conservatory. Both aural tradition and oral tradition are used by ethnomusicologists to describe musics that are either A) passed by by singing or speech (oral) or B) passed down by having newer musicians listen to older ones. Obviously, there’s quite a bit of overlap, so you can have an aural and oral tradition, but in the case of instrumental ITM, it would be most correctly classified as an “aural tradition,” not an oral one.

That being said, there are many oral traditions in ITM and Irish folk arts, so neither is really incorrect, depending on what exactly you’re talking about.

“ And probably the worst possible place to learn the pure drop is the cultural melting pot of a big city.”

This doesn’t ring true when it comes to the London Irish scene.
Names like Kevin Burke, John Carty, Roger Sherlock and Jimmy Power come to mind - along with several others.
However, comparing the London Irish pub scene of the 1950s, 60s, and even 70s with the current “session pub” scene would be misleading. You’d go to hear named players back then. More like a less formal “concert”. The players were either Irish born or from Irish parentage. It was those “Bothy Band” recordings (to use the one band name to encompass several other Irish bands whose recordings transformed the concept of Irish music in pubs) that had a profound effect on the music, and who played it.

Just read Kevin Burke, and how he listened to recordings of Coleman et al to get an idea of how London Irish players might learn through both live experience and recorded masters.

http://www.kevinburke.com/revelations/

http://www.kevinburke.com/misspent-youth/

To expect to learn from “Irish” pub sessions in cities without an inkling of what is excellent, good, bad or indifferent when it comes to decent articulation of the music would be optimistic, to say the least. One of the means of developing the ability to discern those playing qualities would surely be recordings.
Then you might be more aware that there are some cracking players out there - still in London too.
There are quite a few who don’t cut the mustard as well…….

I don’t know about London, I have not been inside the M25 (clockwise) for years. In the rest of England, and I suspect places in the USA, tunes regarded as Irish have been in the local non-Irish tradition for a couple of hundred years - they are in fiddler’s manuscripts etc. So an Irish session in those country’s may (and from comments on this forum often does) include musicians who are familiar with those tunes, and dance tunes in general, from other contexts (rapper, morris, ? bluegrass ? old time). Some of us may like tunes with more of the flavour of the piper than the D/G box player but it can be quite hard to carry away a good enough memory of the tune to practice with.

So recordings can help. Does it matter if they “reflect the past” if they shed light on a part of the present that is of interest ?

Don’t know that I want to even get started on this one. There’s something to be said for both sides of the argument. There’s been too many generations between the tunes composer and the guy or gal playing it today for a song not to have changed through the years.
