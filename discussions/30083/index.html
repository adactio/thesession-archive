Bear in mind that the mandolin is physically quite hard on the fingers - pressure needed is more than say for a fiddle. The two main weaknesses of cheap instruments in my experience, is too high an action which kills your fingers after a while, and plywood body, which kills projection and volume. Remember that the mandolin is not a loud isntrument to begin with.

Re: mandolin

“is there such a thing as a mandolin capo?”

Yes, although in most cases you don’t really need one when you find your way around the instrument.
However, certain tunes may sound better depending on your taste and playing style especially when you utilize adjacent strings for drone, “ringing” effects, “doubling” notes etc.

For instance(I mentioned this in a previous discussion), I may play a tune like Caliope House with a capo on the second fret. So, I’m using the same fingering as in “D” but playing it in “E”.

While, it’s perfectly easy to actually play it in “E”, the overall sound is distinctively different.

Re: mandolin

@piobagusfidil:
“but dont go for the bluegrass style, IMO they are not as suitable being more designed for chords ”

Designed for chords? No, the Gibson archtop mandolin with F-holes was designed (and optimized during Lloyd Loar’s tenure) for playing Classical music in mandolin orchestras of the 1920’s. It was adopted into Bluegrass after that craze died out, but it was never *designed* for anything other than carrying melody and working well in an ensemble, with a tone that could be clearly heard.

The modern A-style version of this design (same thing minus the scroll) is used by several trad mandolin players including Simon Mayor and Dennis Cahill. Check out Dennis’ playing with Martin Hayes here (jump to 1:12):

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VWbBbzOCEmw&feature=related

“I notice some Americans have a fondness for them and get all uptight and righteous if you question their suitability. ”

Right, because they’re obviously unsuitable in that video. Here’s me, getting all uptight and righteous. :)

That particular style of mandolin is probably out of the price range of the OP, where the best thing would be to look for a solid wood top first, and then a good setup second. But stating that carved archtop F-hole mandolins are “more designed for chords” or unsuitable for this music is ridiculous, in my opinion.

One of the nice things about archtop F-hole mandolins is the way they can have enough volume and punch to carry melodies in a session, without going as far as a resonator type mandolin. I play in fairly loud sessions, sometimes with one or more pipers. My archtop F-hole mandolin does reasonably well in that environment, which is a tough one for any mandolin. In a loud session you’re only going to hear the initial attack of a mandolin note anyway, so it helps if that initial attack has some midrange punch, which is harder to get from a flattop instrument.

Anyway, back to the OP: Wherever you buy your first mandolin, make sure it’s a shop that can offer a good setup along with the purchase (in other words, try to avoid buying online). This is important! They’re tricky little beasts, and can be frustrating if the playability isn’t optomized. Look for a solid wood top, and after that, buy the best you can afford.

Oh, and you won’t need a capo.

Re: mandolin

If you get a really good mandolin, the setup will be right to begin with.

A setup may make a cheap (or messed about with) mandolin a bit better, but it if a mandolin doesn’t suit you, a setup won’t change that.

Most aspects of the setup are either right or it’s wrong, there is no room for personal preference.

The only areas where preference comes into it are bridge height and string gauge. If the string gauges that are on it don’t suit you it would be foolish to buy the instrument thinking that you can just change the strings and it will be alright, because you have no idea what it will sound like with different strings on.

You really need to buy an instrument that suits you straight off the dealer’s wall.

Re: mandolin

>>“What about nut height? this is a crucial aspect of any set up.”

Yes, but it’s not a matter of personal preference (unless you like bleeding fingertips I suppose ;-) ) The nut needs to be as low as it can go without buzzing… for everybody.

And no, the nut height doesn’t vary for different string gauges or whatever. If you think about it, the nut is doing exactly the same job as the frets. No one suggests that you ought to change the height of the frets when you change string gauges!

What does sometimes need adjusting when you change gauge is the WIDTH of the slots (the depth stays the same), but again it is a matter of matching the slots to the strings, not personal preference.

Re: mandolin

We’ve all spoken about finding a madolin that ‘suits’ you. But waht does that mean, particularly to someone who has never held a mandolin before?

The first thing is the sound - you need to find an instrument you like the sound of - take a player with you to the shop, or ask the assistant to play them to you - anyone behind the counter of a music shop should be able to play enough to let you judge the tone.

The second thing is that you need an instrument that feels comfortable in your hands. String length, neck width and fingerboard radius all vary quite a lot between mandolins. All these things are absolutely fixed in the building of the instrument, and can’t be altered with a ‘set-up’. So it’s important to get an instrument that feels comfortable in your hands. It’s a long time since I was in the position of never having held one before, but I think that even for someone in that position would still be able to tell what is or isn’t comfortable.

Comfort comes into choice of body shape too. Some people find bluegrass mandolins with that spike on the lower side very uncomfortable to hold when seated, others find the spike helps them cradle the instrument more securely.

Once you’ve narrowed it down to the few instruments in the shop that you like the sound of, and which feel comfortable to hold, then you can start thinking about which is prettiest or best value for money.

Francie mentioned being in America - lots of options for getting a decent starter mandolin from a reputable seller who’ll offer a good set-up as well, check out The Mandolin Store (www.themandolinstore.com) or Folkmusician.com. Check out some of the Kentucky instruments - if you’re influenced by all of the “you need an oval hole instrument for Irish music” talk, then check out the Kentucky KM172 or KM 174, oval hole, A style, all solid woods. Not sure what your price range is but for a bit more you could get the Kentucky KM505, I’ve played a fair few of them and thought they were lovely instruments for the dosh. Oh, but shock horror, the 505’s have f-holes, not an oval hole…. I play an A style mandolin with f-holes (made for me by Colorado builder Jim Hilburn) and it’s the absolute business. If you’re only going to be playing Irish music then maybe go with an oval hole instrument, but if you need more versatility I’d say f-holes are the way to go (that said, I only play Irish music and think my mandolin is very well suited to it indeed)
