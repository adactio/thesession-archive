Re: HISTORY

Michael The Whistler: “But I enjoy music that I think would be of Celtic origins in which I include Scotland and Brittany”

Ireland, Scotland and Brittany (and for that matter, Wales, Cornwall and the Isle of Man) are Celtic regions in the context that the languages spoken there are subsets of the two main branches of the Celtic language.

Goidelic Celtic - Irish, Scottish and Manx
Brythonic Celtic - Welsh, Cornish and Breton

However, you cannot extrapolate from this information that the traditional music played in those regions is also Celtic. Most of the music which we think of as being Irish or Scottish trad only dates back to the 19th century and couldn’t have been played using the instruments that were available to the ancient Celts (e.g. the carnyx, the celtic horn and the celtic trumpet).

Re: HISTORY

Michael The Whistler: “I did not know Cornwall was Celtic”

Well, not exactly Celtic. Only in the sense that the Cornish language a branch of Brythonic Celtic. The Cornish language has many similarities with Welsh. This can be found in both Welsh and Cornish place-name prefixes : e.g. “Tre” = “settlement” and “Pen” = “hill” or “headland”.

Cornish is very much a minority language with only about 300-400 speakers out of a population of around 550,000. Cornish traditional music does differ somewhat from the traditional music of the rest of England. Singing in pubs (which has mostly died out in the rest of England) still goes on in Cornwall - particularly in the far south-west of the county. But you wouldn’t call it Celtic.

To sum up: there is no such thing as “Celtic music” - it’s just phrase invented by the record companies. I am aware of course that this phrase is in common use in a America - but it’s hardly ever used by traditional players on the eastern side of the Atlantic!

Re: HISTORY

The idea that there is no such thing as Celtic music is like saying there is no such thing as a sailing boat, they are all sloops or yawls or ketches. ‘Celtic’ is just a convenent way of grouping together some loosely connected cultures whose main common ground isn’t what they are, but what they are not. Centuries ago, due to their geographical remoteness the ‘Celtic Nations’ retained their old language while the rest of Europe modernized. And in more recent times (probably up to the invention of radio and TV) the same remoteness has helped preserve an older form of music. ‘Celtic’ isn’t about having some common root way back in the past, it’s about not having suffered the later homogenization that has afflicted the rest of Europe.

As for ‘Celtic’ not being used on this side of the Atlantic, it may not be used in England (I can understand the English not wanting to use a term that excludes themselves) but in Scotland and Ireland it’s perfectly acceptable. Yes, a lot of the time we’ll talk specifically about Scottish or Irish or Welsh music, but when we want to lump them all together ‘Celtic’ is the group name that encompasses them all. The biggest traditional music fetival in the UK, run and named by some of the most traditional of traditional musicians, is called Celtic Connections.

In my experience, in eastern Canada, the term ‘Celtic’ became popular as a way of distinguishing music such as that of the Chieftains, Planxty, Bothy Band, Boys of the Lough, Battlefield Band, Silly Wizard, et al, from “Irish Music” - which meant The Irish Rovers - and “Scottish Music” - which meant The Alexander Bros., Andy Stewart (not Any M.!), and Highland bagpipe bands.
