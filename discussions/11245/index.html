Re: What is it with you guys and bodhrán players?

I think the jibes against the bodhran come from the time in the Seventies when large numbers of young (or not-so-young) adults with no musical background, in the UK and across Western Europe, heard the Irish and Scottish supergroups and thought, “I’d like to play / join in with this!” I know, I was one of them.
I didn’t try bodhran but many did, thinking it would be easy to learn, exciting to play, and a short cut to the heart of the action. Manufacturers duly started to mass-produce hideous plastic ones bedizened with luridly coloured Celtic monsters etc., which festival-goers would gleefully run off to the pub with to corner and “accompany” whatever poor bloody fiddler they might find playing there. The thing, in short, was something of a craze.
Of course, some stuck at it and became good players, acquiring, maybe after some time, properly made specimens of the instrument. (I wonder how much landfill space has been taken up over the years by the discarded plastic horrors….)
So Trad Lad, if people are snide about bodhrans, it might just be because of things that happened before your time, and without your consent!…

Re: What is it with you guys and bodhrán players?

bla bla bla … add infinitum. I’m gonna attempt to put it differently:

There are two arguments …

Firstly, and nobody disagrees, sh*t bodhran players are the putrid parasites lapping away at the life blood leaking from the jugular of this glorious music.

Secondly. and this is the difficult one, playing the bodhran well in a session, even extraordinarily well, has to be one of the most pointless exercises ever conceived by mankind. (note my caveat - I’ve heard recordings and concerts where the thing has played an excellent part in the colour of the sound)

Why is it pointless? Think of what a really good bodhran player contributes for a moment. To help you, I’ll quote from above …
1. “ bodhrán playing is great accompaniment isn’t it?”
2. “A well played bodhran sounds great.”
3. “as long as I’m on the beat and not too loud, other players at the sessions I go to seem not to mind”
4. “ Ideally, it should be a very soft sound which doesn’t interfere with the melody”
5. “They trade off, never overpower the melody, ”
6. “it gives a great rhythm to a tune when played right”
7. “the best in the world amounts to little”

Remember, to think about contribution.

Numbers 3, 4 and 5, I think you have to agree, do not amount to a contribution, more an innocent hiding in the wings.

Number 2 is a little esoteric. “it sounds great” kind of depends on what side of the fence you are on. Though I’d argue that sounding great is more than a prerequisite. I’d argue that you have to do more than merely sounding great, you have to contribute also.

Numbers 1 and 6 though are the crux of the matter. It’s a question of the value of accompaniment. I like to view this music as true ensemble music. I like to hear everyone contributing equally, and that includes the “accompanists”. So let’s look more closely at what the melodies are: percussively they are very very dense, though harmonically, they are very open. This gives space for harmonic contribution but leaves room for little more that reproduction from percussion and this is the heart of my gripe. What does a really good drummer, who is skilful and knows the tunes and is sympathetic to others etc. etc. do? A combination of laying down a steady rhythm while closely following the tune? Sounds tough, and it is. But is that not exactly what the people playing the tune are doing, except they play the tune also? Remember the No6 quote: “it gives a great rhythm to a tune when played right”. Maybe if the tune players ain’t great, a good bodhran player can hold it together, but if that bodhran player is so great, imagine what a good tune player they might become? After all, as the horses mouth says, “the best in the world amounts to little”.

Seriously…A bodhran played softly is waste of a goat skin. The bodhran’s purpose is to keep the beat, and if you’re failing to do that as a player, then the instrument is worthless. Obviously, bodhran players are disapproved of because many a person can just pick up a drum, whack at it with a small idea of what they’re doing, and call it a beat. Re: What is it with you guys and bodhrán players?

Micheal:

I’m going to have a go at your question---what does a bodhran (well played) contribute that the other instruments can’t?

I mentioned earlier that percussion instruments are the only ones that deaf people can appreciate because they can feel the vibrations, even through the air. This applies to hearing people as well. You’ll *feel* the beat of a drum in your bones, literally circulating around in sympathy with your heartbeat, in a way that you won’t feel from any other instrument. Melody instruments can play in rhythm, make you tap your feet, but they won’t set off vibrations within your body the way a drum does. This is why I think non-musicians respond so well to drumming---it doesn’t require musical appreciation, just a rhythmic response, which we can all feel. It adds to the atmosphere of sound and vibration of a session or performance. Or it can, anyway. That’s what I think, make of it what you will.

I grew up literally listening to music in my cot. My father played the bodhrán in the house both at seisiúns in my house, and when he was practising/playing along with records. Our lower room in my parent’s house seemed to be where the local seisiún was held when I was very young.

My father is a man called Seamus O’Kane. He also makes bodhráns as well as playing them. He’s been making them all my life (for some 37+ or so years to my knowledge), so I suppose I was always fascinated in the making process of them, as he was constantly inventing new ways of tensioning the skins, curing the skins, so that his bodhrán would sound just as he wanted it.

If I am completely honest, I have absolutely nothing against bodhráns, as long as they are in the right hands, and played well. I would be a hypocrite if I said I had something against bodhráns, as it was probably the first instrument I’d ever seen. On the same note, there’s nothing as bad as playing along with a bodhrán player who beats the sh!t out of a bodhrán, not knowing the tunes, not having any regard for the music that is being played, much the same as any other instrument being played out of tune, or out of time. It’s not something that you just pick up and batter the life out off, like a fiddle isn’t something you grab and scratch and scrape the life out of the strings on, not knowing bowing techiques/where the notes are on the relevant strings etc… Even bodhráns that don’t have the skin tensioned properly, can sound God-awful! Badly made, cheap, touristy loud celtic design painted wall ornament-type “bodhráns” and “bodhráns” that sound too trebley (like a tin can) or too slack (exerting a dull thud) are an awful curse also. However, just because someone has a nice bodhrán, doesn’t automatically make them a good player… like any other instrument, it takes a good deal of practise and an understanding of the music, like actually knowing the tune, and how the particular tune is structured. Until this knowledge is acquired, any instrument, should be kept in the safe-haven of someone’s home (as long as they don’t drive their family and neighbours mad while they are learning) and when they have mastered whatever the instrument in question is, then and only then should it be brought out to seisiúns for themselves and other players to enjoy.

For those who think that the bodrhan is simple, here’s some news for you. I’ve been playing percussion of many varieties and styles for over 30 years now and I had the hardest time learning to play the bodhran properly over all other instruments. It takes a good ear and hours upon hours of practice to get the hand position and skin tension just so to produce the right sound for the tune you are accompanying. It also takes a long time to recognize the changes and ornamentations to many different tunes and to not drop the ball when coming up to those. So it’s not true bodhran players that are despised but those who think they are bodhran players and haven’t dedicated the time to learn this sadly misunderstood and often maligned instrument properly.

Any instrument played badly is a curse on a session. Bodhrans seem to be top of the list for complaints, swiftly followed by guitars. It’s all about simplicity, instruments that look easiest tend to acquire more people who just want to join in. Trouble is, while accompaniment instruments require less of a knowledge of all the different tunes, they need more of an understanding of the rhythms and melody structures in the music. But when people see people having a session, they see the people doing the least physical work and assume they’ve got the easiest job -- and they have, but it’s still not as simple as it seems.

Of course, some people don’t want this music accompanied, with anything, either cos it’s not traditional or they don’t see the point. Which is fine too.

Re: What is it with you guys and bodhrán players?

I play concertina, flute, and bodhran. It’s true that I put far more time into learning concertina and flute, but I have also put in a good deal of time honing my skills on bodhran. I consider it to be a back-up instrument without strings. Even guitars, bouzoukis and pianos require far more attention, but that doesn’t mean the bodhran requires none, or that it’s obsolete. (As Michael routinely suggests) But I do believe that my familiarity with the tunes accentuates my abilities to back up the same tunes with the bodhran… much the same way it would help my backing up abilities on other back-up instruments. In other words, since I play the other instruments, the work I do there contributes to the bodhran playing. If I only played bodhran, I would have to compensate and familiarize myself with the tunes by listening and playing along. The bottom line is that playing bodhran correctly requires more effort that one might first think when deciding to take it up. The problem is that many bodhranists fail to realize this and enter sessions prematurely. Or… they never realize this but plant themselves in the middle of sessions anyway.
