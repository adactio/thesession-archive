P-tifsad

Esteemed colleagues.

We wish to report a recently observed syndrome which appears to afflict a small cohort of the general population, the players of Irish traditional music. As this syndrome appears to manifest itself after these players have returned home from music festivals or so-called “seisiuns” and has an affect on their psychological well-being we have chosen the term Post-Traditional Irish Festival and Seisun Affective Disorder(P-tifsad).

The syndrome appears to have an affect on both males and females, in equal numbers. The symptoms are varied, but are generally transient, lasting no more than a few days to a week at most, and are manifest most often as mild depression, but can often be observed as a medium-term hypomania.

There are two over lapping variants of the condition: Post-festival and Post-seisiun.

Treatment from the post festival variant is quite simple. The patient is sent immediately to the nearest emergency seisiun centre, active involvement in music therapy encouraged and oral alcohol administered.
The post-sesiun variant, although less severe, is a more long term condition, and opinion is sharply divided as to whether it constitutes any threat at all to the patient. Treatment may range from the aforementioned emergency seisiun therapy, to merely listening to recorded musical samples.

We are curious as to whether other workers in this field have observed similar cases in their work.

Sincerely,

Consultant Psychomusicotherapist,

Ed Case.

Seriously, though. Anyone who does feel down after a great festival or a cracking session, just remember - there are more to be had. Try and let the good memories sustain you till the next one. As I’ve mentioned elsewhere, I often get a good vibe that lasts for weeks to months after having been to a fleadh or whatever. And remember, no matter what level you’re at in this big pyramid, we’re all have an essential part in it.

Danny.

A Chairde,
My heartiest congratulations to my eminent and distinguished colleagues, An Dochtuir Mac Aoidh and Doctor Quinn, for weighing in on this most important topic. Likewise to the perhaps less scholarly but no less insightful and perceptive comments of DebWah and Zina Lee. It goes to show that valuable contributions from the profanum volgus or lay community can sometimes permeate the hallowed walls of our Ivory Towers. This is indeed a subject which has interested me for many years: the relationship between ITM (as played at seisiuin/plural, ceilithe, comhdhalacha and other cruinnithe as opposed to that played in the isolation of one’s cell or bedchamber), alcoholic beverages, and subjects (frequently referred to colloquially as seisiun players). To pursue this matter further and perhaps contribute another brick to the edifice of human knowledge, I made application for, and received, funding from the University of Saskatchewan, the Canada Arts Council, and Comhaltas Ceoltoiri Eireann. (Readers might be interested to note that funding was neither obtained nor sought from any institution that had a Royal sponsor.) An experimental design was developed which envisioned raw data being collected at a North American Convention of Comhaltas (CCE). The choice of venue was arbitrary on the part of the researcher and was justified on the grounds of economy and convenience: The number of subjects there would be large enough to satisfy the statistical requirements for the analysis of the data. Otherwise, it would have been necessary for the researcher to attend literally hundreds of ordinary pub seisiuin/sessions in diverse locations (some in very out-of-the-way places where attitudes to scholarly research and to scholars was either unknown or known to be hostile). The multi-seisiun approach was dismissed as being too taxing for the researcher’s health and financial resources. The research design included the use of inferential statistics, specifically analyses of variance and covariance, to test the questions raised in the study’s hypotheses: Is the euphoria the result of (i) the seisiun player’s character or personal traits, age, gender, musical competence, etc. (ii) his/her imbibing alcoholic beverages, (iii) ITM as played in a seisiun setting, or (iv) interactions between and among the 3 variables under study. Space limitations here do not permit the restatement of all the hypotheses included in the study, but a single example may provide some texture or context to this brief summary of the proposed study:
1. That female seisiun players between the ages of 24 and 35 who in the course of their working day dress formally and who imbibe very lightly and normally only on special occasions such as Christmas, anniversaries and birthdays, and who play a wind instrument (restricted for the purposes of the study to the feadog/tin whistle and the feadog mhor/flute) and whose mastery of their chosen instrument was at best at an elementary level (Mary Bergin had agreed to adjudicate levels) would experience a greater degree of euphoria than male seisiun players between the ages of 35 and 49 who were unemployed and did not own a suit or tie, who were habitually drunk and shunned by all respectable and God-fearing people in their home community, who played a stringed instrument (restricted in this study to Stradivari fiddles or copies of same manufactured prior to 1900 having no major defects or cracks, some varnish loss being considered acceptable) and who played their instrument with consummate skill (Skill levels were to be adjudicated by Seamus Connolly. Note. Martin Hayes and Kevin Burke had declined invitations due their busy schedules.)
Sadly, this grand venture failed. The three institutions which funded the post-doctoral research project have returned their copies of the researcher’s report and have commenced legal action to recover funds advanced. (Since the funds have been dispersed and the researcher, contrary to popular opinion, does not have money in both pockets, any donation the reader cares to make would be gratefully accepted.) The researcher, contrary to the claims of the funding institutions, did act in good faith, did attend the CCE Convention, did retain the services of assistants for data collection, did remunerate experts for acting as adjudicators, did collect data, and did analyze data, all as outlined in the study proposal. The problem arose with the data being unusable for ANOVA (analysis of variance) and ANCOVA (analysis of covariance) due to the presence of empty cells or cells with numbers that were too small for any reliable conclusion to be drawn from the data. Specifically, all of the seisiun players were imbibing alcohol and as one of my less scholarly assistants phrased it,“Some of them were blind drunk.” In the absence of a control group of non-drinking seisiun players, the major hypotheses could not be tested. With respect to the above-stated hypothesis, (an adequate number of the females and males so-described attended the Convention, N.females=62 and Nmales=47), the hypothesis was not supported, in that equal amounts of euphoria were present in the male and female subjects. Until we arrive at a more sober age which will be more congenial to a replication of this study, the only safe conclusion we can draw with respect to these variables is that All seisiun players playing ITM in a seisiun setting and imbibing alcohol experience a euphoric state and this euphoria does not seem to be affected by personal characteristics or competence level of the players. If anyone out there would care to replicate the study, would he please NOT contact this researcher.
Is mise, le gach dea-mhein, bhur gcara,
An Dochtuir Mairtin

As such, you are attempting to measure and/or define ‘euphoria’ by examining the variables of (i) the seisiun player’s character or personal traits, age, gender, musical competence, etc. (ii) his/her imbibing alcoholic beverages, (iii) ITM as played in a seisiun setting, or (iv) interactions between and among the 3 variables under study.

Excessively ambitious, I must say, & way too-all encompassing. It presumes you can measure something entirely subjective, the Achilles heel of quantitative data analysis. Your methodology is questionable at best. Assuming you collect the hard demographic data of age & gender, etc, ‘character’ & ‘musical competence’ are hardly variables measurable, reproducable, or of any statistical value. (See MacAoidh’s work from 2002, on the grievous statistical errors & bias engendered in all existing tools designed to measure these variables to date, exceptional work there.) Look kids, don’t do festivals if you’re not ready for the comedown.

You’ve got to *plan* it. If the festival ended on the Saturday night, then chances are (depending on your metabolism) the downer’s going to kick in midweek: the wednesday blues.

If you want to go about it sensibly, you should cushion your psyche for the crash: take the day off work, rent some weepy movies, make sure there’s enough ice cream and whiskey to go into the hot chocolate. Some people like to have a spliff at the ready.

The trick is not to do anything that will remind of the good times, it’ll just set you off groaning and remembering some of the silly things you said to people when you were in highest spirits. Do NOT make any decisions bigger than what film to watch if you can help it. Certainly not big life-changing ones. Repeat the mantra: this too shall pass.
