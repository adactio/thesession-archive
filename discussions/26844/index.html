↳ Posted by Peter Laban on February 21st, 2011.

Re: If you’ve got a recording why do you need the dots?

You can study the whole of the tune written out and compare different parts, see which are identical and how other parts vary: something you can’t do with a recording. And you can compare different tunes at the same time. Also the dots are ‘neutral’ whereas a recording is a snapshot of that one version. And they are convenient to maintain as a library.

I start off by getting a tune in my head and fingers by ear. Then, unless I have someone to actually play it for me on tap (which I do occasionally), I go to the dots if available. Re: If you’ve got a recording why do you need the dots?

ian, I’ve read dots for 45 years, since I was 7. I can usually sight read a new tune at session or dance speed.

But learning by ear is much quicker for actually integrating the tunes. It’s also more accurate, once you’re good at it. And the only way to get that good at it is to do it, and to not rely on ink on paper.

The main purpose and use of dots as they were invented (before any aural recording devices existed) was to store a composer’s intentions. They come in handy when you don’t have an mp3 recorder and you’ve just written a three-part concerto. The dots have become “standard” in music largely because of the demands of formal art music.

But Irish tunes are short and simple. They’re really not that hard to remember. If a Rachmaninoff piano concerto is a novel, the typical jig or reel is a well-told joke--set up and punchline.

Ben’s question about singing what you’ve heard is spot on. Sight singing from the dots doesn’t mean you’ve internalized the tune, just that you can “hear” the tune from the page without an instrument in your hands (and if you’re not doing this, then you still have a long way to go to make good use of the dots). But singing back what you’ve heard proves that you’ve internalized the tune *and* that you can play it back. Most trad players do this a phrase at a time, though some prefer to get the whole gestalt of a tune and then refine the details as they continue to play it with their source.

What’s important here is that we play and store the tunes with the aural centers of our brains. Anything else (e.g.,visual centers) isn’t really “having” the music. And it won’t come out as music if we’ve stored it as dot images or names of notes or just muscle memory. The tunes aren’t in our eyes or fingers, they live in our mind’s ear and voice.

Ian,

I rarely learn tunes from live players. I try to do it as much as possible, which isn’t very much, so I use recordings 97% of the time. The problem with this debate -- and the reason I get so utterly bored with it -- is people’s apparent blindness to the nuances of learning THE MUSIC verses learning TUNES from dots. Someone who “gets” the music can learn a tune from sheet music and play it and sound great. I know plenty of fantastic trad musicians who learn things from the dots all the time. If you have a grasp of the music, you can learn tunes by putting a CD against your head and hoping they seep in by osmosis for all it matters. If you don’t, you have to listen, listen, listen, and develop an ear for the micro-dynamics, the variations, the manner in which tunes move ahead of and behind the beat, etc -- all those things that make good Irish music sound good and will never be captured by written notation.

Re: If you’ve got a recording why do you need the dots?

It’s an oral tradition, after all.

When an oral tradition becomes written, something about it changes; it becomes fundamentally more static. You essentially freeze a moment of transmission. It’s like folklore from an oral tradition that’s been written down for whatever reason (a lot of reasons. don’t ask). It’s great that it’s written down and we can use these as historical sources two hundred years later because of it, but the epic caveat is that the textual source has solidified knowledge that was originally far more fluid. It’s fine, so long as you know that. But don’t think the written folktale, or tune, is THE absolute, “correct” version. Because it probably isn’t. It’s a captured moment, the intersection between the reciter (or player) and transcriber.
