Re: Chin Rests

Not using a chin rest is fine and I went through a phase of doing it myself. It can be a problem for two reasons, both of which can adversely effect the sound of the fiddle.

1) Sweat and chin wear can rub and dissolve the fragile varnish off the fiddle if weight and sweat is aloud to be placed actually on the area. The varnish is actually an important constituent of the make up of the violin that produces the sound and is much less hardwearing than varnish used to coat the neck.

2) The physical weight of a chin can deaden the sound and stop the fiddle from allowing a note to ring. On a fiddle like my German paesold, which is quite brash, this might be okay because it quitens it down and makes the sound more session friendly. This is not the case with all fiddles.

Therefore, I think, correct me if I’m wrong, that using a fiddle without a chin rest is best if you are one of those fiddlers who has a good balance of the fiddle with the left hand and neck (which can of course is the subject of much technique work) and therefore don’t need to clamp the fiddle under the chin.

my tuppence worth anyway!!,

Re: Chin Rests

I don’t use a shoulder rest; I used one once for a few months when I started playing the fiddle, but soon ditched it because I found it too restrictive.

I don’t use a chin rest as such, either, but what I have on both my fiddles is a narrow piece of ebony about 3“ in length and shaped to the curvature of the lower edge of the belly plate of the fiddle, extending along the purfling from the bass side of the tailpiece. The piece of ebony is about 1/4” thick, and in width it tapers from 1/2“ at the tailpiece end to 1/4” at the distal end. The edges are all nicley chamfered for comfort and appearance. A single adjustable tie rod (brass in this instance because it’s a rather ancient bit of kit) links the middle portion of the piece of ebony to a retaining member (also ebony) against the back plate of the fiddle. I fixed a piece of chamois on the underside of the piece of ebony with adhesive so as to protect the fiddle varnish, and similarly with the retaining member underneath (see my note to Nick below, for more details). The entire setup I would call a “chin anchor” rather than a “chin rest”.

My fiddle rests lightly on my collar bone, and the “chin anchor” works by anchoring the fiddle just under the chin. I’m not really aware that my chin is gripping or resting on this anchor, or on the belly of the fiddle, and it feels very secure - no problems whatsoever in moving up and down the positions if I want to. The great advantage is that I can hold the fiddle in any position in the horizontal and vertical planes, rather as if it is connected to me by a ball joint. I find holding the fiddle pointing downwards at 30-45 degrees and towards the side is a very relaxed posture; and if I want to I can raise it to the horizontal with the same feeling of security and comfort. Clearly, the addition of a shoulder-rest would impede this freedom of movement.

Further advantages: everything feels very light - chinrest+shoulder rest is a significant proportion of the total weight, and in my view removing this weight should improve the tone; and, the chin anchor is perched on the purfling only, so the vibration of the belly plate is never impeded.

In warm weather I do what Tish suggests - a piece of chamois to catch any drops of sweat from my chin. Don’t use cloth - it slips around too much on the varnish.

I think these “chin anchors” must have been fairly common many years ago (probably well before WW2 or even WW1) and I was fortunate in finding a second one for my other fiddle in a violin dealer’s. However, what I have described should be fairly easy for any competent carpenter to design and make.

If anyone wants to go real 18c and do without both shoulder-rest and chin-rest comfortably and securely, here’s how. Rest the fiddle on the collar bone and position it so that the TREBLE side of the tailpiece rests lightly against the side of your chin (the chin doesn’t necessarily touch the belly of the fiddle). This configuration stops the instrument from sliding downwards towards your right. If you need to stabilise the instrument for higher position playing (generally when coming down from the higher positions) all you need to do is to briefly grip the belly of the fiddle with your chin - here again a piece of chamois is useful to protect the varnish.

Other ways to play without a chinrest are to use the tailpiece as a chinrest, or to have your chin on the bass side of the tailpiece. In both cases you’ll tend to grip the instrument with your chin. As I’ve pointed out with the chin anchor this isn’t the case, it just acts as an anchor or hook.

I’ve got both an over-the-saddle chinrest on my electric fiddle and an off-to-the-left chinrest on my acoustic fiddle; both are fairly comfortable.

I’m going to try switching them because the e-fiddle is rather shiny (it’s a metallic-fleck, dark grey, gunmetal color) but it’s got an ebony chinrest and the chinrest on the acoustic is textured plastic.

I rarely play with a shoulder rest even though the e-fiddle’s plugs directly into the bottom of the instrument (sometimes it falls out, though). I have a cheap but sturdy metal “Resonans” shoulder rest for my acoustic fiddle that can be easily bent to fit my shoulder.

I know that my hold is more secure with the shoulder rest but I only need that kind of security if I’m going to play with vibrato (which I’m not very good at and almost never do playing ITM) or if I want to play around with practicing higher positions (which I am also not very good at and almost never do, period).

Without a shoulder rest, however, your left hand does have to also support the fiddle more. But, it’s not like a fiddle weighs 50 pounds!

Re: Chin Rests

A hallmark of a good teacher is that the pupil is allowed and encouraged to develop their own style. Any teacher who says “you’ve got to do it my way, or else”, is bad news - this of course doesn’t necessarily apply to the teaching of basic technique in the early stages.
It is also true to say that the best players are self-taught. They may indeed have a teacher, especially in the early stages, but the wise teacher will know that with such pupils the teacher is a guide, a second pair of ears, an alternative opinion.
The good player who is self-taught should also know when to go to the teacher for advice.
