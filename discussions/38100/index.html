Re: Fiddlers - dodgy intonation? Not any more!

luuksz: “… does anyone here know why fiddle, cello etc never got frets, but ”modern“ instruments like guitar and bass have it. ”

To give a relatively serious answer to what appears to be a serious question, it has nothing to do with ‘modern’ vs. ‘old’/‘traditional’. Fretted instruments were around well before violins and cellos, so the early violin makers would surely have incorporated frets into their designs if they thought they were needed. The primary reason behind frets, I believe, is tone production, not intonation - the fact that they eliminate the need for precise finger placement is merely an incidental consequence, which has subsequently been exploited. The presence of frets makes a big difference to the sound of a plucked string (the existence of bass guitars in both fretted and fretless forms is testament to that); stopping a string against a fret (as opposed to a bare fingerboard) causes less damping, giving a clearer, brighter sound with more sustain. On a bowed instrument, however, since the string is continuously receiving energy from the bow, this damping is not such an issue - the sustain is as long as your bow stroke. So frets would probably be more of an encumbrance than an advantage.

Incidentally, members of the viol family (renaissance bowed instruments which preceded the violin family) did have frets. These apparently developed as bowed versions of an older plucked fretted instrument, the vihuela, so the. By the mid-17th Century, however, they had largely given way to the unfretted violin family.

Re: Fiddlers - dodgy intonation? Not any more!

Comparison with early fretted instruments like the viol are interesting. Viols and lutes had a big advantage over this modern invention in that their frets were tied gut. Not only could the frets be moved slightly according to the key you were playing in, but they were also tied as a double thickness, so you could achieve a certain amount of microtonal tuning by either putting your finger on top of the fret, so the string is stopped at the first tie, or behind the fret so that the string is stopped slightly flat at the second tie, so something very close to just intonation was possible. In the same time period there were instruments with fixed metal frets, but they were only ever used on wire strung instruments like the cittern and bandora (presumably because wire strings would cut through gut frets very quickly) and these were always regarded as inferior ‘second division’ instruments.

Digressing slightly, those early metal fretted instruments all had scalloped fingerboards, which allow microtonal variation controlled by how hard you press down on the string - an idea that has been resurrected in recent years by some guitarists like John McLaughlin.

Fiddles don’t need frets because the bow sustains the sound. Frets are primarily there to give sustain to plucked instruments. Re: Fiddlers - dodgy intonation? Not any more!

@Frank,

A fiddler’s intonation is off if he sounds out of tune. Exactly how far off he needs to be for that to happen depends on a lot of things - How fast the music is, what other instruments are playing, and even which note of the scale he has the problem with - If your 5th is off it is much more noticeable than if the 2nd or 6th is off. Most people don’t hear a note as being out of tune until it is about 10-12 cents off, though trained musicians can often notice 4 cents or better. Playing fast jigs and reels with strumming accompaniment, you can afford to be quite a long way off without anyone noticing, but on a slow air with legato accompaniment you need to be spot on.

The difference between just and equal temperament doesn’t really come into it - one will sound sweeter than the other (depending on what else is going on at the time), but the difference is too small for anyone to say “his intonation is off”.

It really is just a case of trusting your ears, and if they say it’s wrong then it is.

Re: Fiddlers - dodgy intonation? Not any more!

Adding to Mark’s good post, I think it depends on what’s playing along with the fiddler. If it’s another fiddle, and they’re playing in true unison and in perfect time with each other, then that’s one scenario where minute pitch differences are most noticeable. For a single fiddler / violinist, they say the intervals which sound worst when not in tune are (in descending order) unisons, octaves, 5ths, 4ths, then 3rds.

‘Sweetening’ up your intervals on fiddle - a typical example would be a major 3rd, eg G+B - for a sweeter sound, the B would be a little below standard B pitch.

You can hear this with guitar and 5-string banjo too. If you tune a guitar by ear, starting from the D string, your G string would be a perfect 4th above. Now your B string - you would tune it slightly flat to get the sweetest sound from the D+G+B strings played together. However, that wont work, because your B string will now be flat compared to your E string, so you’ll need to tune up your B to get a perfect 4th between the B and E strings.

Diane, you said “You can ‘train’ your ears to hear notes out of tune as being correct, just as easily as otherwise.” So true. I had a student, adult beginner on fiddle, but a very experienced and technically advanced guitarist. He could tune a guitar perfectly in seconds, so there was nothing wrong with his ear.

He had spent much of his fiddle time figuring out fiddle tunes by himself, and he was pretty good at that. However, his bowing and intonation were very poor.

He was aware of this, but too impatient to spend the time to correct the deficiencies. I tried to improve the intonation, but it simply wasn’t working. As for the bowing - in a short time, I got him to straighten up and bow and draw a decent tone. However, the intonation was still grim (and he knew it, although he did not realise just how bad it sounded to a listener).

I was about to give up on the intonation, and I thought of a little ‘tactic’. I recorded his playing during a lesson, then I played it back to him some weeks later. At first he did not believe it was him playing, but then after hearing his voice on the recording, he knew it was genuine. He was really quite taken aback (he had been playing fiddle at gigs). That was the turning point for him - from then on, he really listened and practised the recommended simple stuff like scales and Book 1 Sevcik exercises, and in a short time his intonation improved dramatically.

I often wondered about fiddle capos. Theoretically, it would be quite simple - one that fits over the strings, and is clamped / elasticated, or one that slides under the strings (like a cut nut with a deeper base). In practice, it’s a bit different. There’s no identifiable ‘position’ to put the capo on/in. You’d have to put it on, and test to see if it’s exactly a semitone (or tone, or whatever) above pitch - that would be time-consuming.

“I cannot think of other pipers who play fiddle”

Caoimhín Ó Raghallaigh (better known as a fiddler, but he did once come 2nd in the All Ireland on pipes). But, far from adversely affecting the intonation of his fiddle playing, I suspect that the pipes are a contributing factor to his acute awareness of subtle differences in intonation. As a fiddler who makes extensive use of open string ‘drones’, I would say that he leans towards Just Intonation.

When talking about ‘correct’ intonation, there is a danger of ignoring the potential of microtonal variation as an expressive dimension, alongside dynamics, phrasing, ornamentation etc. For many fiddlers, ‘in tune’ does not fit models like ‘equal temperament’, ‘just intonation’, ‘quarter comma mean tone’ etc. However mathematically correct these may be, they are really nothing but arbitrary standards. Listen to Bobby Casey and tell me what type of temperament or intonation he is playing in. His 3rd and 7ths wander between major and minor, from tune to tune or from one point in a tune to another; it makes a mockery of any fixed tuning system.
