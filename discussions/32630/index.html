Blinkered “pure drop” people

Been thinking a little bit, and its been a while since i’ve ventured a discussion.

I’ve noticed that whenever something pops up on here - for example recently a discussion about the Olllam, a fantastic band and recording both i may add; there seems a bit of a knee jerk negative reaction.

Unmerited i think. While there is a lot of crappy “new-wave” trad, these lads clearly are fantastic musicians who have definitely grown up immersed in the tradition and just having fun exploring an idea or two . In a similar vein, any good session has an element of good humour and a willingness to try things out for the craic. For example Hayes and Cahill on the Live in Seattle recording play Pachelbel’s Canon as a reel with great relish.

I think the tradition is robust enough to survive any meddling. I also think that the begrudgery has become an ingrained, joyless habit and is the antithesis of what I love about The Music

Discuss ;)

Re: Blinkered “pure drop” people

A fiddle player, Chris McGrath, once told me that a living tradition needs three things: it needs an avant garde, pushing the boundaries and bringing new things in. (The jigs came from Italy, the polkas from Germany, the reels from England and before that from… France, I believe.) But Irish music wasn’t diddly until a certain point in its history.) A tradition needs regular everyday practitioners, who play from the heart and for fun without thinking too much about it. And it needs the staunch traditionalists, he said, the ones who would consider everything that came after the voice and the pipes an affront to true Irish music. It’s that tension, he said, that makes for a tradition.

And then there’s this: Peter Howell, Toronto Star, 1995:

[quote]

You’ve got to love music purists.

So loyal are they to favorite sounds, and so determined to keep them the way they first discovered them, they mount their steeds like knights of old at the first sign of change. It doesn’t matter whether it’s techno or Tchaikovsky, the Huns must be repelled.

All of which hugely amuses Ashley MacIsaac, the 20-year-old fiddle sensation from Cape Breton who headlines tomorrow’s opening night show of the Celtic Ceilidh festival at Harbourfront Centre.

Schooled in the old ways of the instrument, and never having purchased a single pop music album, he’s a man of pure ability, everything the fiddle faithful could ask for.

But he’s given to impure thoughts, such as how the fiddle would sound set to a disco beat like the Bee Gees‘ "Stayin’ Alive,‘’ and he enjoys engaging the knights who would stop him.

"Their biggest problem with me is their belief that if people are going to follow Ashley MacIsaac, then people are not going to continue to understand traditional music,‘’ MacIsaac says, on the line from Pearson Airport, en route to another of his many music destinations.

"But I think I have more understanding of traditional music than anybody else in the world and Cape Breton, considering how much time I’ve spent in thought and sat back and looked at everything.‘’

His show at Harbourfront with his band (he’s dubbed them Kitchen Devils) is as traditional as MacIsaac gets, considering he plays left-handed, ties a frayed streamer to his fiddle bow and stomps about the stage in Doc Martens. He knows he’s playing a Celtic-themed weekend, not a rock show.

"But what is `traditional‘ supposed to mean?’’ MacIsaac asks rhetorically, speaking as if addressing one of the pure knights.

"Is it supposed to mean your album that you just released, which is you playing the fiddle with a piano player and completely no soul, because you’re doing something that has to be done this certain way?

"Or does traditional mean what everybody in Cape Breton assumes it to mean: the music that people played in their kitchens and was honest and came from the heart and was done without thinking. Which is more or less what I’m doing when I play the fiddle.

“If you want to learn how to play the fiddle like me,‘’ MacIsaac concludes, ”You’ve got to learn how to play it good, first.‘’

[/quote]

Isn’t the last bit the part that matters most of all?

With regard to the Ollam thread responses, I’m puzzled how comments along the lines of ‘good if you like that style, not to my taste’ are a negative knee jerk reaction, just seems an honest expression of personal preference.

Begrudgery certainly does exist in the music scene in general and this discussion board has its share, but I’d see that more as being unwilling to appreciate the merits and abilities of other players. Saying you don’t like a particular innovation is not the same as saying it’s a crime against the tradition or some such nonsense. As others have said above, the music will far outlive us and time will tell what directions it takes. Recognising that people have a good understanding of the tradition and are accomplished players doesn’t mean you have enjoy their music; I regard Sean McGuire as an excellent traditional player but I simply don’t like listening to his recordings.

I’m glad that people are taking different directions with the music, don’t see it as any kind of threat to “pure drop”, but I don’t feel I need to love what they’re doing either.

Re: Blinkered “pure drop” people

I’m not really sure that ‘pure-drop’ people are blinkered or narrow-minded in real life (with one or two famous exceptions!!)

Some years ago a was in a pub in Doolin, off-season, and there was a session going on. I had my fiddle, but I didn’t take it out. I sat near the musicians, and just listened for about 20 minutes. It was amazing - the timing and rhythm were perfect, and it was great to hear fiddle, flute and banjo playing the tunes in perfect unison.

Nothing flash or unusal - just simple tunes being played really well, and it sounded great. There actually was a bodhran playing too, but to be honest I didn’t really notice it till late on, because it blended in so well, Always a pulse, never a thump.

When I first heard them playing, I was going to ask if I could join in, but after a short while I realized that there was nothing I could add to it. It was just perfect.

Later on, the fiddle player saw my fiddle case and asked me if I wanted to join in. I took out the fiddle, and unsure what to play, and suggested a few tunes, but he said, “just play what you want! Anything at all!” So I played a couple of my own (non-Irish) comps, and they seemed to really like them, and made positive comments afterwards too.

There was a bit of chat after that, then they resumed playing their tunes, and I continued to listen. I didn’t join in, even though I was given the ‘nod’ a few times.

Were those guys ‘pure-drop’? I think so. Totally. Were they blinkered? No, they were not. After the initial invitation to play, if I had continued playing my own tunes, and tried to get them to join in, they probably would not have liked that. And why should they?

That experience has kind of stuck in my mind. The ‘pure-drop’, ‘purist’, ‘fundamental’ tags will always be used, and most often seem to be encountered online, or from reports, imo. I’m sure they do exist, but to date I have yet to meet a blinkered one in real life - and I’ve been around for a while :)

Even with Punk, originally just great craic and a statement against the norm and the perceived staid, when people started taking themselves too seriously and getting obsessive the real rot and spoilage started.

So much fusion music seems to not be about doing things well either side or facet to any melding between traditions. I’ve heard a lot of half assed folkies trying to weave jazzy elements into their mix, or ‘pop’, cute tricks, electro drum & bass, etc., but not doing anything justice. I wouldn’t deny them their fun, but so often they actually can’t play any tradition well, with understanding. But, there are always exceptions, but they are, in my experience and from what I’ve given ear to ~ rare… However, when someone does do it well, it’s as rare that I don’t appreciate it… ;-) The worst abuses of testosterone and adrenalin tend to fry themselves, to burn out, to end in a fizz. You do something off kilter and you draw attention to yourself, you get gigs, and then ~ folk get tired of it, the bums on seats, and they go off looking for the next quirky experiment, rush, adrenalin fix. It ain’t nothin’ new, it’s just, again, “The Emperor’s New Clothes”, tarting thing up, and too often with little substance other than testosterone and adrenalin.
