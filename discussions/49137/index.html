Musical Instrument Acquisition Syndrome

Anyone else here suffer from musical instrument acquisition syndrome? My primary instrument is the fiddle, which I have been playing for roughly 10 years. However, starting around 5 years ago I started acquiring new instruments. My fiddle is a hand-me-down, but I purchased a simple Tony Dixon tin-whistle, then a American 5-string banjo (not for ITM), a classical guitar (nothing fancy), then… it became a tenor banjo, a keyless flute, a B/C box. In the mix there is a couple banjo repair projects and a gifted steel-string guitar in need of some serious TLC.

Now I am looking at acquiring a lute, which is a rather cost prohibitive instrument. I’ll see if that pans out, as it seems one lute costs roughly the same as most, if not all, of my other instruments combined.

My issue is of this ‘collection’ is that just the fiddle and 5-string banjo are instruments I am capable of playing well (as in, I would play them ‘for someone’, for busking, or in a session, etc). The others are more in-line with the thought that ‘I will learn them someday’ and I can, on a good day, play one set of tunes (if I have been practicing).

Anyone else struggle with musical instrument day dreams or have any tips to counteract them? I have told myself that I can purchase a new instrument once I am able to bring my box to a session… and play it. If I am determined, that may be a just year of practice but a more conservative guess is that it would take several.

Re: Musical Instrument Acquisition Syndrome

I went through a period, in my 20’s and 30’s, of acquiring and learning to play a variety of instruments.

I had bagpipes like the Uilleann pipes, Scottish Highland pipes, Border pipes, Scottish smallpipes, Northumbrian smallpipes, Northumbrian doublepipes, Cornish doublepipes, Bulgarian Gaida and Kaba Gaida, and Spanish gaita. I had a little bagpipe show I’d go around doing.

I had Irish flutes and whistles, Bulgarian kaval, and all the Andean flutes and panpipes.

I had a guitar, Irish octave mandolin, lap dulcimer, and my grandfather’s fiddle.

With raising kids and working I didn’t have near enough practice time so I divested myself of nearly everything, leaving myself with Uilleann pipes, Irish flute & whistle, and Scottish Highland and small pipes (and my Granda’s fiddle of course).

Over the last decade and getting older hand issues have led to getting rid of the Irish flutes and Uilleann pipes.

So far (fingers crossed) playing whistle, Highland pipes, and Scottish smallpipes isn’t an issue and that’s all I have.

One thing I’ll mention: I’m not a collector and never have been. When I had all those instruments I played them all at gigs, and when I was no longer doing the gigs that required them I sold them off.

And I don’t like having multiples of the same sort of instrument. I know Highland pipers that have 20 sets of Highland pipes, some with 100. I have no interest in that. I have two sets of Highland pipes which I keep going equally well and I bring both to every gig, just in case something goes awry.

I have Irish whistles in quite a few keys which were acquired because I was doing gigs where I needed to be able to play in any key whatever (even Db Major!) but I only ever had one whistle in each key and size.

When I knew my fluteplaying days were done I went on a Low D Whistle spree, owning at various times around 40 of as many makers as I could acquire. I put them through rigorous evaluations and now I have just the very best of the lot, just one.

I met a guy with 100 High D whistles!! If I had 100 High D whistles I’d find out the two best and get rid of the other 98 (keeping two just in case I lost or damaged one). Who wants to waste time playing anything other than your best instrument? Is your 46th best whistle, your 73rd best whistle, your 91st best whistle really worth keeping?

Re: Musical Instrument Acquisition Syndrome

I’d like to say something about the banjo. Both the popular and negative reactions to it all seem to come from the banjo being played in one of just two common styles: 1) clawhammer/strumming along or 2) the fast-picking lead instrument in Earl Scruggs style. Beverly Hillbillies stuff.

I bought a banjo and in the process of learning the finger-picking techniques and peculiar 5-string left hand fretwork possible, I kind of realized that the banjo is capable of much more. There is quite a range of tones available on banjo depending on the use (or not) of that large rear sound reflector, the use (or not) of a metal tone ring, the tension of the skin, the types of wood used (mahogany sounds quite different than maple), and just having some sense to explore possibilities. True it is that the banjo has little sustain and that plucky sound is somewhat locked in, but the instrument certainly doesn’t need to be considered as an Instrument From Backwoods Hell if the player doesn’t want to play the same old same old.

Re: Musical Instrument Acquisition Syndrome

“I know professional recorder players who own more than 50 recorders with different ranges, different pitches and different timbres. It’s not unusual for them to play on 10 different recorders in one evening performance.”

Exactly so, that’s the difference between a “collector” and a professional musician and his tools.

You only need one screwdriver of each type and spec that you’ll need on the job. You don’t have room on your toolbelt or toolbox for 20 similar screwdrivers, nor any need whatsover for more than one (or two, if you tend to misplace things at job sites).

I knew a professional recorder player and he had hundreds of the things, but like your man no two were alike and all were used for professional gigs at one time or another. The reason he ended up with so many is because he did ensemble work, and recorders are made in matched sets, each set having one of each size from sopranino to contrabass. All the recorders in a set were matched for pitch and timbre so you get a smooth ensemble sound.

Likewise I was at a studio gig and a top professional guitarist showed up, him emptyhanded. Soon a pair of guys working for him showed up with two big rolling crates of instruments. The took the sides off the crates and each crate had numerous padded compartments, each having a different stringed instrument. There were electric guitars, acoustic guitars, mandolins, banjos, lutes, Asian and Arabic and Indian stringed intruments, rows and rows of things. All were kept in tune by his fulltime professional tuner.

Several times I worked with a professional studio flute guy. He showed up with orchestra flutes and basuri flutes and recorders and whistles, and oboes and saxes and clarinets, pretty much every woodwind.

And the concept of “collector” would be entirely foreign to these highly paid professional musicians.

They bring everything because you never know what the composer wants! You might be hired for “flute” but end up playing Duduk.

Stuart Peel > If the equation of O = n+1 applies to a “current” situation, that’s great. If however, you have 150 harmonicas and you sell one, that’s O=150-S+D, where S is the number of instruments lost to sales, and D is the desired number of replacement instruments which prevent going into a previous or deprived, dysfunctional or delayed state where the O total doesn’t grow, and musical starvation symptoms accumulate, and nobody needs that.

Re: Musical Instrument Acquisition Syndrome

The two best solutions to the problem of ever-increasing number of instruments was by two old rancher friends, both of whom had many acres of land to expand onto.

One musician in Nebraska purchased at auction from the school board the old two-room schoolhouse Howard and his siblings had attended, which after school consolidation had sat empty for decades. He moved it from the crossroads down the road next to his ranch on the Niobrara River. The whole community showed up to help. He had the original wood stove, and added a refrigerator for liquid refreshments. One room was a workbench for loading shotgun shells and repairing old shotguns. The main room had his instruments, cases, books, LPs, 78s, cassettes, and an audio system. He had lots of straight back chairs and one overstuffed couch where more than few musicians slept off their overindulgence of spirits.

The other musician lived by the Camas Prairie in Idaho and loved playing, fixing and building fiddles. My grandma and aunt used to back him up at dances. Over the years the fiddles took over more and more of the ranch house. He built nice cabinets all around the walls of the dining room to hold his best fiddles but there came a time when his wife had had enough. It wasn’t just all the fiddles, but also the musicians often playing until four in the morning. Mannie then built a log cabin out back. Land was plentiful, timber from the Sawtooths was plentiful and volunteers in a sparsely populated area were, if not plentiful, very eager to help. Out in the cabin he had a fiddle workshop, display cases, his contest trophies, a piano, lots of straight back chairs, and old scrapbooks of photographs of fiddlers.

There was some great music made in those structures. Neither one of them spent very much cash money on their music houses, but they had the land, the materials, the skills and the labor at hand. Adding all those square feet didn’t seem to slow down their MIAS, though. If anything it accelerated it.
