Re: Working in 3 dimensions. Explaining it through illustration & conceptualisation.

I think metapop is right. Music is frequencies being sounded in time. A frequency, however, uses time in its definition (waves per second). there is in music, however, phsycological time where “time” is simply the measure of events and is unrelated to the engineering time standards used to calculate frequency.

time in a piece of music, though, can be looked at as having different levels. Repetition is what allows music, at a very fundamental level, to have a meaningful structure. If you just play notes randomly, you get racket. If you play a phrase and repeat it, now you have the beginnings of a musical form (a simple one parter, but still…)

so time can pass at different levels in music. There is the big pieces of time defined by the overall form (like AABB), there are smaller phrases within the larger sections, and then there is the immediate foreground events like the notes themselves.

All this takes on an even deeper meaning in the compositions of the great masters from what we call “classical” music.

If anybody is interested in the subject, there is a branch of music theory called “Schenkerian Analysis” that is based on the work of Heinrich Shenker. I had it rammed down my throat in music school, but it is pretty interesting and isn’t too bad once you manage to choke it down

Re: Working in 3 dimensions. Explaining it through illustration & conceptualisation.

ok, so here is some more of the idea of using a graph as in illustration. remember that these graphs aren’t like the graphs of function like we learned in algebra. they are just representations of abstract ideas.

Let’s take ornamenting a tune like Morrison’s. If you only used a roll, then there are a limited number of places that a roll can fit. That’s our set of verticies. Since we have the choice to play or not to play a roll, then add “no roll” to our set of verticies. The number of edges to our graph will now represent the number of possible choices we have each time through the tune. since we don’t do everything all the time, each time through the tune we make choices of when and where to play that roll. Each time through the tune then will be a subset of all the possible ways we could have played the tune.

If you add another ornamental device like a cut, you will see that our choices immediately expand

What we refer to as “taste” or “style” then can be represented by the particular subset of all the possible choices we could have made

the fact that we can chose which things we do with each tune means that we can actually calculate how many possible ways the things we chose to do can effect the tune. Our actual choices then are a subset of all that’s possible from the means we chose to use

So this is a way of representing with a graph a sort of blueprint of our interpretation

the rate of flux is an interesting idea. The rate of flux could be viewed as the entropy of the tune. Here’s what I mean…

In the english language, when we begin to speak, the first word out of our yap could be anything at all really. The entire language isat our disposal. Once we being to speak, however, each word that follows is going to be constrained by the words that have gone before. Because of gramatical constructs, there are times when there is really only a few possible choices that make any sense. here is an example…

I like to play _____

There is a limited number of words that could come next, but they could be articles, nouns, adverbs, or even an adjective

I like to play the ______

Now the set of possiblilities is even more constrained by the use of a definite article. the next word has to be a noun or an adjective for our sentence to make sense, but we have a large number of words to chose from. The size of the set of words we could chose defines how much thought we really have to put into the selection of the next word. That is the “entropy”. It is a measure of how much thought went into the choice of the next word.

With a little imagination we can apply this to tunes, particularly to ornamentaion and variation

so if the tune constrains our possible choices, but at diferent points of the tune we have more choices availible to us without destroying the tune itself (which would be like constructing a nonsense sentence in the example) then different parts of the tune have higher levels of entropy

The entropy can be calculated by using probablilities to express the relationship between the size of the set of reasonable choices versus the size of the set of all possible choices, even nonsensical ones.

you could even apply sabremetrics (a statistical tool to analyse human performance used in baseball stats) to this problem

↳ Posted by Nate Ryan on May 7th, 2013.

Re: What’s the difference? Music IS just soundwaves.

Skreech - that’s a good point. Yes, music is sound waves, but there’s a bit more. Someone can perceive a certain configuration of sound waves and interpret it as music. But “being music” is not an inherent property of such a configuration; without someone recognizing it as music, it’s just sound waves.

Consider amusia: a brain damage that prevents you from recognizing music (nightmare!), due to a lesion or something. Affected people still hear the noises, they know that they have heard music before, but they’re unable to abstract rhythm and melody from the sound waves.

This is what I’m getting at. If you just record the sound waves, you have a mathematical model of the sound waves, and nothing more. But reducing *music* to mathematics requires an entirely different approach, because the model should be able to capture and represent the abstracted musical aspects of the noises, and not only the noises themselves.

Transcription software is something different, of course. Dots and MIDI do abstract music, but they’re very lossy. Maybe their approach is just too linear to handle complex aspects like the ones mentioned somewhere above, such as phrasing, variation and other possibilities, etc.
What I was after is an exhaustive reduction!

Re: Working in 3 dimensions. Explaining it through illustration & conceptualisation.

generally it is quantifying the input. That’s a big word for fitting it into a particluar slice of time. So if you play a hair behind the beat, instead of picking that up, it just moves it back onto the beat. Think of it like having say 4 shoeboxes and laying them on the floor. If you took a hanful of coins and tossed them, they would land where they land. That’s how we play in real time. MIDI would take the coins and put them in the nearest box so that no matter how they actually fell, they would only end up in one of the four boxes.

That is very simplistic, but that’s essentially how MIDI does it.
