So Called ‘On Line Sessions’.

Hi! Over the last few weeks I have involved myself with a few mainly Zoom based ‘on line sessions’.
Observations:
1. We all know that until, apparently, Mr Musk’s ultimate lazer dream eliminates all latency (milliseconds of time lag/ delay), playing tunes together is impossible on line.
2. Seeing the people who play and chatting with them, especially between the continents and over oceans, is really great fun.
3. But despite 2. the point of a session is to sit down & play & learn tunes together.
4. This is not achieved.
5. At a real sesh the weaker players are helped along by the others. The on-line scenario just highlights their musical vulnerability, sometimes excruciatingly!
6. The practice of muting and playing along by yourself is pointless. You may as well (takes a large gasp of breath!) play along to pre-recorded YouTubes or albums!
7. I think we will all just have to patient and wait until we are all together in the pub again. The tradition shall survive obviously anyway.

Thank you!

Re: So Called ‘On Line Sessions’.

While I agree it’s not a replacement for in-person sessions, for many of us it’s a lifeline until we can get back to some thing resembling normal again.

I’ve had 20+ players each week since this started on my Tuesday night The Ould Sod virtual Zoom session. Weren’t you on mine last week? I have beginners each week who I know would feel intimidated going to a real session able to listen and play along with more experienced players.

Is it a perfect medium? Of course not, but it has other unexpected benefits, which you also mentioned, that I agree with.

I’ve met and been able to listen and play along with many amazing players all over the country who I’ve never met in person or were previously friends with on Facebook. Now I’m invited to participate in their local Zoom sessions. I would never had done anything like that if we hadn’t started experimenting with Zoom as a bridge until things open up. When things are back to normal and we’re willing to travel again, I have new friends all over the country whose sessions I can go visit who are already familiar with my playing and I am familiar with theirs. That’s been amazing.

I disagree with your initial statement.

It’s entirely possible, using JamKazam, to play tunes together in realtime online. It just requires some willingness to deal with the technical issues that have to be overcome to make it possible. I play every week along with players from 500 to 1000 miles away.

When it works, with a properly setup system and network configuration, you can absolute play along with others within a few hundred miles in realtime with less than 30 ms lag, and feels like they are in the same room with you. It’s more challenging at 1000 or 2000 miles, but still possible. If you can recruit your local players to try the technology, you absolutely can play together.

Is it as easy to setup and use as Zoom? Absolutely not. Is it worth going through the effort, if you are willing, to make it work? Absolutely.

Here’s a paper on the subject I helped co-author:

https://docs.google.com/document/d/1yOGhHu429jCcWu66yvTjhBn-UdTUgmf8dYv82Z6irQI/edit?usp=sharing

I don’t know when/if pubs here will ever be open again, and even when they do, it’s unclear how we could have a session while staying safe until COVID-19 is gone. I can’t imagine that flutes/whistles will be able to be played in pub sessions for a good while.

My experience of the Zoom thing has been that - no, it isn’t a session. As soon as you accept that, you can see it as something else. That something else seems to have some value for some people, both musically and socially. For example, it gives people a chance to hear a tune the way one person plays it and play along with that ‘clean’ version. Being live allows people to request tunes that they don’t have ready access to a recording of. And as a tune player, it puts you ‘on the spot’ in a way that demands a lot of concentration and I’ve found may help towards the time we can go out playing live again. So - is it a session? No. Is it fun or useful? For me - sure.

Re: So Called ‘On Line Sessions’.

“You have to hear each other and influence the ebb and flow, the groove, the music, natural two way traffic. This is why I think that trying to learn whilst playing along to recordings is pointless.”

As Michael pointed out, this is where JamKazam has beaten my expectations, especially with players that are within 100 miles or so. I have both my regular weekly sessions running on JamKazam now, with 6 or 7 of the regulars, and we’re trying to help other people get involved, too. JamKazam can be very frustrating, and if you don’t deal well with technology issues, it might be too frustrating for you in a lot of cases. But when it works, it works, and you can actually get the “two way traffic”, where you’re reacting to the other players and vice versa.

And interestingly, there are some positive aspects of JamKazam that I will miss when we’re back to playing in person. First off, you can turn down (or mute) any player! There’s a player that I play with regularly who is so quiet on fiddle that I can’t hear them playing unless they’re sitting next to me, but now I can turn them up! And conversely, if someone is playing a tune that I don’t know very well, I can simply mute myself, and “noodle” along without causing anyone any distress! So again, it’s not a drop in replacement for a live session. It’s different. But I find it more enjoyable than the Zoom sessions (which are still fun in their own right, without being exactly like a real session).

Re: So Called ‘On Line Sessions’.

We have been fussing around with on-line sessions. A few observations:
* there’s way too much network latency to make playing together viable - what we have tried is having one person play and others play along on mute - at best it’s like two instruments playing, at worst, it’s pretty miserable.
* some people have PCs (or whatever) that don’t work well - the most obvious problem is that they sound like they’re on the verge of some sort of nasty feedback (booming/echoing).
* it’s a lot better than not doing anything - we have lively conversations about tunes, instruments, politics, and what-not.
* everyone has been looking for new tunes - we may be getting more than we would in a regular session and it’s fascinating to see where people are looking for new material.

We are still under lock-down in Michigan but we have had a few illicit back-yard sessions with a very limited number of participants wearing interesting masks. No flute or whistle players yet. We don’t have high hopes for planned gatherings (Goderich, Beaver Island, etc.), but we’re hoping that we can manage to play together without technical intervention in the not-too-distant future.

Re: So Called ‘On Line Sessions’.

Fred, these what we found to be some “best practices” for use of Zoom for music events:

1) Use headphones if possible to reduce echo

2) Use a good microphone if possible

3) In the Zoom Audio settings, enable “Automatically adjust volume”

4) In the Zoom Audio settings, under Advanced settings, disable “Suppress Persistent Background Noise” and “Suppress Intermittent Background Noise”. Leave “Echo cancellation” at “Auto”.

5) The Zoom meeting host can enable “Enable Original Sound” as an option for the participants. Participants should enable this feature, shows up on the upper left of their video on desktop versions, I believe it’s a setting somewhere in the mobile apps.

Neil C said
“Zoom…gives people a chance to hear a tune the way one person plays it and play along with that ‘clean’ version.”

Yes. While that’s not what sessions are traditionally about, it is what Highland pipe band rehearsals are about. The Pipe Major plays the tunes precisely how he wants them to be played, and all the other pipers do their best to meld to his playing. It’s nice to be able to hear every nuance, without having to try to listen through a dozen other pipers.

Is a piper developing some bad habits? Perhaps! Then again, when everyone’s playing together it can be difficult to determine which piper is doing some subtle thing wrongly. For that you go round the table, each piper playing in turn. Zoom works for that too.
