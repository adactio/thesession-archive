BTW, if your instrument is the one you linked to on the other forum, then it isn’t a rustic Hurdy Gurdy - that was based on your suggestion that it was German-Hungarian. Germanic Gurdies are bigger and heavier than French, often quite crudely made (hence ‘rustic’) but crucially have a different arrangement for the trompette - it is controlled by a wedge on the soundboard, rather than the peg and cord of French instruments. (For a typical example of a Germanic HG, look for Youtubes of ‘Melissa the loud’.) In terms of function and capability, your instrument should probably be called a Symphonie, although Symphonies usually take the form of a rectangular box, with all the strings enclosed.

Re: Hurdy Gurdy (yes, I know…)

Do you mean the wheel cover, or the key-box lid?

The wheel cover affects the tone - the gap round the wheel forms a significant part of the sound hole area. Putting the cover on makes the instrument a bit more mellow, leaving it off makes it more strident (and sometimes you just can’t be bothered to keep taking it on and off to make adjustments.)

The key-box lid acts as a wrist rest, and gives you a reference point for hand shifts - it’s actually quite hard to play with it open. With chromatic instruments the only reason you sometimes see people playing with it open is if they are using ‘special effects’ like fingering harmonics on the chanterelles. But with diatonic instruments like the one in the clip, modulating from major to minor means moving the tangents, and accidentals can only be played by fingering the note instead of using the key. Either of those techniques needs the lid left open. (Of course, in the clip it might be something as simple as that the lid rattles when it’s shut!)

And of course, I could be wrong. And I’m only basing my opinion on what I’ve seen from videos, and my knoweldge and experience with ergonimics. The deal is, with fiddle, you are changing the aspect of the bow in relation to the strings, constantly --because of the manual operation of the bow. With gurdy, the wheel contacts the strings. This constitutes a fundamental and huge difference in right arm/hand/wrist use.

Because the fiddle bow requires manual operation in all respects, the variables for how the bow acts upon the strings are immense (why the fiddle is both one of the most challenging instruments, as well as one of the most expressive). In orider to accomplish this, many precise sublteties and nuances are employed in technique--emanating from the right arm/hand

To some degree, from what I’ve observed, this exists also in gurdy technique. However, in my view, not nearly to the same degree as in fiddling bowing--for the wheel is in fixed position, rendering its aspect in relation to the strings, constant and “automatic,”

Re: Hurdy Gurdy (yes, I know…)

We’re back to the difference between bashing out a tune and making music again. Yes, bashing out your first tune on a melodeon is easier than a clarinet. But making music (ie something that others might want to listen to) is harder, because controlling the bellows to produce the accents and nuances that turn a tune into music is harder than controlling your breathing.

The same with fiddle and Gurdy - yes, anyone can pick up a Gurdy and bash out a simple tune in a matter of minutes, but learning to control the wheel so that you can articulate and accent notes to turn that tune into music is far harder. Not to mention the physical effort involved. I play both, and when I practice the Gurdy I like to stop every half hour or so and play fiddle for a bit, to give my arm a rest.
