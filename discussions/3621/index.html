Do you need to feel safe in a session, musically speaking?

Something I’ve noticed a lot on this site and in real life is a concern by players to be able to fit into a session and “know the repertoire” before they attend. Of course, this is natural and most of us will choose our sessions accordingly on this basis. However, I have noticed that certain people won’t play or try out a session unless they feel “really safe”. I’m not talking about beginners here but actual bands and groups of good musicians/friends who have been playing together for a long while. They will often descend upon an established session or set up their own somewhere and play through their sets of tunes. Now, this is often very enjoyable and can be appreciated by the regulars who might join in. However, this is not what a session should be about, in my opinion.
This is probably as much a “social thing”--like going out with friends for a meal and a drink but you all go to a session instead. Maybe, I’ve a tendency to be a loner and live dangerously sometimes but I think that’s part of the fun of going to different sessions. ---
By the way, what I’ve described is not the same thing as going to a session and meeting people who may (or may not be)your friends but the need to have go there in the “safety of numbers”. Again, I’m not referring to beginners or “session newbies” who obviously might be a bit apprehensive about going along to new sessions on their own. I can understand why they might wish support. It’s more those who seem to do this sort of thing as a “matter of routine” and I wonder why? What do you think?

John

Re: Do you need to feel safe in a session, musically speaking?

Only thing I’d say in answer to your comment Orson, is that you have two things going for you that some sessioneers don’t. First you’re a gifted player with an enviable repertoire and second you’re no slouch in the “getting on with it” department.

However, people being people, there are undoubtedly those who feel a little apprehensive about going to sessions where they’re not quite sure what the vibe is going to be like.

A few months ago I went along to a particular session where, at the start of the afternoon’s crack there were only about three of the regular crew and myself. As we had all finished the fuss of getting the tools out and checking we were as near as damn it in tune, yer man asked me to kick off a set. Which I happily did - my signature set if I remember rightly, “An Rogaire Dubh/Na Ceannabhain Bhana”, two oul standards - and that grand feeling ensued as the other heads nestled in alongside me.

HOWEVER … after a while the rest of the regular crew filed in and the gentle start to the day gave way to some of the most spectacular sessioneering it’s been my pleasure to listen to. I realised that I was out of my league. Had I arrived as the session was in full spate, I would have kept the kit in its case and sat nearby with my lugs flapping and my jaw hitting the ground occasionally. As it was I was trapped among the players making the occasional gesture of strumming along and I felt I was using up a seat that should rightly have gone to a more deserving person.

Just as I was about to sneak the implement back into its case surreptitiously, I was asked to kick off another set. I saddled up another team of warhorses and dug my spurs into their flanks and we were off again.

Despite the stellar nature of the players, they were genuinely appreciative and chimed in with the tunes.

I suppose the moral - if there is one - is that a) it deosn’t matter if you visit a new session whether you know all their tunes. As Orson says, there’s no crime in sitting on your hands for a bit and using your ears. And b) if you are visiting a new session and get asked to play a few tunes, don’t go straight off with something you’re only halfway with; stick to the tunes you can play in your sleep. That way you’ll play reasonably well and there’s nothing more likely to get you accepted that the ability to put across a few tunes to your own satisfaction … who knows, if you feel good about your playing, the others are likely to feel pretty good about it too.

And if you do your best and the new crew look at you stony-faced (“Is that it?!”), then pick your gear up, rise up from your seat and, on your way out, tell the begrudgers to get a grip!

Re: Do you need to feel safe in a session, musically speaking?

John wrote: “I’m not talking about beginners here but actual bands and groups of good musicians/friends who have been playing together for a long while. They will often descend upon an established session or set up their own somewhere and play through their sets of tunes.” This is bad form and it disrupts the session in my opinion. When I show up at a session with my cohorts I avoid playing our medleys for the most part. However, if it’s our session, then playing the tunes and medleys that we like to play IS the session, and anyone is welcome to join in.

Your premise for this thread is about whether you should join a session or not based on repertoire. If you encounter a session the correct repertoire is whatever the folks who started it, (or hosting -- whatever) is. Even though they would enjoy your company and the tunes you might bring to the session, you can’t assume that once you or anyone else arrives that the hosts have to suddenly adhere to some top 100 list or something. You’re joining their session; they’re not joining yours.

This is why I always recommend listening first to see if you’re familiar with the tunes being played before you decide to join in. If you do that – you’ll feel “safe” once you do decide to join in. Otherwise – you can relax with a sense of “safety” from your bar stool or whatever and enjoy the music.

Re: Do you need to feel safe in a session, musically speaking?

In your case, Chris, you might be more comfortable in a large session where you won’t stand out so much. But if you play whistle or flute it can create problems. Whistle players pitch goes flat when they try to blow quietly to “pick up one” and players nearby will have to tolerate it. Flutes have a similar problem. The experience from the perspective of a musician that knows the tune and is playing next to you is hard for people in your shoes to understand, and you won’t know until you’re the one who knows the tune and someone else is trying to “pick up one” next to you. By this time it’s too late usually -- it was for me anyway. I’d like to apologize to anyone and everyone I have done this to during my early years. *whew* That feels better now… thanks.
