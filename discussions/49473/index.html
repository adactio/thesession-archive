Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

Hey, without going on a whole spiel about the context behind it all, just wondering if any experienced session leaders here can help me out with tips and tricks for starting my own session group.

I was in another state (US) for a while, and became a regular member of an established session for about 6 months until I had to move, and during my time there I learned the whole session etiquette and got a pretty decent amount of tunes under my belt (I’d probably say I got somewhere between 30-40 tunes memorized, with a decent amount learned but not memorized), and of course met some very good people along the way.

Unfortunately I had to leave, but in my new state, I’m going back to school to study music, however it’s a state and city where (Irish) sessions aren’t really a thing, maybe a small one a long time ago, but not anymore, and so being inspired, I decided to start my own session, taking all that I learned (and have yet to learn) and continue the tradition in my own little corner of the world. I’ll be planning to establish it as a formal university club at first, likely hosting it for a little while until there’s a decent membership and then I will find actual venues to meet at.

With all that being aside, just feeling a lot of self-doubt as to whether I can manage it or not, though I am determined to follow it through, and I’m hoping some more experienced sessioners than I, those of you here who have actually managed to start sessions of your own, can give me some advice on how to proceed and ensure it doesn’t collapse.

I’ve already found one individual who is particularly interested, and she may end up becoming a co-lead with me, but given that I’m starting the group in a music college, I don’t think I’ll have to worry about navigating with the beginner players, at least at the start. Most of them will definitely be unfamiliar with the etiquette for sure, as well as not knowing any tunes. So I feel the onus will have to fall on me to guide the group into learning tunes and creating core sets to reliably play before it can expand.

These are just some of the issues I’m feeling great anxiety and self-doubt over. I’d really appreciate some help here. Thanks.

tl;dr - Intermediate player starting a new session group at my college and unsure how to do it successfully.

Re: Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

Maybe try and get a few things straight in your mind before you start putting the word out.

Like, what you mean by Irish music. Tunes? Mix of tunes and trad songs? Pub songs? It helps to start with a view, even if it evolves later.

Do you only want to focus on instruments recognised as part of the broad tradition? People play Irish music very well on non-trad instruments, but it can also get to be a source of griping.

Is it open to all or do people need to approach you/the co-anchor first?

If you need to develop a common repertoire from scratch, how might that work? If you don’t want to end up always steering it, or have the opposite where people get discouraged by the number of tunes others want them to learn, it needs a bit of management.

Have fun with it!

Re: Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

The two most important things that come to mind, as someone who co-hosted an intermediate session for a couple of years, would be:

1) Make sure you have a backup session leader to keep the thing going on a regular basis during times when you can’t attend. This is what killed the session I led with my wife several years ago. There was never anyone else in the group who could commit to being there when we couldn’t be, and there weren’t enough experienced regulars who could keep things going by themselves. When my S.O. and I couldn’t be there and we let everyone know by email, then nobody would show up. That didn’t sit well with the venue (bar) we had arranged for the session.

2) You may be assuming that you won’t have trouble with people attending who are disruptive, even innocently disruptive. Like the guy who showed up at one local session with a giant banjo bass who only knew OldTime I-V bass grooves, was completely unfamiliar with Irish trad, and played that I-V riff over every tune, out of rhythm. I had to gently persuade him that it wasn’t working, and he never came back.

Things like this will happen, especially if you’re in the USA with so many other acoustic music genres with amateur players. Maybe the music school venue will help, but make sure you or your co-host are able to deal with this in a friendly but firm enough way to prevent the session being wrecked. That said, best of luck with the project and let us know how it goes!

Re: Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

ddevereaux, good on you for your passion for this music, and for sessions! It’s natural to feel some anxiety over starting something so audacious, but this just shows that you care, you want it to succeed.

Based on your OP, here’s some food for thought:

- If your city really did once have an Irish session, even a small one, you may be surprised at the number and quality of musicians who come out of the woodwork if you get word out that you want to start a session. Post a flyer with your contact info at your local music shops and on your uni’s campus. Post on social media too. Search your area on the session database here for nearby thesession.org members.

- Your 6 months of session experience and 30-40 tunes are a start, but you’ll want to grow your tune base. A good way to do that is to find at least one other local person (you say you’ve already found one other musician) who plays this music and commit to sitting down together in private once a week to (1) suss out what tunes you have in common, and (2) learn each other’s tunes. I’ve done this one-on-one in towns where Irish trad musicians were rarer than hen’s teeth. If you can find more players who actually know some tunes, then meet as a tune learning group until your repertoire develops and gels—then you can launch a full-fledged session.

- No doubt you have your reasons for starting off as a “formal university club,” but I can’t help but wonder if that would limit participation from non-uni local musicians. And you say most of the uni participants would likely not know session etiquette or the tunes—by definition, they are beginners, no matter how experienced they are in other genres. In my experience, the more rooted in another genre’s pedagogy a musician is, the more challenging it can be for them to adapt to the feel and mindsets of Irish trad.

- If and when your session is hosted by some venue, it’s absolutely crucial to communicate clearly and regularly with the host. You’ll want to let them know what to expect, and then regularly keep them informed of any changes. It’s especially important to notify them when the session won’t be happening, for any reason, and when you expect to return. Also, touch base with the host for regular reality checks—are they happy hosting the session? Any troubles to address, etc.? If you land someplace with waitstaff, encourage your session mates to tip them even if the host provides your beverages or food at no cost. Clean up after yourselves. If you move a table or chairs for the session, at the end of the night put them back where you found them.

Best of luck and let us know how you get on with it!

Re: Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

I think you are correct that you need to gather and build a bit of a community before you try to start a regular session in a public place. Here are some suggestions that may help you:

1. Use the member search on this website to see if you can find other players in your city, or even in your state. https://thesession.org/members/. There is a search box on that page, and you can search the city name, the school name, the state, etc. You can try messaging any thesession.org members you find, and even if they aren’t living there anymore, they might be able to help you find other interested players, and at least give you any back history of Irish music in the area. But you might also find people that would be eager to become participants! (Another trick is to create a “trip” for yourself on this website, with the destination being your town. That will also help you discover members that are in the area).

2. See if any of the radio stations in your area host any Irish or “celtic” programs regularly. This often happens on public radio stations, and sometimes college radio stations. If you find any, maybe reach out to the host(s). At the very least, they might help you find other players, but they might also be able to mention it on their program, or even interview you about your desire to get a session going. If your school has a radio station, you could even possibly approach them about giving you a time slot to host your own program of Irish traditional music.

3. Work to build your community from within. Being at a music school, it shouldn’t be too hard to find people that are at least ITM-curious. So maybe try holding a regular (monthly?) tune learning session, where you teach a couple of tunes (preferably by encouraging people to learn them by ear, even if they’re fluent music readers, because their introduction to the music should be to tunes played in the Irish style). You can also use this time to talk about what instruments are well established in the tradition, what the different rhythms sound like, how Irish sessions generally work, session etiquette, etc.

FWIW, my first real formal introduction to learning to play this music was in a tune learning session that was started by Matt & Shannon Heaton, nearly 25 years ago. I took over running that tune learning several years later, and still run it weekly. It has been one of the contributing factors to the fact that there are 15 regular Irish sessions in my area now (and there were even more, pre-pandemic). BTW, regularly leading a tune learning session was immensely beneficial to my own development as a player, my knowledge of the music, and my now-1000+ tune repertoire!

4. Use tools at your disposal to get the word out! Social media can be very helpful. Maybe start a Facebook group for your session. Name it something descriptive, like “<your city> Irish Music Fans” or something. Don’t necessarily tie it to the school, which might discourage other folks in the community from being part of it. But if the school has a newspaper or a website for extracurricular activities, you might be able to post information about it for free. And when you do start something, post it in the Sessions section of this website.

5. Be patient. This probably won’t come together overnight, but if you share your passion for it with others, it can be contagious!

Re: Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

I think it will be good in the long run to make it clear that you are starting a specifically Irish traditional music session, with a very standard repertoire, at least at first. Otherwise, depending on where you are, you may have to fight off the old time and bluegrass players with a pitchfork.

Tell the novice bodhran players to come back with a melody instrument, and give them some resources to get started.

Make the first hour or so a tune-learning sesh until you have a critical mass of tunes and repertoire. You’ll have to adjust to the technical and ear-learning capability of the players who are showing up.

Have some recordings of great traditional music that’s also accessible and reasonably playable from a technical standard. If all your tunes are bang easy for the first year or two you’re getting off the ground, that’s probably a good thing. The KISS principle applies.

Make the tempo slow and wild. Discourage the speed demons. The idea is to play great music as an ensemble.

Recruit a backer early who is willing to learn to put in the time to learn the style of Michael O Domhnaill, Randal Bays, Donal Hennessy, or other established quality players with a traditional approach, if they don’t already know it. This serves two purposes: First, hopefully, you’ll get a quality backer. Second, that player will act as a scarecrow to discourage novice guitar players you don’t want at the session. It’s important for quality control, because a bad backer can damage your sound and your experience much more than a struggling melody player can. If you already have someone good, you can tell the strumming sesh wreckers “sorry, we already have a strummer, but come back with a melody instrument, and we’d love to have you!”

I think Mike Eskin will have some ideas on how he screens guitar players before they join the sessions he anchors in San Diego.

Be a leader early on. Don’t “go along to get along.” Be clear about what your vision is and stick to it. This means keeping a focused repertoire of traditional Irish tunes, and ensuring everyone knows etiquette. If you do that your other regulars will help you. Once you have a critical mass of players (5 -10) pulling in the same direction you can ease up a little bit.

Don’t do the “circle of death” thing, where everyone gets to start a set, in turn, as you go around the circle. Sure, make sure everyone gets to start a set or two, at all levels. But the C o D is a ticket to stagnation. Let the more fluent, experienced players keep things moving and the craic flowing. If you keep tempos moderate and stick to standard repertoire, at least at first, you’ll still be including the novices.

Take the time to publish a list of tunes that were played. This is useful not just for novices but for intermediate players looking to learn the tunes actually played in the session on their own.

Be ruthless about banning tablets and reliance on sheet music during the session.

Have fun and laugh a lot, and don’t take yourself too seriously as Sesh Don. Take the tradition seriously.

Adjust your approach for the people actually present. If no one is yet capable of getting through a tune set without sheet music, you need to meet them where they are, and maybe concentrate on showing them tunes by year for a while, until you have, again, critical mass.

Be sure to make the first and last tunes/sets ones that everybody knows.

Put the sesh up here, and also in some Irish music social media pages. You might be surprised who comes out of the woodwork, or who is willing to travel a good deal for some tunes.

Those would be my recommendations. Good luck!

Re: Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

You can do it!

There’s no single magic formula. You play the cards you’re dealt and take it from there. You’ve been a regular at sessions, have some reasonable repertoire to build from and are studying music so I’m expecting learning new tunes isn’t going to break you.

The venue you choose will shape things significantly. Some pub/restaurant venues will be happy to have a regular session - PROVIDING it’s “entertaining”, or at the very least not turning punters away. Many venues just don’t want anything to do with regular sessions and are only interested in smaller performance/groups.

So one option while you’re finding your vibe and building repertoire and members might be to relax with a “behind closed doors” venue such as a hall or college room.

Music college gives you a rich field to harvest for potential players - but accomplished musicians in other genres need time to get the feel and style or idiom right. As well as building a moderate repertoire of say 1 hours worth of common material. The whole ITM scene is highly sociable and should be easy going and playful rather than overly serious. For reasons I still don’t quite understand, ITM attracts wonderful musicians with zero ethnic/ancestral connection to Ireland. It is a passport to craic anywhere in the globe.

Given that you’re likely to be bringing non ITM players up to speed - I’d recommend grabbing any one of the many “core repertoire” collections available and just decide to run with that as the curriculum. The Comhaltas series Foinn Seisiún Book 1 is hard to beat in this regard. But there are dozens of established session Tunebooks with focus on tunes which are played widely.

There is no singular etiquette either BTW. Each group will have a dynamic. And it may be open, or closed. And it may change over time. But if your group is initially closed/by invitation - you really just need to get going and see where the journey takes you. Whatever the vision at the outset - its going to evolve as opportunities and people present themselves.

Best of luck. Nothing to worry about - even if it doesn’t work out initially change the formula and go at it again. Always be learning

Regarding the comment above about how I deal with vetting new backup players who show up at our session.

Because of resources like Facebook and other social media sites, often times a backup player who is new in town or going to visit our session will contact me in advance introducing themselves and asking about the session.

If I don’t know them already, it’s possible to look at their photos and videos and see if they’ve been playing in sessions for a long time and hopefully know what they’re doing.

If a guitar (or bouzouki) player shows up that we don’t know, generally I’ll ask them if they have experience specifically backing Traditional Irish Music.

Depending on their answer, I’ll often say something along the lines of:

“How about we try a set and see how it goes?”

And then we play a set of tunes with our new guitar/bouzoki player and we see how it goes.

If it’s clear they know what they’re doing, great!

If they clearly don’t know what they’re doing, I might ask them to sit out and offer them all kinds of resources on learning to do backup for Traditional Irish Music.

It is unfortunate that this is necessary, but an unskilled or clueless backup player can destroy a session.

A few tips that may help:

Consider proving a list of tunes to learn, with a link to The Session as a resource for the dots.

Re: Advice for Starting a New Session as an Intermediate Sessioner

I run a session in an irish bar for about 1 year now, a few thoughts:
a) go for it, it will move you forward as a musician
b) “for best effect”, you need an anchor melody player and an anchor rhythm player (I can do either role). if you do not have any reliable rhythm guitar players, try to find a boron or a cahon player.
c) you will have to show up for every session. I had no idea how much of an imposition that it.
d) irish bar people love songs, try to find a singer or two. (sessioneers who care not for songs appreciate a break for facebook, smoke, bathroom, etc).
e) consider running an “open book” session (but beware of dead time while everybody is furiously scrolling to the dots for the britches full of stiches).
f) if participants have no common tunes, consider running an additional tune learning session (open book, of course), obviously you cannot do it in an irish bar, try outdoors in a park, in somebody’s home, etc. our sibling french-canadian group have done this with great effect: a tune learning session in the park, a proper tunes session in a brew-pub.
