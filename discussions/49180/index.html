Re: What is an Irish Flute?

The only time I’ve heard one called an “Irish flute” is when someone means they play Irish trad music on flute. Kind of like saying “I play Irish fiddle.” Bit of a lazy way of saying you play fiddle with a focus on Irish music. Doesn’t matter where the instrument was made.

In my experience, it’s more common to see or hear a flute for Irish music called:
- D flute, or concert D (and also C flute, Bb flute, Eb flute, etc.)
- [insert material] flute, e.g. wooden or a specific wood, blackwood, cocus, boxwood, etc.
- simple system flute
- conical bore flute (though some are cylindrical bore)
- [insert design style] flute, e.g., Pratten, Rudall, Nicholson, etc.
- keyless or keyed flute
- [insert maker’s name] flute, e.g., Olwell flute, or Murray, Hamilton, Wilkes, Grinter, McGee, and so on

As for characteristics, most makers and players aim for a darker, reedy tone, warmth (in contrast to the bell-like or metallic tone of some Boehm flutes), and responsiveness for crisp cuts, taps, rolls, and cranns. Some makers go for a slightly flat bottom D so it can be blown into pitch—the so-called hard D. Usually there is no lip plate. But there are exceptions to all of this, not one standard tone, intonation, material, embouchure hole shape and cut, lined and unlined heads, etc.

Re: What is an Irish Flute?

The question is one that I don’t feel needs answering - it is like the question; who is the greatest living Irish flute player? Matt Molloy or James Galway?

The style of music is Irish Traditional Music. Ok, no arguments there. Until it isn’t…

The instrument is either an antique or modern version of the flutes commonly used everywhere before Theobald Boehm introduced the modern classical, mostly cylindrical, flute. They are played in many places and in varied genres. As an instrument I like the names; simple system flute or conical bore flutes, with or without keys. “Concert Flute” applies to both these and to the metal Boehm, depending on who is using it. I would argue that a cylindrical keyless flute is a different beast to a conical one. The main characteristic for me is that the scale is uneven with larger and smaller holes being used for different notes depending on the ergonomics of actuallyy being able to reach them.

Also, _any_ flute made in Ireland is an Irish flute. Hard to dispute… As is one made by someone heritably unconnected with Ireland (though you can never be sure…) in Germany, built, sold and well suited for the purpose of playing Irish music. But what is it when I play Scottish Music on it? Or Klezmer?

(And please, lets not discuss that dreadful TV show where Matt and James both appeared. Arguments about genre sensitivity and appropriateness of technique are not the point of my post here. Anyone interested can find it on youtube.)

Re: What is an Irish Flute?

As with other genres, one way to determine what an “Irish flute” is, would be to look at what the most famous and well-respected flute players are using. They have spent many more years finding their perfect flute than I have, so I think it’s a reasonable angle on the question.

When one does that, one finds that almost all these famous ITM flute players are playing either vintage or modern copies of mid 19th Century conical bore, simple system wooden flutes based on a few of the more famous English designs from the period. Most are playing 8 or 6-key flutes as well.

There are only a few famous ITM flute players using anything else, and it’s usually a Boehm flute. So, working backwards from “what are the famous artists playing?” question, we can arrive at a reasonable definition of what an Irish flute is. Just look at what Matt Molloy is playing. :)

Does this mean it can’t be cylindrical instead of conical bore, or made of metal? Well, if you can play Irish trad on it without difficulty then you’re surely playing an Irish-capable flute, if not the type that the vast majority of famous artists are using. And for that matter, the type I see not-so-famous flute players using in local sessions and workshops.

Re: What is an Irish Flute?

I don’t live in Ireland, so it’s like this. When starting off flute playing by using a silver-plated metal “Concert C” type flute, after a few years of that I was introduced to the great flute playing of Matt Molloy with the Chieftain records I heard, and later, seeing them in concert a few times. Then discovering a long line of “Celtic” musicians on through to the genius performer Orlaith McAuliffe and others.

With just that comparison to go by, I’d say the “Irish” flute to me is the wood body flute with wood tone hole. To the experienced flute player, it sounds very different than a silver flute. No matter how many external key mechanisms are added to such “Irish” flutes, they retain their fine “woody” tone (see Monty Python skit for the explanation of the superiority of woody toned things vs tinny things). That’s not to say that a silver flute is automatically tinny, though some are, and I avoid them. Even shifting to all Delrin doesn’t change the character of the tone much, the construction design is different enough from the silver flutes that the “Irish” tone is preserved. After accumulating some hearing damage, I saw an Irish flute played in a concert and determined that I could “listen to one of those for hours”, vs the silver flute which can be very hard on the ears, especially around 1000hz, where my current preference is for silence. Hence, I like the Irish flutes, alto flutes, North American First Nations style flutes, etc.

Re: What is an Irish Flute?

I personally believe that music is more about expression than mere sound.
What I’ve learned in this discussion is that an “Irish Flute” is one that has the capability needed to play irish tunes with all its twists and quirks, which a Boehm flute does not have.
It has a very important constraint. It operates with valves which are either open ore closed, anything in between is very difficult to achieve. It can be done, as master Ian Anderson from Jethro Tull shows, but it’s difficult.
I’ve played the south american Quena for years, and it is very easy to make it weep or laugh, which I couldn’t do with a Boehm style flute, silver or not. I have played C Boehm flute for a while.
Quenas are made from some sort of bamboo or wood, but they can sing, weep, cry or laugh in other materials also.
Back in my twenties I was playing with a group in south america, tunes from the Andes, very much like what Bolivia Manta does. I made a set of Quenas with bronze tube, each adapted to finger width and playing habit of the group member, just for one peruvian air for six Quenas. Just remembering that performance still sends me shivers down the spine, and that was almost 40 years ago.
Same thing with ITM.
So my summary from this discussion is: an Irish Flute is one that is suitable for ITM and is played as such.
It’s the same as with violins or fiddles - I might prefer the sound of a Stradivari over a Tecchler or one made by a saxon master, but when used for jigs or reels it’s like with any other music than ITM: it is the player that does the music, the instrument gives him a chance to do so.

(I hope this comment qualifies as related to ITM despite mentioning my experience from elsewhere)

Re: What is an Irish Flute?

The term “Irish flute” is a misnomer. There is no “definition” or agreed specification for what is/is-not an “Irish flute”. It is an ambiguous term. Different people will argue various boundaries as to what might or might not be included under that umbrella.

It is generally used to reference (1) conical bore (2) wooden (3) simple system (4) keyed (or, sometimes, keyless) flutes - (5) which are used to play Irish trad. Most “Irish flutes” aren’t Irish - they are original early-mid 19thC English designs or authentic copies thereof. The phrase definitely excludes, modern classical/silver Boehm system flutes - although there’s nothing to prevent such an instrument from playing Irish trad.

The (so-called) “Irishness” is a modern association - because this is mainly where you’d see them nowadays and distinctive vs the silver flute. These were simply ‘flutes’ in their day and played popular tunes and classical music in a myriad of keys. You’d have found them in late baroque or classical period orchestra.

Even though they are near ancestors, the phrase likely excludes renaissance or Baroque era traverse flutes (mainly on the grounds that these types aren’t used to play Irish trad).

You’d rarely hear a similar vintage French/German/Austrian flute of the same vintage being referred to as an “Irish flute” - mainly because those types didn’t make it across in significant numbers to the Irish trad genre. We seemed to favour the power of the Rudall/Pratten style “barking” English flutes over the sweetness of the continental models. Having said that, most folk wouldn’t be able to tell the difference between the English and continental models if they were hit over the head with one during a rendition of the Bucks of Oranmore.

Re: What is an Irish Flute?

“The instrument is either an antique or modern version of the flutes commonly used everywhere before Theobald Boehm…”

I would amend this by omitting “everywhere”.

Because the main instruments played by the leading Irish trad flutists, at least when I started playing flute, were made in London and were specifically made for the 19th century British classical fluteplaying market.

As we know the old-system flutes made and played on the Continent continued the small-hole small-bore tradition established in the Baroque era. Yes there have always been some Irish trad flutists who have played German-made or French-made vintage wooden flutes, but in the main it’s the big-bore big-hole 19th century London wooden flutes which have found the most favour, which is borne out by the fact that the modern makers of wooden flutes specifically for Irish trad have used the London flutes as their models, not the Continental flutes.
