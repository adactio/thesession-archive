Weaknesses - tackle them or hide them?

What do you do if your fingers disobey and find it Very Hard Indeed to execute a particular ornament or bowing pattern or whatever?
Do you whip those fingers into shape so that you can manage the ornament - maybe not well at first, and in the meantime, you’re massacring tunes? But get there in the end, derring do and all that? You’ll be a better person, maybe even a better player, if you don’t duck the issue? :)
Or do you take the cowardly route, and avoid playing that pattern, because your playing sounds so much better if you substitute a plainer or easier phrase? A small sacrifice for the sake of the tune - common sense really?

I tend to the cowardly course. My left hand pinkie just won’t move fast enough or co-ordinate properly with the other fingers, and it persists in remaining flat not curved whatever I do. So fiddle rolls executed on the third finger with the fourth thrown at the string either break down or sound ‘a right fuffle’ (Yorkshire technical term). As I’m retired, I’m thinking that I may have to accept Nature’s limitations.

But has anyone else got experience of this type of personal weakness, and got the better of it in the end by tackling it head on? It would be lovely to hear of your experience - and thanks in advance for any replies.

Re: Weaknesses - tackle them or hide them?

What I do, personally, is irrelevant ;-) But I think that the honourable approach would be:

1. When playing in public or in company, do whatever you have to do to make the best musical contribution you can - i.e. avoiding techniques you’re not comfortable with, starting tunes at a pace you easily can play at, substituting phrases you find tricky with simplified versions, sitting out of tunes that you can’t reliably play through etc.
2. When playing privately, take the opportunity to work on the troublesome bits.

“As I’m retired, I’m thinking that I may have to accept Nature’s limitations.”

On the other hand, as you’re retired, you have all the time in the world to practise. It may be that it’s harder to train your fingers to do what you tell them past a certain age (some would, no doubt, argue otherwise). But there is no shame in playing in a simple style - far better to take the music in your stride than to be running to catch up with yourself.

Re: Weaknesses - tackle them or hide them?

I think that the body and mind always has the capacity to learn… no matter how old.. Naturally the older someone gets, the more things start to slip, like fine motor skills, flexibility, muscle reaction, all fairly natural in the aging process that we all go through at one rate or another. But if the mind sees it as a mountain to overcome, or dismisses the new activity as unlearnable for you, then it will be impossible indeed!

I would say that we all benefit from expanding our abilities and repertoires, that even small victories and gains will benefit in unexpected ways and places. Building up your fourth finger gives the chance for you to throw in those super-cool A/E string doublings (I know they come up more in Cape Breton playing than Irish, but they are sound so darned sweet!) I would agree with CMO, and encourage you to work at these troubled spots, even just a little at a time, and over time they will be able to come out in public, possibly even to your own surprise!

But that’s just my approach. I know a lot of people who play in sessions that fall back on the volume and business mask their playing, they kinda gloss over the hard parts, leave out passages, and more or less “drift with the current” (I think all but the most dedicated players do this to some degree). But some of them are great people to have around, and their personality adds a ton to the session! They look like they are having fun, and so is everyone else around them! Which is really the point, right…?

So my 2 cents is to not let your mind get in the way of your playing. In my experience that is often the biggest hurdle to learning - believing in your body’s limitations and letting that stop you where you are.

Re: Weaknesses… (the tackle approach)

Wonderfully honest question Fiddle Aunt. I would agree with all our friends above, and it also seems to me that since you are bothered by it, you must be wanting it to be better, not just avoided. ;)

I can share on the ‘nature vs practice’ part of this. I have a 70 plus year old student (not the first) who is a total beginner learning to navigate fingers which were more suited for this some 60 years ago. Unless you are seriously compromised by a trigger finger (not usually affecting pinkies) or RA, etc., you can practice 4th finger exercises and fix that pesky problem pretty well at almost any age.

I would suggest doing stretching arm/hand exercises for a couple of minutes before practice and after, because much of our reduced ability to play is from tightening tendons and muscles, more than our years. There’s lots online you could find for that, and there are really only 3 or so you would need. Strong and flexible fingers start with the muscles of the arm, not the fingers themselves.

So with a warm up to get the blood flowing well into your arm and fingers, then do any pinky/finger exercises you want - even just using short reel passages that make you work certain finger patterns. Any 3rd to 4th finger roll itself is a perfect exercise. How convenient!

I’m happy to share some specific techniques for finger dexterity if you wish, or perhaps that’s been done lots in previous threads? Either way…go for it, it’s certainly not beyond your ability, no matter how naughty your left pinky is behaving! :)

cbw, I just love my adult students. They really “get” that challenging themselves with any instrument is really a special endeavor. Children need another 30 years at least to truly know that. :D Plus they don’t pay for it … haha which is part of the great respect us big kids have!

Nicely said dfrost, about recovering gracefully…may I steal that one for my studio? I’m always telling a few of them to play out more when they are practicing and at lessons. If ya make a mistake…make a loud one! Then fix it. :)

For our english teacher… ;) or anyone…one of the best things I learned years ago from some teacher or other was to practice short passages in rhythms. I’ll use one I really like for an example called
Ducks on the Pond. Here’s the dots for it. https://thesession.org/tunes/9946

First example is just fine. Look at the start of the B part. No rolls here but a nifty little 8th note figure after the pick up e note; aebe - 3,o,4,o for fingerings. Please correct me if any of this needs a different Trad type notation. I think I have using small letters right for the notes. ;)

So, instead of just putting our old friend the metronome on and playing it over and over slowly increasing the speed, we could do this passage in various rhythms. You would play this 4 note figure in a loop but with a sort of double dotted rhythm, in order to isolate ONE finger/bow stroke moving fast at a time. (And maybe no metronome yet.) So the phrase might sound like the “a” is a dotted 8th, and the “e” following is played like a fast 16th, the “b” is a dotted 8th again, and the “e” is again a fast 16th. Even double dot the rhythm so that the fast note is played very quickly, this is not just a comfortable hornpipe rhythm, but one where you “force” the faster note to be just that; fast! You don’t need to carry the longer eighth to a full dotted note - it’s isolating the 16th that is the exercise.

Think extreme hornpipe or the Scottish type of dotted rhythms we hear. After you do that for a bit and your bow can synchronize with each note, you reverse the rhythm. How fast you do this is up to you. Do a speed that you CAN do well. You can use the metronome if you wish , one click for 2 notes to stay in some kind of boundaries, but not to push your ability. And it doesn’t matter also, if you start on the a, or an e, which is the pickup note to part B. Just keep looping that figure. (e)-a-e-b-e-a-e-b-e-a-e-b- etc. What ever you start on is the longer note, the second one is the fast one. Then you switch.

Doing it this way, you will also be able to control the placement of your pinky as you will only be managing 2 fast motions at a time. When you reverse the rhythm then you will see how it isolates the other fingers that had a break, so to speak, the first go around.

By the way, I actually first started doing this tune in a set with Old Grey Cat, Speed the Plow, then this with a contra dance group I session with. It makes for a great set everyone. Didn’t have a clue it was on here, but I was happy to find it here.

In the end, rhythm, timing, musicality and feel are more important than tricky fingering. In this realm a lifetime of experience often beats youth and brawn.

On whistle, the LH third finger is weaker than the others, so it takes longer to develop speed & timing with the rolls. When I need the song to work right, I’ll use other methods of attacking the decoration, e.g. tonguing to get the rhythm right. The fiddle equivalent might be rapid bow changes… I don’t know what you call it, but Kevin Burke does it beautifully.

I’ve found that difficult fingerings improve faster when worked in isolation (scales, arpeggios and scale-rolls), rather than trying to get them by playing the tune from top to bottom.

Re: Weaknesses - tackle them or hide them?

“The only secret is keep practising, then the improvement comes unconciously. Now looking back, I am amazed at what I’ve done during practising that now I overcome these pinky problems!”

This is true. As long as you’re not getting pain from the pinkie, your pinkie can be trained by practice over time. And not even that much time.

Here’s what I did -- and still do, sometimes -- to strengthen my rolls. I just set up the metronome to something slow and comfortable, and then do rolls on each string, with each finger. Don’t rush -- get as clean and even a sound as you can. And enjoy it -- enjoy the feeling and the sound. Do that for five minutes each day and you’ll be surprised at how well your pinkie responds.

Also, bear in mind that there are a few different approaches to rolls, as cbw said. The way I look at it is: One approach is where you hammer each finger down for each of the five roll notes; the other is where you more lightly grace the highest of the notes (the one you’ll do with your pinkie). I like to do both types, just for fun. then, in a tune, I can do whichever feels right at the moment.

But keep in mind that for the second approach, the lighter grace-note type, your pinkie will come across the string (perpendicular to it) rather than straight down onto it. This is, at least for me, kind of a key concept, because it tells my fingers to brush across the strings rather than always hammering straight down. This might help your pinkie to do a bit more curling and to be softer in general.

Adding to what Ergo said - you can also hammer down and off the string in a single movement if you want to sound the note’s pitch.

[*Maybe you can help me with double stops too - another of my weaknesses?*]

Double stops - is usually best if you start by playing the two fingered notes individually (a typical simple double stop would be G+B on strings 3+2, using fingers 3+1).

Play each one until they are both in tune, then play them together. After a while you’ll be able to stop the two notes together and bow across them both, without having to make any adjustments to pitch.

Re: Weaknesses - tackle them or hide them?

Yeah, Ergo, that’s what I was getting at, but didn’t describe the mechanics. When I used to play fiddle, I was really happy with my rolls (the only aspect of my playing I was happy with!), and I did them with a quick brushing movement, no matter which fingers I was using. I didn’t push the string down to the finger board but only interrupted the vibration, and the movement was more of a sweeping motion than straight up and down. I found it worked better for me because it was not a “move in one direction-stop-go back the other way” motion, but one smooth sweep. Likely not what a classical teacher would recommend, but I thought it sounded really good, and have seen lots of great fiddlers do it.
