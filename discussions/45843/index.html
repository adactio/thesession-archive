↳ Posted by Will Evans on February 12th, 2021.

Re: The tough approach to music teaching v trying not to be too hard?

It’s a difficult question because as can be seen in the responses above there’s a wide range of environments and students.

Many years ago I was teaching workshops at various festivals. In these one-hour workshops there would be people from a wide range of backgrounds and abilities, so much so that no one-size-fits-all approach could work. It was in effect a number of one-on-one lessons in a group.

I felt that if I didn’t give feedback to each person I wouldn’t be doing my job. The atmosphere was professional and friendly, but my observations were honest. Always at the start of the workshop I would have each person play something so I had a grasp of where each person was coming from, and my comments would be tailored to each person’s individual situation.

I’ve spent a life in Pipe Bands, and those have generally been, one could say, group lesson environments. You’re trying to get everybody playing better, but at the same time it’s an ongoing thing, and you don’t want people to get discouraged and quit.

In band situations the good leaders follow the maxim of being tough on the group but praising individuals. They never single out an individual for criticism in front of others.

After rehearsal, out of earshot of others, the leader might take an individual aside and give pointers on how to improve.

Re: The tough approach to music teaching v trying not to be too hard?

I’ve been teaching a weekly intermediate level, tip-supported, tin whistle group class on Zoom for a few months.

I don’t teach the whole group at once. I alternate between open form style where I work with anyone on the event who has a specific question or a skill or tune they are struggling with, and topic-based events where we talk about for example, dance styles, ornamentation/variation, or my approach to learning and playing airs. I’m not teaching basics, the class is for people who already play some tunes on the whistle and want to improve/refine their playing.

If someone has a specific issue in their playing and make a request for help, I’ll work with them to correct or improve whatever they are struggling with. Everyone else on the event watches the interaction. My style of coaching is always about identifying the gap between where the student is and where they need to be and gently nudging them incrementally in the right direction. I try to keep it light and fun and always acknowledge what the student is doing well as well as where they need to focus their attention.

In my experience with being a student in many group workshops, I always found this “master class” style far more effective and enjoyable than the “circle of death” or “everyone at once” style, so I chose it as how I teach my own workshops.

I’ve had nasty music teachers in the past as a child and they do more damage than good. One can provide honest feedback and coaching about specific issues and how to fix them without denigrating the player.

In my experience of group teaching, with children there tends to be more interaction between the students and they can learn from each other as well as the teacher. Children are also less inhibited about “performing” in the sense of being put on the spot and asked to play something. Re: The tough approach to music teaching v trying not to be too hard?

Been a teacher for a living for over thirty years--not music, but a teacher of adults, especially if you count 18 year olds on the low end of the adult range.

Being “tough” for the sake of toughness is foolish and c0unterproductive. The honest truth is students respond differently to different approaches: some want focused and direct criticism, some want a lot of encouragement, some love a theoretical framework, some hate that and need to get their hands dirty in real experience. It’s just the plain facts. Your Marine Boot Camp approach might work brilliantly for some people and might turn others off music forever. Your warm and fuzzy love might leave some students disgusted with their lack of practical progress. You have to meet the students where they are and then try to intuit what’s going to be effective.

If it was easy everybody would be good at it.

My philosophy is “learning is a process of passionate, rulefull play,” like playing a sport you love. It has rules and takes work. But you have to ignite a sense of entitlement: they are entitled to learn this. It’s not some treasure they are forever inferior to: human activity belongs to humans and they are entitled to pursue it, following its rules.

Re: The tough approach to music teaching v trying not to be too hard?

I have to say that as someone who went through a classical conservatory program, this is a bit of an odd conversation from my point of view (which is obviously not everyone’s!). We had masterclasses (one student up in front of everyone being taught by a teacher, often a visitor of some renown) all the time, not to mention my teacher encouraged us to visit others’ lessons to watch and hear. Sometimes the instructions were essentially “tear it all down and start again,” and yet it was still a hugely valuable experience for both the student being taught and the observers. The idea that other students can’t learn from instruction of one student is ridiculous; that’s something that happens all the time!

If I am doing something wrong the last thing I’d want is for someone who knows better NOT to tell me! And if I’m in a class, I assume that I and everyone else in that class are there because we’re doing things wrong, or at the very least not quite doing things right. If we were perfect, why would we come to class? The idea that someone would not want to correct a problem in a class just seems absurd to me.

HOWEVER, I will say it’s all about how you do it. Making it personal, making mistakes a moral failing, forcing people to do things they expressly do not want to do, berating them, intimidating them, etc., all of that is horrible teaching. It is also unfortunately very common, especially in the classical music world. Someone mentioned “bedside manner,” which I think is a great way to look at it. A doctor can have the best knowledge and skills in the world, but if they’re a jackass they’re a bad doctor. Teachers should have the skills and bedside manner to know how to correct bad habits, even in front of a class, without shaming and embarrassing a student. I suppose for a really shy/self-conscious student this may mean doing so in private, but there’s always, always a way to do it without simply leaving a bad habit to fester.
