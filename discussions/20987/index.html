↳ Posted by geoffwright on March 18th, 2009.

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

I think it’s also important to work on your current tunes. A teacher once told me “it’s not how many tunes you know, it’s how many tunes you can play well”, and we’re often rushed as beginners to build a bigger repertoire, at the expense of the tunes themselves.

So there’s a balance there. If you’re learning enough tunes that you don’t feel you’re playing the new ones well, then slow down and work on the ones you know already. Speed will come with experience. One of the best ways to get faster at playing a tune is to be able to play it precisely at a slow speed. Repeatedly. It can be fun to push yourself to play faster, but don’t play faster than you *can*, or you’ll be practicing bad habits…

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

If your focus on speed, you’ll end up with 6 tunes that you can play fast.

If you focus on building your repertoire, you’ll end up with a better ear, better technique (enabling you to play fast if you choose to), a broader palette of ornaments and variations, a better feel for the music…. and more tunes.

Of course, it depends how you learn your tunes - if you learn them all from sheet music, it won’t do anything for your ear or for your ‘feel’. Learn as much as possible by ear, no mattter how much of a struggle it may be (unlike some people, I am not an anti-sheet-music crusader, but beware of confusing *the notes* with *the music*. The dots are a useful tool for filling in details that an untrained ear might not be quick enough to pick up - nothing more).

Also, don’t focus on building your repertoire just for the sake of haing a big repertoire - If you try to learn too many tunes at once, you’ll end up with a lot of approximations. Learn enough tunes to keep you interested -learn the tunes that interest you - but focus on each tune enough to learn all the notes properly. Then again, don’t feel you have to learn each tune perfectly in one go - half-learn a tune and then finish learning it 6 months later, if that’s what takes your fancy, so long as you know the difference between half-learning it and learning it properly.

Ignore all the above.

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

If you can play those 6 jigs at something approaching session speed, you certainly ought to be moving on to learning some more tunes.

In fact, the more tunes that you know, the easier it is to learn new ones, as the same runs and phrases occur in different tunes.

Reels however, are the hardest genre. You might like to learn a few polkas or hornpipes first, before moving on to those.

When you are ready, these two are good starter reels on a whistle.

Sally Gardens (G-Maj)
Merry Blacksmith (D-Maj)

I wish you well, and good luck with your learning process

But keep in mind that if you play a tune at a session at miles below its natural speed, you won’t be very popular! So the answer is to do both - gradually work your existing tunes up to session speed, and learn some new tunes as well.

Learning not a linear process, but you will find that it’s worth it in the end!

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

No one form of tunes is harder than another--reels can be as readily learned by beginners as any other type of tune. Just pick tunes that sit well on your instrument. (Some good reels for starting out on whistle are Terry Teehan’s, Greefields of Rossbeigh, and Dunmore Lasses.)

And if you’re playing Irish trad on whistle, there’s little point in playing scales and exercises. Just play tunes--they teach you all the fingerings you need to play this music, and they’re more fun to play than scales..

zippydw, yes it can be frustrating to be unable to play at session speed. Which is one area where playing in sessions before you’re ready can be a real detriment. If you push for speed before you’re really capable, you may develop bad habits--leaving notes out, favoring speed over lift or nyah, scrambling the twiddly bits, etc. The worst is not learning to listen closely to what you’re playing. This music is all in the nuances, and speed without nuance kills the tunes. Go to sessions to listen and learn and enjoy the craic, and play at home till the speed issues resolve themselves. My $0.02.

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

Thomaston got there with the good advice, so I’ll only second it: get thee to the session, listen a lot and maybe put together a set of tunes in case you’re asked to play. If the session’s any good, they’ll be hip to the fact that you’re not up to speed and play to support you rather than to beat you. If they groan at the Kesh or start pushing the tempo on you, they’re probably not the people you want to play with at this stage - not that they’re bad people, but playing with them won’t help you at this point.
But listen anyway, and also listen to recordings. When you find that you can identify a tune as it comes by, that’s a pretty good indication that you’re in a good state to learn that one. You can learn a tune from the page without hearing it in your head, but it’s much better to go as you’re doing.

If you were my student, and I had to answer your original question, I’d tell you to develop a daily practice regimen, probably something lincluding a few simple exercises, runing through each of your known tunes at a slow pace a few times, then playing through the tunes that you’re trying to learn, whether that be with the sheet music or with whatever cues you’re using to recall them until they’re in your brain. Then get up and stretch and put down the whistle. The whole thing should be under half an hour. You can play after you practice, but be sure to keep the two separate - play is ludic, practice is intentional, confusing the two is a great way to impede your progress.

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

To expand on the practice regimen:
1) exercises are not strictly necessary for technique, as Will says, but they can be useful at the start, both to help you coordinate finger and breath and to get you mentally into “practice mode”. Try scales against pedals: DD DE DF DG DA DB Dc Dd and back down, same thing also from E, G, A. B and cnat if you want to get wild. Also the inverse, DD ED FD etc., and so forth. With tonguing, then without, etc., etc.. Make up a list of variations, work through the list over the next month or so, two or three per day, if you find them helpful

2) in my experience, practicing fast doesn’t lead to playing fast. Practicing slow does. To play fast, you have to be absolutely sure of where your fingers are going to go next, and not just sure in your fingers, but sure in your ears. You have to hear fast - if you play faster than you listen, that’s when you get lost. But if you practice faster than you can hear, all you do is practice losing the tune. So play slow. If you don’t feel stupid, you’re not playing slow enough.

3) to build repertoire, keep one or two tunes on the burner at a time. If you like, you can read through tunes to get a sense of what they sound and feel like, but keep one or two that you’re putting in your head and focus on those. When you can play through one without cues at a walking pace, move it to the “known” list and pick another to learn.

4) practice should be intensely focussed time in which you’re developing specific things. You can’t do it for very long, if you’re doing it right, and if you don’t do it every day you’re not going to get much out of it. Play, on the other hand, can be loose (though it can also be very intense) and is more about discovery than about learning any one thing.

Or at least, that’s roughly what I tell my students, and they pay me to say it. You haven’t paid, so you can use it or ignore it as you please. Best of luck.

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

Don’t use a metronome, no need. Just never play faster than you can, it’s a simple rule. People will much rather play with you at a speed that is comfortable for you rather than a speed that is comfortable for them, but you mess up.

The only goal you should set yourself is to improve. You can’t say learn a tune a week because when can you ever say you really know a tune? I’ve been playing tunes for decades and still find new things in them.

What’s the point of playing scales when you can play tunes. Scales are just crap tunes.

How will you know if you are ready for something until you try? Your first attempt at a reel will probably be not too great, But you’ve gotta start somewhere. Don’t be put off by people saying they are hard. None of this music is hard, it’s one of it’s greatest strengths.

Breathing helps define phrases. breath wherever you want, just try not to breath in the same place all the time.

Re: Building repertoire or building speed

You don’t need to know all that mode theory stuff to play tunes.

I’m not keen on whistle/flute players who breath at the end of bars. The musical phrases of all the best tunes cross bar lines in inventive ways, let the tune dictate where the phrases start and finish, and get used to playing through phrases. Get used to doing things like taking a few top up breaths across just a few bars, then playing great chunks of the tune right through. Patrick is right in that there are no hard and fast rules, but if anything, mix it up. The one thing that taking breaths at the end of bars - especially at the end of parts - does is to interrupt the flow of the music.

Patrick’s points a, b, c, are good though. And I would go so far to say that taking a breath should be adding to the rhythm of the tune.

However, unless you are playing for dancers, there’s no need to ascertain your speed. It’s simple, if you can’t play it, it’s too fast. Slow down until you can play it.
