Added by Fiddle Aunt on September 28th, 2014

Is it worth learning tunes that you dislike?

I have decided tastes in traditional music. I often just don’t like tunes that a younger player like my fiddle teacher thinks have the wow factor. I like tunes to have marked Celtic features (rather than European or American) and I like them to be or sound traditional, rather than include ‘jazzier’ elements. And as I’m a new fiddler but a Third Age person, there doesn’t seem a lot of point in labouring against the grain to learn tunes that I dislike.
But am I wrong? What do you think? Does the fact that a tune is useful in sessions - or for dancers, or as a way of learning a particular technique - mean that you’d be prepared to spend hours learning it, even though you hate it?
Is it good to ignore your personal tastes - a case of ‘eat up your greens’?
Or is life too short?

Thanks in advance for any replies.

Re: Is it worth learning tunes that you dislike?

I have recently taken on a new mandolin student, in a similar stage of her life to yours. She has previously had some lessons with another (and by several accounts, very good) teacher, who, himself, learned from a classical player and uses this as a basis for his teaching (although he is an accomplished traditional and bluegrass player). The student felt that all the tremolo exercises and uninspiring studies, that the teacher was insisting she played, were a waste of her time and energy, when all she wanted to do was to learn to play traditional tunes. My approach to teaching, by contrast, is to teach the music tune-by-tune, paying attention to any technical issues as they arise. I admit that my teaching capabilities are very limited - having played mostly Irish traditional music for 20 years, I would not claim to be an ‘authority’ (I use the term *very* loosely) on any other kind of (even then, I would not put myself in the same bracket as anyone who has grown up with, or lived among traditional musicians). But the students ambition is, having heard my playing, broadly speaking, to play like me (an eminently achievable goal). Undoubtedly, with the benefit of hindsight, there are a few things I would have done differently when I began playing, but beyond that, why would I want to teach her any differently from how I taught myself?

Whilst I have no doubt that the said classical teacher’s approach is valid, for those interested in classical - and, perhaps, bluegrass - mandolin, much of it seems superfluous to playing traditional music. It could be argued that it is best to be technically equipped to play whatever style of music might take your fancy - a fair point - but if you know exactly what you want to play, then you might as well strip down the technique to the bits you will need for that music.

It seems to me that classical teachers (Mollie: you don’t say your teacher has a classical background but, reading between the lines…) often like to have their students playing pieces that are a bit beyond their technical ability - great for developing technique and, maybe, for producing great musicians eventually, but not, imho, for getting the best music out of you *now*. If you don’t like your teacher’s choice of tunes, perhaps suggest some you *do* like, that you could use some help with. If he/she doesn’t like that, perhaps you should find a different teacher - or it’s time to go your own way.

Re: Is it worth learning tunes that you dislike?

The short answer is yes it is. Much of what this music is all about is social interaction and that involves sometimes sublimating one’s own preferences to support someone else’s. I’ve been known to watch what some would call “family friendly”(read incredibly dull) shows for no better reason than to keep company with people I like. No, that doesn’t mean I have to agonize over a tune I hate but it does mean I’ll try and get a passing acquaintance with one I’m not very fond of (i.e. the Butterfly). Or I just might find some way to make them more interesting…same example. Either way I don’t feel compelled to bring my A game..just play it well enough to not embarrass the group. Of course it’s really OK to just sit out some tunes. Just be careful to not rain on someone else’s parade. It’s also OK to really get into, and get the most out of, tunes you just gotta know.

To paraphrase Barry Foy in his thoroughly enjoyable book…if you only learn tunes you like you’ll be doing a lot of playing by yourself. Maybe I’m missing something here but I understand the concept of the session to be a mixed group of people with a similar appreciation , if not ability, gathering to play tunes together. It’s not a show band and not sequential solo performances.

So yeah, it’s good to learn tunes you don’t like, avoid the ones you hate, and put the group first. As one wise man told me..“ that’s not a dull tune, you’re just playing it dully”. Should we ever play together I’ll do my best to play the tunes you like with you and maybe you’ll do the same for me.

I’m curious about a couple of things. What are some of the “challenging” tunes and why are they “challenging?” If this has anything to do with a “wow factor,” as you mentioned earlier, I’d question the teacher’s ability to help you much. Tunes can be challenging in any number of ways -- some for their subtlety; others for tricky parts, like the tune Catharsis. So I would be curious about what you’re being challenged to do, and whether that fits your musical taste and goals.

Also, realize that many tunes will appear dull and boring if you’re learning them from notation, from a book. Hear a good fiddler play them and they can turn into incredibly beautiful music. So I’d ask the teacher to demonstrate, or to point you to a good youtube or two. If you’re not inspired, try something else for now. but realize that five years from now, with plenty of tunes under your belt, you may hear the same tune again and suddenly love it.

↳ Posted by Kenny on September 28th, 2014.

Re: Is it worth learning tunes that you dislike?

In my view an important benefit of learning Catharsis is that it teaches you to control the challenging cross-rhythms in the B-part, introduces some interesting cross-string bowings and fingerings, and in so doing will have the knock-on effect of helping you cope with other tunes that have off-beat accents. The other benefit of course is that it is a thoroughly enjoyable tune that always goes down well when played well. Unfortunately, as usually printed, the cross-rhythms and triplet “feel” in Catharsis’s B-part aren’t all that obvious because the 4/4 notation tends to point the player in the wrong direction - what better reason for learning it by ear! See my fairly recent comments on Catharsis in the Tunes section where I suggest altering the 4/4 notation to something that usefully brings out the correct rhythm. Remember that the eighth-notes (quavers) throughout the tune always have the same length, despite what my altered notation may “appear” to suggest!

Short answer to the original question:
Yes and no.

I was about to list three kinds of tunes before I saw Creadur’s post. Oh dear, he wanted you to learn Catharsis? Sigh. I agree with Ergo---not an appropriate tune for someone who’s first learning. And personally, I share your taste. I have intentionally NOT learned that one because it’s a bit too trendy for me---not the kind of traditional tune that I prefer.

Re: Is it worth learning tunes that you dislike?

Students might go through all sorts of travails on their path towards mastery. If you are sure where you want to get to, then side trips are not necessary. Take them or leave them as you wish.

People play sessions for different reasons. A shared experience of playing with friends is often an important part of that. I have noticed that some of the very best players have a goal that is a bit different -- it is about sharing the best music with friends. There is a subtle, but important difference. I was once struck by a comment made by Kevin Burke, who was talking about some of his experiences with sessions when he was younger. He played with some very fine musicians, and he would sometimes be reluctant to join in. It wasn’t that he couldn’t play, or that he wasn’t good enough, but rather that he didn’t think he would be adding anything to the music. There is a very important truth in this observation. Sometimes it can be good fun to play along with your friends. But if what people are there for is to enjoy the best music, then you might think about whether you are adding or subtracting by joining in. For example, a quiet mandolin might sound best of only one fiddle is accompanying, but the charm is totally lost when it is five fiddle, three flutes and a box. The key is listening, and a shared appreciation of the best sound. More is not always better -- and, in fact, usually isn’t.

I don’t see much point in adding something you don’t like to the mix. And so, it seems to me that if you can’t find something to enjoy in playing a tune, then don’t play it.
