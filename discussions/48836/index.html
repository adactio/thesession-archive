Your funeral sounds great! 😉

I’m not having one, though it took the pandemic to make me realise this is a real option. By the time the lockdowns started happening, my parents’ generation had reached the stage where none were aged less than 88 and some were in their mid-90s. We ended up having four non-funerals, and once you’ve got over the (mostly imaginary) lack of respect hurdle, it’s quite a nice idea.

Not having to face a funeral service made us talk and think about the people we’d lost in a different and much less stressed way. Some time later, I asked around how people felt about it; one sibling and three cousins said they and their partners had changed their minds about wanting a funeral for themselves. Some plan to leave bar money for a wake instead, the others don’t mind either way.

I might leave a set list too…

Re: My funeral.

A funeral is not for the benefit of the deceased but for those who are left behind. It is important that they can come together, not only to remember the deceased but to remind us of the importance of friendships and how our lives touch those of others.

Whether or not that involves a religious service is entirely a matter of choice, and if the deceased or their family are not religious themselves then there seems no point in just going through the motions. The last two I have attended have been humanist ceremonies. One was conducted by a friend of the deceased, the other by a trained celebrant (who was also a friend), and they were both comforting and life-affirming.

One was for a former bandmate, and I had the odd experience of playing dance tunes with other former bandmates and other friends as he went through the curtain, and then as the rest of the congregation left. This was followed by a grand session in the pub. An entirely appropriate way to send of an old and dear friend.

Call it a wake if you prefer, but to say you don’t want them to have a gathering to say goodbye is unfair on your friends and family who might value the opportunity. If you wish you could specify that no one should feel obliged to attend if they don’t want to.

Sort of off-subject, the more I hear about it, the more I like the S.American “day of the dead” concept.

The idea is each family keeps a small photo of deceased friends and family members and, one day a year, they bring them all out and display in the lounge and tell stories/remember those people in a happy/reflective way.

Re: My funeral.

As I’m getting on a bit now, going to others’ funerals is becoming an all too regular pastime, and a good number of them have been musician friends. It’s quite eerie to walk into the crematorium to the sound of your deceased friend’s singing or playing, though of course, quite appropriate that his or her music should be heard. Some who have been fighting long-term illness, may well have planned which music they want at their funeral: others, whose deaths were perhaps sudden/unexpected perhaps haven’t thought that far ahead, so it’s up to the family to decide what they think the deceased liked. For religious funerals, there are certain hymns that come up again and again: I don’t want any of those, however well-loved they are by some: I chose a lesser known one for my Dad’s funeral, which nevertheless seemed pertinent to his life. But more and more funerals are of the secular/humanist type it seems.
And how often have I observed that the eulogy often tells you far more about the deceased person than you ever knew while they were alive, no matter how close a friend or relation they were - so I would say that is one very good reason for having funerals. I wrote the eulogy for my Dad, using some notes he’d jotted down about his younger years in wartime Britain and on active service abroad: stuff he never talked about. But of course, there were the personal reminiscences too. For my mother’s I searched through 100 recordings of a particular tune my parents both liked, many of them horrible, before finding “the one”.
So for my own: probably humanist, cremation, some nice music - perhaps some recordings I’ve made with friends, as well as some class tracks from my favourite professional players, and a jolly good wake, with lots of singing and playing.

Re: My funeral.

“If circumstances are such that having a service for me would primarily create stress and pain for my loved ones, then please skip it. ”
This is why I think a funeral/visitation can be quite cruel. The bereaved closest to the deceased must, while in the depths of despair and shock and possibly wanting nothing but to hole up somewhere and grieve, plan a social gathering, receive and play host to other people, hold it together and make nice, and manage/assuage the grief of *other people* who may not have been as close to the deceased. Seems pretty terrible to me.

(You’d think it might make sense for a funeral to be more like a baby shower: something put together and hosted *for* the family by other friends and family, and take some of the burden off them. They’d still have to be involved, in terms of things like choosing a casket, readings for a funeral, etc., but at least they wouldn’t be stuck with the minutiae of things like “when is the church available” or “how should we do the lunch after” or receiving other guests.)

There’s also the thorny issue that if you have no close family, who *does* organise it?
