Re: Clawhammer Banjo for Irish music?

Hmmm, that chap is not only a great player but a great teacher!

We had a lovely clawhammer banjo player back in Canberra, by name of Tim Shopen, since deceased. He was a linguist at the ANU during the day and did some great work involving aboriginal languages. Tim became interested in Irish music, but found jigs in particular challenging in the clawhammer style. One day he contacted me in great excitement, exclaiming that he’d found the answer to playing Irish jigs clawhammer, but needed my help. Intrigued, I invited him over.

When he arrived he showed me his great find. It was a fingerpick used by sitar players. It was made from thin hard wire and sat upon the first (pointer) finger. There was a loop that passed around the finger, and a section of wire that passed over the tip of the finger, with a sharp included angle formed at the tip. Essentially, it was a double sided fingernail. Wearing it, you could play downstrokes, as the chap above demonstrated in classic clawhammer style. But you could also play upstrokes, and, more importantly, you could instantly change, to get the tickety-tickety rhythm of double jigs or the dahde-dahde rhythm of single jigs. I ended up making quite a few for him over the following years.

You can see them on the web by Googling “mizrab”.

Re: Clawhammer Banjo for Irish music?

I’m a pretty serious clawhammer player, and I play ITM and STM fiddle as well. A few observations:

1. Dave Hum, Tom Hanway and Jonny Toman are not playing clawhammer style in the posted videos. They are playing 3-finger style. Very, very different.

2. If you want to check out what can be done with clawhammer and “celtic” tunes, look to Ken Perlman. I’m not a big fan of what he does with STM and ITM, but he is very, very good at what he does.

3. If you want to check out the very best of clawhammer, check out Adam Hurt. He’s my hero.

4. As has been discussed/argued at astonishing length in threads on the Banjo Hangout, from a technical standpoint “celtic” tunes can be played in clawhammer style. Whether the style is truly adaptable to ITM and STM sessions is another matter entirely. Among other difficulties, clawhammer players play in different tunings depending upon the key. Re-tuning mid-set in ITM is difficult, and would be pretty annoying to other musicians. There are pretty sound reasons why you just don’t find clawhammer in ITM and STM sessions.

5. In the view of many, myself included, the strength of clawhammer is the use of the ringing 5th string. Melodic clawhammer dilutes that strength, giving rise to the conclusion of many that tenor is really the right tool for the job in ITM and STM.

I play, and love, clawhammer. And I play, and love, ITM and STM fiddle. For session purposes, I don’t mix the two. YMMV.

Matt

Kellie--

I played clawhammer banjo for many years and agree that it is very relaxing (check out the playing of Kyle Creed). For Irish music, the clawhammer player who has worked the hardest at making clawhammer banjo work is Ken Perlman, who has written at least one book entirely of tunes in the Irish tradition (most of his books, and there are many, have some Irish tunes). Ken plays a custom-made 5-string banjo that is a little heavier duty than most and enables him to obtain a bit more volume. My own feeling, having tried it some (including working up a clawhammer version of the Belfast/Sweeps hornpipe), is that it doesn’t work very well and my advice to you is to enjoy clawhammer music in its tradition (Appalachian) and stick to the whistle and flute for Irish music. That’s what I do. But check out Ken Perlman’s books.

Terry, I’m sorry to learn that Tim Shopen has passed away. I was a linguist and admired his work and learned much from it. It may be that his discovery of the sitar pick would help with the 5-sring banjo and Irish music, but I also think that the light strings, normal use of the banjo without a resonator and the tunings work against it.

Re: Clawhammer Banjo for Irish music?

Yes, I have used clawhammer banjo and clawhammer techniques for Irish tunes.
When I saw the subject I expected a lot more negative comments. I am glad to see all the encouraging responses and the examples.
About 20 years ago I played in a pretty decent Irish/Scottish band.
I played a 1920’s vega open back 5 string banjo, clawhammer style, and finger style.
Clawhammer style worked best on 4/4 tunes. I tended to play finger style on jigs, but there are ways to adapt clawhammer style to 6/8.
In both styles, I often played a more melodic style.
Melodic clawhammer can be a bit challenging at first (the thumb is often playing on the off beats) but it can lead to some pretty nice rhythmic expressions; and is fun.
There are some books you can get on melodic clawhammer. They will usually even have some Irish tunes.
Melodic clawhammer tends to need more arranging (like guitar finger style) than plectrum style, so may not work as well in a session environment. But if you can handle it, why not.

I found that double-D tuning(really double-C capoed) and open-G seem natural, so, no need to learn new tunings. D-minor works good on open-G, so a capo on 2 fret gets you A-minor.
If you play in sessions, you probably don’t want to use more that one tuning. Open-G worked the best for me. You can play in D or C on open-G so the Irish modes fit pretty well. And a capo on just the second fret gets you a lot of options (and open A and E strings).

I think that people with negative reactions may have bad experiences with drop ins. Harmonica blues licks or hippy drum circle percussions don’t fit into Irish/Scottish music. But there are some excellent Irish harmonica players, and Seydel sells off-the-shelf Paddy tuning diatonics (they couldn’t do that if there wasn’t a lot of interest). Unconventional methods have always been used in traditional musics. But one needs to understand what it is in the music genre that makes it what it is. For example, if you are playing clawhammer style in an Irish group, you want to be expressing Irish rhythmic structures and not introducing Appalachian rhythmic expressions. When playing an unconventional instrument, you may have to understand the musical conventions and style better than you would on a traditional instrument. You don’t have as many better players of your instrument to mimic. Also, each type of instrument plays ornaments differently. If you also play plectrum banjo, you will have to develop different ornament techniques. One way I played ornaments is to sweep the back nail of my strumming finger across three strings with the notes I wanted. Or, across two strings and hit the thumb-fifth-string. And, cuts and taps are pretty natural, but rolls are pretty difficult, or just don’t fit (for me).

I couldn’t tell you which is “best,” for only you can determine this. For me, after 50 years, I learned enough about myself and what I want from music to reconcile the dilemma. Overtly, we know generally that focused study will take you further in objective terms. Yet we don’t know what YOU want; perhaps neither do you at this point (this is the gist of critique of “youth” mentioned above, another being perspective). For me, there was no way to avoid assuaging curiosity - no matter how I tried to resist, it was futile - and it happens that I had to go through many instruments/idioms to find my way (i didn’t even know about wire harp, for example, until well into adulthood).

Joe Campbell said that in studying varieties of traditions he would never attain the experience of the monk or saint of one tradition. Yet, he pursued the path he did. Re: Clawhammer Banjo for Irish music?

Several terms have been bantered about in this discussion, e.g. frailing, clawhammer and melodic clawhammer. The song accompaniment idea is a good one, but understand that, if playing ITM or STM is one of the goals, then developing a strong drop-thumb technique is vital. I find that most folks engaging in ‘frailing’ are employing little drop-thumb, or none at all. Think Grandpa Jones, or Stringbean. The term is used loosely by some, so no doubt some ‘frailers’ have a strong drop-thumb component to their technique. Learning bum-ditty will be just fine for vocals. But bum-ditty w/serious drop thumb work is another level entirely that must be learned to play ITM and STM melodies. Kellie - you said you like Kyle Creed. He used a very strong drop-thumb attack. Follow his lead.
