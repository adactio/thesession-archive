I have learned by ear and from notation from childhood. I have found that, for me, both are equally efficient pathways to the way I actually perceive music at the most basic level, that is, how it feels. Not some metaphysical ‘it feels good, man,’ but how it feels in my hands. How different keys make different patterns on the keyboard or fingerboard, how the instrument and bow feel when the vibrations get going, etc. I am a strongly kinetic learner if that means anything to whoever reads this.

There is one exception to my second sentence that is very pertinent to this tradition: slow airs. They’re not really notatable rhythmically, at least not precisely, since there’s no steady beat. Every slow air I know I’ve learned by ear, and it’s the only way I’d recommend learning one. Not that I’ve ever actually seen sheet music to one…

Re: We can talk about notation later. This is strictly about careful listening & playing by ear.

I learn most tunes these days from the dots, and then memorise them.

But if I already know a tune, I can play it by ear. I discovered that I had this facility as a child mucking around on my Granny’s piano, and I believe that it’s widespread - I always remember a moment at the Junior Strings Orchestra when the teacher/conductor asked us all to play the theme to Z-Cars together on our violins (the Liverpool folksong ‘Johnny Todd’). We said we didn’t have the music, but he said, ‘Just start on the d string’ and off we set.

Since returning to the fiddle as a retiree, I’ve been asked a few times by Fiddle Guru to learn a tune from his mp3. I have done, successfully, but it takes me a very long time to get the new tune into my head, so for me it isn’t a productive work method. At one time I thought that the tunes I’d learned this way were more securely held than the ones I’d memorised, having first learned them from dots. But now I don’t think so. So there’s no advantage - for *me* - in learning tunes by ear, unless I just can’t get my hands on the sheet music. For example, I learned Lully’s ‘Loure des pecheurs’ from a soundcloud extract. :)

However, I use my ear in all sorts of other ways. I believe my facility helps me to learn the tune more easily - I am in fact not just memorising the notes, I’m remembering the tune and putting my fingers in the right place ‘by ear’. And I have no difficulty in playing the same tune by ear in a different key. For example, my dance tune variant of ‘Gin I were where the Gadie rins’ goes right up into third position in the B-section (written for piano), so I play it, but then I put it down a few notes into another key and play it again in first position - no dots were required.

Some players much prefer ‘by ear’, and some prefer dots. Some have a talent for one - some a talent for the other. I use both methods, as I’m sure many others do. Whatever works…

The important thing is for players to find the method that best suits *them*, in terms of efficiency, time, and enjoyment. As the Chinese proverb puts it: ‘Know yourself - know your opponent: in a hundred battles, win a hundred victories!’ :)

Re: We can talk about notation later. This is strictly about careful listening & playing by ear.

I’ve learnt quite a few tunes by ear now. Probably 20 or so.

When I started learning tunes - I used the music - not to sight read - but to learn the basic notes of the tune and play it as best I could.

As a total beginner - learning tunes that way helped me to work on a diversity of techniques. I.e. The basics of playing the fiddle.

There were perhaps a handful of tunes I was familiar with the melodies of at that stage - so I could work toward getting them sounding ok (but of course my concept of ok was like the baby speaking just similar noises),

Later in the first year I would work on making the tunes I’d ‘learnt’ sound right either by trying to play them with other people or by listening to recordings.

In the beginning I found it very hard to figure out why the recordings sounded so different to the tune I had in my hands and head. I knew my tune was wrong and incomplete and the recording was of someone who was a good player - but I just couldn’t work out what they were doing to make to tune alive the way it sounded.

Anyway didn’t spend too much time worrying about it - figured it’d work out in due time - and it did - the more I listened and the more tunes I learnt and the more sessions I went to - the more I could hear and grasp what other players were doing - not just roughly what they were doing but precisely.

I made attempts to learn tunes by ear alone throughout my second year of learning - but wasn’t all too successful.

This year has been the real breakthrough - I’m about half way into my fourth year.

Now I can learn tunes by ear and it really is no trouble at all. I still slow down intricate parts - but one or two run through a and I’ll have it.

Can’t say I found it scary as such. I went to my first workshops in Milltown this year. The particular tutor I had taught exclusively by ear - at that stage I’d done a few tunes successfully so I knew I could - but I was worried the difficulty of the tunes or speed of the lessons would be too much. It wasn’t - he was a great teacher - in particular emphasising to the class that learning by ear is critical to the music and despite what people think ( no matter what their age) everyone can do it if they simply try. Anyway we learnt 14 tunes that week.

Since then I had a day long work shop with Martin Hayes and another with Yvonne Casey in Feakle - both those players taught us tunes by ear - there was no option to get sheet music from them. Therefore if you’ve neglected to get into learning by ear you’d not be able to get the most from such workshops. That’d be an awful shame in of itself.

So that’s my story - long I know, but sure I’ve it written now.

“Make a recording in a medium you can play back at reduced speed. Invest in a quality digital recorder like a Zoom or similar device. Eulic Mac Aoidh”

I agree with Eulic. I use iLift - many here seem to like Amazing Slowdowner.
iLift has a newer update that can drop the speed to just 25% of the original (in addition to many other improvements/refinments over the initial version). To me, this is a good pace to learn to listen and play by ear.
If your a fiddler, Matt Cranitch has three books with accompanying CDs - the most recent of which “Irish Fiddle Tunes - 62 Traditional Pieces” is especially crisp and clear and played as the notation is written.

The most surprising thing happens when learning the tune at .25X original: The written music begins to “fade away” and one begins to anticipate play along without realizing that the notation is not being looked at.

By the way, new learning theories support the concept of very slow learning for complex mind body challenges like fiddling and even judo. The Great Pablo Casals taught his students - top players from all over the world - the cello only at very very slow tempo to great advantage.

Good books that discusses the concept amongst other brain plasticity topics : Norman Doiges - The Brain that Heals Itself and The Brain’s Way of Healing - Many chapters very useful and encouraging for adult music learners.

Re: We can talk about notation later. This is strictly about careful listening & playing by ear.

Though I’m a pretty good sightreader I learn tunes much more quickly by ear.

For years I would write tunes down… the result was notebooks full of tunes I transcribed but never learned!

To memorise it’s best for me to not transcribe a tune, not look at sheet music, but just play along with a recording.

At a session where there’s a fluteplayer, whistleplayer, or piper (for fingers to watch) I’ll have most of a jig or reel by the third time through, but I’ll forget it just as quickly. To make something stick I’ll have to play along with a recording over and over quite a few times.

As I’ve said before when picking up a tune the first thing I get are the places where the tune parks on a note, the roll-places. Arpeggios and scalar runs (both diatonic and gap-scale) are got quickly too. With many tunes there’s not much beyond these, save for the linking-notes which fill in any gaps between the rolls, runs, and arpeggios, which are the last thing for me to get.

About gap-scales, it’s far faster to pick up a tune if the tune’s scale is understood. I’ve marveled when somebody’s trying to pick up a tune and they play, for example, F# when the tune is in a gap-scale which does not have that note. They probably perceived that the tune was in D and figured that naturally F# is part of the D scale. This would be true except that many Irish tunes are in gap scales, so a D tune might go D E G A B d and have no F’s or C’s at all. Saves time if you notice that right away.

It took me years to get decent at playing airs. Definitely an ear-thing, I have to listen to an air many times through until the phrasing is in my bones, the underlying phrasing, not the specific timing of specific notes. Hard to describe: I’m not being a tape-recorder and reproducing the specific timing of a specific version I’ve heard. Rather, once the underlying phrasing is got the specific timing of each note isn’t all that important and will be different every time the tune is played. This was a breakthrough for me, when I finally comprehended this.

Gobby, many do not have a feel for the intervals between notes and thus, even though they can hum the tune from memory, they don’t know how to finger from note to note. They can finger the notes by knowing the note value (dots), but by sound, they simply don’t know where to go. You see, learning by ear is a two-step process: You have to know the tune but also know where your hands are going. Getting the hang of hearing a note in your head and automatically knowing how to finger that note does not come easily to everyone, but I consider it a vital skill no matter how well you read notes.

Bingo. Jerone; it’s definitely not an illusion! But it can be learned - or relearned, as in the case of musicians who have been mostly “paper trained” as my harpy friend Iris says. Like me.

You spent hours engaging in your love for music picking out tunes by ear only and your brain happily complied to that wiring. All children have that ability - they really do if started early enough. I did too, until I was put in a 5th grade class at age 10, where we learned to read music for whatever string instrument you chose. Violin for me; and what they called ear training from then on was nothing more than singing matching tones here and there. And I learned to play by matching the black blobs on the page to wherever my fingers were told to go. NEVER (sadly) by playing back what someone else played. So now my visual cortex gets the workout and that’s an entirely different pathway getting trained. I could have done both - and I do so in my studio now, but not then, for me.

For example, I can more easily hear and sing back what I cannot play “by ear.” After 50 years in my instrument, 30 professionally, it is truly a relearning process - hence a rewiring one. I have seen amazing “by ear” learning and those (like me) who cannot copy more than a few notes of anything just heard, that was played. And I’m referring to friends who were/are concert masters of City orchestras. The ones who could, had a teacher who spent time teaching the skill of only repeating back by ear, simply tunes - no dots - and so on along with learning notes well. It’s an ability we all have that gets atrophied if not used, even while other aspects of music training can be developed along the way.

You are writing as one who grew up picking up tunes by ear only. There have been many times I’ve learned tunes only from hearing others play them at sessions. Or first learned them then, before I heard them on recordings. Indeed, those tunes are probably still the core of my repertoire.

Re: We can talk about notation later. This is strictly about careful listening & playing by ear.

If I have a recording, or hear a tune in a session, I learn it by ear; if I only have dots, I learn it from dots. I don’t consider myself a good enough sight reader to play from sight in a session or performance (Why would I want to, anyway?), but I can generally play a trad tune from sight when browsing through a tunebook, say - provided it is in a ‘sensible’ key and doesn’t have too many accidentals, weird syncopation or unexpected twists.

I have played by ear since I was a child, working out melodies and chords on the piano and, although I was taught music theory, never learned to read music proficiently. So when I became interested in traditional music, learning tunes by ear (on mandolin) seemed the obvious thing to do. At first, I would *write them down* by ear, to have a reminder to refer back to, these being more ‘notey’ melodies than I was used to - but by the time I had finished writing and checking through the notation, I had usually learned it anyway and could quite easily transfer it to my instrument. But familiarity with the instrument has a large part to play, and as I got to know my way around the mandolin (which I took up at the same time as taking up traditional music), I began to miss out the notating stage and transfer the tune straight from ear to instrument.

The key to being proficient at playing by ear, I think, is learning quickly to recognise patterns of notes, not identifying every individual note. An interesting side effect of this, however, is that recognising and reproducing these patterns becomes almost an unconscious process, so that they never enter your long term memory; you can therefore *play* tunes without really *learning* them. The same can be true for transcribing a tune, where the notes are transferred from ear to page, but not remembered.

Re: We can talk about notation later. This is strictly about careful listening & playing by ear.

I have a really loose non-critical approach to learning a tune by ear. It actually feels like an act of composition when I am doing it. I am not concerned with getting at tune “right”. I don’t even know what “right” is or care. I see learning or playing a tune as a constantly evolving process. I like to listen to the tune briefly to hear what it sounds like and then attempt to “write” a tune that sounds exactly like it, if that makes sense. If I get all the notes wrong then - worst case scenario - I’ve inadvertently written a new tune. If I get all the notes “correct” then hooray! I’ve learned the tune. Usually it’s somewhere in between.

I’ve found that when I later compare my transcriptions to an actual transcription of the music - if available - my transcription (the way I am hearing it) tends to be much more sparse and simpler than what is going on. I like playing fewer notes…not more…so my way of playing a tune is going to be different than the next person. I also sometimes fill in certain bars or sections with generic patterns until I can concretely grasp what is actually going on.

It’s like anything else. You might mishear what someone says but in your mind you can come up with meaning for what was said even if it’s not what the person actually said. Like a misheard song lyric. It’s the same in music. It’s not a big deal. I never really know when I “know” a tune. It’s an ongoing process. I kind of see it from a jazz improvisatory mindset anyway. The tunes are just sketches or portals to your own music within.
