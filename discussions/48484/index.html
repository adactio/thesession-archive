How does one divide one’s time and interest at one’s normal regular sessions?

Consider one’s typical regular pub sesh. 8.30pm till about 11.25-ish is about typical in London. Anyway how much of the three hours is one actually cracking out a tune? How long talking to friends or new acquaintances? Standing at the bar? Having a smoke outside? Blanking idiots one does not want to engage with? Standing at the bar? Tuning up? Changing strings/ reeds? Flicking out gob from one’s whistle or flute? Standing at the bar? Going to the toilet/ bog/ John/ head/ lav/ kharzi/ gentleman’s/ ladies? Talking about one’s trip in August to relatives in Ireland/ the Fleadh Cheoil? Standing at the bar? ‘What’s the name of that second from last tune in that last set?’, discussing leaving to get the last Tube? Standing at the bar?

Re: How does one divide one’s time and interest at one’s normal regular sessions?

My sessions tend to keep the music flowing without too long a break in between sets. Rarely more than 4-5 minutes between sets. Part of this is that the punters get restless without any background music… Happy punters, happy pub, and all that… We do chat and joke, but most of the venues around here have waitress service even for the session, so people don’t go to the bar much. Nobody smokes around here anymore, so there aren’t the 15 minute breaks where the whole session heads outside… There are trips to the loo, conversations inside and outside of the session circle, and players coming and going, but the music keeps going for the most part. On any given 3 hour session, we will usually play between 60-70 tunes. I’ve been to sessions in Ireland where you might be lucky to get 25 tunes in 3 hours, which is fine, but for those of us that have traveled a long way to get there, it feels a little sparse on occasion…

The two sessions I attend run for three hours on a weekend afternoon. One is weekly and the other is once a month. The weekend allows people who work weekdays to get there. Re: How does one divide one’s time and interest at one’s normal regular sessions?

Kenny, I think you missed the lightheartedness I was posting with above. And house sessions can be wonderful, no argument from me on that.

But I’ve played in pub sessions where visitors outnumbered the locals, and some of the visitors were top class. And in pubs where different visitors showed up every week.

Yes, you can ignore the punters in a pub, unless they want to participate in the music or craic and then you make them feel welcome (unless they reveal session-wrecking tendencies). Most of the pub sessions I’ve played in are in pubs known for hosting sessions, so many of the punters are there for a listen. That doesn’t mean they expect the musicians to play to them like a band playing to an audience. And I’m not going to worry about the two gents watching the match on the telly or having a gab in a booth while I’m coursing through my favourite slip jigs.

Not everyone wants to host a party in their house. (Not every pub does, either, but enough do.) Not everyone wants strangers being invited by third parties into their home. Not everyone wants their children kept up late by the noise from the kitchen going till 11 pm. Or wondering who might be allergic to their dogs.

All I’m saying is that there are lots of good reasons for sessioning in a pub that have nothing to do with catering to the musical tastes of the punters. Obviously you don’t want to drive paying customers away, but neither do you have to pander to them.

Re: How does one divide one’s time and interest at one’s normal regular sessions?

It’s always a bit of a commercial project, yhaalhouse. Whether we like it or not. The pubs want -- or dare I say need -- us to generate some interest and business. I’ve played at sessions where if the ‘in-between-tunes’ chat went on too long (in one case, it was like three minutes, so not that long), the pub turned their PA on. That’s extreme and a case of the pub not quite understanding how a session works, but there has to be some kind of positive dynamic between the musicians and punters.

There’s a balance to be struck. I’m annoyed by the tune pouncers, the people who take every breath between sets to launch in with their tunes. Can’t someone else start a tune? Anyone? Do we have to hear you all night?

On the other hand, I get annoyed by sessions where the anchors are very disengaged from playing tunes, more interested in chatting or going outside for a smoke, but no one else seems willing to start anything, either. Usually these people are incredibly good musicians, far better than me. I’m a passing visitor, so I’m sure as hell not starting a set. But I’m not mates with anyone, so I’m just sitting there playing on my phone, not playing music (’cause there isn’t any) or engaged socially. When that goes on for a while, it sucks.

Re: How does one divide one’s time and interest at one’s normal regular sessions?

There are a lot of differences between sessions, and session venues.

In the UK the pub, rather than the home, is where we more often socialise and is where sessions have traditionally taken place. Most pubs are more than drinking dens (although some are) and often serve as a community hub (some are now community-owned). Many are now reliant on serving meals, and this may mean that the session cannot start until the diners have left.

In some venues there is an (explicit or implied) understanding that the musicians should provide some entertainment for the punters in return for use of the venue. That does impose some obligations to keep the music flowing. However at others it is provided as a service out of recognition that a pub is a community hub, although no doubt also the hope it will bring in a few customers.

My current session takes place in a separate room in the pub. Punters are of course welcome to look in, and some do, but we are playing for ourselves. The pub also makes the room available to a cheese club, a whisky club and other community activities. There are no expectations placed on us, other than that we should buy a drink or two. We play or chat as the mood takes us.
