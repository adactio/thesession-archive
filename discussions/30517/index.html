Sets and key progressions

Probably a well-worn topic, but what sort of method do you bring to stringing together tunes in different keys into sets? Most of the tunes our session group plays fall into traditional (or at least habitual) sets, but it’s sometimes a challenge to fit new tunes together on the fly. Of course, you can always play groups of the same key (boring) or major-relative minor-major, but I would be interested in hearing about other approaches. Is there a science to it, or do you just play what sounds right? (Or both?)

Re: Sets and key progressions

I usually play what sounds right to my ear, but, as you say, if it stays in the same key, it can be quite boring. With our Ceilidh Band, when we change tune in a set, we tend to put the next tune in another key : I’ve noticed that if you have the 2nd (or next tune) a fourth apart from the first one, it actually dynamises the thing, and the dancers love it : from D to G, for instance. Curiously enough, if you have a fifth (D to A,), it doens’t give as much lilt. Another “trick” is to have the next tune a tone higher : G to A … I often make sets that go : G-A-D-G for instance… or Am-Dm-G… but it really is a matter of taste… one of our favorite sets is a set of jigs : D-Bm-D-F#m… another one is Am-Bm-A-D… so… even if there are a few “tricks”, your ear and the fun you have are the best judges…

Re: Sets and key progressions

It isn’t so much what ‘key’ the tunes are in as what the notes are doing, as Donough says, and also the character, the nature of the tunes. You can apply all the rules and tricks you like, but if the tunes don’t go together they don’t go together.
A set is like one big tune in the sense that it;s not just a series of notes, it has to do something else, and each tune has an effect on the other tunes in the set.
You’ll know by the vague feeling of dissatisfaction at the end when something is wrong, and when you hit on a happy transition / connection, as with fiddlelearner above, you’ll know that as well, even if you don’t know why.
By all means use your fourths, fifths, minors, majors, etc, to experiment, but don’t ignore your intuition. The ingredients might all be lovely, but the meal might be rubbish.

If you find playing tunes boring, for what ever reason, you only have yourself to blame (unless it’s a crap tune of course … but then … you’ve still yourself to blame, for choosing a crap tune in the first place)

However, conversely, it’s probable that those advocating these dramatic key changes don’t realise how predictable and hackneyed they are. Up a tone, for example. I roll my eyes when someone does it. There’s a very funny web site, the truck driver’s gear change, that should cure you of it:

http://www.gearchange.org

"1. Who or what is a truck driver’s gear change?
Many writers and arrangers feel that when their song is in risk of getting a bit tired, it can be given a fresh lease of life by shifting the whole song up a key, usually in between choruses, towards the beginning of a “repeat-till-fade” section. You may have heard this technique informally referred to as “modulation”, but the correct ethnomusicological term for the phenomenon is the truck driver’s gear change. This reflects the utterly predictable and laboured nature of the transition, evoking a tired and over-worked trucker ramming the gearstick into the new position with his – or, to be fair, her – fist."

So …
Sets of tunes that don’t change key. What’s great about playing sets that don’t change key, or even deliberately playing tunes that have similar phrases in them, is that it opens the ears more to the differences than the similarities. And it’s up to the player to accentuate the differences and cross refer the similarities. It flies in the face of the modern malaise of low attention spans and revives that most important of musical devises - subtlety.

I usually throw sets together on the fly, figuring out what I’m going to play next halfway through the final repetition of the B part of the tune I am currently playing. Re: Sets and key progressions

Nikita, Michael, and Al beat me to it, and made pretty much all the points I would have brought up.

Yes going up a 2nd, say from D to Em or from G to A(m) is a formula that is often used, because it often works, but yes it’s the dreaded gear shift modulation which has become de rigueur in Christian music. (I do church gigs pretty often, and thankfully I have chanters in two keys and whistles in every key, to help me through these seemingly required modulations. At one recent gig every single song went up a step at the end.)

But for those who dismiss it (modulating up a step), just listen to the likes of Matt Molloy use it brilliantly in traditional Irish music.

Yes there are formulae which often work, but on the other hand 1) there’s nothing as exciting as the completely unexpected but brilliant segues that can happen at a session and 2) there are segues which work great, though they don’t seem to make any sense in a music-theory way. I think this is because Irish trad is still essentially a melodic, not a harmonic, idiom.

Anyhow one formula that always seems to work is

D > Em > G

Plug most any three reels into this and Bob’s Your Uncle.

That going up a 3rd (as in Em > G) is built into many tunes, for example the many tunes in Am which have a second part that’s more or less in C Major, or the many tunes in Em which have a second part in G Major.

One comes across a very different approach to set-building amongst older Cape Breton fiddlers, and that’s arranging sets of tunes which all have the same tonal centre, but progressively taking away the sharps, as in

A Major > A Mixolydian > A minor/dorian

You can throw such a set together of Irish tunes and see how it works, such as The Linen Cap/The High Reel/Star of Munster. What’s cool is, after hearing the first tune for it’s three times with the typcial A>E>A chords, is the first appearance of the G chord (and the note G) in the second tune, which uses the chords A>G>A. Then again, when the last tunes starts on the note C natural and the A minor chord it sounds really cool.

Sometimes it’s cool to segue into a tune that’s similar melodically, so that the transition is subtle and doesn’t hit the listener over the head with a frying pan so to speak.

Going from the jig Sport to The Hag At The Churn is an example.

Now, I spend most of my time in the Highland Pipe Band scene and there what has become very popular, nearly expected, is gimmicky segues such as playing a march but have the last couple bars shift into jig time as a segue into a jig, or have transitional phrases introduce the next tune etc etc.

One thing which has quickly become a cliche is the shifting, in the middle of a set of reels, from four-beat phrases to three-beat phrases, so that the thing has an underlying waltzlike feel. The first time I heard this done it was kinda cool but by the three hundredth time not so much. All of these gimmicks get really tiring after a while, and it’s nice just to hear the juxtaposition of nice tunes against one another for a change.

Re: Sets and key progressions

Well these Scottish Highland Pipe Band people probably don’t know what a “hop jig” is, or a “slip jig” for that matter… they usually call slip jigs “9/8 jigs” in Scottish pipe bands.

They call it a waltz, but whatever one calls it, it’s shifting to an underlying 3-pulse, each of the three pulses subdivided into two, “ONE and TWO and THREE and” whereas a slip jig has the three pulses each subdivided into three “ONE two three FOUR five six SEVEN eight nine”.

That video is showing probably the earliest occurance of this, which became an extremely common cliche in Pipe Band music. Still today you’ll hear many top bands doing it, though the “trick” is 20 years old and one might have hoped that it had run its course.

It can be done during a 9/8 jig like there, or during a reel, or whatever. In reels it’s done by cutting of the second half of every other bar, so in effect the meter is going 4/4 2/4, 4/4 2/4, etc. There’s a reel called The Crooked Bridge which is always played this way by Pipe Bands, but it can be done to any reel.

About Cooley’s Reel into Wise Maid, I never felt the “lift” you speak of, and would myself play them the other way round.
