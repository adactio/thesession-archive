Overlap between the Irish session, and Jacques Ellul’s concept of “technique”

I’m currently working on a paper discussing the nature of the Irish session, and how it seems to be particularly resistant to Jacques Ellul’s concept of “technique”.

In the 50s, Ellul began publishing books about the concept of “technique” (which he distinguishes from technology): a sort of single-minded devotion to efficiency and “the one best way”, present in all arenas of life, and elevated over all other human values. Specifically, I’m proposing that the Irish session’s format (and its content, sometimes) provide a musical avenue which is opposed to technique, because of its spontaneous nature.

In particular, I’m looking for: any specific aspects of session culture (or ITM in general) that resist (or give in to) technique, and any books/articles that may touch on these ideas.

If you have suggestions, I’m all ears. Thanks!

Re: Overlap between the Irish session, and Jacques Ellul’s concept of “technique”

Barry Foy’s book, Field Guide to the Irish Music Session, well describes the multifaceted, sometimes chaotic nature of most Irish sessions.

The degree of spontaneity varies from one session to the next, and sometimes in any given session as it unfolds. Some sessions see the same regular musicians week after week, while attendance at others is varied and random. Some sessions play the same (relatively few) tunes week after week, while others draw from a vast repertoire and you never know what tunes will get played. In some sessions, it’s customary to announce the tunes in a set before you play them, and others veer from one tune to the next with no premeditation and no forecast.

Even the “spontaneity” of a typical Irish session is within quite specific limits.

There’s something to be said for how a regularly scheduled concert pitch pub session of reels and jigs with the occasional hornpipe/polka/slide/waltz is a great example of “efficiency” ruling.

Re: Overlap between the Irish session, and Jacques Ellul’s concept of “technique”

Gimpy, thanks for the book recommendation. Good stuff, that.

Big Sciota, I appreciate your thoughts too. I agree with you that the session itself has homogenizing tendencies, and I hear you that it’s not a historically common event, in its current form. I, too, am considering how technique has affected/infiltrated ITM in general, and the session in particular. (Ellul himself described how the technical man we’ve produced is now “always and everywhere like his duplicate”, and that can be applied, sadly, to some things in ITM.)

I still maintain that there are a lot of ways in which sessions do resist technique. I have in mind considerations like the “passing around” of the lead…the fact that some sessions don’t have a paid/established leader…the fact that one often doesn’t know which tunes, keys, or how many repetitions are coming…the fact that it’s a living tradition that’s still changing…that it’s often open to all players of the repertoire, regardless of how far they’ve advanced…the fact that it’s at heart a participatory musical form (even for the “audience” in the room, at times…)

It seems to me like the session itself is not marketable (and that helps make it resistant to technique). Yes, people can and do use it to sell more pints. Yes, the greats sell concert tickets and albums. But the session itself….
I think about American square dances as an extreme sort of example. I suppose there were famous callers, and they probably sold records (we used them in my 5th grade class, I recall). But I don’t think there will ever be mass commercialization of that cultural form (ha, perhaps that’s why you don’t see much of it anymore). There’s not much to “sell”. Like the session, it is a form that is designed to be experienced, to be joined-in-on, to become a part of.

Gimpy, I think your example of pre-flight checklists is an accurate display of technique at work. However, what that example doesn’t display is technique’s tendency to assert itself, and nothing else, as the primary human value. In the case of pre-flight checklists, no one seems to complain much, because safety is such a huge draw.

But I like to share the example of teacher gradebooks. I’m a high school teacher, and everything is computerized. We can make “comments” in the students‘ electronic report cards. There is a “bank” of about 100 comments you can choose by clicking a button (needs to stay awake in class…doesn’t do homework…pays attention…good influence…etc). It’s extremely efficient, just clicking on a button, but it’s also not personal or humane. Most teachers do it anyway. I try to make a point to type out a different comment, often with the students’ name, to try to become slightly less of a mindless cog in the technical apparatus.

Re: Overlap between the Irish session, and Jacques Ellul’s concept of “technique”

Ah, the gradebooks are a good example, enough to give me at least a basic grasp of the concept and its pitfalls. I well recall my own “Has trouble with authority” in 2nd grade, but at least it was handwritten. :-D

Not to tilt at windmills, but I have a hard time seeing sessions as a single class of event. I never know what to expect, even at my own local sessions. Who will be there? What surprise visitor(s) will pop in? What ancient tune will bubble up, begging rusty synapses to fire after 15 years of inactivity? And traveling to other sessions is a crap shoot. I’ve sat in on drunken free-for-alls, teetotaling recitals; round robins; tunes only, songs and a few tunes; strictly local sets, pan-Irish, and world music; beginner slow circles, pros in warp speed flight; and anywhere from 2 musicians to more than 50.

So it might be more useful if your paper acknowledged the sheer range of session phenotypes and then focused on those that are most technique resistant. I tend to agree with you that a good session can encourage spontaneity, a large degree of personal freedom (of tune and even genre choice, musical style, etc.), individual expressiveness, and agency (players can come and go as they please, play or not, etc.). My main evidence for this is personal experience—after a good session, I feel more like myself, less like the person who sits at a desk 40 hours a week.

The part I’m less sure of is efficiency. For all their variables, inconsistencies, and potential for derailments by bongo-ers and dysmusical dictators, when they gel, sessions seem to me to be sublimely efficient if your goal is to spark craic and joy with a high ratio of tunes to pints among friends of an evening. To your point, Sanch, the goal is human joy and craic, not efficiency for efficiency’s sake, so perhaps that’s no longer “technique.”

jamesa, the range of the C/G concertina very closely matches the range of the fiddle and allows that range to be played using primarily the strongest fingers.

As far as C# and E, there are actually 3 mid-range C#s on my Wally Carrol Noel Hill model, which is a modified Jeffries layout. One low C# an octave below and one an octave above. It’s quite common to have 2 midrange C#s on instruments these days. There are also two 2nd octave Es (one on each side), one 1st octave E, and one an octave below that as well as two 3rd octave Es.

Re: Overlap between the Irish session, and Jacques Ellul’s concept of “technique”

“There’s a certain session wrecker in the area who has “resisted technique” for over a decade.”

Everywhere has a few of those, but I don’t think that’s what the OP meant. Jacques Ellul has done that French philosopher thing of taking a normal word and giving it a different meaning: "The totality of methods rationally arrived at and having absolute efficiency (for a given stage of development) in every field of human activity.”

"Ellul referred to technique as “the new and specific milieu in which man is required to exist,” and he offered the six defining characteristics of this “new technical milieu”:

a. It is artificial;
b. It is autonomous with respect to values, ideas, and the state;
c. It is self-determining in a closed circle. Like nature, it is a closed organization which permits it to be self-determinative independently of all human intervention;
d. It grows according to a process which is causal but not directed to ends;
e. It is formed by an accumulation of means which have established primacy over ends;
f. All its parts are mutually implicated to such a degree that it is impossible to separate them or to settle any technical problem in isolation."

Lest you think I know this stuff, I Googled it and pulled the information from this very helpful website: https://thefrailestthing.com/2017/11/24/jacques-ellul-on-adaptation-of-human-beings-to-the-technical-milieu/.

This one explains it in plainer English. https://www.steelsnowflake.org/post/jacques-ellul-and-the-digital-age

How that applies, or not, to trad sessions, I don’t know. He was complaining about society becoming increasingly in thrall to and subjected by technology and obsessed by efficiency, and through all that, we lose agency, individuality, and spontaneity. We’re chatting about it on the internet, and the largest database of tunes is this website, while there’s always some douche texting or checking Facebook during the session (it’s probably me). Some people probably drove cars to the session. From what I can gather reading the cliffnotes anyway, he’s not that relevant to the performance of weird little niche hobbies, but rather how people live their lives and whether they follow the sort of homogenised existence, driven and defined by tech, that we have in western culture.

One can even argue that modern technology has allowed instruments to be more in tune, and check their tuning digitally in busy pubs. If you listen to old tapes of London sessions in the 1960s, say, the sound is certainly different to sessions now.

To resist his concept of “technique,” you have to be like the guy who created a little empire of tents on the verge of the A9 (a major trunk road in Scotland) and lived there for years, totally off the grid (even though he was next to a busy highway), resistent to pretty much everything about modern society.
