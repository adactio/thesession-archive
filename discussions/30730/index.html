Re: Would it be easy for an ITM musician to play bluegrass tunes?

If you go to an established bluegrass session or, especially, if you join a bluegrass band, you’ll be playing a lot more songs than tunes. Tunes are often used more as a break from the vocals. In songs or tunes, though, there is usually some expectation of improvising and embellishing with bluesy hot licks. If you happen to be a fiddler, you’ll need to be able to play in the higher positions and to play lots of double stops. Currently, there seems to be increased emphasis on playing fast. That said, there’s certainly not universal agreement as to what falls into the bluegrass category. Most long-time players of either bluegrass or old-time have no trouble agreeing on what belongs where, but folks with little history in either genre tend to blur the distinction. So, I would have to say that bluegrass is generally more technically demanding than Eye Tea Em. Wether a particular individual finds it hard to cross over is, of course, all about the individual.

Re: Would it be easy for an ITM musician to play bluegrass tunes?

To the OP:

In ITM, we’re playing tunes as they were written - but with small variations mid-tune, during the 2nd round, the 3rd and so on (but everyone will still recognize the tune!). Bluegrass musicians usually play through the melody once, and then any solist takes parts like in a jazz band. At times, the melody is even barely recognizable.

Nothing wrong with either approach, but don’t expect to be able to jam along to reels and jigs - at best, the other musicans will play but a handful of tunes from the British Isles (Sailor’s hornpipe, etc.), and that’s about it. And bear in mind that a lot of material is based on the five-string banjo, the sounds is impossible to emulate on another instrument (so even if you’re advanced on your instrument, you still have to work it all out yourself).

One thing that the both styles have in common is SPEED. Make use of that when you improvise licks for an upcoming break.

Re: Would it be easy for an ITM musician to play bluegrass tunes?

As to the post title, it’s a very general question in a big broad field, so it’s hard to give a definitive answer.

I can only speak for fiddle, really. Many bluegrass fiddle tunes require the ability to shift positions and play double stops in them too, fluently and accurately (I acknowledge that this was mentioned before), and these technique would be out of reach of many ITM fiddlers. So, from a fiddling point of view, the short answer is : not easy, at least for some of the repertoire.

For the tunes that don’t require these techniques, and are mainly single note streams, then I don’t see any difficulty.

Re: Would it be easy for an ITM musician to play bluegrass tunes?

Yep. Get used to playing in B major and E major. You’ll also encounter a lot of cycle of fifths chord progressions, while ITM backers tend to be more scalar. Because of that, there will be more temporary modulation in bluegrass, because you’ll run into the V of V and the V of V of V quite a bit. So yes, the bluegrass tunes will be related to hornpipes in this regard.

Bluegrass etiquette is different than Irish seisiun etiquette, too. You don’t play the melody along with everyone else. Learn to comp or chop, and keep it very low when someone else is taking a break. This seems obvious, but a lot of people don’t have a clue!

Also, you’ll be playing outdoors more. So throw a can of insect repellant and suntan lotion in the case!

Re: Would it be easy for an ITM musician to play bluegrass tunes?

re the original query:

yes, it would be easy for an itm musician with proficiency at grasping and playing itm TUNES to grasp and play bluegrass TUNES, as in tune MELODIES.

it is the 80% of bluegrass playing which is NOT about tune melodies that would be the challenge.

i’m here using “bluegrass” in its genre-specific sense of the term rather than as a catch-all for “americana,” “oldtime,” and god knows what else.

in “bluegrass proper,” about 20% of your playing, and of the chops required, relates to the “tunes.” that is because in bluegrass, you’re playing the “tune” about 20% of the time, if not 10%. the rest of the time you are going to be either doing improvisational breaks, or---the vast majority of the rest of the time--doing backup and filler, rhythmic and/or harmonic. the backup might be complex. but it is not melodic.

so the chops bar in bluegrass relates to the improvisational and musical theory knowledge chops enabling you to do those things on the fly, and at speeds of well over 120 on the ’nome.

to paraphrase a knowledgeable poster on www.banjohangout.org, this is why bluegrass banjo hobbyists vastly outnumber actual bluegrass musicians. it is a high bar. according to those in a position to know, not an insurmountable bar at all, but one requiring a lot of theory and work.

none of this is to say an itm musician couldn’t clear the bar. but no, itm wouldn’t automatically transfer……

btw--“oldtime” as opposed to “bluegrass”---different deal, different bar.

Re: Would it be easy for an ITM musician to play bluegrass tunes?

Yes you can play bluegrass tunes if you play ITM. Playing bluegrass is a totally different thing.

As mentioned above there are stylistic, attitude, cultural and even political differences between the two musics and more variations within the musics than between them.

I know four master level musicians who can play both styles at a master level, everyone else I know (including myself) feels more comfortable with one or the other style but not both. and even with those four, the cultural aspect of the music in which they were not brought up in is a partial mystery.

But don’t let that stop you, the main aspect for most musicians in both bluegrass and ITM is the social one, even though many strive to be the best they can be in either style.

I grew up with bluegrass and have come to ITM later. In the bluegrass world there are plenty of good musicians, a few masterful ones and many social musicians who love the music and the fellowship but haven’t a clue about the music. I suspect the same is true of ITM.

There is no reason one can’t play the tunes in the style appropriate as long as you understand the amount of work and understanding it takes to reach a level in each music that will satisfy you. The more mastery of the style, the more work (and probably talent) and more understanding that you don’t know as much as you’d like to.

I just spent a weekend at the O’Flaherty Retreat with some of the great players of ITM. To be able to reach that level of musicianship requires a life long journey starting in childhood with an attention to great detail inspired by others. The same is true of bluegrass, you don’t get to be Kenny Baker or Byron Berline by reading a book.

On the other hand, Matt Cranitch is a pretty good bluegrass fiddler to my ear, JP Cormier is good enough to play with Jesse McReynolds (he is a great Canadian musician), and Tom Hanway played with Vassar before he turned Irish. It can be done even at that level, but most of the master level musicians I know are not that interested in doing the work and getting away from their music.

So the answer to your question, like most, is “it depends” - in this case on what level you want to play bluegrass. If it is just for fun and for social reasons, there is nothing stopping you except your ability to play music. If you want to be a master level player you have to learn a lot more than just the notes.
