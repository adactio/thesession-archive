Re: Flute half-holing masters?

Mary Bergin and Cormac Breathnach (both tin whistle) are pretty outstanding at bending and half-holing. The technique on whistle is very similar to flute. All competent players will be able to play half-holed notes (it’s not an exotic technique).

Is the real question here - do I need a fully keyed flute or will a keyless do the job just fine? Or by “mastered half-holing” do you mean can play fully chromatically on a keyless flute in any key?

A keyless D flute (or whistle) will play 97% of the ITM repertoire with ease. The few accidentals needed are easily achieved. A keyless D flute would be very challenging to play a wide range of other genre music (unless you can transpose it to play in the “comfortable” D/G/A keys).

With a keyed flute there is a hole - in the (roughly) correct acoustic position and of the correct size to sound the required note. Keyed notes for Eb, F-nat, G#, Bb, C-nat are all better (stronger, more in tune, easier) than any cross-fingered or half-holed alternative. And, with the possible exception of the C-nat cross-fingered OXXOOO the keyed variations are quicker to play than the alternatives.

E and A holes on wooden flute are smaller than the others. E & A notes have a wispiness and weakness that the adjacent notes don’t. Half holing either to get a G# (ok-ish) or Eb (poor) produces an even wispier vaguer note than the full hole A/E. Fine to get away with in a quick passage where the note isn’t prominent but they just aren’t solid substitutes for the keyed counterparts.

The B and F# holes are larger and they both half-hole reasonably well. The Bb is pretty satisfying and the F-nat isn’t bad either.

For C-nat you’re far more likely to cross-finger OXXOOO than half-hole the C# hole (except perhaps in a slow piece or an air where you want that “perfectly in tune” C-nat). The cross-fingered C-nat which is slightly out of tune with a keyed (perfect) C-nat is an idiomatic sound of Irish trad on the flute/whistle. It’s so wrong, it’s right. It is the tuning we almost expect to hear. So there’s no issue whatsoever (for Irish trad) with the funky OXXOOO. However in other genres this might not be quite as acceptable an alternative.

And then when it comes to half-holing there are two variants. They don’t produce the same result nor are they equally easy/fast in snappy passages. Half-holing down is easier than half-holing up. E.g. DOWN = A G# A versus UP = E F# G# A. Basically it’s easier to half cover an open hole (at speed) than it is to half uncover a covered one. For the half-holing up the hole is already covered and you’re looking to half-raise the finger to shadow the hole. The result isn’t as snappy a transition in the note - kinda gliding to the in-tune note versus nailing it. I find myself lipping/blowing G#, F-nat and Eb (in addition to whatever is going on with the fingers) to strengthen / tune them better. But on their best days they are just about good enough.

For Irish trad the NEED for keys is limited because 95+% of the time you are playing in D/G/A keys (and relative minors). The OXXOOO C-nat for key of G (the sound of ITM!) and an occasional or infrequent half-holed G# in key of A no big problems there. G# really isn’t needed that much (frequency of use) even for key-of-A tunes. For key of C you’re going to need to have those F-nat notes pretty spot on because there will be plenty of them and they’re important.

It’s possible to play repertoire in key of E, Bb, G#/Ab on a keyless D - but you’re going to be fighting the limitations of the instrument. For ITM where C-nat and a rare G#/F-nat is all you’re likely to encounter - keyless D still fine.

Amongst trad musicians - when they want to play a piece strongly in Eb, C or Bb (all common recording keys)… they are more likely to reach for an instrument IN THAT KEY (keyed or keyless) than to even bother playing it on a fully keyed D flute. You can hear this on recent albums by Matt Molloy, Cain Sweeney, Brendan Mulholland and many others. Change key = switch flute (or tin whistle). Joanie Madden or Mary Bergin are more likely to grab a C or Bb whistle where a tune is in C/F/Bb than to try to squeeze it out on a D-instrument. By using a transposing instrument you also get the solid bell-note (bottom) in the key you are playing (esp. for C or Bb flutes) without having to octave wrap the tune where the notes go below Low-D.

Part of the reason for using a transposing instrument rather than transposing on the instrument using the keys is that keyed notes don’t offer the same tone, balance and ornamentation techniques as open holed flutes. You just don’t get the same sound or feel.

It is much easier to find lots of examples of excellent use of (a) transposing instruments and (b) keyed flute playing accidentals/other keys than it is to hear (c) tunes played really amazing “out of key” of the keyless instrument.

Re: Flute half-holing masters?

There’s a long tradition of uilleann pipers playing more pipe-friendly versions of tunes which on fiddle, box, or banjo have notes outside the pipe’s normal gamut. You can hear whistlers and fluters doing the same thing.

For example there’s a reel (which I can’t remember the name of now) where fiddlers (etc) park on F natural in the 2nd octave for a beat and a half, while pipers (etc) instead play in quick succession f# d g d f# d.

The f#’s are often “popped” or “barked” and the effect is that what the pipes play doesn’t clash much with what the fiddles play.

On flute, in addition to the arranging tricks pipers do, breaths taken at the right spots can eliminate pesky notes.

In other words you’re not required to play every dot on the page, especially ones with a # or b in front of them.

The “possible” in my original comment for say Eb, Bb, F really should be read as not-impossible (barely). Re: Flute half-holing masters?

Some people have a reputation for playing keyless. Only a few spring to mind.
Seamus Tansey is one. He had all the keys and blocks removed from his famous Rudall Rose.

I can remember meeting him and being so proud to show him (I was 17 and so proud I had finally gotten a a keyed flute) six key Lehart. He quickly put me in my place saying what would you need them for if you’re playing Irish music.

Seamus was such a player but I can’t think of tunes in his repertoire where he needed keys but I’m sure there would be the odd half hole if you were looking.

When I met Eamonn DeBarra and Tom Doorley they were both playing keyless flutes (though some years later Dorley I met playing an Olwell 6 key).

It’s about confidence with the tunes. For years I chased fine keyed and even over keyed things. But after many years it became less important.
As a youngster I would shy away from tunes in A in case of a g#. Or mix G tunes with an F.

Eventually I learned all the tunes are there. Sometimes you can modify to not worry about a note or sometimes you can replace.

At any rate if you’re relatively new don’t worry about it. Surely took me 1/2 of the 30 years I’ve been playing tunes to realise it was time to stop worrying about my instrument and play the music how I could.

For instance Patsy Touhey and many of the American based one row melodeon masters just changed the tunes to suit. Such as just playing G tunes with a c# or glancing over or changing key to suit. Patsy rarely played a c natural though he had the option as he wanted everything so poppy and tight.

After all these years I worry less about the gear (fully keyed) and more about how I could make it work for what I have.

I can play gm tunes on a whistle but would be happier playing them with more options.

Now a days in most of what I play I would only miss the D# on the flute as halfholing doesn’t really work great . On whistle it’s fine.

Re: Flute half-holing masters?

And in early baroque period - instead of keyed flutes… the most common way to change key wasn’t to be a half-holing ninja… it was the corp-de-recharge. Basically a set of switch out bodies in differing tunings for the different keys. The other reason for the corps du recharge was to be able to play at the various A=415, 430, 400 etc. as “concert pitch” wasn’t a fixed thing.

The first key to become common in the baroque period was that pure impossible Eb. I attended a most interesting lecture in flute history by Philippe Allain-Dupré and they were so pre-occupied with that sweet bottom note that some of the Hotteterre flutes had BOTH Eb and D# keys - I jest you not.

Re: Flute half-holing masters?

It was Quantz who made the flutes with Eb and D# keys not Hotteterre and the corps de rechange wasn’t about changing key but changing pitch standard. They would have read the same music and called the notes by the same name. Though a flute pitched as low as 392 is equivalent to a flute with a C tonic when measured against A 440. It would have been regarded as a D flute in its time. It’s only a C flute (tonic nomenclature) when viewed through the lens of a modern 440 pitch standard.

Re: Flute half-holing masters?

Hi Aldon,

That tune has F naturals in common spots and I would play it on a keyless flute without a lot of issue. Some pipers who have access to a key might play it with half holes for the tone and the slidey intonation effect achieved using half holing rather than the key.

I’d suggest learning it on whistle first. Half holing seems easier on whistle. Once you’re confident you can apply it to the flute.

Don’t shy away from a tune like this on a keyless.

I remember getting my first keyed flute and it opened up a lot of tunes to me, but then being slightly annoyed at the person I regularly played with who promptly learned most of them on a keyless. In particular I remember the Yellow Tinker.

There are good deals to be found in antique flutes still. I would keep your keyless flute and find a cheaper antique to practice using the keys at home until you find the right keyed flute from the maker that suits you when you can afford it and when you’re playing style might begin to need it.
