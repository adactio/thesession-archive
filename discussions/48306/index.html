A couple of things that have helped me (remembering that everyone’s different) so far:
Play (sometimes) with a tuner, notice how increased bow pressure can lower the pitch of each note, use loads and loads of (beginner) rosin, understand that if you’ve just been for a run then mandolin playing is fine but fiddle is not. Sounds to me like you’re making great progress and will continue to, especially with your excitement for it.

I think a big thing that has helped me recently is to do a lot of experimenting with the bow on an open string. Just try every variation you can think of of speed, length, pressure, angle, grip, etc. to get a feel for it and notice the feedback of tone and tension/relaxation. Make nice sounds and ugly sounds, try expressing emotion through just the bow.

Also, if you’re open to lessons you can get some online and not have to wait. James Kelly is highly recommended, I have done one lesson with him so far and it’s made a huge difference. He focuses a lot on the body mechanics of playing and I was impressed with how much he could tell what was happening in my playing even over recordings/video.

Re: new fiddler would love suggestions / critique

Philip, you’re doing grand, well ahead of the curve for only a year in! Keep with it, play every day, and welcome to the lifelong riddle of how to make music with tree sap and horse tail. It’s a challenge at times but ultimately one of the most rewarding ways you can spend your time.

One thing to explore with bowing: when you play long bow strokes, your bow arm will bend and straighten at the elbow. You’re basically driving the bow up and down with the movement of your forearm. For short bow strokes, instead of moving your whole forearm, try playing them mostly by flexing your wrist. Now you’re driving each stroke with your hand while your forearm barely moves.

This makes for more relaxed, efficient use of your bow arm and hand, which really comes in handy for playing over long periods (like a 45 minute set of tunes at a session) and for playing at faster tempos.

You can get the feel for this with a simple pattern. On one open string, play a jig rhythm like this:

down----------up----------down ****** up----------down----------up
long-----------short-------short ******* long--------short----------short
forearm-------wrist-------wrist ******* forearm----wrist----------wrist

For the short strokes, don’t “lock” your forearm—you want to stay relaxed and loose. But do be conscious that you can leave it where it is while the wrist/hand moves the bow.

> Might be worth making a list

Yes, definitely. I keep a Google doc which just lists my current practice routine and it’s incredible the difference just consciously hitting the same things daily makes.

You were talking about your left hand - my only comment would be to lift higher with the arm (“push it into the bow”) which will give your elbow more freedom to control your hand. Watch the video back - see how your hand is “reaching” round the neck occasionally? That movement should come from your elbow, not your wrist.

If you want to train your intonation a bit, look up Sevcik Op 1 and try the first few pages with a drone - your intonation is already very stable but Sevcik will make it more consciously directed.

The subject of bow hold is one liable to provoke flame wars, but I’d like to see a little more curve in your pinkie and the first finger curling around the bow a little. The thumb is somewhere opposite the second or third finger depending on which god you worship. It feels weird and counter-productive until it doesn’t.

Lastly my personal hobby-horse, son filé. Watch this video and spend a few weeks doing it on every string, and then two strings at once:

Philip, not silly at all. Much of playing fiddle is counter-intuitive. It’s possible to suss things out on your own, but it’s often more efficient to ask a mentor or pick up tips from other fiddlers. We’re a “community of interest,” and most of us are happy to share what we’ve learned from other fiddlers.

When you hold the fiddle, your left arm won’t be static—there’s no single “right” position. Your arm and hand need to make continuous adjustments to be able to play on different strings, to shift positions (if you need to do that), and to execute certain techniques.

That said, the concept of having a “frame” for your hand can help a lot with consistent intonation, tone, comfort, effortlessness, etc. Here’s a two-part explanation (I like the presenter’s sense of a frame for “moving parts” and that she explains how to make this work for you own hands, given that we’re all different):
Part 1
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PTFzNp0jOyQ
Part 2
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mdPwTPCYHuc

The violin world holds a lot of good insights that fiddlers can use, even if we’re not trying to play Paganini caprices. Many generations of violinists have solved the challenges we face when we grapple with a fiddle and bow. YouTube offers a feast of decent video tutorials.

This is great progress for 1 year of play. I’m not a fiddler. So I can’t give you technical advice. I can give you some general advice from my experience playing with fiddlers and with Irish music in general.

This one always touches off a firestorm of controversy for some reason but here goes anyway:

1.). Because of your advanced age if you want to become one of the greats (yes there is still time) you need to be a fanatic and practice for long hours. If you can set aside a full weekend day or both if possible. It’s possible to play for approx 18 hours in one day. I do it all the time. Skill level roughly is equal to hours of practice. It can be a little practice over many years or a ton of practice within a few. If it’s just a fun hobby there’s no need to do this. Just enjoy the fiddle and have fun. I just want you to know that the option exists.

2.). Start adding some ornaments to your playing. Rolls cuts short rolls triplets etc. It won’t sound truly Irish until you start adding the ornaments.

3.). Be a fanatic about tuning. Play slowly with a tuner. Because fiddles are fretless I find it’s a rare fiddler that can play in tune. The rare bird that does sounds magical.

Keep up the great work and have fun!

Re: new fiddler would love suggestions / critique

You sound good.

From the videos, I see your left hand thumb is wrapped around the neck and the neck appears to be resting in the webbing of your hand and your fingers are not over the string. That is a very natural way of gripping something, but it creates tension and unnecessary movement when playing this instrument.

If you support the neck with the first joint of the finger and rest your thumb against the neck such that you can still support the fiddle when moving your thumb around and even take it away from the neck entirely, and keep your elbow lose and your fingers curled over the string being played at all times, you may find improvements or at least more comfort. You want your third (and fourth) finger to be as ready to go as the first from the start.

I saw you have a cheap bow. The bow is essential and a good bow, or at least a straight one, makes things much easier. You get a better bow for the money with a carbon fibre bow and they tend to be straight and stay straight and are not influenced by the environment.

When you get your own instrument, if this is where you play and keep your instrument, perhaps a stand with a bow holder would be worth considering. Being able to see and pick up one’s fiddle whenever one wants without having to fiddle with a case has its benefits and it takes up less space. And they are more stable than a top-heavy case.

I saw a comment to the contrary, but do not use too much rosin. I do not see any evidence of this in the videos, but over-rosining the hair too sticky and makes a mess. Once a bow is rosined, it generally does not need to be applied that often. Any rosin on the strings or the body of the fiddle should be wiped off after playing.

I will certainly pay attention. I like your playing for what it is.

Moving with the music is fine: fiddlers are supposed to listen to the music being made and enjoy it. The metronome is good for what it is good for, but if one sticks with it in a tune, it will make one sound like a metronome too, and that removes the lilt. So when you use a metronome, one should use it for a specific known purpose, but also know it will be turned off.

Ornamentation and the rhythm should not be strictly held to a metronome, but guided by your own ear. You can remove ornaments and focus on the rhythm first, and then add to the rhythm as you go. You can also use different ornaments.

For your new video, this is not a critique, but I noticed you play closer to the fingerboard. I’m not sure if you do this deliberately, but in case you don’t, the sound you get from the fiddle is different at different points in the string. Closer to the bridge is louder. Close to the fingerboard is quieter and often used for fiddling, however, for your new bridge, you may want to experiment with playing as you are, right in the middle, and closer to the bridge (and the places in between) to see how the sound is in each spot and to see whether the D string is easier to isolate in any particular spot. The terms for this is “contact point” and “sounding point” if you want to look into it more.

Knowing this and being deliberate can help control volume, tone, and make some things easier (or harder).

Double stops are easier closer to the fingerboard, so if you can play the D alone near the fingerboard, then the bridge is good. Any change, even improvements, in an instrument one has used will cause a period of adjustment.

Sul ponticello is playing as close to possible to the bridge and produces harmonics and a very different sound. I do not think there is a reason to do this in ITM, but doing things that are excessive just to know what they sound and feel like is good for learning.

I think one of the reasons you sound good is because you are doing what you like. I do not think anyone (who makes good music) sticks to anything written at all, and if one does, it is because one has written and marked it up oneself.

Re: new fiddler would love suggestions / critique

Lift is about more than slurring and single bows. Regardless of what bow strokes you use, the key to lift is dynamic use of bow speed and pressure. By that I mean you have to vary bow speed and pressure to create little ebbs and swells. You can do this going from one note to the next, and also within the span of a single note.

Each note has at least three parts: how you initiate the sound (violin pedagogy calls this the “attack”), how you sustain the sound (does it get louder or quieter, does the tone change, etc.), and how you end the note (softly blended into the next, clipped short, or somewhat else). That kind of micro-control of the bow comes with time and learning to bow not just with your forearm but with your wrist and fingers. It’s good to be aware of the possibilities, but at this point you have plenty of larger cats to herd toward competency.

Regarding metronomes vs recordings, I prefer playing along with good solid recordings. As you say, a metronome can’t share a sense of lift or living pulse, but a recording can.

Also, be mindful that “pulse” is built not just from rhythm (playing steadily to a beat) but also *timing,* which is where notes land respective to the beat (ahead of, spot on, or behind the beat) and whether you shorten or stretch the length of each note. In a reel such as Musical Priest, you’ll get some swing and lift simply by slightly lengthening the first and third 8th notes of each group of four.

Re: new fiddler would love suggestions / critique

When learning and practicing, there are many things one can (and should) do that one would not do in a performance of a tune.

The metronome is a very useful tool, but it is not accompaniment. There are a variety of videos and articles from people giving good recommendations on how to use a metronome for practice.

For an example, using a metronome can be very effective to get a good vibrato, but as one can imagine, a 60 BPM vibrato does not sound good or sound like vibrato at all. But the point is to train the movement to be even and be able to (eventually) do effortless vibrato. Vibrato is an ornament in fiddling (and Baroque music, etc).

For bowing, the same applies. It is important to practice smooth bowing, even to the extent of doing one minute bows (called “Son Filé”). That kind of practice is not meant for sound, and one minute bows never sound good, but the point of the exercise is to get even speed and control from frog to tip. The same goes for metronomes: you can practice perfect speed with one, but that is not necessarily how one should play. For example, a triplet looks like it should be even: a beat divided into equal thirds, but that is not how it is often played. So one may practice long bows with a metronome, even if one is playing with wrist bowing and interpreted rhythm and timing that no metronome could help.

For wrist bowing, a tune like the Concertina Reel can be very helpful. Wrist bowing is not tiring and for the sorts of tunes that one should use it for are very tiring if bowed with the elbow. The speed of the bow is crucial to the sound, so it something can vary as much as the fingers of the left hand in a tune. Doing scales with wrist bowing is helpful to, if one does scales already.

Tension is always the problem, even in the most advanced violinsts, so this is something everybody works on all the time: everybody works on eliminating tension everywhere, from the left hand to the right arm to the entire body. Having a loose and controlled wrist is needed for wrist bowing, but also for all bowing really.

For context of your learning:

It is possible to play only by ear and with tunes: listening to tunes, playing tunes alone, and playing along with others. This is how fiddling is traditional learned, and the fiddling art is based on what developed from this kind of development. The instruments are what are available, often handed down with what would be cheap steel strings. The best fiddlers we want to emulate or to listen to the most often learned this way.

A traditional violin student would use a method book system, metronomes, sheet music, tuners, and the like, with perhaps the most expensive instrument and bow they could get. They would be, for a few years, still be strictly adhering to written music, following fingering and bowing instructions, and focused on bow division. They would be held to this by their teacher as well: if they do not do it right, they cannot progress. Adults generally would be in Suzuki Books 1 & 2 without vibrato after year of regular lessons and one can see what that sounds like from the many videos available.

You (and most now) use a hybrid: learning with the tools of the violin lessons and the fiddler traditional. That means we have a lot to pull from: some may say never use a metronome or sheet music, and others may say learn traditional violin up to a certain level and then switch to fiddling. All tools besides the ear, fidil, and bow are optional. How and when or whether to use those particular tools will result in many perspectives, but yours is the most important for you.

For playing with a recording, I don’t do that, mainly because I am not set up for that, but I do not think there is any danger. Who is proficient enough to emulate a recording so well, yet not able to do anything else? And one would be playing around with various options to copy an audio recording, and in doing that, one would be doing many things that are not in the recording. Playing music along with music makes sense. You can also use a blinking light metronome that is silent if you can visually follow it.

The points made above by gimpy are good. And further, if one is limited to a single recording, say, the Foinn Seisiún books and CDs, and learns that way, it is still not a problem: someone learning is hardly going to be emulate them perfectly and if one gets to that point, one is likely skilled enough to do far more than what was recorded.

Restricting a tune to a single interpretation is probably a bad idea, but I do not think that happens even when learning from a single source. I wonder, does anyone seek to play ITM without enjoying listening to it being played by others? I want to play it and play it only because I have listened to and enjoyed it. For a tune I want to learn to play, I do have preferred recorded versions, but they tend to be from groups of many different instruments or soloists not playing the fidil.

Re: new fiddler would love suggestions / critique

“I think I do this subconsciously, it’s an interesting question whether I’d benefit trying to think about it in a particular tune, or if it’s best to just let the ear and body do it naturally.”

Agree, it’s a very interesting question!

One school of thought is that our minds and bodies are fully capable of sorting out what to do to get the desired results without consciously analyzing anything. That’s how we learn to walk and to speak our first language, and no doubt a host of other skills. Music can and does work this way, too, and, Philip, if your own progress is anything to go by, I’d say you have a fair knack for it.

Another school of thought stirs in some degree of analysis (dissecting a skill into its constituent parts) and synthesis (reassembling those parts). Members of this school have a saying: “If you don’t know [understand] what you’re doing, you can’t do what you want.”

In learning fiddle, I’ve tended to go the first route. But the second has proven useful when faced with some knot—particularly with bowing—that I couldn’t seem to untangle subconsciously. So I don’t advocate one approach over the other but am happy to use both as needed. Sometimes a little analysis and critical thought unties that knot much more quickly and effectively than just trusting your subconscious to suss it out. (It also comes in handy if you ever end up teaching or mentoring others.)

I will say this. Over the years I’ve run into a fair number of fiddlers who say that they can’t explain their own bowing because they’ve “never thought about it.” A sizeable subset of these folks likes to boast that they “don’t use bowing patterns” and “never play a tune the same way twice.” Yet, on listening to their bowing, an awful lot of them are actually stuck with a limited palette of bowing options, and this limits their ability to vary how they interpret a tune or a phrase. They may sound fine at first, but after fifteen minutes of tunes, you realize everything they play sounds the same. Their range of musical expression is narrow, and it soon goes stale on the ears. It may be that, if you’re not aware of your bowing, you’re *more* likely to get stuck in a rut.

Among the generations of fiddlers before us, and some fine players alive now, some of them have sorted out tricks and techniques for creating pulse and lift, and for making beautiful music despite the challenges of pace, string crossings, tone production, and all the other intricacies of lashing at strings with a horse’s tail. On one hand, the lessons we learn ourselves are often the most powerful. On the other hand, it seems a shame to spend a lifetime of hours reinventing the wheel, discovering what other fiddlers already know and could’ve explained to us in minutes. Food for thought.
