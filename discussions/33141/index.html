Re: hmm… flut? tinwhistle? what should I. start with?

Hi, Danielle. Welcome to the The Session. I like tdrury’s suggestion. Everyone here should understand that you are young (13), but have experience playing violin. James Galway is Irish, but even when he plays whistle, he does not do so in the traditional style. As for flute, he is strictly classical, which is very different. Whistle has the same fingering as Irish flute, but is one octave higher and thus smaller. You also blow directly into it, so it is easier to play because you don’t have to develop your lip to get the sound like you do on flute.

Listen to as much Irish music as you can. No matter what instrument you hear, you will be getting the idea how the music should sound generally. Gradually specialize in listening to whistle. Mary Bergin is a good artist to learn from. Eventually, you may find your way to flute. Good luck and enjoy the journey.

Cheers.

Re: hmm… flut? tinwhistle? what should I. start with?

If price is the determining factor, then sure - go with the whistle. Personally, I find it awkward to go from playing the whistle back to the flute. I have never regretted starting with the flute rather than the whistle - though I really started with the fiddle. I have always felt that playing the fiddle gave me an initial leg up. Danielle’s experience with the fiddle will be of great benefit to her when she starts to play the flute.

I have sold a lot of instruments to kids, funded by their parents. When young people express an interest in a particular instrument the worst thing to do is to try to encourage them to play something other than what they have expressed the desire to learn. You should run with that enthusiasm and try to encourage it and build on it as much as possible. In my experience of selling instruments, kids are always deeply appreciative when their parents buy them an expensive instrument. That appreciation almost always results in a deeper commitment to the instrument. The instrument itself becomes one of the real treasures in a young player’s life.

The relationship between a parent who invests in music for their child, and the child who recognises the parent’s involvement in the child’s musical development, is very deep and meaningful. The child is always grateful, eager to show the parent how quickly he/she is learning to play the instrument. It becomes a partnership in the very best sense of the word, as the parent encourages the child to develop musically and the child responds to that investment of money and concern.

As far as difficulty with embouchure, I tell the young player that if it was easy everybody would do it. The challenge makes them special. When they are genuinely motivated they stick to it and don’t have a problem with embouchure. Why deny a young player the opportunity to learn a challenging instrument as early as possible? Don’t we all wish -- or aren’t we all grateful -- that we started playing seriously when we were very young?

Re: hmm… flut? tinwhistle? what should I. start with?

I understand all the reasons why people might suggest whistle, but at the end of the day, the flute is a much more flexible instrument and you’ll still end up able to play whistle as well with minimum extra effort if you have learnt to play the flute. You can’t say that the other way round though.

So, if you want to learn flute - do! But if you are looking more for a general start in Irish (or Scottish) traditional music then by all means get a whistle and do some more listening - there is always time to decide which instrument you really want to play later (especially if you’re still young). And who knows it might even be the whistle, though I personally know few who really play whistle so that it sounds good to my ears. (I know lots of people who _also_ play the whistle alongside their “main” instrument.) I think the perception that the whistle is “easy” hides the reality that it is just as hard to sound good on it as on any other instrument. Maybe even harder due to the limitations of the instrument (limited dynamics, no real possibility of playing with the tonal colour, etc.) And there is always the risk of OWhAD, which is at least cheaper than OFAD.

One other option is to maybe try to play a Boehm flute - that is not really ideal for this sort of music (IMHO), but the instruments are available for relatively little money and a lot of embouchure, posture and breath technique transfers quite well should you move to a simple system flute later. And you can play lots of other stuff on it too.

Re: hmm… flut? tinwhistle? what should I. start with?

Na, I can’t recall much about making the transition any more, but nowadays it is simply a case of I pick up the Boehm and play classical, Baroque and Jethro Tull on it and I pick up the simple system flutes and play traditional music on it. Sometimes I play the same things on both, though most tunes only really sit (for me) on one instrument or the other.

They are different instruments, but have a lot in common. But to be honest, the differences that are often quoted have more to do with the stylistic conventions of those who play them rather than the instruments themselves.

On a completely concrete level, the F/F# fingering is different. The Boehm flute does Chromatic much better and the simple system flute takes finger slapping/flipping traditional ornaments better. Both can bark or play smooth.

YMMV

↳ Posted by o'muirgheasain on January 17th, 2014.

Re: hmm… flut? tinwhistle? what should I. start with?

What we call an Irish flute is simply the flute that was in use before the silver Boehm flute was invented. Boehm is the last name of the inventor, and his version of the flute is not as old as most people would suppose. Mozart and Beethoven never saw what you call a normal flute. The main difference is that an Irish flute does not use keys to cover the notes, except for accidentals - you use your fingers only. The old flutes had keys for the accidentals, but since they are seldom used in Irish music, a newly made Irish flute won’t have them unless you ask a maker to add them, and then only at substantial cost. Irish flutes are usually made of wood, not metal. However, flutes made of different types of plastic are becoming more popular because they are cheaper and more durable (they don’t crack from extremes in weather as wood can).

↳ Posted by o'muirgheasain on January 17th, 2014.

Re: hmm… flut? tinwhistle? what should I. start with?

I will just add the Ailin’s excellent summary that the flute we refer to as an “irish flute” (and the ones Beethoven and Mozart would have known) has six open holes for fingers that give the same notes as a tin whistle. That is why people above have been saying that a lot of what you learn on a whistle would transfer to flute. That is not the same for a “silver” Boehm flute, though people who are good at learning instruments say that the differences are not a big barrier.

I found that it was useful having learned some tunes on the whistle before moving to flute because I could try to play those whilst working on getting notes out of the flute. I could think about the sounds I was making rather than where my fingers had to go. Lots of people have not trouble doing both at the same time though.

Re: hmm… flut? tinwhistle? what should I. start with?

I think Sir James could have done a better job of describing the purpose of ornamentation. He makes it sound like a crutch for a player that does not have classical expertise. What I think he meant is that ornamentation fills out the sound so that no accompaniment or additional parts are needed in the music. The flow of notes is pretty much constant, whereas without the ornaments the tunes would sound pretty spare. There isn’t a lot of music aside from ITM that can hold its own being played by a single melody instrument, especially one that cannot play double stops like a fiddle or harmonica, or use a drone like pipes have.

About switching between flute and whistle, yes, I use the same fingering for both instruments, but I would play the same tune in a rather different style on high whistle that I would on flute. On whistle I’m trying for the classic whistle sound of Mary Bergin (I’m falling far short of course) with lots of articulation, on flute a flowing sound with little articulation but lots of ‘breath pushes’.

It’s not that the instruments require a different style, it’s just that they’ve typically been played that way.

Now that after 35 years of playing flute I’ve switched to Low D Whistle I’m finding that my style is gravitating somewhere in the middle between the way I played flute and the way I play high whistle.

Anyhow if your goal is to play Irish flute, get an Irish flute by all means. Yes Casey Burns’ “folk flute” is a bargain.
