For me I think steady tempo, timing & rhythm are the aspects which should be solid in playing a tune. The thing about Irish tunes is these vary depending on the tune type. So it takes time to get each ‘type’ ingrained. Which is why it might be easier to play or learn a more familiar tune type and free up your brain. But once a rhythm is ingrained you begin to recognise the general shape of a tune (or tune type) and you don’t have to think as much in order to learn it.

Re: Muscle Memory vs Conscious playing

It’s not a zero-sum game. Not either/or. Depending how you learn new tunes, you should work at it so that it all becomes a coordinated whole.
Once you have a tune, or even a set of tunes, you shouldn’t need to think about it at all, or not much.
Granted that different brain regions will have different functions in the tune learning process, but once it’s “in” there, the system will work as, well, a coordinated whole.
It’s the learning that requires the thinking. Even with that, as your musical brain matures, you can play phrases in tunes without thinking. Then you should be able to fit all the bits together like Lego. And that still needs a wee bit of thought.
Keep on with the practice and expanding your repertoire and ornamentation etc skillset and it will all soon fall into place.

There are some excellent responses in this thread that has made me think (a rare process) about my own playing.

If I’m learning a new tune or even if I’m playing a tune I know well but at an unfamiliar session, then I’m very conscious of what I’m playing while trying to match their settings and ornamentations of tunes.

When playing tunes I’m comfortable with while playing with friends I’ve played with for decades then I can enter a Zen-like groove and go with the flow. However, even then I’m very conscious on a certain level of what the other players are doing, particularly little changes each time through. For example how the piper slides up to the C in Rakish Paddy or how a fellow fiddler bows through the first part of Heathery Breeze.

Overall, I think the best advice is to play, play,play. Practice by yourself, play in small groups with friends, play at sessions and ceilis, and keep that foot tapping away.

Re: Muscle Memory vs Conscious playing

If you’re relying on the “muscle memory” to know how the tune goes, then you’re relying on it too much. For instance, if you add or remove an ornament in a tune you play reasonably well, do you get “lost” for a second?

Ultimately, the kinesthetic memory helps you keep things going, but your conscious brain should be controlling it. Similarly to how you type a message on this website, where your muscle memory knows how to type but your brain is deciding what to say, your music should ultimately be driven by your conscious brain and you can just rely on these neural pathways that you have built to help you play the notes the way your conscious brain wants them to sound… This is how you ultimately go from simply regurgitating a tune that you learned, to expressing the melody intentionally and eloquently…

There’s no doubt that kinesthetic memory plays a big part in learning to play an instrument in the first place. It plays a big part in learning tunes. And it plays a big part in being able to play relaxed at speed… But if you use it as too much of a crutch, it can hold you back…

Re: Muscle Memory vs Conscious playing

Hmm. I can tell you what works for me. Whether that’s a universal right answer is another question.
So there’s a lot of technical stuff that has to be muscle memory for me when I’m playing. If I think too hard about bowing or *how* I’m going to play ornaments in a session I’m going to come unstuck.
I suspect the same applies for breathing or bellows work or what have you, but I can’t say for sure.
What I can do is consciously decide to break the bowing up or link it all together for variation’s sake, or to blend in with other musicians.
Or I can choose to hit the back beat hard, or stick to the one.
But the actual ins and outs of where I slur and where I don’t, or whether I’m on an upbow or a downbow are all kind of instinctive.
Now they work that way as a result of some thinking and trying things out when I learn the tune. I can fake it and just fall back on my muscle memory if I’m picking up a tune on the fly, but the results are better if I consciously play through a new tune slowly and figure out how I’m going to bow it. And once I have a good set of ideas I practice them and ingrain them in my muscle memory.
The same goes for variations and ornaments.
I can kind of fall back on my instincts, but some conscious thought out practice makes the tune sound much better.
When I’m playing, I’m making conscious decisions about what variations and ornaments to play. Mainly the actual notes and things are just muscle memory, but I’m deciding to go up instead of down, or play rolls on those three notes, or play the arpeggio backwards or whatever. And most of these little phrases and twiddles are in my muscle memory from practicing the tune or just from practicing the same figure in another tune.
Sometimes I’ll take a risk and consciously play a different note, but just let muscle memory and instinct get me back to the “standard version” of the tune. This can just be a spontaneous idea, or sometimes it’s when I’m trying to copy someone else’s variations on the fly.

Re: Muscle Memory vs Conscious playing

I don’t know if there’s any real difference between what I think of as consciously learning a tune and learning it by “muscle memory,” but I do think of it as two different modes of practice when learning a new tune.

Most tunes I can learn the usual way with my mind fully engaged. Starting slowly, breaking it down into phrases, learning the A part first and then the B part. Eventually stringing the whole thing together. It feels like a conscious process.

And then, there are the weird tunes. The ones that mostly sound like other tunes except for one part that has a twisty or illogical note sequence, which is why they’re so interesting. For example, the third part of “Ian Green of Greentrax” with that odd syncopation run. Or the last part of the “Monaghan jig” with the stairsteps of climbing and falling notes. Or the last four bars of the “Prince Charlie” reel with notes jumping back and forth.

Those little twisty sections need what I’d call focused or muscle memory practice. Burning it into my fingers with constant repetition until I can play the notes without thinking. I may not actually be using a different part of my brain when learning these sections by repetition compared to the normal way I learn a new tune, but it definitely feels different. It feels like I have to get my conscious brain out of the way and just repeat, repeat, repeat until I’ve got it under my fingers and I’m not thinking about it.

Others with more experience in this music, or just better sight readers or ear learners may not find these sections of a tune as challenging as I do, but it’s the only way I can get them under my fingers. The rest of the tune, and most other tunes feel like I have my mind more engaged while learning it.

Re: Muscle Memory vs Conscious playing

When this topic came up before someone gave a link to an academic paper titled “When paying attention becomes counterproductive”. Google will find it.

It’s heavy going but the experiments reported are easy to follow. One of the conclusions is “… that skill-focused attention benefits less practiced and less proficient performances yet hinders performance at higher levels of skill execution.” Which I take to mean that as novices at a task we need to pay attention but once we are proficient it’s best not too.

Several times I have been at workshops where the tutor has slowed something down so much in demonstration that they have time to think (or need to to explain a point) and start making mistakes.

Re: Muscle Memory vs Conscious playing

Of course postie makes a solid point further up. Once you’ve been playing for hours and maybe knocking back a few pints, you can’t really think fast enough and you just have to let go and allow your fingers to work away without too much interference from your brain.

Sometimes the results of this are sub par, but sometimes you can sort of ride the wave, especially with people you’re used to playing with, and good music comes out. (Or at least what sounds like good music to your Guinness addled ears.). And the feeling when it actually works for a sustained period is great.

A good example of the brain and the fingers at work is when you spontaneously go up or down the octave. You’re consciously going from note to note and you really have to focus. But your fingers are just instinctively following the sound in your head. And you’re instinctively bowing it in a new way, because all the string crossings and stuff are in different places. You have to really really know the tune and really really know your instrument to do it at session speeds. (I’d only rarely do it without practice and only on simple tunes that I already know really well.)

But when you’re practicing slowly you can do the same process with lots more time to think. And the next time the tune comes up in a session, you can just do it.

And then you can take some of the unconscious little twiddles that fall off your fingers in one octave and consciously replicate them in the other octave.
