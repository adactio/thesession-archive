Turns that aren’t built on a new tonal region

First things first to define “turn” and “Hook” the “Turn” of a tune is the second half the “Hook” is the first half. Anyway, ever notice how the turn of some tunes isn’t built upon a new key or mode, but rather an inversion of the same chord the foundation was built upon? The Creel Of Turf for instance this setting should be in E dorian, but we can ignore that for now.

X: 1
T: The Creel Of Turf
R: jig
M: 6/8
L: 1/8
K: Emin
|:EBB BAG|Fdd AFD|EBB EBB|AGF EFD|
EBB BAG|Fdd AB^c|ded BGB|AGF E2D:|
|:Bee ede|fef dBA|Bee Bee|fe^c d2A|
Bee ede|fef dBA|BdB GBd|AGF E2E:|

“EBG” is the outline of an E minor chord.
“Beg” is the second inversion of an E minor chord. (Omit the g because the turn never climaxes to g it climaxes to the f# which would in theory indicate that the D major tonality is “winning” or dominant in this particular tune )
This E minor chord is the foundation of the tune. The hook’s core loop is the EGB to DF#A to BdG back to EBG. While the turn’s core loop is more complex, not built around full chords, but rather the first bar is built upon intervals of 4ths, the second bar 6ths, third 4ths once again, fourth bar is a little harder to tell because it could be harmonized as an ec#A or an f#dA but for the sake of analysis we shall state that this is merely a first inversion of a D major chord or the latter option, so it is built upon 6ths and a 3rd. Let’s continue, the fifth bar of the turn is the same as the 1st, the sixth bar is again the same as it’s counterpart (the 2nd) the seventh bar is where things get interesting because it adds new material outlining a G major chord in the first inversion bringing us (nontraditionally of tonal practice) back to an E minor chord to close off.

This is interesting because I was always under the impression as a composer that the turn would explore a different region of tonality in other words, change key, but it doesn’t here and in a few other instances, however I’m still “cooking” with this theoretical model. I also noticed that Irish music doesn’t have traditional cadences, but rather a sense of resolution without them. There is no V to I in a traditional sense of Western tonal practice, but there still is a sense of returning “home”.

Some new terminology:

An Bunsraith = the chord upon which a section of a tune is built.

An Cnámharlach (maybe an creatlach) = the reduction to the tune’s notes of the most overall structural importance.

An Duán = the first section of a tune.

An Casadh = the second section of a tune.

An Ceist = the first half of a section of a tune

An Freagra = the second half of a section of a tune.

Ar Ais = The modal Cadence.

Just to throw a bag of spanners in the works…

I do understand why many musicians might be reluctant to approach ITM in purely theoretical terms as in the OP. Personally, I don’t have a problem with the chord-by-chord breakdown approach, I’ve done the same sort of thing myself from time to time. However, I am generally conscious that when I do, I’m almost always overthinking the tune. On the plus side, it can open up new ways to experiment with the tune (variation, double stopping, harmony etc.). On the minus side, it can make the tune sound overworked, over-rehearsed. Playing more for the stage than for the session.

(On a brief tangent, someone recently posted a video of a performance by Ciara Ní Bhriain where she blended traditional tunes, classical motifs, Bach, jazz etc. etc. in a sort of personal musical melange. She very openly talked about the process she had gone through to create/arrange/compose the pieces she performed: very, very interesting and without any doubt whatsoever she is a highly accomplished musician, a polished and talented performer… but what she was playing was also undoubtedly music for the stage; not for the house, or the session, or the dancing. Head music, not heart music.)

I’m not particularly arguing for or against arranging ITM for stage performance, by the way, it’s up to the individual performer what they do when they get up there. It comes down to taste. What one person perceives as ‘groundbreaking’, or ‘genre-expanding’ might represent an absolute betrayal of the tradition to a purist. Who doesn’t love the Bothy Band now, yet were they not also heavily criticised at the time?

A lot of these reactions are subjective. I personally have a loathing for the sort of navel-gazing, self-reflexive musical onanism that comes from a musician who thinks they are bigger or better than the tradition. I shall name no names. Instead, the kind of musician I admire is one who expands the tradition from within, who creates a unique space for their own music and who doesn’t simply bung in a load of jazzy chords and unnecessary syncopation in order to make the tunes sound ‘more interesting’. Boak. (The same can be said for composers of new tunes, by the way - some are exemplary, creating pieces that slot instantly into the tradition; too many churn out a jazzy hash of notes that have no place in it at all.)

Aaaaand, here come the spanners…

I recognise that in the OP, the bar-by-bar or chord-by-chord analysis is being used as a means of discerning patterns with a view to deriving a methodology for composition, but this leads me to some questions, for many of which I’m not even sure if there are definitive answers. So:

How do we categorise a ‘standard’ tune? For example, a two part reel of 8 bars per ‘half’, in a major key (say D) where the second ‘half’ is in the same key, idiomatically linked to the first ‘half’ but starting a fifth higher. Or an octave, or a third. How many tunes in ITM actually follow this pattern? How many patterns are there? There’s a whole rake of tunes in G that dip into F/Dmin and back up again. Are there enough of those to represent a pattern? What about tunes that are major in the first ‘half’ and minor in the second? Or go to the relative minor, or a modal form of the fifth? Or don’t. Or do both? Or finish with the last phrase of the first ‘half’?

What if the chord pattern of a particular tune is subject to change? What if a variation in the melody requires a different chord or a different variant of the same chord? What if the base note of the chord were to change - using the third rather than 1 or 5? What if we harmonise using a discord? What if we’re avoiding certain notes of the chord so we don’t clash with what else is going on?

What about the limitations of the instrument we’re composing for? Are we being pipes and flute friendly and not using notes below the open D? Avoiding too many awkward accidentals? Or do we accommodate the button accordion and include that lovely low F natural, just to piss the fiddlers off?

What about individual composers who do the strange and unnatural, like Paddy Fahey composing G neutral jigs that exist in a quantum state where both F sharp and F natural are viable alternatives, and where accompanists fear to tread?

(Worryingly, a good number of these thoughts are generally floating round my head while I’m playing. I am given to understand that this is Not Normal.)

Ultimately I would wonder whether it is even possible (let alone useful) to try and derive patterns in this way, especially as a precursor to composing. The thought process is valid, and will inform composition, but the scale of the task involved in analysing the infinitude of possibilities is… Well, I wouldn’t personally know where to begin.

Instead, I would suggest that a good tune (by which I mean a well-written modern tune that sits alongside and within the tradition without being either too derivative or too ‘experimental’) is probably one that begins with a phrase, or part of a phrase, something distinctive that the rest of the tune hangs around, plays with, complements and completes. I don’t know for sure, but I feel that intuition, invention and inspiration might be more likely and more valuable starting points than exhaustively deriving a pattern-first approach. To return full-circle, it may be better to think first with your heart than with your head.

“…I was always under the impression as a composer that the turn would explore a different region of tonality….”

And that impression would be wrong. In trad, it’s all about melody (not tonality). And it’s not at all uncommon for the first phrase of Part B to share the same tonality as the first phrase of Part B. In fact, Much of B often mirrors A. Consider Rolling in the Ryegrass:

|:A2AB AFDF|G2BG dGBG|ABAF DFAF|GBAG E2D2:||
|:ABde f2fd|g2ge fedB|ABde fefa|gfdf e2d2:||

No one knows for certain, but it’s likely that most trad tunes, old and new, are “composed” not theoretically, algorithmically scribbling with pen on paper, but rather by noodling around on a whistle, fiddle, chanter, buttons, etc. Or by being of long intimacy with such instruments and lilting a melody in one’s mind.

Part of what briantheflute and others are getting at here is that new tunes mostly bubble up not from dissection and analysis but from synthesis and this distinction is itself fundamental to this musical tradition.

A fluter like Vincent Broderick, say, or a box player like Paddy O’Brien, understands a lifetime of playing this music on their instrument. They’ve assimilated hundreds or thousands of tunes, as well as spent countless hours swimming in the community and craic of sessions and ceilis and house dances and fleadhs. In short, their tunes come from immersion and synthesis.

And as others have said, it’s not as though analysis hasn’t been done. Fleischmann, Breathnach, Ó Canainn, Grasso, even Staib et al. at Stanford.

Analyzing chords to understand how to compose a melody in this music feels bass-ackwards to me. One of the things that make ITM so fascinating to me compared to other genres of music, are the quirks and twists in the melody line that baffle accompanists who come into this music from other genres. For example: Why do some tunes play games with shifting between Cnat and C#, like it can’t decide what mode it’s in? Why does ‘Kid on the Mountain“ constantly shift between modes like that? Why is there a single distinctive F natural note in ”Chief O’Neil’s Favourite“ hornpipe? Why does ”Beare Island" have those unusual G# notes in the A part?

These quirks are one of the things that makes ITM distinctive. Not every tune has them, but so many do that I think it has to be considered an important part of the style. So I’m skeptical that trying to figure out how to compose a good ITM melody by analyzing harmonic structure is a productive approach. Some of the best tunes will resist being fitted into that straightjacket.

Intuition isn’t something separate from knowledge or understanding. Most fields of serious thought define intuition as some form of unconscious or inner knowing and insight. You’ve fallen into the common trap of thinking that Irish music is unique. It isn’t. Of course it has a distinctive flavour, but as Nico says that is a matter of style. Irish dance tunes largely follow the same structures as those from England, Scotland and Wales, and very often tunes are shared by all these traditions. ITM is just one part of a shared musical tradition which covered the whole of these islands, and its distinctive character comes from the way it is played, just as it does with the other national traditions and of course the regional variations within them.

I’m looking at Harvest Home on the the Traditional Tune Archive. There are nine versions. From simply looking at the notation, in most cases you’d find it very difficult to tell where they were from. Give any one of them to an Irish, Scottish, English or Welsh fiddler or a classical violinist and they’d all play it very differently. One of the Irish versions is actually titled “The English Hornpipe”.

Analysing the notes may or may not tell you something about traditional music, but what it won’t do is define “Irish” or help you write “Irish” tunes. It’s know it’s said on here repeatedly, but if you want to understand any genre of music the only way is to immerse yourself in it by listening to proficient musicians.

Another thing: be careful when inventing terminology. “Hook” already has a meaning in music, it is a phrase which makes a tune particularly distinctive. “Turn” is an ornament, similar to a “roll”. The parts of a tune are usually referred to as A part, B part etc. Anyway, your terms are imprecise - how long is a “section” or “half a section”? If you mean 4 bars then say so.

Here’s the Cliff Notes on the “theory” behind why Bcd e2f takes an Em chord.

The accented notes are B and e because they fall on the strong beats of the bar (ONE two three ONE two three).

In this case, the e2 jumps out because it’s twice as long as the other notes.

The B is the 5th of an Em chord. (The flatted 3rd, a G natural, isn’t in this bar, so [Be] as a dyad could be construed as an inverted Emaj. Try it and see if that sounds right. What happens to your inferred harmony when you do get to the G nats in the melody? Would a G6 make more sense? Or does Em suit it best?)

the “cd” and “f” bits happen on unemphasized parts of the jig pattern (see ONE two three ONE two three above). So they’re passing notes or pick up notes.

Em/D chords aren’t the only way to accompany this tune, or the only possible chords you could assign the note groups to, but they suffice. In fact, they sound good beneath the melody. They don’t detract from its Irishness. (FWIW, I’ve occasionally run across backers who throw a Bm chord on the d3 at the end of bar 4. The bowing - although it has a weaving pattern in crossing the strings - is not consistent, it invents and disrupts its patterns as quickly as they are formed. New technique and ornamentation are offered as alternatives to previous iterations, and discarded just as quickly.

What else? If we learn the tune and replicate it “just so”, listening endlessly to capture every nuance and inflection, working towards perfecting our performance to coincide with the recording above, have we captured the essence of the tune? Do we “have it”? Is it ours?

The second time through HE CHANGES EVERYTHING. And the next time. And the next.

Improvisation. Adaptation. On and on forever.

In his entire lifetime, it is vanishingly unlikely that Coleman ever gave the same performance of that piece twice. Ever.

Let that sink in for a moment.

What Dave has so expertly reconstructed is a frozen moment from one playing of one tune that happened once in 1934 to create that one recording. It is, by definition, a singularity. An archaeological sample of one individual performance from nigh on a century ago.

There are incalculable levels of complexity in every passing moment of this rich, wonderful music.

Will, “natural minor” means “aeolian mode”, as opposed to dorian mode. The difference is whether the 6th is sharp or not. E aolian would have a Cnat, but E dorian has a C#. The generic E minor usually refers to aeolian, but in Irish traditional music, minor tunes centered around E are predominately dorian (whereas Bminor tunes are predominately aeolian).

It is my understanding that gapped scales pre-date both the diatonic scale and instrumental music. The pentatonic scale is the easiest to sing, avoiding semitone intervals. Early church music uses the pentatonic scale in it’s various inversions (modes), then as part of the evolutionary process one or other of the gaps gets filled to create two flavours of hexatonic, before we get to the full diatonic. It certainly isn’t just that some tunes happen to not use those notes: if you look at the earliest tunes, none of them use them.
