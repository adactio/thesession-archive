Tuning down

So listening to recordings, I found that I enjoy that mellow ‘antique’ sound of the tunes when groups play tuned a step down…

…for me on fiddle, it’s easy to tune my A down to G and just tune the other strings to match, and then I’m off (playing all with same fingers, only it sounds different.) But I can’t imagine how other instruments deal with this! Players have to change all their notes and fingerings on the fly, and guitar players change all their chords (or use capo?)

So…I think playing “tuned down” is not feasible at the average session, am I right?
( Also just moving a tune one string over…easily done on fiddle…I guess that would also not go down well! )
Just curious how that works - is playing “tuned down” done only by Bands or solo fiddlers?

Re: Tuning down

Tuning down is great, but so is transposing tunes down – two different approaches, with different results. From the perspective of a fiddle/mandolin player, transposing a tune places the open strings at different places in the scale, giving whole tune a different feel – no doubt, it has an analogous effect on instruments such as flute and accordion, where the tune sits differently relative to the fundamental resonance or to bellows changes. It takes more work, of course (perhaps someone diligent in practising scales and arpeggios could play any tune in any key at the drop of a hat… not I, alas), but it can pay dividends.

I enjoy playing tuned down but, as Loughcurra says, it has to be agreed upon beforehand if it is in a session. Playing the odd set in ‘the wrong key’, however, can add a bit of interest to a session and, if anyone is excluded because they are not confident to transpose spontaneously, it is only for a few minutes.

Many uilleann pipers have a set, or at least a chanter, in the key of B (natural).

So get some pipers together and a B session might happen.

The terminology gets scrambled because uilleann sets lower than D are call “flat sets”.

So a set in B is a “B flat set” not to be confused with a “B-flat set” which is a set in Bb.

Yes there are B-flat flat sets but they’re not nearly as common as B flat sets. :)

Re: Tuning down

In an article/thesis which I read some years ago, violin historians insisted that the instrument, which was designed/evolved more than 450 years ago (and which has hardly changed in its body structure since) was specifically developed to play best at the then standard tunings. These were up to a full tone-and-a-half below A=440, though subject to regional variations (some early tuning forks sound at 377Hz, for instance). They postulated that any given violin may produce a more pleasing sound through tuning it one full tone lower than the modern standard.
Certainly, most fiddles I’ve played tuned down seem to produce a richer tone and a fuller vibration throughout the instrument at the lower tunings. Perhaps we should all just tune down to F,CGd , and re-learn our fingerings…

↳ Posted by Jerry O'Donnell on April 20th, 2024.

Re: Tuning down

In fact, back in the day, “standard pitch” was all over the map, some much lower than A440, but some much higher. Here’s a select list of A= from piano-tuner.org:

1640 Vienna Franciscan Organ A 457.6
1663 Bernards Schmidt’s Organ in Durham, England A 474
1699 Paris Opera A 404
1711 John Shore’s tuning fork A 423.5
1780 Stines, for Mozart, A 421
1714 Strasbourg Cathedral organ A 391
1722 Dresden’s chief Roman Catholic church organ A 415
1759 Trinity College Cambridge organ A 309
1762 Stringed instruments at Hamburg A 405
1780 Stein’s tuning fork A 422.6
1751 Handel’s own fork A 422.5
1811 Paris Grand Opera A 427
1812 Paris Conservatoire A 440

(The list continues up to 1939 when A 440 was adopted as the international standard. It’s revealing that the list’s highest frequency for A — 474 — dates from 1663.)

So it may be that some old violins were carved and braced to resonate best at whatever frequencies were in vogue at the time, in a specific locale (perhaps to match the local church organ). Over the years, I’ve owned fiddles that were most resonant when tuned a half- or full step lower than A 440, but I’ve also owned fiddles that would sing best at A 440 or a half step higher. When tuned down, some of these fiddles tended to become muddy and less responsive.

I don’t love A 440. My main fiddle sounds fine at A 440, but it really comes alive at A 415 (a half step down) or A 392 (a full step). And that’s with strings designed for tensions at A 440. A world tuned to A 415 would make me happy, but I long ago gave up hoping the world would adapt to me. ;-)

Another happy option is to kit out a fiddle with octave strings. It’s like having a cello under your chin!

Re: Tuning down

“the instrument…was designed/evolved more than 450 years ago [and] has hardly changed in its body structure since”

The latter half of this statement is incorrect. A major change was made to the length and angle of violin necks near the turn of the 19th century. The earlier so-called short-neck instruments were refitted wholesale with longer ones expressly to alter their initial playing characteristics, and the bass bars were changed to withstand the increased string tension. These modifications reflect the pitch levels of their day, not those the instrument was originally designed to accommodate. The story is told in greater detail at
http://blog.feinviolins.com/2015/06/your-violin-neck-used-to-be-shorter.html

Re: Tuning down

@Susanne.

At a flat session, string instruments tune up/down and play the tunes like they would normally. Other musicians will have to bring out their instruments that are made/tuned to that key (flutes/whistles/chanters/accordions/concertinas that can play in Eb/C/Bb and other keys). The benefit of this really is how the music sounds being played in those keys and it is more of an auditory/sensory experience. For example, I love tunes played in Eb because there is a brighter, cheery quality to the session and people tend to play faster and more upbeat in this key. While flat sets like Bb has an almost soulful and mellow quality that. In some ways, is ethereal when played well. I am more than happy to sit next to a piper playing in that key.

I’ve also heard of cross-tuning for fiddle/banjo and someone here can probably explain the benefits of doing it better than I can.

Will I be playing in these keys always? Probably not and only if the session host says it is a session played in that key. I won’t be bringing my flat instruments to a regular D session. But it’s fun to be part of one, or even sit and listen to one if you get a chance.

Cheers,

Melany

Re: Tuning down

Yes, most of the down tuned sessions I’ve been in were just a step down to C. But I’ve also enjoyed some wonderful Bb sessions, not just slow airs, but the usual jigs and reels just played at a slower tempo.

As for fiddle technique not changing at all, that’s not true.

When you tune down a fiddle, especially as far as Bb, the strings are slower to respond. So the bow hand, the feel, is completely different, making slower tempos and a lighter touch welcome. And fingered ornaments (e.g., cuts and rolls) also beg for a less rapid execution. It feels like an entirely different instrument. If you don’t adapt your technique, the music turns to mush.

Melany, cross tuning a fiddle (such as to AEAE or GDGD) allows for greater use of an open string as a drone, and also for more open strings to resonate in sympathy. So the fiddle’s voice is richer, fuller. Also, for many tunes written with cross tuning in mind, it tends to simplify the fingering.

Susanne, tunes routinely get played in keys other than their original. Calliope House jig was written in E, but is widely played in D and G. On fiddle at least, it comes in handy to know it in a handful of keys. But many tunes are most often played in their customary key. A good example of that is The Banks hornpipe, which was written in Eb and is a show piece in that key for fiddlers.

Re: Tuning down

@Susanne, this is not rocket science and terminology is a bit imprecise. For example, an “E-flat session” actually plays in D# instead of D, so you would expect it to be called a sharp session, but D# == Ef and it’s a flat key, hence the name. Simplest way to think about it as “A440-X”Hz sessions, an example would be A416Hz “baroque tuning session” (if any existed). The attraction is that A440 instruments like fiddles and mandolins play differently (better?) tuned down to A440-X, and people get to play their flat key whistles and chanters. in a way, it is like g=8.8 and g=10.8 gyms where the same dumbells and exerciser machines work just different enough to make low gravity and high gravity gyms interesting and worthwhile to build and operate.

Re: Tuning down

@Susanne, you’ve got it all sorted out – I’ll just add that calling the sessions “flat” is just a convention, not a definition. The key of C does not have any flats – but any chanter lower than D can be labeled as “flat” and Eb sessions also sometimes get this label by extension. Literally anything other that “concert pitch” is “flat”! :-D

To add to gimpy’s comment, I’ve played in a number of sessions in C#, C, B, Bb and Eb and I find that the fiddle behaves very differently in each of those keys. To me, Bb and Eb are extreme while everything else in between is fine.

Now, I know some fiddle players who never retune on principle and some of them are geniuses who can transpose tunes to Eb etc. on the fly (although even they say it’s tiresome). To each their own – I find that tuning down is a very interesting experience that has a potential to expand one’s imagination and technical skills. For one, retuning the strings changes the fingering slightly and you have to be conscious of that, especially in the middle of a busy session when hearing your own intonation becomes harder. Then there’s the question of strings – unless you’re using the sturdy chrome steel or staying in that flat key for a long time, chances are, your fiddle would be going out of tune after each set for quite a while. The Bb sessions are the hardcore mode for the fiddlers that just retuned from D – at least for those with good enough ear to hear themselves going sharper and sharper as the tune progresses! Even if you keep your fiddle in Bb, it’s bound to get out of tune pretty fast – it’s that delicate. “We tune because we care!” Tuning to Eb has even more potential problems – depending on the strings, it can put a lot of additional stress on both the strings and the instrument so it’s better to know in advance how they would respond.

What hasn’t been mentioned is that Eb isn’t just brighter, it allows for the music to pierce through the noise of a busy pub – seriously works like a charm. But I still find playing in Eb a stressful experience :-D On the contrary, playing anywhere between C# and B makes for a relaxed session and music that’s easy on the ears – but one that would also require a quiet room or a respectful audience since it’s so easily drowned out by the noise. As a bonus, all the slides become noticeably richer the lower you go!

A curious observation, Denis Murphy often used to tune somewhere between C and C# when not playing with other fixed-pitch instruments – and I find that to be the sweetspot in terms of the richness of the tone and perceived obertones and how my strings behave. Certainly more pleasant to practice in that key in a small room, too! Highly recommended.
