Daver,

please take my opinion with a grain of salt, since i’m a flute player and not a fiddler, but flutists go against some of the same issues.

Thing is, modern tuning is a compromise system in which pretty much every interval except the octave is wrong. So, when you’re playing on your own, if you’re playing “right”, you will probably play a little different from what the tuner says.

When you’re playing against a keyboard instrument, then you’ll be doing your best to play the same wrong notes as it.

When you’re playing against a wind, then it’s worse, because each instrument has its own “personality” (notes that are naturally sharp and flat). Notes that are sharp on the clarinet are flat on the flute and vice versa.

When you’re playing in an orchestra or session, then oh well, use lots of vibrato. :-) :-) :-) :-) :-) :-) (actually i don’t like vibrato and i use as little of it as possible)

If your tuner is able to generate tones, you can play against the tone it generates, in unison, then thirds, then fifths, searching for the note that sounds in tune. It will sound in tune when there are no low frequency vibrations (wah-wah-wah) in the combined sound. If you’re married, your spouse will attempt to murder you after about 10 minutes of this, so use your time well.

Re: intonation on the fiddle

Well, if we’re talking *intonation* (placing your fingers) here and not just tuning the fiddle, then I’d highly recommend practicing a bit to match the tuner (or some other fixed and properly pitched sourse, such as a midi file). On fiddle, accurate intonation is less a matter of finger memory and mostly a matter of whether your ear prefers flat, spot-on pitch, or sharp. Your fingers will follow what your ear tells you is right. And most of us tend to hear one or two intervals on the scale just a hair off from what they should be, so we play slightly out of tune.

For example, many fiddlers have a hard time finding f natural or B flat when they first start playing tunes that require those notes. It’s simply because their ear isn’t accustomed to that interval and isn’t sure precisely what the interval is. When I was just starting out, I know I tended to favor slightly flat notes on some intervals (say B natural in a G scale) and had to force myself to reach higher with the pinky on first string, but also to slide my index up on 2nd string for proper pitch on that note. Eventually I was able to recalibrate my ear so that spot-on B sounded right.

So if you want really accurate intonation, you’ll have to train your ear to accept the proper pitch. Playing along with a midi file or accurate fixed pitch instrument (confirmed with a tuner) is a great way to do this. Play slow enough to concentrate on the pitch of each note (airs work well). I found that making a midi file of whole notes from G, (open 4th string) on up allowed me to fine tune my intonation by listening to the “beats” as I played along on each note. If you’re slightly off, you’ll hear the waver or beat of the overtones from one note being slightly out of sync with the other. Adjust until that waver goes away.

Oh, and then don’t worry too much about it at the local session (unless people keep hinting that somethings not right with the moans coming from your fiddle :-). I’ve heard of studies that showed even the best concert violinists are only spot on with their intonation about 70 percent of the time (though how you could tell, with all that vibrato, is beyond me).

Um, Brad, a good tuner *will* work---assuming your fiddle’s not out of whack. Several things can affect intonation--worn out strings, improper bridge placement, warped or worn fingerboard, inconsistent grooves on the nut, tailpiece in the wrong place, even faulty proportioning of the neck. A fiddle that’s in good shape, set up well, with a properly dressed fingerboard should play in tune when the open strings are tuned to correct pitch. If your ear tells you otherwise, I’d wonder if something on the fiddle is out of kilter. Just a thought.

Will, if you think the “Irish” flute is bad, you should try the Baroque flute, where an F# is almost the same as F-nat. Sounds pretty nice, though. The “Irish” flute has 2 challenged notes: E and A. That’s because, mostly, of the compromises between where and how big the venting holes should be, and the normal finger reach of modern primates. One thing that makes ITM easier in the intonation department is that mostly we play in unison. And most of the time we’re more concerned with the rhythm than with precise intonation. Being a little out of tune adds charm to the session. :-)

Re: intonation on the fiddle

Glauber is right on the mark! There are different systems of temperament, ‘just temperment’, ‘well temperament’, ‘even temperament’, and others, I suspect. The problem arises due to the fact that the pitches of our scale are based upon harmonic factors. As a result, there are 2 different sizes of whole tones, and at least 3 sizes of semi-tones.

When the clavichord was developed, a new system of tuning was needed, to enable the instrument to play in different keys, so it was proposed to tune all the semitones to the same size, (even temperament). It was found that some intervals, primarily thirds, still sounded a little offensive, so another system was developed, in which, minor adjustments were made (well temperament).

I am not sure, but I suspect that midi files use even temperament, and that is the reason the chords sound so blah…. well, they don’t sound sweet and pure.

On the fiddle, we are aiming for the natural harmonic temperment, which (I believe) is called just temperament.

Most electronic tuners are set up for even temperament, which is
is close enough for tuning the open strings. However, with time, and practice, you are likely to learn to tune your fiddle closer to the natural tones. Some tuners will allow you to make adjustments, so that you can find the precise pitches that you are looking for. They have a “cents” function, which allows you to
test the precise pitch of the natural tones.

Are we splitting hairs? Maybe,…. maybe not. As an example, the b note (1st finger on the A-string, first position) is different in the key of A than it is in the key of G. In the key of A, it is found
about 1/8" (3 mm.) higher up the string!

Above all, take Glauber’s advice… don’t worry about it. Trust your ear, and you will develop the ability to match your intonation to the others.

Cheers,

Re: intonation on the fiddle

I have been following the discussion on fiddle intonation with great interest. I am asking Cathy to forward my comments, because I have lost my Session password.

I noticed more than 50 years ago (!) that when I learned a tune
unaccompanied, I had to change my intonation when I tried to play it with a piano. I now understand, or think I understand, the reason for this. I think we instinctively tend to play “just” or “pure” intervals, even melodically, on an instrument with adjustable pitch such as a violin. “Just” means you don’t get the “wah-wah-wah” on double stops that somebody in this thread talked about. An equal tempered piano, which the great majority are these days, has badly out-of-tune thirds and sixths, which we mostly don’t notice because we are accustomed to them. Except when we try to play along with a fiddle.

“Tempering” is something you are forced to do with any fixed-tuned instrument in order to choose pitches for the, typically, 12 notes in the scale in such a way that they “work” in a given context. Equal temperament is just one of those choices and is a compromise that works equally badly in all situations. “Mean tone” is another that was in common use for several hundred years, which provides nearly pure intervals for keys that use up to three sharps, two flats, and of course the white keys. This
includes most folk music, hymns, etc. for the past few hundred years, and it works in major and the related minor keys and the modal tunes. Cathy, the keyboardist for one of the bands that I play in, and I have been experimenting playing Celtic music with me on the fiddle and her on a harpsichord tuned in mean tone. It sounds absolutely lovely! I tune the harpsichord using a Korg 1200 tuner, then tune the open strings of the fiddle to the harpsichord. My impression is that my fingers just fall naturally into place, especially on double stops against the open strings. Much easier than playing with a piano.

There are several fiddlers out there who are doing Celtic-Early music crossover CDs, including David Greenberg, Laura Risk, and Abby Newton, who SHOULD be aware of the mean tone implications, but as far as I can tell they don’t talk about it.

I think something like mean tone must have been common with fiddlers over a long period of time, arrived at empirically. It must have been developed and in use for a long time before the theorists got hold of it, formalized it, and gave it a name. Easy to do when most keyboards were also mean tone. Among the other observations that seem to support this theory is my recent observation that the tuning scheme on hammered dulcimers seems made-to-order for mean tone, in fact there was a recent article supporting this in “Dulcimer” magazine. Fretted instruments, on the other hand, were tuned to equal temperament for a long time even before that system was adopted for keyboards, which led to historically documented catfights concerning who was right and who was wrong and who needed to retune!

I could pull together a fairly extensive biblography on this that I would share with anyone who is interested, although it would take me a day or two to do it. It is a subject that has been discussed in print for at least three hundred years!

I would like to expand a bit on my posting regarding the use of meantone for Scottish Baroque.

For one thing, when I said that “a lot of things had been published for at least 300 years”, that pertained to the intonation issue in general, not just mean tone.

Having said that, one question seems always to have been whether the pitches played on the violin were or should be part of a scale system consisting of specific reproducible pitches, and if so how many notes were in the scale, or if not part of such a system, then what? In the Baroque period, Peter Prelleur published a famous fingering chart indicating that musicians were expected, at least in theory, to understand and be able to
play the tempered scales that were in use at the time, including different versions of various intervals, e.g. different sizes for diatonic versus chromatic semitones. Without having recordings, it is hard to know what violinists actually did, except for the published comments of contemporary observers who said that the good players had exquisitely accurate pitch. In fact, I think there were experiments with monochords that supported that observation.

Another question was how the practice of violinists compared with that of fiddlers. I just got a book by Kate Dunlay and David Greenberg on Cape Breton fiddling, which includes considerable discussion of performance practice, and they mention the “gesunkenes kulturgut” theory, which says that "elements of culture descend from the elite to the folk where they are
retained as relics of an earlier era." It is known that Scottish fiddlers in the 18th century were aware of and were influenced by art music on the continent, so it would seem reasonable to assume that some of the contemporary ideas about scale systems would also have been part of that package. To the extent that keyboards were used to accompany music based
on folk tunes, then meantone would have been in common use, and it makes sense that the fiddlers would have played in tune with the keyboard. That does not necessarily imply that this would have influenced solo playing. The only thing Dunlay and Greenberg say about intonation is that contemporary Cape Breton fiddlers use “neutral tones” in ornamentation.

It should be pointed out that most of the intonation practices discussed by Prelleur and others of the Baroque period were in the context of chromatic music, which is not so important in Celtic folk music. The black notes in meantone are C#, F#, and G#, E flat and B flat. Strictly speaking D# and A flat don’t exist in the system - they are not enharmonically equivalents
to E flat and G # as would be the case in equal temperament . But as far as I can tell, most Celtic music doesn’t use many accidentals, except of course as ornamentation, which resolves to the notes which do exist within the scale system. So you can play forever in keys including up to three sharps and two flats with few problems. It is obviously not practical to
try adopting this temperament without access to a keyboard instrument like a harpsichord or a hammered dulcimer which are easy to retune. But it sure is fun if you can find a way to do it!
It is difficult to appreciate how badly “out of tune” the equal tempered piano is until you have heard the alternative. Meantone sounds “pure” in the same way as do good a-cappella choirs, barbershop quartets, and string quartets. Useful only in a limited context, to be sure, but that context seems to include most traditional Celtic music.

In the 20th century, performance pedagogs like Leopold Auer advocated flexible pitches which depended on context, and I have a book which represents a PhD thesis by one Janina Fyk at the Chopin Institute in Warsaw (fortunately translated into English) where she describes experimental measurements of the pitches actually played by classically trained violinists, and indeed they tend to be flexible, varying from “standard” pitch by typically 10 to 20 cents, depending on context. My impression of modern fiddlers is that many if not most are classically
trained, so it might make sense that they would follow the same
practices. I attended the Rocky Mountain Fiddle Camp a few weeks ago and my impression there was that the teachers played very accurate pitches, probably corresponding to equal temperament, since that is the prevailing scale system in our musical culture. Even as I type, I am listening to a CD of David Greenberg playing Cape Breton, and my impression is that he may be better “in tune” with the piano than, say, Theresa Morrison or Willie Kennedy. If that is valid, I don’t know what it means.

It would be really interesting to measure the actual intonations of
fiddlers and compare them with classical violinists. There is material here for a PhD thesis, I’m sure. Maybe somebody has done it, but if so I have not run across it yet. I would expect that the practice would vary with context, in particular whether the fiddler was playing solo or accompanied.

It is fairly easy to explore ones own intonation by playing solo scales slowly into an electronic tuner. When I have done this, I found that I varied from whatever standard I had in my ears at the moment by 10 cents or so, consistent with Ms. Fyk’s results . It changes depending on whether I have been playing recently with the meantone harpsichord. But my guess is that my pitches would be more accurate than this when actually playing along with the keyboard, and this might be a little more difficult to
measure. I am thinking about how to do it. Perhaps recording the tunes from a pickup attached to the fiddle, then running a spectral analysis on the results. That should work, at least with slow tunes. That is basically what Janina did in her research. I guess you could even do it in “real time” if the tune were slow enough.

Interesting stuff!

Re: intonation on the fiddle

David, it does create a few problems with the open strings. For example, the open E is a little sharp for the key of G (and also for all modal scales with one sharp). The G and D strings are a little flat for the key of C (and for all modal scales with no sharps or flats). The A string, is, by definition, in tune for all keys (assuming that it is properly tuned).

In another sense, it doesn’t create problems for me. It is a rare day when my intonation is that accurate. I would be thrilled if all my notes fell within extremes of the different systems of intonation.

When a string is bowed on a fiddle, other strings will vibrate a small amount, along with the bowed string, depending on which note is being played. (This is a rather weak explanation…)This is known as sympathetic vibration. It the other strings are not well tuned, the sympathetic vibration is muted, or non-existent, and the fiddle doesn’t “sing”. It becomes harder to hear when any note is well in tune. Recently, I put a fine tuner on my G string, after playing without one for years. Typically, my G string was tuned a little flat, and I never worried about it much. However, I did notice an improvement in my tone and pitch on all strings after I added the fine tuner to the G string!!! It is more difficult to play on a fiddle with just one string a little out of tune, even if one never plays on the mistuned string.

I have been told, (I haven’t done the math on this) that the difference between a perfect fifth, and a tempered fifth is so small that the difference is almost inconsequential. There are days when I have difficulty tuning, and get better results using a chromatic tuner, than I get by ear. But, I can still hear that the fiddle is not at perfect fifths. On days when my ear is ‘crisp’, I believe that I tune the fiddle a good step better than an electronic tuner. Now that I have the fine tuner on the G-string, I seem to have more days when I am able to get the fiddle very well in tune.

Some tunes, especially slow airs, or marches played with drone strings, can be played with accuracy that is very close to the natural scale. It is attainable. On the other hand, I have been picking up tunes from recordings made by a great fiddler in the 1950’s, and when I slow the recording to half speed, I can clearly hear some notes with questionable pitch. Today, there are countless fiddlers with much better accuracy.

Re: intonation on the fiddle

Some thoughts about intonation in violin playing in general: we all hear in tune - that’s how we know if a note is off, even slightly. The only interference I know of between the intention of the next pitch played and the finger action, is hand tension, usually in the thumb, because its position opposite the fingers means we use it in most activities, but it’s almost unnecessary for playing fiddle (only for tactile feedback in orienting our left hand on the neck). My solution to this most common problem in violin-playing, is finding various ways to re-educate the thumb when practising, taking it right off the neck while playing slow, simple series of notes, then replace it without allowing it to start pushing or squeezing again. My own intonation has improved significantly since I’ve applied this exercise, and it works for my students too.
Best of luck!

Re: intonation on the fiddle

A few comments on some of the points raised previously.

Why not try using the old-fashioned tuning fork? They are still available at A440 pitch (and some other pitches), and don’t need batteries. They are indeed soft sounding, but you can usually find a quiet moment. At the start of a session I am usually aware if my A string is out of tune with concert pitch. If there are no pipe/box players present I’ll retune it to the tuning fork pitch. I then tune the D to the A as a perfect fifth by listening for the beat (or wah-wah) tone to disappear. Then tune the G to the D similarly, and then the E to the A. Then I do a final quick check, because changing the tension in one string can sometimes slightly alter the tension of an adjacent string. I tune at the same dynamic level that I’m going to play at, rather than a quiet whisper. The reason for this is that an open string played loudly will be sharper than when it is played softly. This effect is particularly noticeable on the floppier G and D strings. Get a cellist friend to illustrate this by playing his bottom string (the C) from pianissimo to fortissimo. In passing, it is possible on the cello to achieve the trick of playing as much as a semitone below the open C by applying heavy bow pressure in a certain way - this trick works better with a gut C.

The famous concert violinist Ruggiero Ricci once said that, when he was in the green room before he was due to come onto the platform to play a concerto he would always tune to the general pitch of the orchestra playing the previous work (a symphony or overture), rather than use a tuning device. One just hopes that the orchestra wasn’t playing in something ridiculous like D-flat minor!

A quick quiet check on tuning during a session can always be done using harmonics - lightly touch the E on the A string without pressing it to the fingerboard, and compare it with the first harmonic E on the E string (the octave halfway up the string), again touching the string lightly. If the strings are in exact fifths then the harmonics will sound the same pitch. Similarly with the other strings. This trick is frequently used by orchestral players and by soloists in the middle of a concerto, and in practice is inaudible to everybody except the player doing it.

Intonation (as opposed to tuning the strings) on a violin is very much an individual thing, depending on the context (and the player’s personal perception of intonation), as has been pointed out above. Try this experiment. First, make sure all the strings on the fiddle are tuned in exact fifths. Then find the F# harmonic on the D string. This isn’t very easy to find, but if you touch the string lightly in the vicinity of the F# you would normally play on the D string you’ll eventually find it. Keep that pitch in your ear. Now play the same pitch F# on the E string. Now play the struck or arpeggio chord (open)D-(open)A-F#(the one you’ve just found on the E string. If you have done this correctly you will have a very resonant singing sound. This F# will also sound appreciably flatter in pitch to the F# you would usually play. So now you have two quite different F#’s to worry about - sorry :)

Two fiddle players (or concert violinists for that matter) can never play mathematically exactly in tune with each other because of natural and inevitable differences in finger placement. Three or four players will sound better because the differences get averaged out. This is a reason why there are up to a dozen first violinists in a symphony orchestra and incidentally one reason why a solo violinist can make him/herself heard above the orchestra - the tone quality of the solo violin is different to the “average” tone of the violins in ensemble.

I have seen beginners on the fiddle holding all their other fingers down when they are, for example, playing the A on the E string with the 4th finger. This immediately tightens the hand, preventing free and relaxed movement, and makes the acquisition of good intonation next to impossible. I would advocate having only 1 finger at a time in contact with the string, unless you’re playing a roll or other ornament, or playing a chord or rapid arpeggio. This enables the fingers to move much more easily and for fine adjustment to intonation to be made instantaneously. Vibrato, if required, will also be a lot easier.

The human ear, according to physiologists, cannot distinguish between pitches less than 5 cents apart (50 cents = 1 semitone). Also, when more than 16 or 18 notes are being played in 1 second, the human ear cannot distinguish the individual notes. This might go some way to explaining the mysteries of the ultra-fast ornaments played by some of the top fiddlers!
