Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

Added by Jerone on September 26th, 2023

Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

I’ve wanted to have this conversation for a long time, but I didn’t know how to approach it because it’s controversial and loaded. However, now the time has come for me to talk about this, because it’s something that matters. I ask that if we’re going to dig into this, please let’s respect each other and acknowledge our potential cultural differences and outlooks.

Disclaimer: It is not my view that someone doesn’t ever have a unique claim to a thing, and my questions and anecdotes shouldn’t be interpreted to suggest such.

To contextualize why I’m bringing this up now, I have an announcement: I’m selling the sheet music for my transcriptions of Irish tunes. I mean that literally to the tense. I am grateful to say that someone from my small Youtube community purchased two of my transcriptions today (09/25). I’m proud of this because it’s the fulfillment of several short and long term goals. However, as an artist, I feel that we have certain… responsibilities and obligations. One of those is maintaining the dignity of our subjects. What I have going on with transcribing Irish tunes for piano is just the beginning, and one of my main themes as an artist is appreciation and enthusiasm for traditional music from around the world. Understanding cultural appreciation is requisite.

One thing that has become abundantly clear from my experience as a street performer is cultural pride. There is a quote I picked up on in my languages studies, that I just learned is from Nelson Mandela - “If you talk to a man in a language he understands, that goes to his head. If you talk to him in his own language, that goes to his heart.". The look on people’s faces when you play them the music of their people; The twitch of their ear, the pride in their voice, the twinkle in their eye, the lift in their body language. It’s to die for. Now, a lot of my experience as a musician has been self-centered. The joy of learning, the thrill of the challenge, the pleasure of self-satisfaction. Ah, but now being able to give something so meaningful to people… cultural pride… it’s a different form of fulfillment. And I hope to deliver to the best of my ability and to the depths of my sincerity.

With all of that being said, I’m here to ask: What does cultural appreciation mean to you, and when is the line crossed into appropriation? Or, in your opinion, does it even matter? It’s such a strange conversation, no matter what context we’re discussing it in. In the one sense, you have the fact that we are all people and we can’t help what speaks to us and what moves us. Yet, in another sense, we have the identities, histories, and experiences that are shared amongst groups of people; facets that are abundantly meaningful and probably shouldn’t be taken so lightly and casually. Then there is the question of where our place is as artists. I can’t say for sure but I would think that there is a consensus amongst us, as artists, that there are human conditions shared by all of us; And in that respect, what does appropriation really mean?

I can’t speak to Irish music, because I’m not Irish.

I can speak to Appalachian music. I come from a musical family, my grandfather was a locally respected fiddler and banjo player. I feel that indigenous mountain music is my heritage. I feel pride in it and a certain ownership of it.

These people, I assume, would be respected by native players.

And there are times when the outsiders playing our music feel like they’re floating in a fuzzy middle zone.

↳ Posted by Richard D Cook on September 26th, 2023.

Re: Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

Certainly, Jerone, your post brings up a complex and nuanced topic that many of us in the traditional music community have pondered. First off, congratulations on achieving your goals with your transcriptions and for the meaningful connections you’ve made through your music.

To your question about cultural appreciation vs. appropriation, I think it’s a delicate balance. Traditional music, often labeled as “Irish,” “Scottish,” or otherwise, is a melting pot of influences. It’s a living tradition that has been shaped by various cultures and historical contexts. So, when we engage with it, we’re not just interacting with a set of notes but a rich tapestry of human experience.

In my two decades of involvement in traditional music, I’ve found that the key is to approach it with respect and a willingness to understand its roots and nuances. It’s about more than just playing the tunes; it’s about understanding their history, their emotional core, and the communities from which they originated.

Cultural appropriation becomes a concern when there’s a lack of this understanding and respect, especially if it leads to the commodification or trivialization of another’s culture. As artists, we do have a responsibility to honor the dignity and complexity of the subjects we engage with.

So, in summary, I believe that as long as we approach traditional music (or any cultural art form) with a genuine desire to understand and respect it, we’re on the path of appreciation rather than appropriation. It’s a continuous learning process, and open dialogues like this are crucial for growth and understanding. Thank you for initiating this important conversation.

Re: Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

Yes, I’ve thought about this subject too but resisted starting a thread as I realised it would be very controversial.

Basically, I see nothing wrong with enjoying and playing the music itself.

“Black face Minstrels” and other similar attempts to imitate the culture and people who originally performed the music are now, quite rightly, frowned upon.

However, I’d see no harm in having a pint of Guinness while I’m playing Irish tunes or dressing like your average Irish person who wouldn’t look any different to me anyway. Wearing costumes or dressing as a leprechaun (I know, that’s nonsense anyway) might be a little insulting.

On the other hand, it would be OK to join a dance group or musical band where costumes are worn if it’s done respectfully. We have lots of Irish dance outfits globally and although many do have Irish people and descendents, there are other interested participants too.

Same with “Scottish” pipe bands and tartan events etc. We have people from all over the world attending such events and taking part.

As Tom says, the real issue is that of “ “Taking” from another culture in an exploitative or disrespectful way”. So, that might include dressing up as a “pretendy Scotsman/woman” and so on and waving a haggis around, or American bands singing hackneyed Scotttish songs while draped in saltires and so on. Just one extreme example but, of course, many Scots entertainers are just as guilty of misrepresenting themselves to “please the tourists” and so on.

It works both ways, of course. Scots and Irish musicians may be very interested in old time American and/ or blue grass music etc but dressing up like cowboys, hill billies etc and shouting “Hee haw” might not always be considered appropriate either.

I think it’s all down to to respect at the end of the day but we should be allowed and encouraged to enjoy each other’s music.

Re: Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

Some great comments so far, lots to contemplate.
I think the attitude of the musician towards the spirit of the music, their attitude towards using that music in any way, their attitude towards their audience, and some influence of self-perception in action, will determine the outcome. If you love the music, appreciate the cultural background and want to offer the audience authentic value, then you’ll use processes and principles guided by that vision.
Alternatively, if you use the principle of being a parasite or con artist who is trying to artificially lure people into listening to your plagiarized music so they give you their money like hypnotized cattle at a feed lot, then you’ll use a process designed accordingly and produce a product that rings of superficiality, falseness and aggressive usury, at least to those who know the music’s history and deep authenticity. Someone mentioned cowboy hats. Exactly…..

“Traditional” music communities tend to integrate great appreciation for the deep roots of the music origins and character, no matter how they may then alter it within the vision of remaining authentic. If you yourself aren’t the musician who created the music, then by simply playing it, you’re bringing in your personal adaptation, a shift of some kind, it can’t be helped. But that by itself doesn’t indicate appropriation or false process. It can be a pathway to learning and further appreciation; musical growth, and the audience should be able to detect that naturally.

“Pop” and highly commercialized music industry practices tend to produce musicians, processes, market manipulations and music that intends first and foremost to take money and corral more dependable cattle-like reactions. Especially if oriented to getting on pop radio or pop market sales lists.

Each musician will need at some point to clearly define what their definition of musical success is. It may also depend on what stage of musical learning you’re at. Many musicians, quite innocently, start out learning about music with exposure to some kind of pop music, and from there, tend to want to dig into the roots and more authentic cultural expressions of different kinds of music. Many musicians may find themselves taking time, years actually, to grow into appreciation of some music. “Appreciation” becomes a long, healthy path.

Creating music often involves cultural appreciation and integrating many influences into your own music. That’s appreciation if you genuinely want to carry forward an aspect of music’s cultural heritage that you value.

There are examples worldwide of this duality in action, between the roots and origins of music being nourished by musicians, vs the superficial dabblings into music done by crass commercialism, or on a tangent, that third option of people with little musical experience starting to appreciate music and mimicking the music they like, likely including a lot of pop music to begin with. Eventually, a musician may find a realization where they ask “what am I doing?” and hopefully they dig deep when looking for the answer.

The problems, the negatives, happen with bad attitude towards the use of music, a predatory attitude towards the audience, or in some situations not having access or experience of the more deep roots of music and traditional factors influencing music. It really helps to learn from traditional music from around the world. Don’t stop with the Irish. Consider Hawaiian, Egyptian, African of all kinds, blues, ragtime, bluegrass, jazz, South American of all kinds, Chinese, North American First Nations, and if you really want to learn fast, go to Open Mic nights where solo singer-songwriters show up to play their own songs. Surely you can find some of those. YouTube is a great resource. Artist websites are a great resource. Find radio stations that play a lot of unique non-Pop music.

I think the most deeply musical and successful musicians are those who focus on trying to transfer great musical experiences to their audience, from the deeply musical experiences they’ve learned to develop for themselves first.

A question lurking in the corner of all this (and equally appropriate for the yes and no camps) is whether Irish music is (anymore?) the music from and of one particular small part of Europe - or whether it is now just a convenient label for a genre of music that has become universal. I’m not saying it can’t be both - but which way one leans may be relevant for those wider attitudes.

The former implies those of us who are not Irish or at least living in Ireland are indeed borrowing or visiting someone else’s culture - and therefore presumably ought to treat it with a degree of respect as we presumably would in any such situation anywhere.

If on the other hand you consider ITM to be just a genre of music played globally that happens to have its origins in Ireland, then I guess it belongs to us all and we can do with it what the hell we want.

Personally, I tend strongly towards the former (but use the latter to justify myself to myself as I can’t change where I’m from…)

I’ve only ever had great welcomes from Irish players, but maybe that is *because* I’m careful and respectful?
I leave it up to the Irish to mess with the music as only they probably can get away with it. It bothers me when I see other people messing with it. Which perhaps begs the question whether a nation can misappropriate its own culture - it think some of the modern Irish takes on ITM-derivative music may actually be doing that.

I’m also conscious that like it or not, as an Englishman, history perhaps dictates that my countrymen need to be a little more careful than most. Those from other nations perhaps aren’t carrying the baggage that is there between the English and the Irish; no matter how well we have got over it now (which in general I believe we have), some people’s memories are long, and I would rather not cause offence, no matter how unintentionally.

Re: Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

I think it’s all about “respect”, as I said earlier.

Most people from all cultures and countries would likely be delighted that we are enjoying and playing their music although some might comment along the lines of “It’s not quite right”, “That’s nae way to play a strathspey, son”, “You haven’t got the nyaaah” etc. :-)
However, it’s a different matter when you deliberately appropriate other parts of their culture and traditions just for “effect”, commercial gain or, even worse, to make fun of it.

There is a subtle dividing line between respecting and complimenting a culture and disrespect. Quite often, the “offenders” might not realise what they are doing.

For example, in the small town where I live “Here in Scotland”, there has been a long history of fishing tradition. Until recently, the surviving fishwives and their descendants would take part in an annual walk and there would be celebrations. There are still local people who might carry such things on but I think financial considerations could be a factor as to why this hasn’t been continued.
However, there are a couple of women not from the area who enjoy dressing up as fishwives and make an appearance from time to time at the harbour and at other local coastal towns.
I’m sure they mean well but they do this “off their own bat”, so to speak, and aren’t part of the local community as such nor its traditions. The costumes are different, for a start.

Of course, there’s no outright animosity from the local people. We’re all too nice for that but I sense that some of the locals might feel that part of our traditions and history have been taken away.

Re: Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

Yes, good thread. Hardly a new topic but a fascinating one. I think the ‘Irishness’ issue needs to be seen for what it is – just one expression of a far wider cultural matter. It’s more relevant, too, to “world” musics than either internationalised pop or internationalised art music. Jonny Jay, I think you’re on the button.

But I don’t think there is anything that is an even remotely water-tight ‘answer’. Perhaps the best approach is to see that as a positive thing – it keeps us on our toes. I’m not sure that sweeping the question under the carpet helps.

I doubt many people wanting an Italian meal would go to a German restaurant; there is an implicit acceptance that making the best Italian food requires some kind of cultural insider-ness. Even bad Italian food still has a kind of Italian-ness…. People will go a long way and pay a lot for the Italian accents coming out of the kitchen – and the things it implies. And avoid any “Italian” ready-meals you see in your local supermarket like the plague.

So it is with music – I saw Téada last weekend: great music - and as always the natural Irish accents and personalities were part of it. Later, I watched a clip of some Aussies playing perfectly good ITM, but the patter just didn’t feel right. I have the same issue myself… Having played the music with many Irish people, I am pretty sure my musical ‘accent’ isn’t quite right either – as with my “Italian” cooking. No matter that those people are always very generous about my efforts…. It is probably different from the inside looking out – or are they just being kind?

Seeking “Authenticity” risks being a middle-class affectation – but it can also be an expression of someone who has learned to appreciate the subtleties of the “real thing”. I don’t think that’s bad.

But it’s also possible to be too precious. We have to accept what we can’t change. The only other option is to remain locked in one’s own culture. My “Italian” food is very serviceable; it is practical in a mixed veggie/non veggie household. One can integrate even if not assimilate. I seem to have picked up some Irish turns of phrase over the years; what would have happened if I had moved to Ireland? I have been told by Irish friends that I am “part of it”. That means a lot. But never stop learning.

The thing to avoid is bastardising the original – worst of all, unknowingly (as I suspect Germans – and others – do to Italian food). You can’t get entirely rid of your own cultural trace. Accept that you will never be an indigenous player – but remain ever-open to learning from those who are. It’s easier than ever in the YouTube age. It possibly means playing the thing ‘straighter’ than the ‘natives’ do, because it is too easy to look like you are taking the rise.

What does bother me is those who play ITM as one amongst a dozen different styles.

Throwing in more and more colours or ingredients only cross-contaminates. You don’t end up with something extra-bright, just a grey mush. Many abominations (whether on the table or in music) come from people mixing too many ingredients, which I think cannot but happen if you spread yourself too widely. The issue is there as soon as you cross cultures, without exacerbating it. Fusion music *can* be brilliant - but more often it isn’t.

The risk is greatest from people who do not think – or care – about what they are doing. That’s the risk of the “I just play it” approach. I see the results regularly.

Irish music needs to keep a strong Irish element – the rest of us need to treat it as the Pole Star from which to guide our own efforts.

Re: Cultural Appreciation vs. Cultural Appropriation.

To me, cultural appropriation is quite limited as a definition. I’d see it as the inappropriate or exploitative use of cultural elements, or the theft or misuse of something essential or sacred to a group of people. There’s often (possibly always?) a significant power imbalance involved.

The music deserves respect as well as appreciation, and of course there are indigenous players with insights, histories, and ways of knowing that can’t be possessed by outsiders. But in broad terms, the tradition doesn’t feel quite as vulnerable to appropriation and misuse as some cultural artefacts (the styling of Black hair would be an example).

But it’s slippery, because Irish traditional music isn’t one thing. There are styles and traditions that have been strongly associated with, or used as identifiers by, particular localities or groups, eg travelling people. Perhaps there’s more potential there for ignorant stereotyping or the taking of something precious than there is from somebody making money from turning the Tarbolton into a synth pop tune, complete with drum machine.

I was born in Ireland quite a long time ago of Irish parents who were born in Ireland etc. My grandfather was a fiddler and I wound up, in my early twenties, owning and playing his fiddle. In late 1973—by then I was living in Canada—I teamed up with another Irishman, two American players and two Canadians to form what was to become a fairly successful Canadian Celtic band. In 1978 we were invited to open for the first ever Canadian appearance of a ‘Very Big Irish band’ Like any players of Irish traditional music, we looked up to this ‘very big Irish band’ and were thrilled to have the chance to meet them.

The concert night came. We were understandably nervous and hoped we could acquit ourselves satisfactorily.
In the end everything went off well and we rounded out the concert playing a couple of tunes together. After the show, I was chatting with the fiddler and the fluter when the bandleader/bouzouki player (the perceptive reader will by now have figured out who the ‘Very Big Irish band’ was) came over and took me aside. “You know” he said, “that was nice lads, but I think you’d be wise to stick to your own folk music.” A bit stunned, I tried to respond, but the noisy backstage scrum had moved on.

Who was it said “Don’t ever meet your heroes.”? Forty five years later I’m still trying to parse that remark. Needless to say, I brushed off the ‘advice’ and carried on. In the immortal words of the late great Fred Torak: “There’s only twelve notes folks and none of them belongs to nobody.”
