A trap for the unwary … :-(

If you play in a ceilidh/ceili band (or other situation where a PA system is needed) you’ve probably come across one of those recessed power sockets found on the floors of some stages. You simply lift up the trap door, plug in your power cable - then close it, being careful to route your cable via the cable-exit cutaway.

Last Saturday our ceilidh band played in a church - the performance area was in the chancel, and the floor of that chancel was tiled with large heavy-duty slate slabs. When we enquired about the location of the power source, the venue manager pointed to one of the floor slabs which was hinged. Sure enough, when I lifted the slab, the power sockets were in a recess beneath the slab. So I plugged in our gear, but - as there was no cable-exit cutaway - I gently lowered the slab, allowing it to rest on the cable.

When I switched on the PA, we had no power. So I lifted up the slab to investigate, only to discover that the razor-sharp edge of the slate slab has sliced clean through our heavy-duty power cable - just like a knife going through butter.

I mentioned what had happened to the venue manager and was told: “Oh, loads of people have done that!” Great, I thought - thanks for the retrospective warning.

Fortunately, we had had a spare cable and we were able to connect to a socket elsewhere. And lucky too that no-one caught their fingers under that slab … :-(

Re: A trap for the unwary … :-(

My favourite story is when I was playing for a wedding ceili(dh) near London. We were a 6- or 7-piece band, including drummer with a full kit, crammed onto a rather small stage in a marquee, which resulted in my being right at the back of the stage. We were playing Dingle Regatta for some dance or other and I deigned to participate in that terribly corny stand-up-sit-down charade in the third part (just what an English non-traditional-music-enlightened clientele wants). Unbeknown to me, however, during the standing up and sitting down, my chair was inching its way closer to the back edge of the stage, to the point where, on sitting down, my mandolin, chair and I disappeared down the back of the stage. Fortunately, the flimsy plastic garden chair I was sitting on absorbed most of the shock with its bendy legs, and the fact that there was only a small gap between the stage and the wall of the marquee meant the fall was further broken. So I got off with no more than a lightly grazed elbow and was able to return to the stage and carry on as if nothing had happened.

The tragedy, though, was the wasted comic potential - only the drummer and one of the dancers saw it happen.

All sounds familiar, Creadur - albeit without the Dingle Regatta jumping angle.

Normal stages are fine, but stages constructed from blocks are rarely big enough. Several times we’ve had chairs going over the edge in similar circumstances.

Ever played in a marquee where there is no stage? We have, many times - including once where the chairs that were provided for the band had thin tubular steel legs. As the evening progressed, we just sank further and further into the ground!

I guess that the only answer in these situations is to play standing up.

Further to problems with power sockets, we once played a gig in a remote village hall that hadn’t been re-wired for years. Our gear had plugs with (UK) standard rectangular pins - but the sockets in the hall would only accept plugs with round pins. Square plug in a round hole, so to speak.

We solved that one by borrowing the plug from a kettle that we found in the hall’s kitchen and re-wiring it to our system …

… Every band needs to carry a small toolkit to cope with such emergencies.

Playing in a pub with a
trio some years ago. I plugged my amp into the allocated power point only to find there was no power on the amp, which was strange because I checked it before leaving home. Checked the power point with my tester. It had power so started checking out the amp. Checked the fuse etc ….still no power. A friend lived about three mile from the pub and had a spare amp. He drove off to fetch it. Pub starting to fill up and the landlord starting to look panicky and by now all the pissing about was causing me to sweat buckets. Eventually my friend arrived with a spare crappy old amp. Plugged it in and it didn’t work. Plugged it in behind the bar and it worked. Managed to find a long extension lead that stretched from the bar to the stage. Plugged my own amp in and we had power. Started playing nearly an hour late. Later on I found out that the negative wire in the stage power point had come adrift hence the positive reading on my tester but no power on the amp. Another hard earned lesson never to be forgotten……….

Not fire- or electricity-related, but one gig we played in a marquee in the garden of a big house in the summer. They had a swimming pool out the back and we were invited to use it during the interval if we wanted. A dip sounded like a nice idea after playing a set in a marquee, so I changed and swam around the pool. Near the end of the interval I launched myself to the other side of the pool underwater, only to have my glasses come off (yes, I swim in them, otherwise it’s all furry). I saw them disappear into the bottom of the pool (which was quite deep at the diving end, way more than 6 feet deep). I realised I couldn’t drive back after the gig without my glasses so I *had* to rescue them. This involved holding my breath and swimming straight downwards to try to grab them. After a few attempts I was getting really tired, and found a snorkel and mask to help me look. By this time the band had started without me, so I could now hear wobbly music coming through the water as I went under. Pretty panicky time but I did find them, and eventually managed to grab them with my feet. I emerged to rejoin the band, somewhat dripping wet.

Re: A trap for the unwary … :-(

Ooh - I have another (nearly) electricity-related gig story. We were asked to play in a carnival in Sardinia. It turned out to be a 4-hour procession and we were given a float (basically a staged trailer pulled behind a vehicle). It was quite high up the back and because people go wild squirting silly-string, which is incredibly sticky, I decided to go up that end with my harp, despite not being that comfortable with heights. Cagliari has an electric tram system and I didn’t really become conscious of this until about 20 minutes into the event I felt something brush past the top of my head. Looking back at it, it was a little metal sign swinging from the overhead wires, with a “Danger High Voltage” message on it, with a lovely symbol of a harpist (probably) being electrocuted. I obviously must have looked rattled as some dignitary leapt onto the float and gabbled away in Italian, pointing alternately at me and at the overhead line. To this day I still don’t know if that meant “It’s OK, it’s been switched off”, or “Shit, you nearly died”. Anyway, for the remainder of the event I swapped places with another band-member who was shorter than me.
