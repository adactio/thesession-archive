Fiddles… fine tuners?

I recently purchased an antique fiddle with a single fine tuner on the E string. I struggle to keep it in tune with the main pegs so I am considering putting another 3 fine tuners on to ease this issue. I have heard however that adding fine tuners greatly reduces the value of antique instruments and I am wary to mess around with it too much. What are your thoughts? Whilst I’m not bothered about conserving its value for re-sale ( I bought it purely to play it) at the same time I do not want to reduce its entire worth..

Re: Fiddles… fine tuners?

Fine tuners are added to the tailpiece. They are no more part of the fiddle than the strings are. They make no difference to the value. HOWEVER!!! If you take the mechanism to its limit, you may find it is touching the belly of the fiddle, which will cause damage and loss of value.

Re: Fiddles… fine tuners?

Many would say that fine tuners adversely affect the *tone* of the violin; they will inevitably affect the tone in some way, although I have not experimented to find out exactly how or to what extent. The ratio between the speaking length of the string and the length of string behind the bridge (6:1?) is said to be critical to the tone, owing to the resulting resonances in the short portion of string; most fine tuners upset this ratio, although there are some modern designs (usually on a tailpiece with built-in fine tuners) that preserve the ratio. But this is all a question of set-up and is entirely reversible; unless the tuners do touch the belly (which, anyway, would probably have a worse effect on the the tone than the above) and cause damage thus, I cannot see why fine tuners would affect the value.

There is another option, in the form of these geared pegs http://www.musicroom.com/se/ID_No/0704734/details.html
if you are prepared to spend a little more money.

Re: Fiddles… fine tuners?

The cheap ‘n’ nasty tuners are also more likely to cause string breakages. Tone-wise, add-on fine tuners don’t do anything for the tone - to be a little technical, they affect the important ratio of the after-length to the bridge-nut length. A safer and better option in every way is to have a tail-piece with integral tuners fitted (a lot less expensive than geared pegs, good though they are, which have to be fitted professionally).

Unless you know how to, and have had experience, I’d recommend a luthier or violin technician fit the new tail-piece, if only to to get the after-length right and to be able to handle the possibility of the soundpost falling over. It’s not an expensive job to do, and could be done while-you-wait.

But, personally I’d go along with piobagusfidil’s recommendation and have the pegs sorted out. Fine tuners are only really necessary for steel-core strings, and well-fitted pegs should be able to handle everything else.

Re: Fiddles… fine tuners?

Bazfaz, the after-length (between bridge and tailpiece should 1/6 of bridge to nut. I measure these distances from where the string leaves the nut to middle of the bridge, and from the middle of the bridge to where the string contacts the tailpiece or fine tuner. There’s no point in measuring accurate to less than 1mm because there are so many other variables when playing, mainly movement of the bridge, change in length and tension of the string as it stretches while played, and the winding on the tailpiece end of the string.
The theoretical basis of this 1/6 ratio is that the frequency of the afterlength on one string (say the G) should be the same as a strong harmonic of the next string up (the D), and so on. Getting that ratio right may help in reducing wolf notes, but I can’t promise!

Re: Fiddles… fine tuners?

I think screwing into the peg holes makes it a NON- reversible process: you are damaging original wood, and if you want to revert to proper pegs it will mean reaming out the holes to the depth of the thread, then either fitting oversized pegs (and risking bursting the back of the pegbox) or bushing the holes, which is expensive.

Why would you want to switch back? You would if you ever want to sell the instrument. Violin buyers are a very conservative lot, and fitting mechanical pegs will wipe a lot of value off the value of the instrument. It’s like fitting plastic handles to a Chippendale sideboard - it might be very practical, but the value of antique objects is directly proportional to their originality.

Re: Fiddles… fine tuners?

The actuality of course is that the inside of the peg holes have to be near enough a mirror finish for conventional pegs to run smoothly, and according to the fitting instructions, not only are you cutting a thread into that surface, but there is glue involved too. So reaming would be an absolute necessity if reverting to conventional pegs. And reaming by just 1/2mm on a 30:1 taper runs the peg in an additional 15mm, making oversized pegs or bushings a necessity.

As to whether you think it should devalue the instrument or not, you’re probably right - on a factory fiddle it shouldn’t affect the value. But in the real world it does. Violinists don’t like buying instruments with odd fittings, they don’t even like Wittner style tailpieces, They’d rather buy an instrument with an ebony tailpiece, fit a Wittner themselves, and believe that the ebony one they keep tucked somewhere safe is the ‘original’. So anything you do to ‘modernise’ or ‘improve’ a fiddle reduces its salability, and so its value.
