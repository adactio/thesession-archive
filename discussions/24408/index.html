Art or Science?

Following from earlier posts, I would like to put forward a hypothesis that has been taking shape in my mind for years; but which I have never really given full consideration. It is this:- there are two distinct types of musicians, which you might call the intuitive and the analytical. The former ‘just plays the effing tune’ the latter has to know what key it is in before he can make a sound. I suppose I first became aware of the difference in my own family. My brother was scientific where I was artistic, he analytical where I was intuitive. He was a draughtsman, I was a fine artist. We both learned the pipes, and then the guitar, but he stopped after a while because he thought it was hard work, while I have continued because to me it’s pure pleasure.
Over the years I have met several people of both types. There are those who can pick up a tune as if born to it, who might know nothing of music theory, or who can’t read the dots, yet have accumulated the names of chords, keys and notes though osmosis, as it were, from fellow musicians. And there are those who can (and usually do) tell you instantly what key you are in, what the chord progressions should be. Who talk of the circle of fifths and intervals and augmented and diminished as though it is second nature.
There are undoubtedly those who by dint of hard work and sheer interest have expanded their abilities to cover both camps, but I suspect that the division is as real as left- and right-handedness. I would be interested to know what you think, and into which category, if any, you would put yourself.

Re: Art or Science?

Analytical vs. intuitive also raises the question of nature or nurture. For me, both have been a mix of both. It’s not a matter of either-or.

I’m ambidextrous--eating, writing, even pitching baseball, everything worked with either hand. So all my life, when people asked me whether I was right or left handed, I answered, “Both.” In the sense that I have both a right and a left hand, and I use them both, and one is not dominant in the way it is for most people. I think I was born this way, to some extent, but also ended up developing the two-handedness through sports and especially juggling (which at a professional level emphasizes symmetry--you have to be able to do any move or pattern equally well with either hand or in either direction--clockwise and counterclockwise). Also, my dad had started out a lefty but contracted polio when he was nine. it paralyzed his left side for the better part of a year. Part of his treatment was to do everything right handed (the sisters at the Catholic hospital he went to saw lefty-ness as the devil’s work, so this presented an opportunity). Dad recovered fully and went on to be a powerful switch-hitter in baseball and could write fluently with either hand.

I think my brain works the same way my hands do. It just so happens that I have *both* a right and left hemisphere and I use them both. :-)

More than some activities, music seems to fire up the whole noggin, not just one side or a few disparate centers. I wouldn’t be surprised if research someday shows that music enhances that whole-brain capacity for lots of other activities, too. I suspect a life spent playing music activates analytical *and* intuitive abilities and pathways that then work in other areas of our lives.

That said, I first learned music by ear and feel. When I started teaching music at 15, I dealt with students who wanted sheet music and tablature and theory. My older brother was getting a music degree, so I picked his brain and raided his library. It hasn’t hurt me any to be able to read written notation and to have a middling understanding of music theory. And it wasn’t hard to learn. It certainly makes me a more versatile teacher, though I still mostly teach by ear (cuz it’s music, not fingerpainting).

Relying on your strengths will get you only so far. If you really want to be good at something, to reach your full potential, you’ll need to change your weaknesses to strengths, too.

I have a good friend who is a fine flute player, and has worked hard to become one. He’s an engineer-type by trade, and a very analytical sort of brain type. We’re total opposites with that, and we joke about it, themath fluter and mad fiddler.

When you get right down to it, we’re hearing a tune and playing a tune. You must be able to technically play your instrument in order to simply express the tunes in your head. Different strokes for different folks and all that.

Analysis and science are not needed for folks to hear and play tunes. They can help some, but not all, and often I see folks stressing over technical things unnecessarily. Usually a little nudge to just play the damn tune boyo is all the Irish Zen they need to let them escape the scientific trap they’ve set for themselves.

Of course, some of us really need to agonize over their musical slide rulers, and that’s OK too, but I often think of my tech geek fluter buddy. We do gigs together in a band, and loves the technical stuff about setting up the mikes, sound checking, dorking with the levels on the mixer, etc.

A guitarist who can quickly grok the chords behind a new tune is likely just picking up on familiar patterns. Whether s/he has a vocabulary to describe that process makes a difference in how s/he and others perceive the accomplishment. If the guitarist says,“ I dunno, I just hear it,” we think s/he’s intuitive. Re: Art or Science?

David, I think Batlady is right -- you’re doing a lot of different things, but for one outcome. In the driving example, the desired outcome is to make the car go without hitting anything or careening off the road. If you’re watching TV, reading email, surfing thesession.org, and writing a paper, all those things have different, and unrelated, intended outcomes.

I don’t know if that holds any water with cognitive science, but it’s a nice theory.

The better theory is probably this: with driving and playing the pipes, a lot of the tasks you do are in fact unconscious (for the experienced driver or piper) You don’t think about how much pressure you’re putting on the accelerator to steer the car, nor do you think about how much pressure to put on the bag or how many times to pump the bellows to get the proper noise out of the pipes. So while you’re doing say half a dozen different tasks, you’re consciously thinking about less than half of them.

I’d love to meet someone who can read, write a paper and post on thesession.org unconsciously. That would be cool.
