Views on Bb Major & Eb Major in Playing ITM and Trad Scottish Tunes?

I would really like to know folks’ views on on these Flat Keys for traditional music here.
Saw 1 inquiry asking for tune suggestions in Bb Major - from 3 years ago (https://thesession.org/discussions/47654).
F major, and the ‘relative minors’ of Bb major & Eb major are represented in the drop-down box for tune searches here - but NOT Bb major or Eb major.

Happened to be thinking about this today because it’s been a while since I played ‘Fly By Night’ & thought I’d check myself. I had learned Joe Burke’s version in Eb major - pretty much the 4th setting (https://thesession.org/tunes/3122), but saw that setting says Fdor. I realized there was no choice (and I realize this is NOT a ‘teaching site’), but got me curious.

Originally, I thought maybe there just aren’t that many traditional tunes in Flat Keys… but I keep running across good tunes that are.
So I did a quick query at The Traditional Tune Archive. Irish tunes, from Ireland in the key of ‘E’ with 3 flats - popped up about 150 tunes. Did the same with the key of ‘B’ with 2 flats - different tunes, but about the same amount. Didn’t even query about traditional Scottish tunes.

I expect someone will tell me tunes can be played more easily in other keys - which may be true on some instruments, but don’t think that’s the point.
Aren’t ANY tunes in Bb major, or Eb major, being played at sessions?

There may not be as many tunes as in D major, but are we missing out by not including them here, not making it easy to share them?

There was a Fiddle Hell workshop (last year?) on helping (mostly fiddlers) learn to play tunes in flat keys. I sat in because I was curious as to why the workshop was even offered & surprised to learn quite a few fiddlers avoiding anything with flats! I was wondering if some folks just get too comfortable in a couple keys & don’t see a reason to venture outside the ‘comfort zone’ - is this an issue?

Re: Views on Bb Major & Eb Major in Playing ITM and Trad Scottish Tunes?

I’m guessing everyone already know this, but it’s important to distinguish between the concepts of “tunes in Bb major or Eb major” and a “session tuned to Bb or Eb”.

The first are extremely rare, the second is essentially just everyone playing tunes exactly as they would as far as fingerings on a standard tuned instrument, but on instruments that are tuned sharp or flat of standard pitch, for example using an Eb or Bb whistle instead of D. The two concepts don’t really have any relationship.

re tunes in flat keys - “there is a deficit of good tunes in the key of Fmaj” (quoting Ryan McKasson). that’s one flat, lots of tunes in Dm. two flats, lots of tunes in Gm, not so many in Bfmaj. more flats? “special effects time”.

re fiddle hell - I also was at that flat keys session by Amy Cann (of Catharsis fame), she is an excellent teacher and she made it obvious that key of C is a flat key (in violin/mandolin).

re flat sessions - I once accidentally caused one, brought my new delrin F flute (by Dave Copley), for the next hour, all fluters happily played their favourite tunes on it, all fiddlers fumed.

re practicality - sessioneers who bring one key-of-D whistle or one keyless Irish flute will be forced to sit out any flat key tunes. (yes, they should bring the C flute, and the Bf flute, and the F flute and learn to play Boehm flute in every key, sure thing).

re why tunes in flat keys - Dave Wiesler (a well known ECD tune smith) has a youtube demo where he shows how instruments resonate differently in different keys. Fiddle is most bright and resonant in Amaj (lots of open strings), least resonant in e.g. Cm (zero open strings). This gives a tune smith a handle on the mood they want to create - bright/resonant vs dark/somber.

Re: Views on Bb Major & Eb Major in Playing ITM and Trad Scottish Tunes?

“The GHB, although nominally pitched in A, in fact is pitched in B flat so playing in that key should not be foreign to Scottish players.”

As Michael points out there’s a distinction to be made between

1) playing in your ordinary keys and having the music come out in different keys due to you not playing a Concert Pitch instrument, and

2) playing, on your Concert Pitch instrument, in a key that’s outside the keys ordinarily employed on that instrument.

So it is with Highland pipers that though their music is coming out in the keys of Bb and Eb they’re fingering in the written keys of A and D. Just put sheet music in front of them of a tune written in Eb Major- it’s utterly foreign to them.

The question then is, what about Scottish fiddle players? All I know is that our local Strathspey & Reel Society plays everything in the usual written keys, so pipe tunes are in A Mixolydian (not Bb Mixolydian).

Some historical background:

Playing on the fiddle in the flat keys has always been more of a north of Ireland thing. This is likely due, of course, to proximity to Scotland, where it’s always been part of the music. Jimmy Shand commissioned the 1st B/C/C# button box with full stradella basses (bass/counterbass plus major/minor/7th/diminished chords) in the late 1930s because he was losing business to piano accordion players, who could navigate around any key with ease. Learning tunes from books and local composers coming up with tunes in any key has been a thing for centuries now; William Marshall came up with tunes with up to 5 flats, and famously said his music “wasn’t for bunglers.”

The famous Sligo fiddlers Michael Coleman and James Morrison recorded tunes in C/F/Bb/Eb, and this was something later fiddlers of an ambitious bent like Sean Maguire picked up on. Sometimes it was ignored - Morrison’s recording of his composition, the Skylark reel, was in C, but people just played it in D for the sake of the wind instruments. He followed that with Maud Millar - in F. No one followed his lead there, either.

Fiddlers in NYC still trot out tunes in the flat keys, something that was handed down to them firsthand from the above mentioned Sligo musicians. And it’s still very much alive in Donegal.

It isn’t that big a deal to play on the fiddle, either. A Scottish friend who plays the piano accordion knows tunes like the Banks and President Garfield, I would always play them in the original key, but he’d just want to play them in G…

Billy McComiskey composed the Flowers of Brooklyn and the Palm Tree in Eb/Bb to show you could play in the remote flat keys on the B/C box - assuming you’re as talented as him…I just play the Fs of Br in D.

My understanding was that the GHB was originally pitched lower, and that pipers’ settings and ornamentation were as all over the map as their colleagues in Ireland. The Bb thing was to get them in tune with brass bands, all part of Victorian England - Queen Vic was quite a nut for all things Caledonian, mind - and the technique/music was set in stone by Willie Ross - not to be confused with a Willie Ross who later made some wonderful 78s. But I never read up on the history of the big pipes much.

Re: Views on Bb Major & Eb Major in Playing ITM and Trad Scottish Tunes?

It’s important to remember that the pitch standard of A=440 is a modern one and any time you’re talking about pitch in a historical context - pre-1960, say - you need to be careful about what is really meant. The other issue when addressing recordings is that it can be very difficult to know the correct playback speed. If I had to guess, I’d say the Willie Ross recording is too slow, and hence flatter than it was in reality.

There are various examples of chanters floating around that are substantially flatter than modern instruments but they all predate A=400 as a standard, even though some can be reeded to this pitch. Most chanters can be balanced to any point within a semitone or so.

> I’d imagine the leader or Pipe Major would have to decree the exact required pitch and everyone else would fall into suit? Or would the band members all be required to purchase “standard” chanters?

Typically a band supplies chanters and reeds. The exact pitch is more of a process than a target, because it varies so much according to climate. You can’t mindlessly set a number and then chase it - the aim is to be at the right pitch on the contest field. Pitch creep has really dropped off in the last couple of decades, though it’s still going on.

@TheSS:
“I don’t percieve the bottom note as being a Bb. It’s still a D, even if it isn’t really D on an A=440 scale. ”
A very nicely succinct description of transposition. The bottom note / 6-fingered note (flute/whistle) is always D, “actual” pitch doesn’t really matter.

Classical players (not string players though) should get this, too, because it’s how all wind instruments work. No one wants to learn a new fingering pattern when switching between brass instruments or saxophones, etc. So just rewrite the music so that the “actual” pitch desired by the composer or conductor comes out while the player just plays the easier fingering…

One fun fact, Irish musicians use the D note as the reference - so we call an alternate pitched instrument by what the “actual” note comes out as (Bb, C, etc). Classical musicians use the C note as the reference - so our D instruments are their C instruments (same thing) and our Bb instruments are their Ab instruments.

*“actual” meaning the concert pitch at A440, which isn’t always the best reference, especially for instruments based on historical instruments that don’t follow A440, such as my B pipes (which are closer to B at A431)
