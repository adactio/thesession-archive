Tunes as part magic spell, part currency…has something been lost?

People used to ‘have’ tunes, and they used to give them to eachother.

When I listen to older musicians talking (on podcasts mainly of course, the irony 😊 ) the sense that tunes were hard won and in some cases jealously guarded is clear. A musician who ‘had great tunes’ was one to be envied.

Tunes were part currency, part magic spell.

Tunes survived or thrived if they were remembered and played - a Darwinian process.

While the modern plethora of recordings and annnotated versions is amazing and opens up the tradition to far more people than would otherwise be the case….has it dulled the magic?

The greatest downside is perhaps the emergence of a very broad delocalised and common mega repertoire.

Re: Tunes as part magic spell, part currency…has something been lost?

Certainly, there has always been push and pull between technology and the tradition. And there has always been people that have the magic and people that don’t. So I guess the answer to your question depends on how you look at it.

The fact that more people have been exposed to it because of the technology has probably made the average quality of the music go down… But the fact that more people have been exposed to it also means that there is more magic happening, too. Let’s say that in 1880, there were a thousand people that played Irish music, and 70% of them had “the magic”. (These are just made up numbers, of course). Then after people started recording the music for wider consumption (like Michael Coleman), say, in 1940, there were five thousand people that were playing the music, but only 50% of them were “magical”, did the music get dulled because the percentage of magic dropped by 20%? Or did the music have new shine, because the number of “magical” players increased from 700 to 2500?

It’s all a matter of perspective, and I, for one, tend to believe that the overall increase in magic outweighs the drop in percentage of magical players… And if you think of it, the magical players are all the more magical because the technology helps them spread the magic all the way around the globe, instead of just to their town.

Re: Tunes as part magic spell, part currency…has something been lost?

I am in no doubt that recordings are a detrimental, homogenizing effect, particularly when they are used for ear learning. I have always preferred to find tunes by listening to recordings, but then learn them from dots (or sometimes a hybrid system where I transcribe a set of dots from the recording and learn from that) That way I get the essence of the tune, but play it in my own style, not copying someone else’s.

But I think the area where technology has had the biggest negative impact is in new composition: in the olden days when you wrote a tune you would start with a simple phrase, build upon it and play it over and over, trying various variations, gradually refining and polishing it until you had the finished article. What some composers today seem to do is just noodle in front of a microphone until they play something they quite like, then go back and transcribe it from the recording, job done. And the end result is almost always completely forgetable.

Re: Tunes as part magic spell, part currency…has something been lost?

Those old Irish florins (and shillings and halfpennies) would turn up in English change occasionally and I think they counted as legal tender, being the same size and weight as the British ones. Irish currency was pegged to Sterling for a long while, wasn’t it? I was always very excited to come across one and I’d try and hang on to it… Until the lure of football cards became too great. All those pre-decimal Irish coins had lovely animal designs.

A lot of the threads on here seem to be attempts to pin-down and define what a “session” is, and ITM more generally. I’m not really sure if that’s even possible, let alone worthwhile. I’ll try and explain what I mean…

We are involved in an oral tradition which means so many different things to so many MILLIONS of different people, all over the world. I’m not Irish and I don’t pretend to be, but, if I were, it occurs to me that I would celebrate the trad. music as an incalculable gift my country has given to the world. ITM’s universality is one of its great strengths.

It seems to me that no-one can say for definite when what people consider traditional Irish folk-music began or when and from where it’s influenced, except in round and vague terms. That’s part of the thrill, of course: every time someone plays an old tune, they can imagine a line of musicians who played it before, but not the one who actually dreamt it up.

Meanwhile, all the innovations which have blessed or cursed this music since that mysterious composer wrote the tune (standardised pitch, written notation, competitions, music licensing, banjos, bouzoukis, audio recording, bloody DADGAD tuning etc.) have gradually changed it along the way. Along with people with fallible human memories trying to remember how it went in the first place, of course.

There’s nothing anyone can do about this and to suggest otherwise is delusional nonsense. I am automatically sceptical of anyone or any organisation which tries to preserve traditions (or tunes) at some arbitrary point. Certain political movements try the same trick: in England constantly harking back to a sort of mythical inter-war period of friendly Bobbies and red telephone boxes… And only white faces, of course.
But I digress. Unfortunately, the dichotomy facing all tradition is whether to risk adapting or risk stagnation. Deciding which bits to keep and which bits to change can’t be done by committee, but is decided by usage and almost imperceptible, incremental tweaks.

All things evolve or die: new generations bring new ideas, older generations have a choice to either try to adapt (willingly or not) or jealously keep the old way going until there’s only one or two of the old gang left, raging at the dying of the light in metaphorical garden sheds, shaking their heads at the impetuosity of (relative) youth. Time and the very tradition they love will pass them by, eventually. Sorry for my rambling; it’s 38°C, if that’s any excuse.

Re: Tunes as part magic spell, part currency…has something been lost?

There’s a phrase that’s come in useful for me from time to time: The road to hell is paved with least resistance.

Most technologies exist because they make something easier to do that had previously been difficult or at least effortful. This is fine, but it’s useful to remember that sometimes the hard way is going to be the right way. There are people that I’ve seen showing up at sessions for years - literally for five or ten years in some cases - carrying their books of tunes or more recently their phones. They sit there and when they can determine (or think they can determine) what tune is being played, they look up the dots and play whatever it is they read there. Sometimes it’s close to what’s being played, sometimes not. They never seem to mind. When I ask them about this, they always have a reason why they can’t learn tunes at home, or why it’s hard for them to pick up tunes by ear, and as evidence they point to their lack of progress in learning tunes. I’m talking about people who have been coming to sessions for years and might know ten tunes, sort of.

The technology of written music enables this behavior, but it would be silly to “blame” the technology (not least because “technology” is not an ethical agent, has no ethical responsibilities, and makes no ethical judgements). The technology of tune-recognition software coupled with databases such as the monster that we’ve all assembled here over the last few decades has made it easier than ever to not learn tunes. This does not seem to have brought great joy to anyone - either the people who are using this new freedom to its full advantage, and are busy not learning any tunes, or to the people who would like to play tunes with other people and find themselves increasingly playing to the tops of other peoples’ heads as they crouch over their devices.

I said before that it’s not the technology, it’s the people. You can’t blame the technology for the way people use it, and I don’t. You also can’t really blame people for using the technology that they find lying around. But I do point out, gently and politely, that some technologies make misery the easiest option, and are worth avoiding. A technology that exists to allow you to avoid hard work is great - unless there’s something gained by that hard work. And if what you really want is only gained by that work, then using technology to avoid doing that hard work is self-defeating.
