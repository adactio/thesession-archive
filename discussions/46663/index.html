The varry best o the twalmant

If the discussion heading doesn’t mean a lot to you, you’re probably not alone.

However, you might be wiser after watching this years Scots Trad Awards or “Na Trads” as it now seems to be more commonly known.

https://www.bbc.co.uk/iplayer/episode/m00129hp/na-trads-2021

Most of the introductions are either in Gaelic or Scots(?) although Mary Anne kindly provides some English announcements from time to time.

This was, indeed, the strangest “Trad Awards” I’ve seen yet both in terms of nominations and winners but I’ll let you decide. :-)
Also, much of the music was barely traditional although there were one or two worthy moments.

Also, there seemed to be more technical gremlins than usual. Many of the winners were not mentioned during the main part of the programme either but “highlighted” at the end. It wasn’t always clear who the actual winners of each category were and the compilation seemed to have been hastily put together.

Re: The varry best o the twalmant

“Not convinced a lot of what I heard last night will be around for long.”

Perhaps it’s the difference between session tunes and performance tunes. The function of the latter could be seen to be to raise excitement in a band performance rather than make you think “nice tune”.
Most of us probably enjoyed the “Strathspey Band” the most because they played (for Scotland, anyway) session “standards”, which, for a performance, might be called “covers” by some. There seems to be an idea that you have more credibility if you play your own tunes or sing your own songs. Thing is, being a great player or singer doesn’t automatically mean you can write great tunes or songs. And it’s an exceptional musician indeed that can compose more than a handful of great tunes or songs.

However, having said all that, if you don’t try you won’t achieve. And if you look through some of the old collections there’s quite a bit of mediocre stuff, so compositional mediocrity isn’t exclusively the preserve of the 21st century.

Re: The varry best o the twalmant

I’ve never been comfortable about referring to the performance of tunes or even songs in “our kind of music” as playing covers.
I’ve even heard some of the younger artists do this which is just another sign that the promotion of “trad and folk” music on a more commercial basis now seems to be the accepted norm. So, singers and musicians are expected and encouraged to compose much of their own material and if performing that of others to come up with some new, different, and “exciting” arrangement.

I don’t want to reopen the old “What is traditional music?” discussion but, of course, it doesn’t mean that the original composer is unknown. The music may be “passed down” through generations or between fellow musicians and peers. Or it may just be performed/written in a so called traditional style.
However, much of what was on offer didn’t really meet any of the criteria I’m afraid.

Re: The varry best o the twalmant

Dishonest is perhaps a little strong IMO. I get where you’re coming from though I don’t see a difficulty in accepting new music to a “tradition” if it is clearly of that idiom. Tradition is a many and varied thing though and will always be the source of many a disagreement. Allan MacDonald’s life work on the “tradition” of pibroch is highly contentious in as many circles, probably more, than it is accepted and celebrated. The wizz kid folk acts of today with their new tunes and arrangements may seem like a different breed but sat round a table in a pub they are as likely to join in with the standards as they are to shoot off on some tangent in my experience.

You have to point to the influence a healthy folk music scene has in a wider context too. The new acts are the public face of the music, they attract the airtime, the record labels, the instrument makers, the investors who create a wider circle of influence providing the means, inspiration and motivation for people to open their doors to sessions and their hearts to the music.

Re: The varry best o the twalmant

For me, it’s about “accessibility” (if that’s the correct term to use?).

Some of the young brilliant musicians will often create a spectacle of excitement in a performance which is “for the moment”. So, if you are there in the midst, such a performance can be very enjoyable even when, as is often the case, there doesn’t seem to be much of a tune at all. :-)
Certainly, it’s not usually something that sticks in your head or you would rush off home to learn. Probably, even other professional musicians wouldn’t or, maybe, even couldn’t replicate it exactly.

So, for me, traditional music is something which I feel that I may want to play myself someday and feel confident that I could with some time and practice. Of course, I realise that I’ll never play it as well as the professional musicians but that’s not the point as long as I can feel that the music can be for everyone/anyone and not just the performer on the stage.

For instance, a tune like “The Road to Errogie” ( tricky as it is) feels OK to me as a trad tune and I can make a half decent enough attempt of playing it in my own way. The same goes for many good tunes which can, on the face of it seem quite difficult.
On the other hand, some pieces of music are little more than exercise in virtuoisty and technical expertise. Great to listen to but really just for the actual performer and that moment in time.
