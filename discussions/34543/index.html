How much mastering matters

I’ve been thinking a lot of late about how much production values matter in making and mastering recordings. It’s become a bit of an arcane art, and as a musician, i’ve become more & more aware how much i’ve ended up taking for granted in the past, by not really having the technical vocabulary to explain what you want to the poor sound guy. If he is competent to great, its okay, and you end up with a good recording, but sometimes not one that represents how you play!

I’ve spent awhile learning how to mix and finally felt competent enough to have a go, anyway my question is this:

How much difference does it make? On some subconscious level there is a comparison process between it and other CD’s the listener has heard. But does it make enough of a difference for an album to cross the line between ‘yeah, nice enough playing’ and ‘wow! must buy’ ;)

In particular, for a band that plays stuff that’s not pure trad, and are looking to be in the same wheelhouse as say At First Light or someone like that?

Re: How much mastering matters

Mastering can make or break a recording. It’s not too difficult to produce a mix that sounds reasonable in your own listening environment. But not everyone is going to listen to your recording in your studio. They’ll hear it in mono on the radio (hopefully!), as a fuzzy MP3 on earbuds, on a £10,000 hifi, on headphones, in cars, in rooms of all shapes and sizes. And that is where the real art of mastering comes in - you’ve got to make it sound good in all those situations. It’s quite difficult to improve a recording at the mastering stage, but it’s dead easy to ruin it.

Re: How much mastering matters

I think the sound engineer in the pop/rock industry has, in many ways, an easier time because those styles have evolved into two sub-genres - ‘live’ and ‘studio’ music. The qualities expected of a live performance by a rock band may be very different from those expected of a studio album by the same - it is taken for granted that the studio recording may include arrangements and effects that cannot be reproduced onstage. (Of course, there are rock bands that want to recreate an unadulterated ‘live’ sound in the studio.)

In traditional music, however, most recordings are trying to capture, as faithfully as possible, the sound of musicians playing acoustic instruments in natural acoustics - in essence, we expect to hear *a session*, minus the noodlers and the background noise. The ‘natural’ acoustics may be artificially simulated in the studio, but this (and any other use of effects) needs to be done in such a way that nobody can tell. Any hint of studio tinkering sticks out like a sore thumb.

Re: How much mastering matters

Yes, recording trad is much harder for the engineer, and it’s much harder for the musicians too. With the rock band you can just put spot mics on everything and sort it all out in post-production. Someone makes a mistake? no problem, you can overdub or punch in.

But to capture an acoustic band authentically you usually land up working with a stereo array of some sort, with just a little bit from spot mics blended in to correct level balance or stereo positioning problems. That means the balance and everything else has to be right in the performance. And you can’t use overdubs - if anyone makes a mistake you have to do the whole piece over again.

Re: How much mastering matters

I’ve been working with home recording equipment for many years.

One popular scenario : recording yourself playing multiple insturments, recording one instrument per track at a time, then mixing and processing those track on to one final stereo master, for making multiple CD copies.

Here are some things which I’ve found useful in mastering - some I’ve got from the recording magazines over the years, and some from pro studio engineers too.

Analogue recording with a mic - the single most important item in getting a good sound. A high quality instrument mic is very unforgiving, and that’s the whole point. “Telling it like it is”. A cheap mic might appear to give better results (eg recording a fiddle), but usually all it does is hide a lot and muddy the sound (which will get even muddier as the track is processed to masterdom). It’s easier to tone down an over-bright sound than it is to brighten up a muddy one.

Mic placement - no hard and fast rules, but the closer the better, although proper positioning can only be got via trial and error. The stronger the signal, the less noise will be generated further down the chain.

The recording room - to be as acoustically dead as possible, so there is the minimum of natural reverb during recording. Going for gold, you can have the expensive option of having the whole room fitted with acoustic tiles, or any cheaper option like carpet underlay, heavy cloth etc on parts of the room. Anything to stop or lessen that unwanted reverb.

Mixing the tracks down to a master - instruments with similar frequencies (eg fiddle and mandolin) should be placed left and right, about 10 o‘clock and 2 o’clock.

Bass frequencies (eg bass guitar, low notes of percussion) should be placed in the middle.

Just a small sample of what’s possible, but those tips have worked for me.

Re: How much mastering matters

Yes, this thread has got a bit muddled. Usually ‘mixing’ refers to everything up to the point where you have everything down to a single stereo pair, ‘mastering’ is everything you do after that, where you are working with the whole sound, not individual instruments. As Mark Harmer says, most of this will be about making the individual songs/tunes work together as a coherent whole, but equally important is to make it work together with other CDs in the same genre - if someone has to adjust the volume every time they play your CD they just won’t bother. A fresh set of ears is more or less essential at the mastering stage. If you’ve been slaving over those tracks for weeks you will have become blind to some of their weaknesses, which someone else will spot instantly.

Panning within 9-3 o‘clock: it isn’t really anything to do with mono listeners, it’s for headphone wearers, to avoid the ’bee in the ear’ effect: In real life or on speakers, even if something is way off to one side you still hear it with both ears. But in headphones if something is panned hard over you only hear it in one ear, which sounds very unnatural. Coincidentally, stereo placement is one of the main reasons for recording with a stereo array rather than close micing everything and then using the pan knob. If you pan a mono track to one side all you are adjusting is the volume from either speaker. But when you listen you your brain uses other cues, like phase differences, to locate the sound source, which means a recording made with close mics never sounds entirely natural.

The big problem with mono listeners is that two instruments that are separated by panning may well overlap and turn to mud when everything is centred. The solution to that is simple - do all your mixing in mono. Keep everything centred while you mix and use EQ to separate the instruments. Only pan things apart at the very last moment, just before you render the track ready for mastering.

Re: How much mastering matters

I think a rough rule of thumb is that mastering can’t make a crap recording good, but it can make a good recording great. It can also, occasionally, ruin a good recording.

The question seems more often asked nowadays because many home recording apps offer semi-automated mastering as part of the package or, alternatively, many small studios will offer to master for you as well as record and mix.

Fair enough, but the skills involved in mastering can be subtly different from those involved in recording and mixing and, as other commentators have noted, by the time you’ve got that far, it can be of immense value to have a fresh pair of experienced ears give your recordings a good listen.

The problem is that with the fall in value of recorded music, people make economies and this has had a devastating effect on mastering studios. They simply do not exist in the same numbers that they did in the 1980s through the 1990s, and if you can find a good one, hang on to it.

At the very least, if you’re preparing a CD or vinyl (note that it is often better to master differently for both) to go to manufacture, or to be made available as downloads, the mastering studio will ensure that the factory or digital aggregator will receive the correct files for the job.

Re: How much mastering matters

Jim. on the mono thing, a little common sense will give you an answer: if you record a mono spot mic it doesn’t matter where you pan it to, for anyone listening in mono it is always dead centre. So for the mono listener it makes absolutely no difference how wide you make the stero image, he always hears everything centred. But for the listener with stereo headphones it makes a huge difference: if you pan too far over the headphone wearer only hears the sound in one ear. The only time that happens naturally is if you have a quiet sound source very close to one ear, and that is how your brain interprets a guitar panned all the way over. Which is why it is called the ‘bee in the ear’ effect.

But the real issue in mixing for mono comes in instrument conflicts: In THC’s recordings the fiddle and flute are conflicting - they are both using the same portion of the frequency spectrum, so they become muddled and you can’t tell which is which. For the stereo mix that is easily resolved by just panning them apart. But for anyone listening in mono they are brought back to centre and the conflict re-appears. The way most people deal with that is to resolve the conflict not by panning, but by mirror EQing - giving each instrument a portion of the frequency spectrum where it dominates. That way the brain is able distinguish between the instruments, even in mono. That is why I suggested earlier that you should go through the whole mixing process with everything centred, then pan them apart at the very last moment, when you’ve already got a good mono mix with no conflicts.

As for recording techniques, it’s not about ‘old’ and ‘new’ ways - both close micing and stereo array recording have been in use continuously for the best part of a hundred years. It’s about what you are trying to achieve - do you want a photograph of a landscape, or an oil painting? The painting will be prettier, but the photo more accurate. And that is how it is with recording techniques - if you just want to create a great sound you use spot mics, if you want to accurately portray acoutic instruments you use an array. Pop and rock use spots, classical uses arrays. Trad lies somewhere on the border - for acts like de Dannan and Flook most people would spot mic, but for intrinsically acoustic acts like the Chieftains they would us an array, to preserve the natural sound of the instruments.

Re: How much mastering matters

Yes, using two mics close to the instrument is usually a recipe for disaster, with phasing and comb filtering giving the ‘two instruments’ illusion Mark talks of, or the instrument seeming to wander about and come from different places at different pitches. There’s a great illustration of comb filtering in your clip from 0.53 - as you go up the scale you can hear the filtering change, and it sounds almost as though you are using a wah-wah.

Spaced pairs are always problematic, particularly in small rooms, and if you want a stereo image you are probably better off trying an OTRF or coincident pair, but they still need to be fairly well away from the instrument. If you have to close mic then a single mic is usually a better bet (you can then duplicate the track to give a pair that you can pan apart to give the stereo width).

The other big problem with recording fiddles with close mics is bow noise. On that particular track it adds crispness and probably enhances the recording, but in general bow noise doesn’t project, so the audience don’t hear it. If you close mic you capture all the noise and it doesn’t sound natural (what you capture is more like what you, the fiddler, hear rather than what the audience would hear).

Re: How much mastering matters

For the technically minded or curious, this is how I set about doing the remaster.

Andy’s original recording was a very clean, very straight recording of fiddle, whistle and guitar, with a sensible central guitar, and fiddle and whistle stereo-panned at 70:30. The recordings were typically ‘live’ takes, with the instruments given their independent rooms and warmth by Andy. There was no overall compression and no overall ambience or compression, a deliberate choice. This was lovely, and very ‘faithful’

When you analyse the phase distribution against frequency, you get a wide bass, narrower mids, narrow treble, and ultra-narrow high end. This is the exact opposite of what you get with most modern recordings, where the bass end is tightest. The main result of this is the ear reads the mix as very ‘cold’ as the ear reads it as very anti-correlated for phase. (it’s actually not, but the bass end is)

So, I used a tool to reverse this, tying the guitar down and effectively stereo-seperating the mid and high frequencies a bit more. I then looked at the overall mix, and raised the bass and high end fractionally, and then added a tiny amount of gloss by a small amount of tape saturation at the extreme top and bottom of the frequency spectrum. this works by extrapolating in extra odd harmonic frequencies, if done sparingly it can add the nice overtones of old-school DAT tape.

Lastly, unlike Andy’s mix, i had released it over 0 DB, so I used an intelligent limiting process which means no clipping or transients getting loose and giving clipping in the analogue domain.

If i was doing a mono-mix, well, i’d have to rethink it i guess. I’ve never done a mono mix, and i didn’t realise people listened to stuff in that way. I would also have a think if for example i wanted to go to the opposite extreme and master in 5.1 Surround, it would be an interesting process. Looks like by hook or by crook the remix was worth doing. I’ve still got the original masters, so nothing is lost, anyway!

Re: How much mastering matters

gam, that’s basically adding reverb (though it can be subtle so you get “diffuse” rather than “cathedral”.

Adjusting the proportion of left:right levels (pan control) gives you left-right positioning, adjusting the proportion of reverb (usually a “reverb send” or similar) gives you close-distant.

Loved the BBC sound desks where the reverb controls weren’t just “sends”, they altered the percentage of direct and reverb, so you got 100% reverb if you put them all the way clockwise, or 100% direct if you had them anticlockwise. Very nice and intuitive thing to use.
