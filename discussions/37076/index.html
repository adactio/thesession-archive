Re: Whistle playing and the right pinky

Most flute players, at least those who play a flute with a long foot joint, anchor the small finger of the right hand. You can see this easily because almost all flute players with a keyed flute turn the foot joint so the keys face forward and are thus out of the way of the small finger. When I played whistle, however, I did not anchor the small finger. For me, anchoring on flute is more about keeping my other fingers in their proper place over the tone holes than supporting the weight of the flute. On whistle, that never seemed necessary. I wonder what the case is for other flute players.

Re: Whistle playing and the right pinky

The lower-hand little finger was a non-issue for me for many years because I learned whistle fingering it the way many old-school people did (and do) which is to use the lower-hand ring finger as the anchor finger.

Though I never rigidly stuck to any one particular pattern, it was usually like this:
D xxx xxx
E xxx xxo
F# xxx xoo
G xxx oox (or) xxx ooo
A xxo oox
B xoo oox
C oxx oox

It depends on the particular musical phrase, the context.

Now, the last few years when I’ve got into playing Low Whistle mostly, it was dismaying to find out that

xoo oox

doesn’t work for High B on many/most Low Whistles. Having that Hole 6 closed makes High B sound rough and/or difficult to attack. Of course this was a non-issue for the century (or centuries) that Low Whistles didn’t exist and still is a non-issue for those who only play high whistle. But when playing Low Whistle I’ve got into the habit of using my lower-hand little finger as the anchor in passages that involve High B.

Re: Whistle playing and the right pinky

I began with Boehm flute 40+ years ago and became very familiar with all classical playing techniques. Then I got into Irish traditional music about 20 years ago and among my first discoveries were the very considerable differences between classical techniques and “Irish” flute playing techniques, especially of the embouchure and the grip. For what it is and for what it needs to do the Irish embouchure works delightfully well, but it is limited in the overall range of what a flute can do. But the Irish grip is a monster, a very bad form that actually could lead to physical injury, notably to carpal tunnel syndrome.

Only more recently have I taken up a study of the whistle. Oh, I have had a couple of them for many years, a Clarke in D and a Feadog in D, on which I would occasionally noodle around, but because whistles can be transported so easily and then whipped out of a pocket and played that I now have a new interest in them.

And so given my background with flute I have adopted much of that to the whistle, in particular to the grip. And once again I go to a three-point grip. The thing that makes it easy is that a whistle is so small and lightweight that each of the three points of the grip are delicate. Its whistle end I place between my lips at the center of my mouth, not biting on it but just between my lips, and then for breathing I use a technique I once learned from one of my flute teachers, to open the sides of my mouth to inhale. Hey, he was from Dublin, Ireland so he must be onto something and he was the guy that got me into Irish music in the first place! The thumbs should be approximately opposed to each other but the trick here is to push with them in such a way that the wrists remained balanced, not tending to rotate to one side or the other.

That is a complete three-point grip, no other contact points needed. And that grip then allows the fingers to fly freely. Beyond that it becomes a matter of finger discipline, that a finger does not move unless you tell it to. However, as a matter of good form, the pinkies should remain slightly elevated above a flute/whistle.

Re: Whistle playing and the right pinky

@ Madoc

I thank you for asking because I just now turned this computer back on to clarify what I said about the thumbs!

Sorry, piper’s grip fans, but this thumb position goes directly to classical technique. The thumb IS NOT held out to the side of the hand with the pad of the thumb resting on the side of the flute/whistle. Rather, the thumb is tucked under the palm of the hand so that the tip of the thumb is approximately halfway between the first and second fingers as seen when looking down between those fingers. That position is what keeps the wrist(s) balanced. For example, with your thumb in that position put the tip of the thumb on the edge of a shelf or a table and push forward, and notice that your wrist does not tend to rotate toward one side or the other. To see what side-to-side rotation is all about, put the thumb out to the side of the hand with the pad of the thumb on the edge of the shelf or table and push forward again. You will experience the rotational force immediately. The main reason to eliminate the rotational force is to help keep the wrists as relaxed as possible and that is the trick to allow the fingers to fly, FAST!

On a whistle the tips of the thumbs are placed on the side of the whistle and the thumbs are, naturally, not opposite each other but are opposed to each other, such that one thumb pushes to the right while the other thumb pushes to the left while the lips are holding the whistle just firmly enough to keep it from flipping away, but the exact position of the thumbs is up to the comfort of the player.

Re: Whistle playing and the right pinky

“the very considerable differences between classical techniques and ”Irish“ flute playing techniques… the Irish grip is a monster, a very bad form”

I question this because there’s no one “Irish grip”.

What I don’t question is that the Boehm flute has a long established pedagogy, and the way Boehm flutists are taught to hold the flute, position their body, create their embouchure, etc are all the result of centuries of experience, a vast number of master > pupil relationships and are the methods which, for the vast majority of people and over a lifetime of playing, have been found to be the most effective.

Thing is, there’s far more variety in grip and embouchure in Irish fluteplaying because so many of the people are self-taught, or learned from people who were self-taught.

Many Irish fluteplayers have an embouchure and grip that’s more or less the same as those that Boehm fluteplayers are taught. I know I did; after years of playing Irish flute I studied Baroque flute for a time at university and my embouchure and grip weren’t questioned, likewise when I took some lessons (on Irish flute) from a respected local Boehm flute teacher. Self-taught, on my own, and with only Irish players as my models, I had ended up with more or less the same grip and embouchure taught in the Boehm world.

For one thing I frequently used the low C and C# footjoint keys on my c1830 R&R flute so my little finger was in action more than is common in Irish fluteplaying.

What I didn’t do was to hold the Eb key open for most notes as is standard in Boehm playing. When I took up Boehm flute (briefly) I bought a French Model, on which E sounds pretty much the same whether or not Eb is open. Interesting in that regard is a woman I know who has played Boehm flute exclusively her whole life, took lessons growing up, and was serious to the point of considering a music career before she switched majors. When we were talking about venting E with the Eb key she told me she has never done that!

But yes there are Irish fluteplayers who use bizarre grips, the strangest to me being the one where the flute is held pointing straight forward, the flute gripped like a whistle, the head twisted far round to the side. Ouch! My neck couldn’t stand that for long.

Re: Whistle playing and the right pinky

@ Richard D Cook

You are right, that I have generalized too much about the so-called Irish grip. We all know the story about simple-system wood flutes becoming widely available in the later 19th century as the metal Boehm flute gained popularity and that those wood flutes could readily be adopted to traditional Irish music and be purchased at an attractive price. Yet those wood flutes did not come with instructions about how to play them and so for the most part the Irish were left on their own to figure out how to make them work. It was then quite natural for the Irish to adapt traditional piping techniques to their transverse flutes, including the piper’s grip.

As a classically trained Boehm player I can trace the history of my own teachers and of my teacher’s teachers back for more than a hundred years and with their teacher’s going back even further than that, so yes, I am privileged to have been exposed to a wealth of knowledge. And the techniques I have learned apply not only to Boehm flutes but to virtually all transverse flutes, back to the Baroque, back to the Renaissance and back to earlier than that, too, all of which techniques I am here to help pass along. Got any questions?

But then about 20 years ago I got involved with Irish traditional music and got to see how the Irish had adapted the transverse flute to their needs. My goodness! I could see some jaw-dropping flaws, some to the point where they actually could be physically hazardous, the various modifications of the piper’s grip in particular. If a transverse flue player exposes their wrists to strains such as that on a long term basis they really do risk contracting carpal tunnel syndrome which potentially can be permanently debilitating, which even trained Boehm players could develop and, so, much emphasis is placed on the correct use of the wrists. Always keep the wrists as straight and as relaxed as possible!
