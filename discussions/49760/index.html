Tension

Flute player here, with a bit of a conundrum:

I’ve been at the flute for about three years, but I had an interruption in there and have been back to it solidly for about 6 solid months now. I’ve done pretty well, but I wanted to take the next step, so I’ve started taking lessons. The teacher told me I’m doing very well: great tone over the full range of notes.

But, after each lesson, I play horribly for a while, until I say “Forget this. I was doing better before that lesson.” A guy in the local session told me I need to get out of my own head.

So, I’ve tried to think about it, and I think it’s a counterintuitive problem. By choosing to take more lessons, I’ve chosen to be more serious. And psychologically, that gives me more pressure to perform, so I stress more in practice, and thus get more tense, so I play worse, so I get more tense…

My teacher said we could talk about this my next lesson in two weeks, but I thought I’d ask this wonderfully helpful group. How do I get out of my head, while also trying to practice the “assignments” from my teacher? How do I relax about it, while also taking it seriously enough to take formal lessons? Or… How do I not take it too seriously while trying to take it more seriously?

I know, it’s a psychology question, not a musical one. But maybe someone out there has been through something similar.

Re: Tension

A good teacher will expect you to make progress, not attain perfection. It may surprise you to know that teachers listen for things you’re doing well, not just what you’re doing wrong. They’ll hear your improvement even when you don’t hear it yourself.

Also, yes, technique and mechanics need to be practiced, but the end goal is musical expression. If you practice without feeling the music and expressing it, that’s how you’ll end up playing—mechanically. I find it easier to relax when I keep the music in the forefront while I woodshed on technique.

Re: Tension

I’m a fiddler.
I can think of two different things that I do:

First, I separate the different parts of my practice time. I do practice mechanics slowly - long, slow notes, phrases, intonation, different bowing patterns. I focus on the small things and take as much time as they take. Then I set that aside and “just play.” There are tunes that I can’t play at speed yet, and I will play them more slowly, but when I’m “just playing,” I am hearing the music and not thinking about the mechanics.

Second, my concentration isn’t the same day-to-day or at different times during the day. I try to notice how well I’m doing, without judging. When I find myself losing focus, I stop playing and find something else to do.

Hope this helps.

Re: Tension

Tension/frustration combines with usually embouchure, and occasionally grip/posture problems. I’m no teacher, I’ve been on the flute 4-5 years now so I can only speak to my experience. Here’s what I do: first, get the flute lined up properly. If you look at the head joint on a modern metal flute, you might see little engraved lines where it. meets the body. You can (carefully) make your own alignment marks, once you get the head and barrel lined up so you can blow a clear tone on most of the low octave notes. I put mine on the “back” , so they’re not noticeable by others. If you’re on a black flute, some White-out correction fluid will make them visible (apply with toothpick). While you’re at it, make sure you have all the other joints of the flute where you’re most comfortable. The goal here is to have everything where it needs to be, so you have removed that obstacle. Next, work on your embouchure with a bit of exercise. Blow low octave G, nice and clear, and , without moving anything unnecessary, switch down a 1/2 step to F-sharp, then low E, then D. All good? Now go back up the first octave, trying to maintain that smooth volume and tone, and allow about 4 Quarter-note beats per each note. Now try the second octave. Here’s where I had trouble, every time I’d get to G, going to A, I’d “break”
and make a soft fuzzy sound, so the objective is to try to get your embouchure to sneak up on the A. This is where your teacher may come in handy, better than I can describe how to do it.
One more thing, avoid trying to use the F and B-flat keys until you get a bit better, because they’ll cause your hands to move around too much. One more thing: sometimes we have “bad flute days”, which is a misnomer because it’s not the flute, it’s us. Patience, put it down for 5 mins, or whatever, and start again on G. And, if you’re having a good day, try for the third octave…

Re: Tension

Humans are terrible at “not thinking” about something. If I say, “Don’t think about an elephant,” you’ll instantly think about an elephant.

What tends to work better is to think about something else. Instead of thinking about trying to improve or hoping to show your teacher that you’ve been woodshedding, focus on some aspect of playing that helps you relax. That’s what I was getting at above when I suggested focusing on expressing the music.

When I was first learning flute, I was struck by how playing a tune on flute felt so much like singing. (This was a revelation, coming from decades of playing fiddle.) Yes, I woodshedded on my cuts, taps, rolls, etc., but I also enjoyed just belting out tunes, kind of like singing in the shower. It’s good sometimes to play flute like no one’s listening. Happily, that singing quality seeped into my fiddling. On both instruments, focusing on singing the tunes helped me avoid thinking about technique or mechanics in a way that might make me tense.

Speaking of rolls, you said your teacher moved you right into those. Did she perhaps skip over cuts and taps? They are the building blocks of solid rolls. Do you maybe need more time to consolidate your cuts and taps before launching into rolls? Or would it help to at least warm up on cuts and taps before practicing long and short rolls? Trying to learn skills before you’ve built a foundation for them is a recipe for tension and stress. It’s easier to relax when you follow a gradual progression of skills.

Re: Tension

If technical finger exercises on the flute eventually produce musical downgrading, then after those exercises, take a 15 minute break before expecting to play music well, so your finger muscles and nerves can relax and rejuvenate. Same for embouchure exercises; you’re working muscles that are small and need time to relax and re-energize.

In my experience, if I stop playing flute a while, it takes about two weeks of embouchure exercises done several times daily before those muscles are again able to reliably create the exact air stream control I want. Just like bodybuilding exercises. Gains come a little slow, but steadily, and regular workouts after that keep things in peak condition.

So if you’re doing both types of exercises at the same time, all I can suggest is play through the mistakes and awkwardness, push yourself through the specific exercises but take it with a sense of humour, you’re dealing with physical conditioning and rest is no less important than the workout. With bodybuilding, a direct parallel, the gains (except the gain of asserting willpower) don’t happen while you pump the iron for two hours, it happens after, the growth response to stress, the microscopic damage to muscle cells and the response of the circulation system and nerves to grow in response to the need to perform more. From bodybuilding I learned that the most dedicated bodybuilders don’t exercise one muscle group more than about twice a week, but they’re going through major stress each workout. They will do minor light weight aerobics to help the muscles get good circulation, and regular STRETCHING, but major workouts are scheduled carefully. With embouchure work, it’s more often, probably daily, but take at least one day off weekly, play until you get some fatigue, but then stop and rest for at least 5 hours! In total, maybe playing flute about 3 hours daily max? And as weird as it may seem, embouchure stretching can help as well as rest periods. Not sure how to describe that, but however, be gentle, don’t pull your mouth apart to make funny faces and pull a muscle by mistake. Flute embouchure is also unique; it’s not sax, clarinet or trumpet embouchure. If you play those other instruments, don’t expect to switch from one to another and be Miles Davis and Matt Malloy in the same minute. Miles Malloy never recorded anything.

Ted,

I think that your struggle comes from still learning the basics. You said it there, “I have so much in my head…I can’t even get the basics…” Letting the experience be what it is every time you pick up the instrument is all you can do, that’s life really. It’s hard, you want to live up to the sound in your head all of the time, especially when you feel like you’ve already mastered something. I suggest letting go of something for a bit and telling your teacher you need to slow down. Record the things she wants you to master and don’t keep it in your head; focus on playing music, not just the technique. Wrong is how we learn, and sometimes a tune without the ornaments is fine, especially when you’re still thinking about embouchure. If on any given day you can’t play the notes and want to practice the fingers just hold the instrument and practice, or listen to the tune and visualize, visualize, visualize. You’re freezing up somewhere in there because you’re asking for under-myelinated synapses to fire and your brain stops. Do you ever just play the headjoint? Are you tapping out the tunes/ornaments all of the time without an instrument in hand? Are the tunes absolutely engrained in your head? How’s your muscle memory in other activities? There’s a huge gradient as to how quickly that myelination occurs. You must make those brain connections before you can stop directing each of the actions. Often it gets worse before it gets better too, especially if you aren’t doing it really slowly. Changing how you do something is very different from learning something new too. You have two things you’re thinking about at once; what you know you’re supposed to do and how you don’t want to do it.

Even having played flute since I was a kid and Irish flute and whistle for nearly a decade, there are days that feel like a million bucks and days that feel crummy. If you want regular practice, the main thing is to not focus on what you can’t do, but to say I did what I intended and that’s what I can do today. I don’t drop the flute these days, but there are definitely days that I can’t get the sound I want out of it and it’s my embouchure, or the tension in my hands, or the weather. Lots of great musicians acknowledge that getting better means that you can work around yourself on the worst days. Take a break, shake it out, focus on what you’re hearing smelling or seeing in detail, meditate for a few minutes, take a walk…in the session take a bathroom break or grab a drink…we all have to do that occasionally, so you need to figure out how to be in your body again. It won’t mean you don’t have really bad days, but that’ll probably get rarer.

Just some thoughts. Take what you like from that, but I’m a musician and a dance teacher/dancer and have spent a lot of time considering how to improve learning. It’s not a nice upward curve approaching the asymptote of perfection; day-to-day performance during learning is incredibly variable. It sounds like you’ve made lots of progress, but mastering the basics takes a lot longer than any of us assume and the frustration of that can be a huge barrier. I really do mean it about accepting all of the parts of the experience and know that your teacher shouldn’t be surprised by a rough lesson. Of course, we want to master what’s thrown at us, but it takes the time that it takes and that’s different for each of us.

Re: Tension

Kind of late to the party here, but in reading the thread, a couple of things occurred to me.

First off, you mentioned that the teacher was working with you on changing your rolls. When you have been doing something one way, and then try to change it, you’re fighting against kinesthetic memory and habit. It was very common occurrence in the first 7-8 years of playing, that a teacher would point out something that I was doing that should really be changed, and it would feel like it completely *ruined* my playing for a period of time. And when you’re in the earlier stages of development, any step backward feels like failure! For me, the way through it was to just accept the regression, and work my way through the new technique until it became ingrained in my playing. It’s hard! There were a couple of times that it took 3 months before I felt like my playing was back to where it was before the change!

The problem with saying to yourself “Forget this. I was doing better before…” is that you’ll just end up reinforcing your old way of doing it, which will make it harder to actually make the change.

But I feel for you! Especially if it feels like it is ruining your playing in sessions. And that’s a conundrum. You don’t want to reinforce bad habits, but you still want to enjoy your time in the session. And it’s very difficult to play in public if you’re in your own head and thinking too much about any one aspect of your playing. So what I did in those circumstances was to try to relax in the sessions, and not worry too much about whether I was doing it “right” or “wrong”, but still allowed myself to make note of when I was doing it “wrong”. Then you let your practice time be the painful time, where you are working to ingrain the new technique into your playing, however slowly and painfully as it takes. And ultimately, the new technique will become what happens when you’re relaxed in the session.

It is a tough thing to have to work through, bit it will pay dividends down the road! I watch this struggle with my students, where they can do some technique well during the lesson when they’re fully concentrating on the technique, and working on it at a slow tempo. But in a session, there are so many other pressures that they revert to their old habit. I don’t usually point it out to them during the session, but I always try to let them know after the session that I noticed it, so that it is still in their mind that it is something to be worked on at home.

What we’re really trying to do here is to build the neural pathways that know how to do the techniques right. Once you have gotten the technique for an ornament (for instance) ingrained into your playing then it will be what happens even when you’re not paying direct attention to it.

I wish you the best of luck in your journey! And give yourself some slack. Learning new things is hard, and replacing older habits is even harder!
