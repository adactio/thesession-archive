↳ Posted by Steve Ewing on August 11th, 2025.

Re: Listening, playing &/or practicing outside of sessions.

Sessions aren’t for practicing. You practice on your own time, in your own space, at home or in class/lessons. That’s the social responsibility you take up when you decide to play music socially. It’s not fair to everyone else who is putting in the work, for someone who isn’t spending any time with the music to ruin it for everyone else. Sometimes there are designated learning sessions, or slow sessions, those are practice spaces. And sure, there are times in a session where a tune may be reviewed, revisited, analyzed, compared, etc. And I wouldn’t go so far as to say that sessions aren’t learning spaces. They just aren’t for practicing.

As for actual practice? Hmm…

(Music you don’t know)
1. Pick a tune you want to learn(preferably a tune you’re already familiar with by ear), or a system/technique you want to practice(like a certain key, or an instrument specific skill.)
2. Find a lesson if you need help learning, or start a self-guided practice if you have self-teaching skills.
3. Get a material copy of the music you’re learning for reference, whether it’s a recording or sheet music.
4. Make sure you have an appropriate setting(sometimes the settings of tunes that we have access to aren’t settings that are commonly played, usually a key change).
5. Learn the music phrase by phrase, slowly, with as few mistakes as you possible.

(Music you already know)
1. Identify problem areas and determine what’s giving you trouble.
2. Active listening, slow practice, and accuracy repetition works for most issues.
3. If #2 doesn’t solve your problems, do some research and learn more about the problem you’re dealing with.

Like others, I don’t “practice” in the sense of doing exercises to improve my facility on an instrument, but I do play every day, usually some mix of tunes and songs on various instruments.

My approach to learning tunes is pretty simple: I have a notebook, when I come across a tune that seems interesting (either because it comes up in a session or because I’ve stumbled across it in some collection) I’ll jot it down in the book. When I get the urge to do so, I’ll leaf through the book and play through some of the tunes. After a bit, I realize that I’m playing the tune, not the notes off the page, and that’s when I know I’m starting to get it. Usually I know that a tune is well established when I can play it on more than one instrument - if I’ve got the tune on the flute, sometimes that’s just finger knowledge, but if I’ve got it on the box or the banjo as well then I’m pretty sure it’s all the way in.

Of course, the key step in learning the tune is forgetting the name. Once I’ve got there, I might as well have known the tune forever.

↳ Posted by SJ on August 12th, 2025.

Re: Listening, playing &/or practicing outside of sessions.

@Chris - I’ve found that that’s not always the case. Sometimes I don’t know whether I’ve played a tune, but I know how it goes, and if I listen through the first time and hear no surprises, I can generally play it. Other times there’ll be a tune that comes up a few weeks running and after hearing it played through a dozen times I’ll start to know where it’s going, and I can play at least a good chunk of it, which usually helps me to find the rest. Obviously, if I’m not sure about a tune I’ll lay back, play simple, and cut out if I find that there’s a spot that I actually don’t know.

Frankly, this music does have a lot of “standard moves”, and after playing for a few decades you can hear them. Some tunes are made up entirely of “standard moves” and those are the ones that an experienced player can pick up. I also suspect that my old habit of “mental practice” that I’ve talked about here a few times (and do a lot less of lately) might help here. For years, I would read through a tune and play it “in my head”, visualizing (or tactilizing?) the playing of it on a whistle or a banjo. I’m pretty sure this is part of why I find this sort of thing works for me.

To be clear, I don’t exactly want to recommend this as a general practice, though. It’s certainly not something I do on every tune, it’s still a pleasant surprise when it happens (though when it happens, it’s not a surprise when it works)

My advice to other players: If you hear a tune and you think you might maybe know it, that’s not going to be nearly good enough. You’ve got to have been playing long enough to have all of the “standard moves” absolutely in your head, and a clear path from your head to your instrument. If you have any doubt that you’re at this point, yeah, just lay back and listen and try to fix the tune in your head, and then learn it at home.

Good comments. As ppl get experienced yea they can hear better and employ more implicit knowledge, so the cognitive load of acquiring new tunes is lower.

BUT…and there is an ocean of research on this…when we listen to new stuff, and we try to play, we are multitasking. But the brain does not multitask. All it can do is switch between tasks (the speed of switching varies between individuals). And when it switches, it experiences delays (slower processing and other stuff). ↳ Posted by David50 on August 13th, 2025.

Re: Listening, playing &/or practicing outside of sessions.

David50, thanks for the clarification. “Attention” goes a long way toward explaining how it’s possible to pick up a new tune on the fly at a session. You don’t have to pay attention to playing your instrument because (like tying your shoes) you already know how to do that. So you pay close attention to the tune as it unspools and go with the flow. As others have said, these tunes are often fairly predictable, so it’s not too hard to anticipate common phrases and motifs. And any unusual phrases or motifs stand out for that, making them more sticky in the mind’s ear and memory.

It’s the same whether you’re learning a tune on the fly at a session or just blending into another player’s setting of a tune, or picking up variations. As Mr. Kiparsky suggests above, paying attention to the sounds around us while we play is the root of how a group truly plays together—same rhythm, same tempo, same degree of swing, same intonation, etc.

“multitasking”, when you walk and chew gum at the same time, your brain is multitasking. specifically when playing music: task 1: run the current tune in the head, task 2: move hands to operate music instrument, task 3: hear difference between tune in the head and output of instrument, correct as need, task 4: listen to the rest of the band, correct own playing as needed, task 5: start running the next tune in the head. if you play a flute, task 6: run a PLL to adjust flute tuning/pitch/frequency to external reference. if you are classically trained, task 7: run the counting loop (one-e-and-a-two-…).

Re: Listening, playing &/or practicing outside of sessions.

@mandocello - if you have to think about these tasks, then yes, they’re multitasking. However, a player with a reasonable amount of experience (20 years?) won’t need to devote attention to any of those, except possibly thinking about what the next tune might be - unless they’re playing a set that they’ve been playing for ages, in which case even that probably isn’t necessary.

I would count all of those as the single task of “playing a tune”, just as the single task of “playing a song with own guitar accompaniment” could be broken down into stuff like hold down the fingers on the current chord, remember what the next chord is, remember the little fiddly bit that I play to get to the next chord, move my fingers to the next chord, pick a bass note with my right thumb, get ready to pick a treble note with my index finger, prepare my middle finger for its next note, remember the next line, remember the melody I’m going to sing on the next line, get my vocal chords into the right shape to make the next note, adjust pitch of voice to match that of guitar, etc, etc, etc.

I don’t think about any of that when I accompany myself singing a song, except once in a while I have to remember how the next verse starts. I know the song, I play it, just as when I play at a session, all of the tasks you list are done without conscious attention.

To be honest, I’m not sure how I would consciously adjust my pitch and get it right. It’s like the centipede who could remember how to walk until someone asked him how he managed to keep all his legs from getting tangled up - if I think about adjusting my pitch, it’s going to go wrong. Also not sure what would happen if I tried to think about moving my fingers or adjusting my vocal cavity etc for proper resonance, or any of the other myriad stuff that needs to happen for a tune to come out.

Re: Listening, playing &/or practicing outside of sessions.

@mandocello - For me, the process of learning a new tune usually goes like this (1) intentional listening to several recordings of the new tune; (2) browsing sheet music until I find one close to the recording I like best; (3) reading along while listening; (4) playing along with sheet music as reference while listening; (5) playing along with recording without sheet music; (6) playing the tune without listening to the recording. Once I reach step 6 without errors, I’ve successfully internalized the tune. At that point, I don’t consciously think about any of the steps you listed - I just play the tune from my musical lizard brain. And if I’m playing it in a session, I’m not multitasking because I’m not consciously thinking about how the tune goes.
