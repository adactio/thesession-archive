Re: Musical notation query - a straight-line slur?

There is some right nonsense being spouted on here from posters who I would have thought knew better.

MacLean of Pennycross is a 2/4 march, not a strathspey, reel, klezmer, slow air or fiddle-de-dee. That is as close to gospel as you can get.

You want to make a reel out of it, you do that. But as it is, it isn’t, in any time signature…and that’s without even getting into the actual differences between a reel and march from a structural point of view.

To answer the original question - two bows, same direction, bow stops momentarily between them. Exact details of how it is played are pretty subtle and depend as much on style as anything else.

For notation, unless you are specifically intending to reproduce the score exactly for fiddlers, I would suggest replacing it with a normal slur. Any fiddler for whom a straight slur is part of their normal style will put them in as required anyway. Anyone for whom it is not will ignore them.

To write down what is intended without using the straight slur, I’d suggest something like changing A>B (with a straight slur) to vAz/vB/ - but as I say I wouldn’t do that unless your aim is to transcribe these pieces with a fidelity very few practising musicians will use.

Re: Musical notation query - a straight-line slur?

The symbol itself is called a ‘straight slur’, and the technique is called ‘snap-bowing’.

The bow can move in any direction, but the two notes are played in a single bow, with the bow staying on the string, with a very quick ‘start-stop-start’ action (staccato).

In some of the older manuscripts the ‘straight slur’ is sometimes written as a symbol similar to “ } ” , but rotated 90 degrees.

Re: Musical notation query - a straight-line slur?

The straight slur indicates snap bowing, which is very common in Scottish music. It is a lot less common in classical, so probably doesn’t need it’s own symbol, and could be dealt with using individual bow marks where necessary.

It’s worth noting that Peter Milne’s ‘cupid’s bow’ and Scott Skinner’s looped tie (I don’t think he used it as a slur) were their personal markings on their own working manuscripts, I don’t think they were used on music intended for publication.

My teacher used to use the little ‘v’ reminder to indicate a semitone (in phrases where there was an accidental, so you didn’t make the mistake of misreading, and think it was a full tone.

I rarely see those symbols in the various studies I have, although sometimes there is a ‘[’ symbol (rotated 90 degrees) above two notes as a reminder that pre-emptive fingering is needed. A trad example of that would be :
https://thesession.org/tunes/535 (1st setting) , notes 1 and 2 in bar#1 and
https://thesession.org/tunes/92 (1st setting) , notes 6 and 7 in bar 13/14

The ‘straight slur’ in Scots fiddle music is really just a more readable form of the classical standard slur over two staccato notes (it means the same thing). It just avoids showing a dotted note, plus another ‘dot’ to indicate staccato.

The same symbol has been used in other books to indicate a slide from one note to another, using the same finger.

Authors often make up their own symbols and codes for the sounds they want to represent. In “English, Welsh, Scottish & Irish Fiddle Tunes” by Robin Williamson, he uses “sk.” (squawk) - “sharply slide the finger up to the written note from a semitone below”. Then he uses “sm.” (smear) - “slide more slowly up to the written note from a tone below”.

Some authors use whitespace to represent silence. Get that :) Presumably they did not know of the existence of the ‘rest’ symbols.

Re: Musical notation query - a straight-line slur?

Jim,the straight slur doesn’t mean staccato, and isn’t equivalent to a dotted slur. The straight slur indicates snap bowing (or J bowing to our American cousins), where the bow stops briefly between notes, but both notes are given their full value, not played staccato. If they are to be played staccato, then the straight slur is dotted. (If you have a copy of Hunter there is an example in the last line of 175 - Master Francis Sitwell)

Diane, >“And ”J“ ? that’s a new one on me for slurred, but articulated bows” - although that is part of it, it is not really what snap bowing, J bowing or the straight slur refer to. They are normally used to refer specifically to the method for bowing scots snaps (short/long pairs) as in Strathspeys. At the end of the preceding note the bow stops short of the end, then the snap is played with a short stroke in the same direction and a long stroke the other way - essentially a single hook or ‘J’ shaped stroke. Done right it’s all one movement - the wrist and fingers throw the bow forward as the arm starts to pull it back.

Re: Musical notation query - a straight-line slur?

Staccato doesn’t just mean detached, détaché means detached (and is how the notes would be played in this instance) - the notes are separate and distinct (not slurred) but occupy their full length so that one note runs immediately onto the next with no gap. Staccato means the notes are shortened, so that they are separated by a short silence:

From Wiki:
Staccato [stakˈkaːto] (Italian for “detached”) is a form of musical articulation. In modern notation it signifies a note of shortened duration,[1][2] separated from the note that may follow by silence.[3] It has been described by theorists and appeared in music since at least 1676

Staccato doesn’t specifically mean separate notes on the same stroke either. The majority of staccato notes are hack bowed, it is only if two or more staccato notes (indicated by a dot above or below) are joined with a slur that they are played on the same stroke.

Jim,
I think you’ve almost answered your own question: you’ve found no contradiction in the definitions of staccato as an effect, but when you look for a definition of ‘staccato bowing’ you find no consensus. That’s because there is no one thing called ‘staccato bowing’, staccato only refers to the effect, and there are a number of different techniques for achieving it. If you asked a dozen experts ‘what is staccato bowing?’ each would simply describe their favourite technique for achieving the effect.

I can see where the confusion comes from if you are looking at Galamain. On p78 there is a heading ’Staccato, with a description of the simplest way of playing a staccato run. But Galamain isn’t a reference book, designed to be dipped into (there is no index) it is written to be read as a continuous text, the headings are like chapter titles in a novel, not separate sections. And if you read it that way you’ll realize that even as you go past other headings like flying staccato and ricochet, he is still talking about ways of playing a staccato run, he hasn’t moved on to a different subject.

As for double stops, they are also well defined. If you play two notes together it is a double stop. If one of them is an open string it may also be called a drone or ringing string, but it is still a double stop. Stop is as in organ stop, not ‘put your finger on the string’. If you play two strings on the fiddle it is equivalent to playing an organ with two stops pulled out.

1 - dyad : two notes sounded together
2 - stop : a string is pressed against the fingerboard
3 - double stop (formed) : when two adjacent strings are pressed against the fingerboard, using one or two fingers
4 - double stop (played) : as above, with those two strings sounded, either with the bow, or plucked

In this case you’re hoping to indicate a very specific marking which even when handwritten has opened up a few questions as to it’s meaning. If I were in your situation I’d either render it exactly as it appears in the original score, use a standard curved slur, or something which is easily understood by most readers & as faithful as possible to the intent of the original transcription, composer &/or a well known recording.

The limitations of abc notation are in direct relation to how far you’re willing to go & the context in which they will be used. If the abcs are to be typeset the sky is the limit. If they are to be submitted to a website’s database then they will be used by various applications for the score as well as MIDI applications. In the latter you might think twice before using the marking intended for tuplets.

In the score the tuplet marking [as a bracket] will not be the same as just the straight line appearing in the scan. In other words, close but no cigar.

The MIDI playback may not work since tuplets are intended to play a series of notes evenly & you’re using it for a rhythm which is not even.

Let me know if you want to try something with abc, though frankly it would add significantly to your work load.

Cheers,
Ben

%%postscript {/gchshow load cshow}

T:McLean of Pennycross

M:2/4

(e>d)|{c2d2}(c>B)+beambr2+(A<a) c<ec<A|(c<e{g2}a>e) {f2g2}f2e>c|“@0,5:cp /yy exch def /xx exch def”A“@0,5:gsave xx yy lineto stroke grestore”>.B+beambr2+(c<a) (c<f){g2f2}e>c-|d>c+beambr2+B<A {B2c2}B2(e>d)|

U:~=!beambr1!
