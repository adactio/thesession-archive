The cranky Extremists.

So, I am relatively new to the Irish session scene. I am originally a piper (GHB) who fell in love with the fiddle tunes and the culture as a whole. I play several instruments, guitar being one of them (Banjo, Mandolin, tin whistle). Although when I have shown up at certain sessions (which will not be named and were advertised as “open”) and as soon as they see the Guitar case I get the cold shoulder.

What’s with the extremism? Isn’t this supposed to be a way have some craic and drink a few beers? Perhaps meet some interesting new people? Isn’t this folk music? The music of the people? Last time I checked I was a people! So whats the deal? I play well enough to back people up on most tunes. When I don’t know the tune I don’t play, or at least I play quiet enough to figure out the chord structure and then join in later. I know plenty of tunes on the Mando/banjo. And god forbid I sing a song!

Don’t get me wrong, at most of the sessions that I attend this is not a problem. People are friendly, warm, and welcoming. But I do like to stretch out often and see whats around. I’ve been to sessions in Vermont, New Hampshire, New York, Maine and Massachusetts. All slightly different and unique. But every so often I come across the cranky bunch of “Extremist” pure drips. It’s a bummer. A Penny for your thoughts? Stories?

Re: The cranky Extremists.

Some people are very much into “the tradition”, whatever they might define that to be. Nothing wrong with the tradition whatsoever in my opinion, but it is a living tradition. Some might not be open-minded to guitars or other harmony instruments, because in their mind, that’s not trad. I happen to disagree, because there are accepted forms of harmony, like uilleann pipes, button accordions, and whatnot, in the firmly established tradition, and, like I said, it’s a growing tradition. Guitar has been around for quite a while, I’m fairly certain, anyway. I think these people should be a little more open-minded, but to each their own, I suppose.

Some people have preconceptions that guitar players are going to come in and bash away with dull chord progressions, or worse, incorrect chord progressions (there are no “right” chords, but there are certainly wrong ones!), ruining the sound of the music. Those preconceptions may very well be from experience. I think THESE people should be more open to newcomers, but have a polite way of informing anyone who needs more work that they should practice some more.

People have their sessions, and I won’t blame them for wanting them a particular way, but I do prefer the slightly more inclusive people. Fortunately, I’ve never really run into a session that is very cold-shouldered before. I’ve been fortunate. That, and I play the flute. That helps. :P

Hello Jeff! You are not the first to ask this question - in fact you could kill and afternoon looking back through past threads about this exact subject. While there is no excuse for rudeness, guitar players who don’t understand this particular music style can quickly trample the melody players. That’s not to say it’s impossible to pull off - I’ve sat in with some guitarists who had very little exposure to Irish music, but a brilliant set of ears on their head and were able to feel their way right into the tunes with delicacy and taste. Not once did they bellow “what key is this in?!?!” Not once did they start hammering away on chords without first giving a listen to gauge where the tune was going. Unfortunately, people like that are the exception and not the rule. I guess if I could suggest anything, it would be for you to understand that each session has its own culture and past experiences. I’m not saying this is you - you could be brilliant for all I know - I’m just giving you one person’s opinion on why, from time to time, you may get a sideways glance when you first pull out your six-string. I admit, when some random guitar player shows up at our Tuesday night session at The Ould Sod that I don’t know from the local SoCal trad scene or my trad music friends on Facebook I always feel a bit of trepidation and for good reason.

Then there are the other 5% that know the tunes, know how to listen and adapt, understand the role of a backup instrument in a session, and are an absolute joy to play with…

Re: The cranky Extremists.

Tongle, just because a session is “open” it doesn’t mean that the players who show up don’t have some responsibility for respecting the music and the other players, as well as know their own abilities and participate in an appropriate manner.

The session I’ve co-hosted for 17 years running is open, but it has four simple rules that have served us very well:

1) Tune your instrument and do your best to keep it in tune through the session
2) Play the tunes you know, don’t play the tunes you don’t know
3) Play the tunes at the speed that the person who started at the set chose.
4) Be kind and respectful to each other

Now, if a clueless guitar player walks in and starts bashing away semi-randomly, it’s clear that he doesn’t know the tunes and isn’t being respectful of the other players. So, he’s likely to be asked to not play. Then there is the mandolin player who randomly noodles on every tune. It’s pretty obvious he’s playing tunes he doesn’t know, and again not being respectful of the other players, since they may not be able to hear the real tune through the random noodling. The beginner who only knows 5 tunes who won’t give up his seat when the more advanced players arrive later in the evening, he doesn’t respect the other players.

Do this kind of thing at our session and either I or my co-host is going to have some direct words with you explaining exactly why it isn’t working. Don’t want to follow some simple rules, we don’t want you there. End of story. Go find another session that would be a better fit. Do some people think we’re “cranky extremists” or “session nazis”? Of course they do. I’m thrilled they don’t come to our session anymore. Our barn, our rules.

We kind of run it like an Aikido dojo, it’s an open session, but it’s a beneficent meritocracy. We make sure the newer players to the session know they can come for the first hour and we’ll do our best to go through the tunes they know, but since our long term players generally arrive later, and we have a limited number of seats, they are expected to give up their seats. We encourage them to stay and listen, record the tunes, etc.

Would some people say our session isn’t “open”, that fine with me, but I can tell you, it works and thrives. Other session hosts I’ve talked to who have had problems with their sessions find that things go much smoother if they impose a similar structure.

In my opinion, an open session (at least one that has any hope of surviving in the long term) isn’t a free-for-all, any more than an open-league bowling night is an invitation to randomly throw balls down any lane you want. There is still structure, a minimal set of reasonable rules, and most importantly a commitment to respecting the music and the other players.

If you don’t know, the chances are good that you don’t fit, and you might expect some cold glances and perhaps some harsh words.

That’s fine that you value the social aspect primarily, but there’s also magic when you combine a group of people on all levels with this music who understand their strengths, limitations, and understand their role in the musical community. What doesn’t work for many more experienced players, and what you see often in “open” community sessions with no structure, is a session that’s brought down always to it’s lowest common denominator musically with perpetual beginners, multiple guitars/bodhrans/shaky eggs/etc. They will make the same argument that you have, that having fun and everyone having a good time trumps the need to produce something resembling quality music. That’s fine, but not what everyone wants. You really should try to understand that not everyone has your preference. There’s room for both types of events to exist. It’s when one style of session player tries to impose itself on the other style session that problems arise. This often happens in places where there is only one session, the only solution I’ve ever seen actually work is to have several sessions in the area with different priorities.

Re: The cranky Extremists.

IMHO, an “Open” session means you are welcome to join but not necessarily to do and play as you like.

You should only play if you know the tunes or can add something to it without clashing or disrupting the proceedings. The latter would include accompaniment etc on guitar or bodhran. However, you should still be confident about the tune structure even if you don’t know the notes.
I think having a go at playing a tune the second or third time around is OK too if you have picked it up by then although not everyone might agree. You sometimes even need to wait with tunes you already know if you are caught unaware or don’immediately recognise it.

Also, there’s no harm in just sitting and listening when you don’t know all the tunes. I don’t believe in forcing myself on a session although many people are less reserved. I’d rather just play those I know although I may politely ask to start a set or do it if asked.
The only issue is, if it’s a small session, you shouldn’t block up space if you aren’t going to play. Sometimes the bar like to limit the session to one or two tables or the “cranky extremists” might even arrange the chairs that way themselves to limit the numbers. :-P
In that case, get there early or wait until there’s room. Always ask politely if you can sit down especially if you aren’t invited to do so first. Even in “open sessions”, many players won’t appear to welcome you with open arms but that doesn’t mean they are unfriendly. Just a bit “cranky” but that’s the charm as I said.
