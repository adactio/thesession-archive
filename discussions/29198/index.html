Re: UC Berkeley Course on Irish Music and Song

One of the presuppositions of the course, both as stated in the objectives and the summary, seems to be the tenuous theory that Irish music has entered some kind of “stasis” since 1985 and that this is a problem that needs redressing. First of all, it’s wrong. Look through the Celtic Connections program, for instance, and there are more innovative “modern” stuff like Guidewires and KAN than there are pure drop “just the tunes ma’am” gigs.

Second, it’s not a problem. Is it not fantastic that so many people are interested in the “old” (early twentieth century anyway) ways of playing and the old tunes? I think you should examine the assumptions guiding the course, mainly the one which assumes innovation (which in this case seems to ONLY mean fusing Irish music with other genres. Innovation within the boundaries of Irish music, i.e. Ed Reavy’s tunes or Willie Clancy’s piping, does not get acknowledged) = good and just playing the pure drop = bad and “stasis.”

Third, you also seem to be presuming that NOTHING happened in Irish music before the 1950s and blame it on colonialism, that it was the quaint and static oral tradition, unchanging and hiding in the cultural basement until the folk revival. This is also wrong. Quite a lot happened in the 1920s and 1930s, such as the introduction of recording technology. People like the Taylor Brothers and Michael Coleman were bringing Irish music to the stage, and the Taylors pretty much invented the concert pitch set of uilleann pipes, which had a huge impact on the way Irish music is now played (uilleann pipes in bands and sessions, for one). Then there’s the contention that the playing of dance tunes was the result of the “high art” music being “driven underground by colonial forces.” I don’t know what to say to that, except, “What?”

They should be payin’ me to do this. I’d do a better job, and I need a job. Anyone?

Re: UC Berkeley Course on Irish Music and Song

Erigena, if you found my first post useful, then I hope this one will be as well: Jon Kiparsky is quite right in calling your description of stasis, and indeed your definitions for most of your basic concepts, “remarkably idiosyncratic usage.”

I would recommend changing the course title or course content in one direction or another. It’s fine to focus on the Irish music recording industry, and you might get away with describing its own stasis issues and its troubled relationship with the actual playing of music. It would be fine to teach a course on “progressive,” “fusion,” “Celtic” musics, or whatever other outgrowths of the tradition you like, rather than suggesting that the tradition itself is in danger by not being those things. Just clarify a few core ideas and only deal with the ones that make sense and can be logically supported.

Along those lines, your remark about diatonal chord shifts, aside from its musical opinion, is simply untenable in a basic, scientific way. I’m still not totally sure what you mean by a chord change in a diatonic context, but whatever it is, you can’t claim to know that tons of accompanists in smaller bands and in everyday sessions aren’t trying it and pulling it off just fine. This may be another place where it would be more useful to state outright that you’re only focusing on the industry and on recorded music, rather than “the music itself.”

I hope you can get somewhere productive with the course.
--Dan

Re: UC Berkeley Course on Irish Music and Song

The political slant has got to go.

Not only does it pander to knuckle-dragging sectarianism; that weeping-into-your-green-beer romantic-victim stuff simply obliterates a huge amount of relevant history. Whatever the British state may have been doing to Ireland in the 19th century, that period saw the greatest growth and diversification Irish music has ever seen, with the traditional dance forms put to socially functional use all over the country. This is the exact opposite of what the “repressed culture of resistance” model predicts. Irish music actually did at least as well under British rule as Scottish music did at the same time, and way better than English music, which was all but marginalized out of existence. (You could argue that the Brits did the working musicians a favour by keeping the Church off their backs).

O’Nuallain’s paper on modality doesn’t get a traceable reference in the bibliography. “Benjamins, in McKevitt et al eds.” - what does that mean? Nothing by anybody called Benjamins or McKevitt is cited.
