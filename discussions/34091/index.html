What Else Can Be Done With Traditional Music.?

Someone asked me this question a few days ago, and I thought it would make a good title for a topic of discussion. If we play the tunes the same way, it will get boring. It is hard to know where it will go, or what else can be done with it. New ideas, can often offend purists. Mick Foster once said, “everyone can only take so much, of their favourite music”. He also added, “if you play 3 reels for someone who is not a fan of traditional music, it all sounds like the same tune to them”. I have to agree. It was a very good statement. Anything repeated too much can be boring. You would need real dedication, to do the same thing over and over again. Reels, jigs, and hornpipes, seem to be the main tunes in traditional music. Flings, mazurkas, and barn dances are rarely played. Here is another opinion of mine. It might be harsh, but I am being honest. Ceili bands, do nothing for me. Band members just play the same instruments, and the same style of tunes all the time. If someone can play a few different instruments, it might make it exciting. It has to be something, that will keep the older style. There would be some variety then. The old would be mixed with something else. Does anyone have more ideas.?

Re: What Else Can Be Done With Traditional Music.?

It’s probably just me but I find this post terribly difficult to parse, even if one assumes it to be another turgid trot round the “Crosbhealach an Cheoil” debates of the mid-90s.

“If we play the tunes the same way, it will get boring”. If you mean at the micro level i.e. playing a tune the same way each time, then, of course. But trad musicians don’t do that, even at the level of phrases. Sure the difference may be small, perhaps indiscernible to a listener new to the genre but so what? It’s a music of small things, by and for the community, that doesn’t necessarily have to attract a wider audience.

“It is hard to know where it will go, or what else can be done with it.” Trad is predicated on the passing of an aesthetic between generations, one invariably different from the other as times change. Tunes fall in and out of favor but some essence remains steady, flexing and contracting over time as tunes are adopted and fall out of favor with the community. The same with instruments and performance practices. It’s evolving all the time. Like any creatives, musicians are constantly exploring boundaries, sometimes led by artistic curiosity, sometimes for that wider audience, so have combined the music with other genres for years. Some experiments are successful, others less so; some influences passing, other more long term. I’m sure others can find more, and better, examples but rattling off the top of my head on a grey Sunday morning between mass and a pub session:

Paddy Glackin and Jolyon Jackson (“Hidden Ground”) with early electronica. Brian Ó hUiginn (a fabulous piper) doing all sorts of similarly interesting things nowadays; John Cage (“Roaratorio”), Donnacha Dennehy (“Grá Agus Bás”), Dave Flynn (far too many to mention), Dé Danann, the countless trad musicians that have played with classical string quartets incl Máirtín O‘Connor, Martin Hayes, with classical music; Seán Ó Riada (“Mise Éire”), Shaun Davey (again too many to list) with concert works and the film score aesthetic; Lorcan MacMathuna, Jack Talty and Neil O’ Loghlen’s Ensemble Ériu, Neil Yates, Niall Vallely with jazz; Caoimhín Ó Raghallaigh with ambient music (Caoimhín with all sorts of things really e.g. This How We Fly, with Nils Økland, and to a lesser extent The Gloaming); AfroCelts with trance; Máirtín O’Connor’s interest in the dance music of South America, so too The Chieftains (“Santiago” - linking Galicia and Latin America). Then there’s the lads with Ry Cooder, and Nashville session musicians, and Chinese musicians; Donal (led by Andy) with music from the Balkans, then as part of Mozaik (in both its incarnations) with music from all over the place; Kíla with world music(?); Dé Danann again, Mary Custy, The Spook of the Thirteenth Lock, Horslips, Sinéad O’Connor, The Pogues, countless “Boston Irish” bands with popular music; Is there an album from a young band over the last twenty years that hasn’t included a tune from Brittany or Asturias? (I’m joking!); "Electric guitar accompaniment, electric fiddles, electric banjos, an electric bodhran, anybody? All done to death over the years.

I suspect my children would be able to double this list with ease. I teach and play with a lot of young musicians. They ask the question “What else can be done with traditional music?” all the time, though with far more nuance and thought than in the OP. And it’s great! Whether the OP realizes it or not, Eliot’s “We shall not cease from exploration, and the end of all our exploring will be to arrive where we started and know the place for the first time.” is the credo of most serious trad musicians.

So ceili bands do nothing for you. How daring? Here’s a “surprise”, I don’t like ceili band recordings or even live playing in concert either, and I’ve adjudicated bands at All Irelands. Trad isn’t a monolith, one doesn’t have to like or enjoy every aspect of it. Tons of musicians don’t like pub sessions, others don’t like accompaniment, others slow airs, or sean-nós, or competitions. But here’s the thing with ceili bands. How often do you play in a band (formal or ad hoc) for dancers, how often do you set dance? Once a week, fortnightly, monthly, half a dozen times a year? Put a ceili band in it’s natural environment and I’d find it hard to believe that anyone with an ounce of grá for trad, someone who dances, someone who plays, wouldn’t be exhilarated by, and adore, say the Kilfenora at full throttle in a sweaty marque in July.

As ever, I wonder if the question reveals rather more about the OP than it’s seeming subject.

“making them more interesting and accessible” usually involves a retrograde step in terms of musicality. A bit like taking the Mona Lisa and sticking a poster of Tretchikoff’s ‘Green Lady’ over it. Re: What Else Can Be Done With These Continuous Questions?

I think lots of interesting things are being done with traditional music all the time. Young groups of musicians are mixing instrumentation, adding harmonies and accompaniment, juxtaposing the tunes in different varieties, and blending them with different forms. Some of it ends up being trad with other influences, some goes over the line and becomes other music with trad influences. Some of it blends into the mainstream, other ideas (like those electronic keyboards of the ’90s) get abandoned, as folks move on to new things.
But, the best thing that can be done with trad music is found in the subtle, not the large, differences. Little twists, turns and variations never stop making it new. It is the nuance that makes it always fresh.

Re: What Else Can Be Done With Traditional Music.?

Recently I came across clips of “lessons” that were really conversations or master-class type sessions with B. B. King, who is now going on 90 and still performing in his “folk” art form. There are a number of them on youtube in this grouping, not sure where they come from. The two links I pasted below are conversations in which B. B. King talks about other great players who influenced him, and how he absorbed and then chose and used certain ingredients from their style which became part of the mix that became his own style. In one, he talks about T-Bone Walker; in another, he discusses what he absorbed from Charlie Christian as well as Django Reinhardt.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jG9CnhzffAE
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tfDIiWbYG6I

One of the fascinating things about these clips is, if you scroll down and read the comments, there are a number of instances in which people express amazement that B.B. King is so intelligent and has so much musical knowledge. (I hope this condescension isn’t racial. I hope it’s that condescension you often encounter about players of “folk” forms from any ethnicity, since that is condescending enough.) I do think that kind of global condescension about the assumed limits of folk playing, is somewhat relevant to this discussion.

The point I’m trying to make, is that the thought, intelligence, discernment, and creativity displayed by B.B. King in these clips, is something I’ve seen over and over again in really great folk musicians no less than great classical musicians. If you ever get to hear somebody like Jackie Daly, Paddy Keenan, James Kelly, Tony Macmahon, Kevin Burke, any number of great musicians, talk seriously about “the music”----again and again, you will see fierce intelligence and discernment. It may not wear a “uni” hat, but intelligence is intelligence, and you almost always see it in the really, really good ones. Fierce creative choices, albeit often subtle ones. Fierce perceptiveness. Lots and lots of thinking going on, about the deeper, wider meaning of what is being done, about the choices being rejected versus the choices being adopted. Lots of thought about how the perspective changes over years and decades working in an art form on a deep level, and how that reflects and expresses the journey through life.

So that’s a little more input about “what you can do with it,” and how people go through a lifetime in a traditional art form.

Re: What Else Can Be Done With Traditional Music.?

I second that, David and ceemonster. The “story of the tune” has always been at least as important as the tune itself. If you learn at the knee of a fine trad musician (I don’t mean in a one to many summer school type class but one to one) she’ll invariably show you how she plays the tune now, how she used to play it, how the person she learnt it from plays it, how well known fiddler x takes the turn, how the piper y does this nice variation, how there’s a lovely setting that z and the lads around that area play, how so on and so on. And that’s at the macro level. Get a serious trad musician in a comfortable setting, among her own people, and even the seeming private will often open up and talk for hours about a single phrase, how they do x to shift the pulse, how a different choice of cut or tap darkens the sound palette, how the timing of a roll changes the whole sense of the phrase.

It such a shame that so many musicians that teach in this way week in, week out for thirty, forty years, have felt hounded off this site over the years.

@ Hurler-- re “hounded off this site over the years.” Forums naturally degrade. It’s called “thread degradation.” People new to trad music are coming to the site continually, and they tend to block out the more experienced people. The new punters ask the same questions, as you might expect and which is natural. They have a right to ask these questions and an understandable need to have them answered. But accomplished players find it tedious to wade through and to answer the same questions that they’ve heard and answered many times before. So they remain silent. The field then seems open to people who’ve been playing for just a couple of years (or perhaps months) and who fancy themselves knowledgeable.
It’s easy to pretend to be something you’re not when nobody knows your face or your real name. It’s a lot harder to be a poseur on a pipes or concertina site. The expense of the instrument implies a deeper level of commitment. Posters tend to be older and wiser. Whistles and flutes (and fiddles), are comparatively inexpensive to start with and the players/posters are younger than on the pipes and concertina sites.

Re: What Else Can Be Done With Traditional Music.?

It’s a strange question, implying that there is a “core” with the possibility of many different offshoots. However, to establish that there is a core in the first place, a standard or ordinary way of playing tunes, is to denigrate somewhat the unique style and nuance of all those musicians we refer to as touchstones. There is infinite variety among traditional musicians - you only have to listen - and if you think you’ve heard and seen it all, believe me you haven’t. While traditional music is big enough to absorb and support fusion and other experimentations, it’s always a good thing to take the time to listen to how the person sitting next to you at a session phrases a tune or ornaments a note differently to you; that’s its own reward.

Seriously - look up Wittgenstein regarding language games…

We as humans assume there is an essential core to many of the concepts we speak about when instead there is only a looser family connection between different distinct phenomena that we bunch together to refer to as one concept.

Eg ITM - it’s not got a specific essence - we only create the distinction through our own personal socialisation into what should be considered within and without the tradition when really it is much more fluid than there being a very specific set essential core…
