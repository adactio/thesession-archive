Critique Request: Piano Transcription, The Morning Lark.

This season last year, I started a music project arranging and transcribing a mashup of the two classical pieces, Pachelbel’s “Canon in D” and Chopin’s “Etude Opus 10 Number 5”. I’m proud to say that after a year, 4 music applications, and maybe over 200 music hours, I finally finished the 1st phase of that project; Creating an official arrangement ready to be learned note for note. Now that I am finished with that project, I’m ready to start my next big long term project; Learning how to arrange Irish tunes for piano. I intended to do this last year, and even started taking lessons again for Irish piano, but the mashup ended up taking priority.

As to not take up too much of your time, I’ll get straight to the music. The music is in two parts. The 1st part is the tune once through with no variations or articulations, and the 2nd part is an example once through with variations and articulations. This is clearly visible from the sheet music, as both parts are separated by 2 measures and barlines. It’s been years since I posted a critique request, and this is my first time posting a critique request that’s not a video; So to those who may not know me, I promise not to take offence to negative critiques. Thank you for your time and continued support.

Here’s the link: https://musescore.com/user/7247256/scores/9894910

Re: Critique Request: Piano Transcription, The Morning Lark.

Thanks for sharing this. I’ve had a play through it.

I may not be the best person to give a critique on this as i. I am a terrible sight-reader, especially when there are two lines to read (although it helps that I already know this tune) and ii. I have tinkered around with playing tunes on the piano for 30 years or so but have never come with a satisfactory way of accompanying them in the left hand. That said, the piano was my main instrument until my late teens or early 20s and, although I am not and never was especially good at it, still feels in a way like my ‘native’ instrument.

Anyway, my unqualified critique:
i. I feel that the harmony could be a little more explicit now and again – perhaps the odd two-note chord (open 5ths or 3rds/6ths) here and there.
ii. It may not be necessary for the left hand to keep the 1/4 - 1/8 rhythm going all the way through – it would not hurt to break it up here and there with some dotted 1/4s. This goes back to point i. – you can use those longer notes/chords to emphasise the chord changes at particular points in the tune.
iii. There is one bit of the left-hand harmony that I don’t understand – C#-F# in bb. 10 & 24. I get that there is sometimes a trade off between consonance with the melody notes and making sesnse within a harmonic progression, but these notes, to my ear, do not seem to fulfil either criterion. I have no doubt that there is a sound rationale behind them – perhaps one that my musical intellect is insufficient to comprehend – but I feel this might be a case of the music being ‘better than it sounds’. Perhaps it would make more sense to me if I were to hear you playing it – my sight-reading is so slow and faltering that it can be hard to hear any flow in it.

You have always shown yourself to be someone that pursues your endeavours in earnest and I have no doubt that you will crack this. I’ll keep my eye out for your book in a few years time… ;-)

Having seen your recent posts about your health troubles, I wish you the very best in that regard as well.

Re: Critique Request: Piano Transcription, The Morning Lark.

Thank you for those details and elaborations Creadur, I can’t say that I disagree with anything.

i. One of the main challenges of the style that I am trying to develop, is keeping the harmony uncluttered with the common “bass note leaping to chord” style, while also keeping the harmony full and satisfying. It’s a delicate balance that has proven to be difficult to achieve. The right hand tries to make up for this, but the lacking rhythmic variation in the left hand caused by it brings it’s own problems, it appears.

ii. Being a concertina player now for a respectable length of time, I think about breath a lot more than I used to. I considered the “breath” the entire time I worked on both hand parts, but unfortunately, I have difficulty hearing it. Attempting to keep the music simple but interesting, while giving it the space of breath is a special challenge. I mean, between points i and ii, I feel like the “math” to solve these problems is there; But the core issue may just be as simple as I just need to learn and practice more “math”.

iii. I have a musical philosophy to “always keep it moving”, but the unspoken rule of that philosophy is to always maintain the flow while in that motion. So in short, I was following the melodic contour while creating what was hopefully an appropriate cadence for the turn. At my current skill level, that was all I was able to come up with. So we can attribute this to my lack of experience. I am certain that you’re a much more experienced listener than I am an arranger of tunes. I’ve tried transcribing several tunes, and though I’m proud of what I’ve done so far, all of them have flaws that have driven me to go through the “critique and development” process, as I re-dedicate to learning the practice; rather than accepting things as they are.

Thank you again, so much, for your time and thoughtfulness!

Re: Critique Request: Piano Transcription, The Morning Lark.

…Putting the above a slightly different way: The onus does not fall upon the left hand to *provide* the rhythm, any more than it does upon a guitarist or spoons player – the tune already contains the rhythm it needs. I know you know this perfectly well already, having heard your concertina playing, but perhaps your training and prior experience on the piano lead you to think differently where that instrument is concerned. I think you could learn something by listening to how accordionists use their basses, and even to how you yourself use chords and harmony notes on the concertina or open-string notes and double stops on the fiddle. You can do a lot more than that with a piano, of course, but maybe you don’t need to do much more.

I realise I am going down a critical rabbit-hole here, all in response to your ‘keep it moving’ philosophy. It is not so much that keeping it moving is necessarily a bad thing – it certainly works for Cape Breton pianists (although I think that Irish music calls for a rather subtler approach) – it is just that I don’t think the left hand *has* to shoulder that burden (if a left hand can ‘shoulder’).

“I think you have to consider that the tune itself always keeps moving, so allowing the bass the odd little rest does not necessarily mean losing momentum.”

That’s an excellent point, and a thought that has eluded me up until now. I never thought of it that way. I’ll have to meditate on this and give it some practice. The breath is such an important element, especially when it comes to maintaining rhythmic interest. I think of it this way; if the music doesn’t breath, how can the dancer/singer? Thanks again, your critique by itself has already given me a lot to work on, and work with, going forward.

Edit: About the bass line not being necessarily responsible for the rhythm, that’s also a great point. I think what may have gotten me here is that usually, accompanists fill the rhythmic space as much as possible, and instead create breath in the harmonies; whether it’s droning a chord or keeping the overall chords simple. Or not even using chords at all, but using open intervals. I like how you said that with the piano a lot can be done, but how that doesn’t always mean it needs to be done. Definitely a lot to think about, but all good stuff.

About the Cape Breton pianists: As much as I appreciate their style, and plan on cultivating influence from it, especially when it comes to “chromatic fluency”(using all the notes). it’s safe to say that this is the opposite of what I’m going for. I love the energy and the drive, but I like my sound to be more open and light.

Re: Critique Request: Piano Transcription, The Morning Lark.

jeff, you’re absolutely right about your point, and i’m not disagreeing with you. Listening to backing and self-accompaniment has been a long time practice for me on all of the traditional instruments. Unfortunately, reproducing what we hear isn’t as easy as listening. “Input” and “Output” are just different. You know what one of the weirdest things about this experience has been for me? As I was playing “The Morning Lark” on concertina today, I realized that I forgot about a lot of the variations and harmonies I use when I was transcribing it for piano. Even for a single play through, there were a lot of things I could’ve included that would’ve still maintained the simplicity and lightness I was attempting. But they were lost on me while I was transcribing the music for piano. Isn’t that strange? And who is the culprit? Compartmentalization?

With that being said, I will admit that I feel like backing is different from self-accompaniment because backers can use their entire instrument for accompaniment, specifically chords. For piano, chords and harmonies could be played in the right hand well enough; But the lower you go, the heavier and more cluttered the sound gets. Now given, this perspective could easily be due to my inexperience, and at my low skill level for arranging, i’m just not hearing more out of the bass register.

Maybe I should transcribe more tunes with backing that are already recorded? I have a library’s worth of material. That way, instead of just listening to the music, I’m reproducing it.

Another late entry: Something that CMO said, “I think you could learn something by listening to how accordionists use their basses,” struck me. When I sat with Billy McComiskey’s class at the O’Flaherty Irish Music Retreat in 2017, it occurred to me that he was playing his basses in much the same way uillean pipers use their regulators.
