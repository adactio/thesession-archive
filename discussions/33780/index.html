Irish/American songs. Are they best forgotten, or should they be remembered?.

I recently heard some Bing Crosby, Irish/American styled songs, on Irish radio. It was great to hear them, as it proves the past is never forgotten. He was very proud of his Irish roots, which came from his mothers side. Her maiden name was Harrigan, and she came from County Cork. Bing Crosby, really made those songs his own. However, while listening to them, I could totally tell that they belonged to another era. I’m sure any Irish/Americans on this site, of a certain generation that is, will remember songs like, Did Your Mother Come From Ireland, Toor a loor a loor al, (The Irish Lullaby), Galway Bay, Come Back To Erin Mavourneen, With My Shillelagh under my arm, and My Girl’s an Irish Girl. I am glad to say, there still Irish/Americans who come to Ireland, and want to hear those songs. Some critics believe that the songs , painted a backward image of Ireland. I think they portrayed a memory of home, for people who had emigrated. Were they of another era.? Are they best forgotten.? Is there still a place for them.? What view do other members have.?

Re: Irish/American songs. Are they best forgotten, or should they be remembered?.

I grew up with these songs, hearing my father sing them to me as lullabies. I think they have immense value if they are acknowledged for what they are. They take their place in the pantheon of musical tradition as a touchstone for Irish-Americans in the previous century, and in this context I find them quite poignant and beautiful.

I think it is vital, however, that we don’t muddy the water of their origins. Growing up, I knew these songs simply as “Irish songs”. I was given no frame of reference for their context, and culturally, I think this is where it stops for most people (Americans, at least). To the culture en masse, it seems the most Irish thing you can do while swilling your Guinness at the local silly-named Irish pub is to intone When Irish Eyes Are Smiling to symbolize your affinity for the mother country (never mind that it was your mother’s father’s mother that apparently had a vaguely Irish sounding name, so you’re mostly assuming). Only after I became enamored with actual Irish songs from actual Ireland did I go back and learn the true origins of these songs.

Are they valuable? Yes. Do they have a place in any sort of authentic Irish tradition? No. Are they important to the history of the Irish people and the diaspora? I think so. Should we associate them with Irish culture? Maybe. Kind of. Sort of. It’s all about context.

↳ Posted by shanty on April 21st, 2014.

Re: Irish/American songs. Are they best forgotten, or should they be remembered?.

I couldn’t imagine songs written in New York, often by people not of Irish descent, being considered “Irish music”. Yes many of these songs have words like “Irish” or “Ireland” in the name, but many don’t. Are you really going to draw the circle wide enough to include any song that mentions anything remotely connected to Ireland (such as the subject having an Irish-sounding name) written anywhere on earth by anyone?

One example: in one of these “Irish songbooks” there’s a song called “And The Band Played On” written by John Palmer and Charles Ward. In the three verses printed the only thing remotely Irish is that the guy’s name is Casey and there’s a “girl with strawberry curls”.

In the same book is “Give My Regards to Broadway”.

Some people don’t realize how vast the output of these Tin Pan Alley songsmiths was. I was at some shop and they had all their music books on sale and I picked up several of these book, in total several hundred songs.

To the vast majority of Americans, and the vast majority of Irish-Americans, these songs (and these songs alone) ARE “Irish music”. They’ve never heard of The Clancy Brothers or any other Irish group; the only “Irish singer” they’ve ever heard of is Bing Crosby. They are astounded when they’re told that these songs were written in New York; they imagine them to be ancient traditional Irish ballads.

Re: Irish/American songs. Are they best forgotten, or should they be remembered?.

Richard Cook makes some good observations. Many of these songs come from the glory days of American vaudville and musical theatre which were, for all intents and purposes, heavily influenced by Irish and Jewish songwriters and designed to convey romantisized notions of Ireland for the children of the diapora. By 1920, these songs had put a nice bow on one of the most remarkable imaging turn-arounds in American history; that of going from the most reviled and dispised immigrant group in the country to that of an established, power-broker in all levels of society in roughly 80 years.

Both sets of my grandparents loved this stuff and when I hear it, I think of their generation and what it meant to be Irish immigrants in New York in the early part if the 20th century. It’s a snap-shot of the time. Those of us that have a connection to the songs will remember them. Those that do not - hey, I wouldn’t blame you if you never listened to this smaltzy syrup in your life. Why would you unless you’re a music historian or something?

↳ Posted by shanty on April 22nd, 2014.

Re: Irish/American songs. Are they best forgotten, or should they be remembered?.

“Many of these songs are speaking about the immigrants experience in a new country. And yeah a lot of them were not written by people of Irish heritage but who cares.”

Perhaps it’s not a case of caring, but pure observation. The songs were not written “from the heart” but to make money. They appealed to an Irish immigrant population, and it was a considerable population, because that was a lucrative market.
Compare this with a song that was composed simply to express an emotion - not with any commercial gain in mind.
I think these commercial songs should be remembered, not for their content, but for their value as a social statement, as has been suggested here. The content hit the sentimental spots purely to reap rewards, and they were exaggerated on that basis. Much like more recent commercial works. That they still have appeal to (mainly older) folk is relevant, but only as relevant as Thin Lizzie’s rendition of Whiskey in the Jar still having some nostalgic appeal to a number of dudes.
You will no doubt find examples of songs (and tunes) which do represent an “Irish-American tradition”, but I don’t think it’s the kind of kitsch that was churned out by New York publishing houses (though they might have helped portray an “Irish-American” image to the masses). This stuff is not really comparable with Carolan compositions (even though his compositions were his meal ticket) and certainly not with sean-nós songs.

↳ Posted by Michael Dineen on April 22nd, 2014.

Re: Irish/American songs. Are they best forgotten, or should they be remembered?.

“I, too, would consider them part of the Irish-American tradition rather than strictly ITM.”

Not only not ‘strictly ITM’ but not even remotely ITM, because they are neither Irish nor traditional (written in America by known composers).

“Are Irish pub ballads less Irish than Sean-Nos”

Irish pub ballads is a rather different genre than Tin Pan Alley pseudo-Irish songs, and if the Irish pub ballads are Irish, then they’re just as Irish as Sean-Nos. American songs are not only less Irish than Irish pub ballads and Sean-Nos, they’re not Irish at all.

“I would suggest that Irish music is comprised of many traditions (Irish Dance Music, Sean-Nos, Irish Ballad Singing in the English Language, Irish-American Music, O’Carolan’s Harp Repertoire”

I would say all of the IRISH examples above, yes, but exclude the one NON-Irish one, American pseudo-Irish Tin Pan Alley songs. Ireland is a nation with borders. America is not within those borders. Songs written here, regardless of subject matter, are American songs and part of American music history. Being not written in Ireland, nor intended for performance in Ireland, the Tin Pan Alley pseudo-Irish songs have nothing whatsoever to do with the history of music of the nation of Ireland.
Yes they’re an interesting window into a time of AMERICAN history and AMERICAN songwriting.

Keep in mind that many of the people who churned out hundreds of pseudo-Irish songs also wrote pseudo-Italian songs and pseudo-‘Negro’ songs etc. New York was filled with people from various places sentimental about wherever they had come from (the southern US, Ireland, etc) and there was a profitable market in pandering to these groups.

At some point, breaking things down into categories becomes a futile exercise. Irish. Trad. American-Irish. Irish-American. American-Irish-not-quite-trad-but-pretty-close-with-some-blues-influence-and-a-jazz-riff-we-got-off-that-street-guy. You draw a large circle and someone complains “those don’t belong.” So you draw smaller circles. More complaints, more circles. Eventually, we have one song per circle. Better to enjoy the songs you like. If you play for an audience, and want to keep them, play the songs they like. Call your collection something if you must but don’t get hung up on the label. Music is art. And art doesn’t fit well in tidy boxes.
