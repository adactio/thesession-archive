Gig or Session ?

Nameless pub in Sligo last night..attended a session only to watch four unwelcoming young musicians play in Eb , a well rehearsed 2 hour set of obscure tunes. At 10:30 two musicians entered the bar, one with a fiddle the other a guitar, they were snubbed and never took the instruments out . I am sick of this, its obvious the four young musicians were well practised but im sorry, this is a gig not a session. The poster outside stating Trad session..but this is NOT a session..it is a private gig and other musicians are definitely not welcome…. as was very evident last night.

I have been playing for over forty years and have watched this changing face of traditional music evolve into an arrogant display of Comhaltas competition individuals, who seem to have taken over pub sessions and removed any social interaction, demanding hush and raised appreciation for their well rehearsed sets… Sessions in my day were first and foremost a gathering, they were a teaching platform, a social platform and should be a relaxed environment were all musicians and the audience should feel comfortable and participate. This has disappeared. It has been replaced by arrogance, ignorance and self promotion.

It is my suggestion, that if such a group of individuals wish to play obscure tunes in obscure keys, they do so on a stage, as a gig and charge people, rather than hijack what was once the heart and soul of Irish traditional music, the welcoming warm friendly session…

Re: Gig or Session ?

The idea that sessions should be all inclusive is what is killing sessions and losing venues. Like it or not, a session in a pub is a performance, and you have a duty to provide music that the audience will enjoy. A session where anyone is welcome to join in and bash away at tunes they half-know all evening might be fun for the participants, but it can be pretty dire for the punters, and very bad for the pub’s trade. A session where a core of musicians who play together regularly provide music for most of the evening, but anyone else is welcome to get up for a song or tune or two works very well - even if the ‘outsiders’ aren’t very good it only lasts a few minutes and doesn’t wreck the whole evening. That is the way traditional ceilidhs and get-togethers have always worked, the idea of a session where anyone can join in with everything just because they want to is quite a new thing. There is a place for it - it can be great fun in spaces which are occupied by just the participants, but it doesn’t work in public spaces like pubs.

If you want to run a successful session in front of an audience but still let anyone join in, the way to do it might be to run it like the current crop of TV shows - let everyone play at the start, then vote someone off after each set.

Re: Gig or Session ?

“Why should four friends who want to informally play acoustic tunes not everyone knows and in Eb not be allowed to do so because of some fixed notion of what a session is? ”

I agree but the key word here is “informally”. The situation described in the OP and what happens in many advertised sessions these days is not really “informal” albeit there may not be an actual set list as such and the players might not necessarily play the tunes as in a gig situation. They have still been “booked” to play there as a foursome and it has not been made clear by either themselves or the management whether any other musicians are welcome.

To my mind, playing “informally” would be if the four friends had just decided to go down to their local boozer “for a tune” or met by chance there and decided to strike up a tune. There was time when this sort of thing happened fairly regularly. Sometimes the music might be brilliant and, at other times, not so special.

I can understand why many bars are concerned about trying to please or, at least, not upsetting punters but I think part of the problem is many of these session are now held within establishments which are completely unsuitable. It’s something for “the tourists” after the meals are finished etc.
There are very few authentic and traditional pubs with suitable space these days which doesn’t help either.

However, when sessions are restricted to “set times” and advertised as “Trad Sessions” etc, then this causes more potential for confusion and, maybe, unrealistic expectations from visiting or “drop in” musicians.
If I am on holiday etc or visiting a different town/village, I am often on the look out for a welcoming session but I don’t take it for granted that the arrangements will suit me or even that I might receive a welcome. Even if it’s claimed as such!

I do agree that the “free for all” dream is an issue too. Sessions shouldn’t be “free for alls” but that can still be “inclusive” to the extent that visiting musicians and/or “improving” musicians have an opportunity to play. Either on the sets or tunes they know or they may be “invited” to start something themselves. That’s quite a different scenario from players just turning up, barging in and trying to take over which is my idea of a “free for all”.

To my mind, it is because that most sessions are “organised” these days and advertised to take place at set times which tends to cause much of the confusion and friction. I don’t blame the management of bars for wanting to restrict the number of musicians and trying to ensure that a certain level of musicianship is maintained but they should be much more careful with their manner of publicity and not just advertise the event as as “Traditional Session”. Maybe “Traditional Music” might be a better descriptor? It’s a subtle difference but it does allow for the possibility that the event may be more of a gig as opposed to an open session.
Also, the fact that sessions are advertised, this also encourages “all sorts” to come along and regular musicians are naturally wary. Social media is a “double edged sword”, in this respect. When people relied on “word of mouth”, you might still get visiting musicians of varying standards dropping in from time to time but this happened on a less frequent basis and was much more manageable.

Re: Gig or Session ?

We all have our own opinions about what an ideal session should be and have discussed such matters many times before here.

While I respect the right of all musicians to attend or organise whatever type of session they wish, I would still prefer that “closed”, “eclusive repertoire”, “half gig/sessions” etc etc do not become the norm. However, there is a place for everything.
My ideal scenario whether Scottish , Irish, or even a hybrid is a good mixture of old and new, with a few good popular standards(not to be confused with overdone cliches). The regulars/leaders would be good competent musicians although not necessarily virtuosos and, within reason, be encouraging to visiting and “improving” players. Licensing Laws and Access facilities permitting, there should be no age barriers for either young or older players and all should be made to feel welcome.
Of course, that doesn’t mean it should be a “free for all” at all and the usual unwritten rules of “Session Ettiquette”(We’ll not go down that road again) should still apply!

One further point which hasn’t been mentioned here. Young groups of musicians gathering in pubs and having their own sessions is not really a new phenomenon. I can recall many sessions in Edinburgh as far back as the seventies which were comprised of “bands” or musicians who eventually went on to form some very well known bands. In many cases, they would play their “own stuff” whether traditional or not and with their own arrangements. Of course, visiting musicians and friends might have joined them from time to time but it was still very much “their session”.

All that is fair enough as long as there are plenty of alternatives going on. The consensus in this thread seems to be that such sessions are now becoming the norm and the more traditional and inclusive sessions are dying out. The other day I noticed someone advertising an “Old School Session” in Edinburgh…presumably, a session like “they used to be”! Ideally, I’d prefer if such sessions were still in the majority.

Re: Gig or Session ?

Undoubtably, things have changed over the years.

I don’t know anything about the particular musicians in the session to which Leitram Lad refers but, from what I’ve experienced in Scotland, I don’t think many of these younger players are actually aware of what is usually expected of them when asked to take part in sessions and the like. Many have not really been “brought up in the tradition” as such or have spent many of their younger years in music schools/Trad Uni Courses and the like. As such, they are geared towards learning music with the aim of performance, composition, and maybe teaching(Once they realise there’s not much in the way of career possibilities with the first two).

So, with a few exceptions, they just aren’t used to the general session scene and what is usually expected there. Of course, they will want to enjoy themselves informally but they usually tend to get together and have their own sessions with their own repertoires which will often sound very “exclusive” to the rest of us.

Now for the good news!
Most of these younger players do broaden their horizons as they get older and gain more experience. They usually start to “mix in ” with the rest of us eventually or are happy enough for us to join in with them. They also make very good “teachers” as I’ve mentioned above and our general repertoire is often “enriched” by the better new tunes and ideas which they may pass on to us. Of course, the more successful of them will no doubt be very busy “gigging” and recording or involved in other projects and not have a lot of time for sessions but the majority will inevitably have to “blend in” with the rest of us one way or another.

As an example of what I was saying:
About 10-15 years ago, there was a folk festival session advertised which was to be led by a young female fiddler(about 17-18). She was attending one of these “degree courses” at the time.
The rest of us had already arrived in the pub and were “having a tune”, as you do. However, rather than joining us, she sat a few tables away with her family and started to play herself. She was very good, of course, and we did join in on the tunes we knew. However, while she was happy enough for us to play with her, she didn’t encourage us to start any tunes ourselves. One of the company, in an attempt to be more pro-active, asked if she knew such and such a tune hoping that she would suggest that we could “start it off”. However, she just seemed to think that we were asking for a request!

Fast forward to 2018. This woman is now a very highly respected musician, tutor, and mentor on the Scottish music scene. She is very approachable and “down to earth” and anything but aloof. I would describe her general attitude towards more “general players” as “inclusive” and encouraging.

I’m not going to mention names but I could think of quite a few younger musicians who have matured in a similar fashion. So, maybe, we shouldn’t worry or despair too much as things might settle down in the long term?
