Re: co-dependence in Irish music, or does it really pay to try to & read minds?

Hey Em, I hope you get some sleep.

One of the reasons I advocate the session style where you announce your intentions before you start playing is to avoid all this. I don’t mean that every single tune or medley is announced, but just as a general procedure. Why? Because people who are shy or have small repertoires have the opportunity to suggest tunes they know, but also the ones that they like to include in a medley. Then they have everyone else helping with the changes etc. and don’t find themselves put on the spot at the changing point.

You don’t have to know the names of the tunes necessarily; you just play a wee snippet until others recognize it. I usually give everyone around the table a few chances depending on how many musicians are present. Of course people aren’t expected to do this, and I don’t instruct anyone, but I’ll do it myself and after a while others who like this style will follow suit.

Having said this, I should add that the stronger and more senior players in my local don’t like this approach and will whine about it when I try it. They claim that it’s silly, or that I’m trying to control the session. Ironically, I think what bothers them is that it takes some of their power away to hijack medleys or take control themselves. Back-up players tend to appreciate it because they’ll get a clue what keys the subsequent tunes are in. I like it because more than one person is in on the change and that can add to the craic

In the sessions I co-host my cohorts aren’t always in agreement with this session style (for the reasons I mentioned above) and I’m often forced to abandon what I see as a good procedure.

By the way, I didn’t make this session style up -- I watched musicians in Ennis doing this.

Re: co-dependence in Irish music, or does it really pay to try to & read minds?

Alright, so I’ve licked my wounds and I’m ready for action.

There’s a good kind and a bad kind of mind-reading. The good kind is general respectfulness and what Andee has described as empathy, which feeds the spirit and makes the people it touches feel good.

The bad kind is paranoia and antipathy, which kills the spirit and makes the people it touches feel bad.

An example of good mind-reading: The person next to you is not playing. You think maybe she is having a bad day and think up some little thing that might make her feel better.

An example of bad mind-reading: The person next to you is not playing. You decide it’s because she hates you and hates your music, therefore she must be an insufferable snob, so you decide you hate her too. So there.

I made a New Year’s resolution not to read minds any more about three years ago. I don’t allow any suspicious, paranoid, self-deprecating or irritable thoughts to enter my head based on an assumption of what somebody else is thinking. If I desperately need to understand some odd behavior for the sake of my sanity, I settle on the simplest, most positive explanation.

Following up, there is another box player that was responsible for keeping the torch lit around here and has been a huge influence on many local players. His name is Martin McHugh from Roscommon, and has been a regular session leader for years. He does not introduce tunes, other than the occassional “let’s play these ones”. However, he had a very standard repetoire for years. People knew or came to know his sets, and we play them even when he is not around.

He’s retired now and splits time between Castlerea and St Paul. In recent years, he has learned or relearned a rake of tunes, so his tune selection is more varied. He’s a great man!
