Re: Is it okay to mix slides and jigs or jigs and slip jigs in a set?

Mixing different types of tunes in one set isn’t the norm in any Irish trad session I’ve sat in on over the past 50 years. When it does happen, the set is often one lifted whole cloth off a modern-era album.

Mixing tune types is much more common in Scottish and Cape Breton trad, particularly sets that go march/strathspey/reel.

For Irish trad, I don’t mix tune types. If someone else does it, I’ll play along, but the change usually kills whatever pulse and flow we had going in the tune before.

That said, there are a few grey areas. Some people tend to play slides with more of a jig feel, making them somewhat interchangeable. Some people also play hornpipes and barn dances too more like reels, which allows them to intermingle. Neither of these is my cup of tea, but YMMV.

And the Golden Rule applies here. Play tunes unto others as they would play tunes unto you. If your session is okay with mixing tune types, go for it. Just realize the same may not be well accepted at other sessions.

The only combination that works rather smoothly is jigs into slipjigs (or vice versa). Some jigs that have a lot of ”long short”/”crotchet quaver” figures might go better with each other, and/or slides (which also have that kind of structure).

Re: Is it okay to mix slides and jigs or jigs and slip jigs in a set?

To be clear, there’s no shortage of mixing tune types on professional recordings and at performances. These are prearranged, obviously, and rehearsed, and everyone in the band knows precisely what’s coming and how to navigate it. It’s also common (though not always the case) that the “next tune” features a different musician than the tune before it.

None of which applies to a typical session, unless your session isn’t really typical because it’s into doing arrangements.

And if you’re stringing tunes together out of desperation rather than tasteful musical choices, then it may not work well musically.

For the OP, my suggestion would be to woodshed at home on building some sets within the same tune type—jigs into jigs, slides into slides, etc. Sort out which jigs, say, go together well among the jigs that you can reliably start. Once you have a few solid sets, woodshed some more to find a jig or two that can be substituted into your set so you’re not always playing the same set week after week.

For instance:
Saddle the Pony/Tripping Upstairs/Scatter the Mud
Could also work as:
Saddle the Pony/The Cordal Jig/Scatter the Mud
or:
Saddle the Pony/Tripping Upstairs/Sliabh Russell
and so on.

Another option is for your sets to be no more than 2 tunes. This means only one transition, easing the stress of coming up with something else to play. You can play each tune 4 (or more) times if you want the set to feel longer. Personally, I’d be happier if whoever is leading the set did just 2 tunes than a longer set of mixed tune types.

Re: Is it okay to mix slides and jigs or jigs and slip jigs in a set?

It’s likely the OP isn’t deeply versed in playing Irish trad. So Nico’s right, but I’d word it, “If you’re mixing slides with jigs, you likely don’t understand how slides are customarily played.” Indeed, the pulse and phrasing are different than in jigs.

The OP asks the question, which suggests an openness to learning.

Jig pulse and phrasing are based around two beats and six quavers (eighth notes).
Slides are built around four beats and a mix of crotchets (quarter notes) and quavers (eighth notes).
If you play slides the same as jigs, anyone accustomed to hearing slides as they’re played for dancing will know something’s off. It’s akin to playing polkas like reels.

The different tune types exist for a reason (dancing), and for many of us trad musicians, understanding and sustaining those differences is an important part of playing traditional music.

I get that some sessions are free-for-alls and that’s what the participants want. Fine by me. Perhaps that’s how the OP’s session works, and mixing tune types is welcome there. But there’s much to be gained by delving deeper into this music and furthering your understanding.

Re: Is it okay to mix slides and jigs or jigs and slip jigs in a set?

gilmo, yes, some single jigs are also played as slides, and vice versa. But when a single jig is played as a jig, it’s a jig, not a slide. When it’s played as a slide, it’s a slide, not a jig.

Bear in mind that the playfulness of this tradition means you can take a reel and turn the melody into a jig if you want, and that’s fine. But first you have to understand the differences between a reel and a jig, and then play them accordingly. Unless you’re aiming for a train wreck, it’s not wise to announce at a session that you’re going to play the Collier’s Reel and then launch into the Collier’s Jig, or vice versa.

And there’d be no point in converting a reel to a jig and then playing it as if it’s still in cut time.

Obviously, slides and jigs (particularly single jigs) are related—they’re built on the span of three quavers per beat. (Or the metric equivalent of three quavers, such as a dotted crotchet or crotchet-quaver combination.) But that doesn’t make them the same type of tune.

Re: Is it okay to mix slides and jigs or jigs and slip jigs in a set?

Okay, that helps. I can see where you’re coming from. But thank goodness there are sessions that aren’t chained to the old recordings, or even tethered to modern era recordings.

The research (see Hammy Hamilton’s dissertation) suggests that pub sessions grew out of the WWII-onward gatherings of Irish people working in London and a few cities in the States. Did some of them play sets from Coleman, Killoran, and Morrison recordings? Certainly. Is that all? Certainly not. For many, sessions were a chance to play with people from the same county or region, sharing tunes that perhaps weren’t on any recordings.

And it’s even more important to acknowledge the musicians grounded in other regional styles who kept their music alive against the onslaught of Coleman records and the like.

I’ve sat in on sessions that played a fair amount of tunes from Bothy Band, Lunasa, and other influential bands, but they rarely play the same sets. They break them up, joining tunes to other tunes, giving the tunes their own interpretations. That’s how the sessions were back in the 1970s, too, even among players who grew up on Coleman and Killoran. They all knew the Tarbolton set, but they weren’t hidebound to it. Just as commonly they’d play Sailor’s Bonnet or Longford Collector with entirely different reels.

I can’t imagine spending 3 or 4 hours week after week imitating the old masters.

Re: Is it okay to mix slides and jigs or jigs and slip jigs in a set?

“Depends on the session” is the right answer. I know sessions which are a Ceilidh band at play and they tend not to mix up tune types. In some sessions people just play what comes to them and have no idea what sort of tune it is and some deliberately switch rhythms. I used to know a well established Irish player who loved to go from a waltz straight into a fast reel. In some sessions it’s polite to tell the guitarist there will be a change of rhythm (as someone remarked here) and in others trying to confuse the guitarist is a mainstay of the fun. Some guitarists and bodhran players like it that way. They get bored. Others can be outraged.
