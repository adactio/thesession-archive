Re: Session Stories

Fortunately, I am getting a pretty good amount of music playing in with other people (JamKazam, mostly) during the pandemic. But I, too, really miss the interaction between sessions and punters. Especially my Sunday session, which happens (or at least used to) in a small pub that would get crowded every week with people who wanted to hear us play. There would be nights where they would be loud and annoying, but there were also the magical nights where they would be very engaged in the music, people would get up and dance, or clap to the beat (which can be annoying, but shows their appreciation), and be so quiet during songs that you could hear a pin drop. On those nights, our playing would feed off of the crowd, and the crowd’s energy would feed off the music. That’s what I miss the most!

For a specific fun story, which was a cross between a session and a performance, one of my bands used to be the “house band” at the Molly Brown House Museum in Denver. They used Irish music because of Molly’s involvement on the Titanic (and the music from the movie). They were having a private dinner for their members, to celebrate (or maybe commemorate would be a better word) the 100 year anniversary of the sinking of the Titanic. So they hired us to put together a session in the courtyard of the Molly Brown House (with more players than just the band), where people were eating dinner. So we were just playing along, having fun, and it turned out that two of the people that were attending the dinner were Irish step dancers (and they had no knowledge that we would be there playing music). So as dinner moved on to dessert and drinks, the two of them got up and started dancing, and eventually every single person at that dinner was up and dancing, rather spontaneously. That turned a session-gig into one of the most fun times I’ve ever had playing music!

Another heartwarming evening was a night in Ireland. A friend and I had decided to go to a filming of a Geantraí in Kiltormer (E. Galway). It was a long evening of filming, but interesting, and worth the trip. The filming wrapped up around midnight, and the place completely cleared out. So we were finishing our pints, and getting ready to leave, when everybody walked back in with their instruments for a session. There were probably 30 people playing music, from the 13 year old box player sitting next to me, to the elderly ladies with their electronic keyboards. The session went for a couple of hours, and it seemed to be pretty much nothing but standard tunes, so we played on basically every set. I won’t say it was the best music (sessions of that size are never really great), but it was organic and real, and people were really enjoying themselves. The best part was the 13 year old kid next to me (who was a great player already), who just couldn’t believe that a couple of yanks were sitting in his hometown pub, playing all the tunes - even the ones he didn’t know. At the end, he asked us to start something, so we played a Fahey tune (since it’s only a few miles from where Paddy lived), and got a large round of applause at the end. It’s a night I won’t forget.

A quiet pub in Fort Lauderdale on Easter Sunday about a dozen years ago.

Re: Session Stories

Michael’s story reminds me of many, many years ago when in San Pedro there was a wonderful Irish pub called Looney’s Tavern owned by Clare fluteplayer Ray Tubridy (cousin of Michael Tubridy of The Chieftains).

Ray was a quirky guy, given to clandestine machinations and enigmatic hints.

One day I came home and there was a message on my phone:

“Cook. Ray. The Boys. They’re here.”

Who were The Boys? Where was here?

A call to a mutual friend established that “the boys” were a number of Ray’s relatives visiting from Clare, and “here” was Ray’s home. It was an hour away, and in an hour I was there, flute in hand.

It was amazing. They had put a huge piece of plywood in Ray’s main room, and it was full of people dancing Clare Sets. The music was spectacular, loads of great musicians including one of the very best fluteplayers I’ve ever heard. He was playing a flute he had made that afternoon out of bits of pipe he found in Ray’s garage. (Ray was a plumber.)

Needless to say there was no end of dancing, of music, of drinking, of craic.

Re: Session Stories

I was visiting some friends who live in Kinvara, south of Galway and playing in a session in Greene’s pub. I think the spelling is correct. Anyone who’s been there will know the long shelf behind the bar that sags with the bottles of whiskeys.

At some point I took a break and went to the bar where my friend Stuart was talking with a fellow who I thought looked familiar. He introduced me to this fellow, John, and we chatted for a while.

I went back to playing. A little later there was a small commotion at the other end of the table we were sitting at and this fellow sat down, was handed a guitar and persuaded to do a song. It was John Prine, and he did a few of his songs - always humble, and playing another only because he was asked.

He did maybe four songs, then got up and went back to the bar. Two older listeners sitting behind me were chatting. One said “Pretty good singer.” The other said “Yeah, but they should give our local boys a chance, too.”

Re: Session Stories

A couple of years ago, I learned a set of tunes on concertina for street performing. The first tune seemed to have a very peculiar rhythm to it, so at the local session I asked everyone if they could help me figure out what kind of tune it was. I start playing it, and not even a few bars into it the session leader and several others go, “It’s a march!”. At this point, I hadn’t learned any marches so it made sense to me that it sounded unfamiliar. Satisfied having finally solved the mystery, and figuring no one else knew the tune because it was “obscure”, I stopped playing it.

I think the question came up of the name of the tune, and as I was having a few words with one of my session partners(who was sitting right beside me), he went back into tune. Excited that someone else knew the tune, I jumped right in and played along with him. It wasn’t a brilliant performance, it being a relatively fresh tune for me, and there were the usual hiccups you get from such tunes. But there was something deeply intimate about sharing that tune with him, amongst our friends and peers. And then, to my shock and delight, he goes into the 2nd march from the recording that I learned the set from. This would be from Altan’s “A Horse with a Heart” album, and the tunes would be “Come Ye By Atholl”, and “Kitty O’Connor”.

https://youtu.be/k_DGVB1CFSw

Now, I already adore this gentleman. Over the years he was always very insightful and generous with all kinds of tune and musical knowledge. And he was always warm and open with me. He helped me build my confidence as I was transitioning from fiddle to concertina, and he gave me one of the most invaluable musical insights I’ve ever received. So learning these two tunes thinking I’ll only get to enjoy them on my own, and then getting to the session and being surprised that I have them in common with someone I have a massive amount of admiration and respect for, I was reeling. From a simple duet with a couple of simple tunes. And I will never forgot those moments. The look in his eyes as we played the final notes. The woots and cheers of our peers. The exchange of smiles and handshake afterwards. I’m reeling again writing about it now.

When I started playing Irish music almost 10 years ago now, I had a strong awareness that for the most part, learning tunes would be an “unloaded” experience. “Unloaded” meaning no memories, feelings, or attachments associated with anyone. I didn’t like this at first, because it made me aware of how much of an outsider I was, even with all of the inclusion I experienced, even in the forums. I would even become acutely aware of this “unattachment”, when going to concerts and shows, and listening to the performers share very personal stories about their tunes. Who they learned them from, the history of the tune, funny stories, etc. But I came to appreciate this unattachment, because I knew that eventually I will have built those relationships, and have those memories, and one day even have my own history of playing Irish music. And I was absolutely right. Memories like this show me that this is what tradition is about. This is what tradition means. This is how tradition is experienced. And I’m forever grateful to now be apart of it.

Several from various festivals, but I was sitting in a busy session in The Bedford Bar at Sidmouth, when in came Lulo Reinhardt (great-nephew of Django): he sat on the stool next to mine, and asked if he could borrow my guitar. I did know who he was (having seen his photo on the festival posters), so suggested that there were better guitars in the room that he might prefer, but no, he said, mine would do. There was also another jazz guitarist there, and between the two of them, they just blew us away for the next wee while and more: the other guitarist later said he felt like the cat that got the cream!

Talking of people borrowing instruments….

I took my mandolin to The Fiddlers Arms in Edinburgh for the first time in 1983 although I couldn’t really play it at all then. However, I intended to “play chords” (I didn’t know any better back then). Big Jim Sutherland of The Easy Club asked if he could borrow same and, of course, showed me how it should be done. Unfortunately, he also broke a string and that was me out of action for the night. Maybe done on purpose, who can tell? :-)
During the remainder of the eighties, I became quite distracted and neglected my instruments although I still enjoyed the music itself and going to festivals and sessions as a punter.
It was thanks to the Scots Music Group (or ALP as it was) in the nineties that I regained interest and actually started to play “the tunes”.

Dougie MacLean has also borrowed my fiddle on a couple of occasions too. The second time was when I was visiting the loo and he obviously recognised it from before. Normally, I’d be a little irritated to come back and see someone playing my instrument but, of course, I was quite chuffed in this case.

Día dhaoibh! Greetings all!

I don’t get to the Internet every day and just found this discussion. Well, I suppose I could write a book about some of the sessions with which I’ve been blessed
However…
…it would appear that there was a session which was very special.

So, greetings to you Trish, obviously, and others here who might recall that beautiful day.

A bit of background helps set the scene.

I worked as a sylviculturist on the Wyndham Estate in Somerset and that’s how I met and had some fabulous times with Paul.
I lived in a beautiful 16th Century ‘Bailiff’s House’ and hosted many weekends including much music and all that goes with it, not necessarily always healthy :-)
Anyway it was at that time I used to visit Exeter too, and met up with a really nice Scottish guitarist of whom you might have heard, Tony McManus, and we had some great fun times and tunes together, and he stayed a number of times too. It was through Tony that I met another Scot, Dick Banovich, yet another very talented and sympathetic guitarist. We did some lovely gigs together, and we were playing one at Sidmouth, ’98 was it? We did our bit, and the next day Dick said to me
“Brian, I’d like you to meet my friend, I think you’ll have a nice time playing a few tunes together!”

So…
“Dougie, this is Brian; Brian this is Dougie.”

Well, we just clicked and one of those magical sessions happened. It felt like we had known each other and played together for a lifetime. He was also trying out a Stefan Sobell cittern and wondering if he should buy it or not.
Tune followed tune, jigs, reels, hornpipes, slow airs… everything, it was effortless…
… and Trish you are right, there was something magical about it. And it lasted and lasted

The silly thing was that I didn’t know who Dougie was, he didn’t know me, and it wasn’t until the end when we exchanged addresses that I got his surname. (Dick was quite a laid back guy)

And yes, I had a grey drainpipe flute which I’d made myself at a party in Cumbria, November ’83. Actually I used an old fashioned up and down can opener to bore the holes and I had a perfectly pitched Bb flute, purely by chance. It was perfect in intervals.
I played it in The All Ireland Slow Air Final in Listowel in ‘85 and the adjudicator said he would mark me down because it was a ’wavern pipe’!
Believe it or not, when I got to Tassie, in one of my first sessions here, a lady said to me
“You’re Brian the Flute, you play a drainpipe flute, I heard you in Listowel!”

Small World eh?

Well I hope this brings back some nice memories.
And hello to Kenny and Paul!

Keep safe and well everyone
All the best
Brian x
