Hobbyists vs. Trad Musicians

I was reading through the “slow down” thread and it occured to me that the argument is happening (I think) between two different camps: those who “grew up with” or were/are around the music enough so that they probably don’t spend a lot of time “practising” or learning tunes (they just play), and those folks who do not come from this background and do spend considerable time trying to build up their repertoires and get better.

Well, if you’re not around the music, why would you choose to play it in the first place? I never chose this music - it chose me. Which is not to say that people who don’t grow up with the music in some way or find themselves immersed in it should not play. It does put them in a different catergory, though. If you choose to play this music in the same way you choose some flavor of ice cream or choose to take up knitting, then you are a hobbyist.

It’s no wonder that these two camps do not agree about the best way to learn tunes. It’s fascinating to me the way that this forum gives everyone the same authority (at least until they say something ridiculous.) It’s both great and frustrating and annoying. So sorry if you feel like I just called you a hobbyist and that you take offense to that, but think about it…. I didn’t say that there is anything wrong with being a hobbyist. Anyone agree with this assessment?

Re: Hobbyists vs. Trad Musicians

Chrishty - there is no polarisation in the way that you suggest. If that were true, there would only be “beginners” and “experts”, with no levels in between.

There are, however some “truesms” that I’ve noticed, with regard to traditional music:

- The more tunes you know, the more you realise how many you don’t know.

- The more tunes you know, the easier it is to learn a new one, as similar phrases occur in many different tunes.

- The more tunes you know, the more diffficult it becomes to instantly recall the title or the melody of any particular tune. Not much of a problem if you are only following tunes that someone else is leading. However, if you are leading tunes (and trying to think of some to play that you didn’t play at last week’s session, this can be an issue.

As a beginner, you have to work very hard at ornamentation, but it something that you can do automatically and without hardly thinking about it after you have been playing for many years.

Re: Hobbyists vs. Trad Musicians

I am definitely a hobbyist. If I’d been a “traditional musician” in the immediate tradition of my own family, I would have gone through piano grades and maybe been a chorister. As a small boy I didn’t want to be “raised” in this - it seemed to involve intimidating demands on one’s powers of comprehension and industry and to invite ridicule - so I wriggled and kicked my way out of it. I am not proud of this now. But it has struck me ever since that *real* traditions of any kind are weighty and formidable, particularly to the young.

My gravitation to folk in general and Irish music in particular was incubated by boarding-school daydreams of being someone else, somewhere else - a crowded 1960s boarding school environment could promote a fairly sustained desire to escape from it by endless fantasy and displacement. I listened to The Incredible String Band a lot and tried to play tunes of theirs on a recorder, the first instrument I tried to play off my own bat. The rest, in time, followed on from there.

So yes, I have been a wannabe and have no problem with labelling myself a hobbyist. My hobbies have always been my main motivators in life, other realities being mainly there to be coped with or got round. To them, I am merely reactive; in my hobbies, I am - to whatever puny extent - pro-active. It is a privilege to include Irish music among them and I hope, as an outsider to Ireland and its core tradition(s), to treat this music in a grateful and trustworthy manner.

Dubh, I suspect (could be wrong) that the distinction Chrishty is trying to make is the difference not between hobbyists and professionals, or beginners and experts, but between people who find themselves already immersed in the music when they wake up to the music in the first place, and people who stumble on the music despite it’s scarcity in their neighborhood, and then contrive to create their own immersion tank with cds and sheet music and festivals and workshops.

Look, it’s one thing to turn 13 and suddenly realize that your parents, aunts and uncles, older siblings, next door neighbors, whatever, have been playing these great tunes your whole life and you just realized that you want to play them too. And a completely different thing to inadvertently catch a tune off the radio or a “celtic compilation” cd at the coffee shop, and then decide that you must learn to play it, even if you don’t know a single soul who plays or even listens to this music.

I’m in the latter camp myself. Maybe I’m doomed to be a johnny-come-lately outsider interloper the rest of my life, despite 30 years already wallowing in this music.

On a more grandiose scale, maybe Eric Clapton is never going to be the African-American blues guitarist he sounds like.

I think Chrishty’s point is that there IS a difference. Someone who awakens to this music having been surrounded by it - because it’s integral to their physical and cultural environs - is going to bring a different mind and understanding to learning it than someone who awakens to this music without a stitch of previous exposure to it.

And at least some (though not all) of the people who come to it without any previous exposure to the music or the culture that spawned it seem to think that they can “get it” by reading the dots and listening to cds, or maybe dipping their toes in at a festival or workshop.

When I awoke to this music at 14, I didn’t know anyone else who played it or knew anything about it. Despite having been to Ireland, having Irish grandparents, and living just 15 miles from Ed Reavy, I might as well have been on a desert island. So I had to seek it out. Lots of other members of this site grew up in families who played this music. Bb, for example, down in Australia, had family who played the tunes. She held it at arm’s length till she was 18, but she brought an understanding of the music-as-life that I’ll never have, even though I’ve been at it longer. And compare that to fiddleruari, in Ireland, whose parents both play this music at the highest level. From cradle to grave, he’s drowning in the tunes and the culture and understands it more deeply--for him, it’s just what life is--than most of us ever will.

My hunch is that this is the distinction Chrishty’s trying to make. And I agree that it’s a valid one, in so far as it explains in part why some people on this site defend the dots and slowdowner software while others lament the demise of learning by immersion, person to person.

Well, Ben, I’ve spent years listening to recordings of Bobby Casey and Junior Crehan and John Kelly and Paddy Canny, as well as as much time as I could within earshot of Kevin Burke and others who are still with us. And my aim all along has been to make music that would fit, blend, with theirs. Yes, it sounds like “me.” We each bring ourselves to our sound and style. But I’ve very consciously wanted to sound “traditional Irish” rather than “American.” Maybe this is because I played bluegrass, Appalachian, folk, and rock before Irish trad, so I was acutely aware of the differences between these genres, and knew that Irish trad sounded different.

It’s easy to let bluegrass and Appalachian influences seep into your playing when you live in the States. And I do play some of that music still. But I want my Irish music to sound like Irish music, not something else. So I’m careful to listen to what distinguishes this music from the others, and to emulate that in my own playing.
