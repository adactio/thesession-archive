Re: Banjo help

Friction pegs are a nuisance when you’re not used to them, especially on the G string, which requires only the slightest turn to adjust.

I would have your friend take the instrument to a shop or luthier to have them file the nut and bridge for heavy Irish strings. (Tenor banjo is typically tuned CGDA, with much lighter strings. So switching to heavier strings for Irish often makes them bind in the nut and bridge).

Then I would have her consider getting geared tuning pegs on it. It will almost certainly require enlarging the peg holes, which should be done very carefully. (I use a hand reamer, NOT a drill, and you have to be very careful not to crack the peg head, especially if the holes are close to the edge…) It always seems a shame to take original equipment off of a vintage instrument, but in my experience, it doesn’t lower the value unless it is really a collector’s item, in which case, you wouldn’t necessarily want to play it anyway… There are some fairly narrow-shaft pegs available that limit the amount of hole enlargement required…

A good setup is really worth the money…

Re: Banjo help

I don’t think the bridge has anything to do with the problem described.

The originanl post describes problems with a sticky nut and plain pegs.

The nut is easy - Use an old set of strings of the right gauges (or the ones currently fitted if you haven’t got any old ones) like files - pull them back and forth though the nut slots a few times to smooth out the slots and widen them to fit the strings, then use a bit of powdered graphite or a 2B pencil to lubricate the slots.

The solution to the peg problem will depend a bit on what the instrument is - from the playability point of view the Reverend’s suggestion of fitting geared pegs is far and away the best solution.

But they’re not cheap, and not original. So on a cheap instrument (which this isn’t) or a vintage instrument which would be devalued by modification, the first thing to do is check that the holes and pegs are actually round. Take all the tension off the strings and try turning the pegs in their holes. If either is oval you will be easily able to feel it. In that situation either the pegs need shaving, the holes need reaming, or both. Both situations need specialised tools, which cost more that a luthier will charge for doing the job, so take it to a professional. But if the pegs feel basically round, but ‘click’ and jerk as you turn them, they probably just need working in. Dab a bit of French chalk (or talcum powder if you can’t get FC) on each peg and give them a few turns, pushing hard into the hole. You might have to repeat that a few times untill they turn smoothly. If the pegs turn smoothly, but won’t stay put and jump loose as soon as you let go of them, get some ‘peg paste’ from a violin shop and draw a single stripe along each peg (DON’T use too much). Again, turn the pegs several times, until they feel right, before putting the strings back on.

Re: Banjo help

Well, I at least believe that I have an inkling of what Eddie is talking about …

We would all agree that the pitch of a plucked string will alter according to its distance from the nut.

But the question is, if we move the bridge to alter the pitch, do we position it so that it is parallel to the frets, or do we position it so that it is at a slight angle to the frets?

The argument for doing the latter is based on the fact that the strings are not all of equal thickness. The need for an angled bridge is likely to be greater is the instrument has a high playing action, as fretting a bass string will bend the pitch more than fretting a treble string

My three tips for tuning are:

(1) Ensure that the head has sufficient tension.
(2) Set the action as low as low as possible consistent with the avoidance of fret buzz.
(3) Fit new strings, as old worn strings can generate unwanted harmonics.

Thanks for the answer FastEddie.

What you say is true.

In fact the tiny differences in tuning you describe will be of the same magnitude as the differences already talked about, in terms of string stretch due to high action or pressing too hard. They will also be of the same sort of amount as the differences inherent between tempered and untempered scales - and as these are differences that you can never overcome on simple fretted systems which fix the notes to the 12throotoftwo step.

And so it is indeed true that angling the bridge is not the answer, because there isn’t an answer (unless you create a completely new fretting system for the banjo, with separate frets for each string, and with which you can only play in the one key to which you tune it).

Re: Banjo help

Mandolins have compensated bridges to give you a slightly different scale length per string and to give you a fighting chance of getting 12th fret fretted and 12th fret harmonic in unison.

However the compensation does not progress uniformly with string diameter. On my mando it goes:
E (unwound) short
A (unwound) long
D (wound) short, but not as short as E
G (wound) long, but not as long as A

There is a reason for this. I can’t remember the exact details but I think it’s something to do with only the core of the string determining the required compensation, not the windings. But anyway it suggests that by angling the bridge on a banjo then you might get at best two of the strings correctly compensated.
