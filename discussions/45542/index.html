Your method of learning tunes

I’m curious how others are leaning tunes so I’ll describe my method. I play by ear, can’t read music at all! I started playing fiddle and whistles at 32 years old with no music background before that. I’m now 72 years old now. The amazing slowdowner and recently Tunepal are my go to for new tunes. I use a soprano ukulele tuned like my fiddle for most of my new tunes to work out the fingerings at moderate or slower speeds. I use the nylon strings that came with the ukulele and tune up on E A And D but use a metal mandolin G for the low note. The ukulele is great, I just flat pick, has about the same neck length of my fiddle and can play laying down in bed, or sitting in car, traveling. Also very easy on fingers and joints and can be muted with a sock that you cut off the toe end and slip over right wrist. You put wrist with sock over ukulele strings right in front of bridge. Depending on how far out on the strings the sock touches. Will determine how quiet it sounds which my sleeping wife really appreciates !!!! When considering the vast number of hours I spend leaning new tunes and keeping up on tunes I already know the ukulele is my choice ! I already can play Irish music on the fiddle so my biggest challenge is pounding new tunes into long term memory and my moto is “repetition makes up for lack of talent” !!!! I noticed that there are a number of different stages in learning by ear. I get where I can play along at a moderate or slower speed even with out knowing the tune name or key !!!! It takes more concentrated playing to learn to start a tune without first hearing it and lastly more concentrated playing to group tunes together !!! In the last couple of years since I discovered Tunepal I’ve got much faster at hearing a tune and playing along !!!!! My Tunepal list is around 550 tunes I can play along at a moderate or faster speed and I add in bow and finger ornamentation on the fiddle!!! So how are the rest of you learning your tunes???? Cheers from Alaska ,Mike Richardson

I think your post is noteworthy because you live in Alaska and because the format in which you learn. Learning with a Uke is odd enough yet to hear the way you confidently retune the strings and all I say, it is worth taking note. I play the accordion, so I wish I could play while my wife sleeps but I can’t even play when she is awake! Learning by ear is the way and if you are slowing tunes down, then you are fortunate.

Although, I must say, bowing in a traditional style is very important. If you can play tunes on Uke that is great but knowing the bowing technique of your favorite fiddler and the way they approach a tune can be a pretty important thing in fiddling circles. As far as bowing is concerned, I would suggest Oisin MacDiarmada’s bowing technique. Listen to everything and then reproduce what you absolutely love. Do you prefer Donnegal fiddling, Sligo fiddling or are you more prone to Kentucky Blue Grass? Important question for a fiddling Ukulele who is on his 500th tune.

If you are looking for a good collection of Irish tunes with main stream bowing techniques, look no further than Comhaltas “Fionn Session.” Getting a good footing on Michael Coleman tunes is a good start also, have fun. My dad always says there is more Irish in Alaska and Montana than in Ireland. Best of luck

Re: Your method of learning tunes

I play flute and I am a learner of the pipes.
When I want to learn a tune that I discovered at a session or from a recording / youtube video etc. I usually try to find a version that seems the easiest for me to learn be ear. Easy usually means for me that not so many variations are included and that it is not played on a banjo :D I am a flute and little bit of pipe player. Learning tunes form accordion, concertina, fiddle and even mandolin is ok - but learning by ear from banjo playing is somehow highly problematic for me!

When I have found a recording that seems suitable for learning the tune, I usually let recording or tune be played at 75 % (a really fast placed reel a but slower) in a loop and try to get the whole tune “on the fly”. Later when I think to I have roughly approximated what is going on in the tune, I start focussing on the bits that I couldn’t figure out so well - and when I get on a another plateau there and notice that I don’t make progress I usually look into sheet music. I do that for the last bits that I couldn’t figure out well and this somehow helps me getting one “exact” version that I could play even without any support at a session. Spending a very long time for the last bits that I couldn’t figure out by ear is just to frustrating for me :D I believe that the best way for learning tunes(and for many different things, for example fitness etc.) is a way that keeps you motivated and on track!
When I learn a tune only by ear I often only get an idea of the tune that has some fuzziness. For playing small variations on the fly, this is great, but for being able to start the tune at a session by myself and “hold the ground” I need one sharp, exact version of the tune as a fundament. Therefore I finish learning a tune by finally looking into the sheet music.
And then I often look for some other recordings of the tune to get an idea how much different it can sound when played on various instruments and in various versions.

I don’t know if this is the best way to learn a tune, but I helps me to keep the fun. Learning a tune phrase by phrase in very low speed always as the risk that I loose motivation…and learning a tune only from sheet music is doable for me, but I fear that I loose the sense for the right groove, variations etc. when I learn from sheet music. Another problem (at least for me!) of learning by sheet music only is, that I don’t remember the tune for a longer period. I can learn the tune very quickly from sheet music, but I also tend to forget it very soon!

When I learn tunes on the pipes I have to do that with sheet music, even if I already play the tune on the flute. Simultaneously playing the pipes and thinking about what I would do on the flute is too much for me at the moment :D And learning by ear on the pipes is something that not me nor my neighbours would enjoy.

Re: Your method of learning tunes

Most of the tunes I learn these days are picked up in sessions. Even during the pandemic, we’re still playing on JamKazam twice a week. Fortunately, the group of players that I play with have wide ranging repertoires, and we try to put a focus on playing tunes that we haven’t played in a while, or tunes that the other people don’t know yet… This is especially important for us now, since each session for the last 7 months has been with some combination of the same 10 players. So we have worked hard to keep variety in our sessions so that we don’t go too crazy. ;-)

So my process is usually to just pick up the tunes I don’t know on the fly. A nice thing about playing on JamKazam and other internet platforms is that you can mute yourself, so that you’re not annoying anyone. If someone brings up a tune I don’t know, I will often have most of it by the time we’re done playing it, and then I will encourage the person who started it to keep playing it semi-regularly for a while. After I’ve “learned it on the fly” 3 or 4 times, it usually gets put under the “known” column in my mental list of tunes. Sometimes I will struggle with parts of the tune and need to go look it up, and find other sources of the tune.

Other than that, I tend to pick up tunes that grab my ear from recordings, and in those cases, I always start working on it at full speed. Then, if needed, I will use ASD or similar to slow it down and fill in the parts I’m glossing over.

Re: Your method of learning tunes

I’m going to be a bit contentious here and say that, like the majority of people that I know playing traditional music, I primarily learn from dots.

That’s not to say I can’t learn by ear. I do an awful lot of transcribing and probably can pick tunes up by ear as quickly as most people (I have been playing music for nearly 50 years). It’s just an awful lot faster with the dots.

Having said that my motivation for learning a tune will have been hearing it live or on a recording. I will then transcribe it or seek out dots, either in books or on this website. For a two part jig, with an instrument (either guitar or mandolin) in hand, I’ll have it memorised in about five minutes. For a four part Scottish 2/4 march it might be a wee bit longer! Then I get rid of the dots and practise playing the tune, gradually increasing the tempo and working on phrasing and ornamentation. It might be a few days (and in some cases months) before I get the tune consistently (i.e., without flubs) up to the sort of tempo that I want to be able to play the tune in public. For tunes that I’ve heard a lot but never learnt to play, it might only take a few run throughs.

If I’ve learnt a tune on the guitar it takes very little time to learn it by ear on the mandolin. It’s slightly longer the other way round, simply because it’s often harder to play on the guitar.

Re: Your method of learning tunes

I’m 41 years old and I started learning tunes with the harmonica and tin whistle a bit before my teenage years.

My method has always been 100% by hear.

And when I say 100% it is 100%.

That means that:

- I never knew the notes of the tunes.

- I never knew the notes on my instrument.

This method has downsides:

- You cannot learn some tunes, they are difficult to understand, you don’t have the capability to learn them yet. At the beginning there are quite a lot of these.

- It is a slow process to learn.

But there are great advantages, the main one being:

- Once you have a developed hear after a few years and you understand the “language”, you just can play almost everything.

But you have to be careful, you always need to double check that you are correct playing in sessions, with recordings or asking directly to other players.

In my personal experience, I got it right with the notes and the melody, but I did a lot of mistakes with the ornamentation with the tin whistle. I thought I was going to get the ornaments by hear with time but I was wrong. I got a lot of bad habits that are very difficult for me to change. That is why I almost never play the tin whistle now (but I keep playing the diatonic and chromatic harmonica without doing very complex ornamentation).

Re: Your method of learning tunes

How I learn a tune, well, first to figure out whether I want to learn it. I’ll learn tunes, at least well enough to play with some who know it better, that I don’t much care for as long as somebody else likes it. Note: I am reluctant to say I don’t like a tune until I can at least take a barebones stab at it. That somehow seems disrespectful to me. Then the learning starts with listening followed by more listening, often from several sources. Though I find no fault in learning from a score, and I do sometimes (I’ll sometimes even score a tune I’m learning), the ear is a most excellent way to learn how to play a tune. There is a strong argument to be made for learning to sing a tune before playing it. Now comes the tricky part. I prefer to learn tunes from back to front. By that I mean I’ll learn the resolving figure of the part I’m learning first. You might say I want to know where the tune is going to wind up. By the way I prefer to learn it the way I want to play it as opposed to a series of notes. Then I learn how to get there. Why, you ask, when most of us learn figure by figure, in order? For me it seems to connect with our evolutionary imperative to anticipate what’s coming at us (a tiger in the tall grass). Each figure in it’s turn resolves the one before it. C’mon, how many times do you hear, or play, a tune with a strong start and an iffy finish? Admit it, be honest, we all do it. Add that to our common feeling of well being with things that end well even if they start badly and we come with good reasons to learn that way. Even when I’m learning “on the fly” I’ll try and grab the ending (resolving) phrases first. I’ve heard from many who play in symphony orchestras that this a common practice in rehearsals. The final step is to play it often in good company. That’s the best way for me to truly lock a tune in place. I find that tunes I learn and never play with others fade all too soon and given the current state of the world all too often. Anyhow, that’s how I do to when I’m on my game and not being lazy.

Re: Your method of learning tunes

This discussion comes up fairly frequently and I often marvel when people explain learning methods that are alien to me, that I sometimes can hardly believe. It goes to show how different people’s brains are wired.

I used to do quite a bit of studio work- it was my fulltime job for a couple years- and that world was entirely about sightreading. It’s just eye-to-hand coordination, you read (often a bar or two ahead of where you’re playing) and you produce what’s written. There is zero learning involved; the instant you play something it’s gone and forgot.

This is so ingrained in me that trying to memorise a tune by sightreading it doesn’t work.

It’s by ear that I memorise. I don’t want to see the dots; they’re an impediment. I listen and play along and I pick up the tunes quickly, and they stay in my memory.

Re: Your method of learning tunes

I’m at the beginner side of learning so this thread is super insightful for me as I am working to figure out the answer to this question myself.

Since everyone is sharing, I’ll also share my beginner’s view. This will (obviously) change and evolve over time.

I find a version of the tune that I can “hear” the melody while feeling the rhythm. This is especially important as my grasp of “what is a polka (or jig or reel)?” is in some ways more important than any individual tune itself. This has naturally led me to good common beginner tunes, like Geese in the Bog for jigs.

Then I’ll listen a lot (on repeat) both actively and passively for awhile.

At some point I’ll feel the desire to start, and will use the core tune setting and use listening slowdown and dots on the fiddle. I can’t really read music which makes it slow, so the ABC would be sufficient and maybe faster, but the dots help me see the step differences in the notes, which I hope will help cultivate my ear over time.

Once I am able to play the tune once through I close down the written music for good, and if I feel lost or forget, I either work on it until I get it or use the reference recording.

I then alternative between playing it, playing it with recording slowed down, and playing it with metronome.

I (rightly or wrongly) perceive the best way to learn tunes by ear and being able to sing them first, using the fiddle to merely manifest the tunes that are already inside. I haven’t tried to do this yet because:

-I have zero musical experience and that includes singing so it’s quite difficult
-I’m working on the fundamentals of tune types versus number of tunes
-I’ve felt an internal push to just start playing (this eagerness may be leading me astray)

I’m now working on being able the sing the tunes I know as a way to get to know them / improve. I also expect (hope?) that this will help me learn to sing tunes I don’t know.

I’m less worried about getting off using written notation since I’m not visually memorizing the tune. I also get frustrated at the tools (I know I know, many of you didn’t have slow down technology so this is a pretty comfortable complaint).

That said, as I write this out, I think “you’re being lazy man, you should learn a tune by ear starting with singing even if it takes you a long time and is frustrating!”

I also want to try learning a tune in reverse phrases.

Re: Your method of learning tunes

I just turned 60. I began playing the guitar at age 8, entirely on my own with the help of an old chord book. I played entirely by ear and developed my own finger style technique. I was taught and grew up with Sacred Harp Singing. It relies upon the shape of the note to reference the intervals between Do, Re, Mi and so on. I sang and played in High School, and during my Junior year, our choir teacher “discovered” that I couldn’t read and reproduce a note (very embarrassing for her since I was 1st chair of the Bass section). So began an intense period of learning to read music and music theory. Around the same time, I began to relearn playing guitar properly, mainly unlearning a number of bad habits I picked up being self taught. I first went to College as a Music Major (Teaching). Thankfully, the College I went to, used students (even Freshman and Sophomores) to keep the local school district supplied with ready substitute teachers. I discovered that I wasn’t enough of a disciplinarian to be a teacher, so I dropped out.
To this day, I consider myself a mediocre sight reader. It’s much easier to listen and replicate than reading and replicating. So although I’ve had a fair degree of classical training, it wasn’t intense enough or early enough to replace the learning by ear habits I picked up. This is both an advantage and a disadvantage in my opinion. The major advantage is that I hear more going on than just the melody and supporting chordal structure. I hear the harmonization and counterpoint even when they’re not present. It allows me to replicate what I hear (in my head) and add in a more complex supporting arrangement of a tune/song. From a Sessions standpoint this isn’t desirable, as too many lead instrument players can be distracted by it, especially those who lack the experience of playing in a formal band or orchestra. From a performance standpoint, it opens a whole new world. Audiences become as enthralled with the support as much as the melody. The disadvantage is that I’ve never became comfortable with relying on sight reading alone. I have to work at it, as it doesn’t come naturally for me.
As I stated way back when I first joined this group, Sessions are not an activity the wife and I’ve engaged in very much. Our experience has been performance driven since we first began playing ITM/STM 35 years ago. So when it comes to the rules, protocols, formalities and so forth of playing in a Session, it’s an experience I’ve not had.
Instrument wise, I’ve added a bunch over the years since that first Sears Roebuck plywood guitar sitting in a corner. Learning to read music facilitated that process. Bouzouku, Banjo (4 and 5 string), Mandolin, Cittern, Lute, a decent barroom Piano, Whistle, GHB’s, Bellows Blown Scottish Smallpipes, and Bòdhran. A few decent tunes on a 32 string Lap Harp (though I don’t consider myself a Harpist by any means), Bass and Tenor Recorder. Currently, the wife’s teaching me to play the Viola. Fingerings fine, but my bow work is shoddy. Funny thing is, my bowling on an upright Bass is pretty good, but when I slap the sucker under my chin, it’s like I’m starting all over.

Re: Your method of learning tunes

How I learn tunes?
Either I find a recording of the tune I want to learn (Youtube, Spotify, CD etc),
or I have recorded the tune at a recent session. About 50/50 for these alternatives.
When I started out, I recorded using a Sony Walkman. Nowadays I use my mobile phone.
I listen to the tune a couple of times around, and then pick it out by ear. On whistle, flute or mandolin depending on the tune or what instrument is closest or handiest. I then transcribe the tune and check that I got it right.
When I started out, I transcribed using pen and paper; since 1995 I use ABC.
I usually pick out tunes when listening at full speed, though I might have to go back a few times to listen to details.
When it comes to styles I’m not so used to, e.g. Bulgarian tunes, I might have to use Amazing Slowdowner to pick out the tune. Before Amazing Slowdowner was available I had a cassette recorder that could do half speed (but everything came out an octave down of course).

Re: Your method of learning tunes

Yes - I sight-read the sheet music while whistling the tune (with pursed lips, rather than with a tin tube). I can’t sing something like a complex reel with much speed or accuracy so whistling works better for me, but I think some people do a similar process but singing the tunes.
The trick is not to think about what actual note you’re whistling and concentrate on scales and intervals. So if it’s a tune in G, pick an arbitrary whistled note to be your G and find your notes in relation to that.

"I have a hard time listening to a tune with variations…my brain just gets fried trying to sort it out…

I’ll play the first melody over and over…this makes it much easier to learn.

If I like a variation I can add it to the tune later."

That’s very much how my first teacher/mentor taught me: he would play what he regarded as the “core tune” over and over until I had it, then he would throw in a variation which I followed, then another, then another, until I could play the tune a dozen or more different ways. He would say “better to learn 20 ways of playing one tune than learn 20 tunes”.

When listening to performances, with players who are constantly generating loads of variations it can be difficult to get a handle on what their “core tune” is, if indeed there is one.

Then there are players who play the first couple times through pretty much the same, so that the listener has a clear idea of “the tune”. Then when they throw in a variation it has a big impact.
