Open vs. Closed piping

What is meant when someone plays open or closed? I think it’s something to do with the way the chanter is handled. Closing the chanter seems to be covering all the holes and placing the bell on the knee, so it must be using the standard piping fingering (Cross fingering every note) whereas maybe the open style is more treating the chanter like a whistle where you don’t put your fingers down to close in between notes.

↳ Posted by Kellie on March 9th, 2022.

Re: Open vs. Closed piping

Closed-piping generally means opening the minimum number of holes required to produce the tone for a note as well as often closing all the holes on the chanter between notes to allow clear staccato articulation of every note.

Open-piping generally means allowing additional holes to be open beyond those required to produce the tone for a note and not closing all the chanter holes between notes to allow smoother legato transition between notes.

Re: Open vs. Closed piping

@Kellie: “Also I play the E by only lifting the ring finger and keeping the pinky down, is this normal?”

E is normally played with both bottom fingers off, with the one finger down type of E reserved for playing off the knee. In other words, if you have to spend some time with the chanter off the knee (going back and forth to the bottom D, for example), the pinky-down fingering might help you stay in tune. But for regular on-the-knee playing, get used to lifting both fingers for E. You’ll learn to treat them as one unit and it’ll feel just as natural as on the whistle.

Re: Open vs. Closed piping

Without having a set of pipes in hand, you’re very much putting the cart before the horse. I get that you’re excited and trying to learn as much as possible, but you’re going to get answers that are either wrong, or that you have interpreted wrong, and that will hurt as much as help your progress. The best way to soak up as much knowledge as possible is to listen to a ton of music - for piping, specifically solo piping will be the best. Literally listen to the same album for a year.

And then get a teacher, when you have the pipes, who can guide you through the basics until you come to a point where you can start developing your own style (several years into playing). In those years, your teacher will have helped you learn all the technique needed to play in either a “closed” or “open” style. The fingerings are the same in “both” cases - keep in mind that as said earlier and on other discussions, no great piper in the world plays in a completely open or closed way - every piper uses a mix of these “styles”. You’ll have the tools to then develop your own playing into whatever you want. In that time, you’ll hopefully be listening to a ton of piping still, and you’ll realize that there are no clear lines between open and closed, but a spectrum of different styles, all personal and yet related and similar.

Re: Open vs. Closed piping

Yeah, the definition of articulation can be a bit vague in this context. When I say notes are articulated in the closed style, I mean specifically playing the note by opening the minimum (ideally) number of holes required to produce the pitch starting with a closed chanter, for the next note, closing the chanter, then opening the minimum (ideally) number of holes required to produce the next note, rinse and repeat. The timing of the closing and opening influences whether it produces a staccato vs. more semi-legato feeling. There are very similar distinctions on the button accordion as well based on button press vs. release times during note transitions and can produce very similar results to closed vs. open piping.

Re: Open vs. Closed piping

The way to think about this is from asking the question “How do you even start to map an instrument as complex as the Uilleann pipes chanter to a electronic stick with some sensors?”

Well, the way I did it was to consider that there are 7 holes on the front of the chanter, one hole on the back of the chanter, and one hole on the bottom of the chanter.

So 9 possible open/close states.

0 000 0000 0
to
1 111 1111 1

If you treat that as binary, there are 512 possible combinations, you need to come up with which MIDI note on number needs to be sent to the synth for every one of those combinations.

Now, there’s some simplification that can be done. We’re still talking about raw pitch here, not timbral expression, not finger vibrato, none of that yet.

The back D hole pretty much always produces a second octave D pitch when opened, so you can just eliminate that case and send a second octave D note-on when that hole is opened.

So now we’re down to just:

000 0000 0

to

111 1111 1

or 256 possibilities.

So, the bottom of the chanter, again as far as pitch is concerned, is primarily used to produce the bottom D note when the rest of the chanter is in this state:

111 1111 0

So, that’s one special case you can detect and pretty much ignore the bottom D sensor for the rest of the notes. Again, just talking about pitch here, not about the changes in timbre that one can achieve when opening the bottom of the chanter, or hard vs. soft E’s none of that, just basic pitch. I’ll talk more about what’s possible with the bell sensor later.

OK, so now we’re down to:

000 0000

to

111 1111

only 7 bits so 128 possibilities.

So, now you take an actual chanter and you finger every single one of those possible combinations and record what note is played, and if that combination might be slightly flattened because of “finger vibrato” lowering by lower fingers. I did exactly that for my iPad Uilleann app many years ago with my own concert-pitch chanter, and it’s the basis of the WARBL fingering map as well.

From that you construct a chart that has every possible combination, the basic pitch, and a flag if it’s a “finger vibrato” combination. The first number in each entry in chart is the MIDI note number that gets set for that specific bit pattern, if there is a second number, that’s a flag that says “this is a finger vibrato case” and the firmware knows to send a pitch bend along with the note on event to slightly flatten it. The amount of pitch bend is controllable both by the WARBL settings and the synth receiving the data:

The next level of this would use the additional information provided by the bell sensor to send some additional modulation (it’s beyond the raw note number + vibrato flag) control MIDI message that a synth could use when the WARBL is off the leg, for example to change the frequency response of a filter to alter the timbre of the tone being played. That enhancement has not been implemented yet for the WARBL, but certainly could be in the future. It would also require a synth module that would be able receive this additional information and act on it.

I hope this provides a bit of clarification about how the WARBL does what it does and also about some of its fundamental limitations today compared to a real set of pipes.

Re: Open vs. Closed piping

Yes, it does, more or less.

If you have a whistle try fingering a first octave A:

xxo ooo

now try alternating

xxo xoo
xxo ooo
xxo xoo
xxo ooo

and

xxo xxo
xxo ooo
xxo xxo
xxo ooo

and you should hear that the amount of the pitch bend is pretty much just a fixed offset. The WARBL models that offset for the “xxo xxo” and “xxo xoo” fingerings, but doesn’t really know about minor differences in the finger vibrato offsets that might happen for some different notes. In actual practice, the current system works pretty well, but again, could be made more sophisticated in the future.

Additionally, the WARBL supports more than just finger vibrato, because it uses optical sensors, it’s possible to do true half-holing as well as finger vibrato. I don’t personally use that feature, but I know others who do and get great results.

To me, as a low whistle player who has recently started on the pipes, the difference between open and closed pipi is very much like the difference between slurring and tonguing on the whistle.

Some whistle players slur all their notes, articulating entirely with grace notes with only a soft glottal stop from their mouth (e.g. Paddy Keenan on low whistle).
