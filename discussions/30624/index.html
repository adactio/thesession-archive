Re: Keyed Wooden Flute info Wanted

The so-called “Irish flute” is a modern copy of the standard orchestral flute of the last three quarters of the 19th century.

These flutes, without any keys, produced the notes
D E F# G A B C# (plus a very good crossfingered C in the lower two octaves, and a decent crossfingered Bb in those octaves)

The standard flute had 8 keys

Low C
Low C#
Eb (these three keys were on the footjoint)
short F
long F (these two keys offered alternated fingerings for the same note)
G#
Bb
High C

For a modern “Irish flute” you really only need four keys for a fully chromatic instrument
Eb
F (either one will do; you don’t “need” both)
G#
Bb

You don’t “need” the high C key because the crossfingered C’s in the two lower octaves are quite nice on a properly made flute.

Some 19th century professional flutists had their flutes made with only one of the two F keys, and some of them had their flutes made with no high C key.

Re: Keyed Wooden Flute info Wanted

BTW the footjoint keys for low C and low C# weren’t there to make the instrument chromatic, but rather to extend the range downward from D to C.

You’ll sometimes see late 19th and early 20th century “old system”/“simple system”/“Meyer system” flute with keys to extend the range down to B, Bb, A, and sometimes G.

“The so-called ”Irish flute“ is a modern copy of the standard orchestral flute of the last three quarters of the 19th century.”

That’s not true. The 8-key simple system flute reached its final form around 1820, or just before, and was pretty much superseded for professional use by about 1860. Many commentators put that date earlier than that.

Re: Keyed Wooden Flute info Wanted

It depends on the repertoire one has in mind. Ask here and you get answers centred on the irish repertoire. Go only a little outside that and (as in the case of Scottish bagpipe tune) the advantages shift.

Scottish D minor fiddle tunes (and some O’Carolan) really need the Fnat key, but often don’t use the C# below the lower D. Transpose them into E minor and the Eb key is usually needed. More tunes use G# in the second octave (which often cross-fingers well) than the one in the first octave (usually very veiled). English tunes often seem to need that lower C and C# that recorders have.

Re: Keyed Wooden Flute info Wanted

The old system flute was replaced by the Boehm flute by 1860??

Let’s remember than the Boehm flute with cylindrical bore was only invented in 1847.

Nancy Toff, in The Flute Book, writes:

"While the French were quite quick to take up the Boehm flute, making only minor mechanical changes, flutists in other parts of Europe took other approaches to remedying the defects of the old system flute. In Germany work continued on the traditional conical flute; in England there were several entirely new designs as well as substantive modifications to the Boehm flute.

Other flutists rejected both the old system and Boehm’s and set out to devise their own solutions.

The end of the 19th century witnessed a significant change in orientation among flute makers; they acknowleged that the Boehm flute was here to stay.

By the first decade of the 20th century the Boehm flute was still not common in Germany, Italy, or Russia."

Re: Keyed Wooden Flute info Wanted

Have a look at the history, Richard. Boehm registered his patent in 1847. However, he had been making the new system flutes since 1828, and, by the 1830s, they were already widely made in England and in France. By the early 1840s they were even being made in America - that’s how fast the spread of this new system was. The new flute was so self-evidently superior that, yes, it pretty much replaced the older form within about a few decades (from the 1820s, when the system originated). In France, it had more or less completely replaced the older flute by 1850.

When Demersseman was 19, in 1852, he had already been considered a great flute virtuoso for many years. However, he refused to adopt the new Boehm system flute, and was therefore refused a position as a Professor at the Conservatoire de Paris. It was already, by that time, the only form of flute accepted at the Conservatoire.

The fact that some people (mainly amateurs, from what I gather) continued to use the older form of flute right into the 20th century does not mean that it hadn’t long before been superseded. 1860 is a very late estimate of when that occurred.

Re: Keyed Wooden Flute info Wanted

It really depends what country you’re discussing, so one can’t generalize “professional flautists.” The Boehm system became prevalent in France decades before it was widely accepted in England or Germany, for instance.

There aren’t many clean delineations in 19th century flute history. Ardal Powell’s The Flute is a must-read that ably describes the instrument’s circuitous history. (And corrects some of the incorrect assumptions in other histories, such as the Toff book referenced above, which is built around rather un-nuanced assumptions about the march of progress.)

As far as the OP’s question goes, there really is a lot to be said for the standard 6-key set-up. I’m playing a piece this weekend that involves several key modulations, and I’m grateful for both F keys (I use both in one bar in the key of Eb) and the C key. If you’re strictly playing Irish trad, fewer keys are fine.

One quick clarification: what Irish flute players call a ‘D flute’ is a ‘C flute’ in classical/Boehm flute nomenclature. Regardless of the nomenclature, it’s a non-transposing instrument. If you get a simple-system flute in C, you’ll wind up with a transposing instrument--probably not what you want.
