Controlling your sound

I know that ITM has a pretty well-defined house style, but nonetheless, different groups of people playing it do all sound different – at least to those in the know. I’ve just been surfing YouTube to satisfy myself of that point (and discovered Zoe Conway in the process. You can keep The Corrs…). She clearly plays in a different way depending on the genre – but does the same thing happen *within* ITM?

So to what extent do people here ‘choose’ their sound, either individually or collectively, and to what extent does it just happen as a function of who is playing? I’m thinking of style rather than different instrumentations. I was also watching Folk Hibernia last evening, and there was Christy Moore describing Planxty as “the sound he had been dreaming about”. So I wonder to what extent they ‘engineered’ their sound, and to what extent it was, even for them, a happy accident. Likewise the Bothies etc.

To which it is possible deliberately to alter the sound of a group of people such as in our newly-formed band, (should we want/need to), or is /should the music (be) so ingrained that you can only do what you do? Or this is just something that you have to be professionally good to have much real control over?

I am also curious as to whether anything similar happens in sessions.

Re: Controlling your sound

Nothing similar happens in sessions, apart from when someone loud and obnoxious arrives with a similar instrument, and has to be asked to tone it down or leave. At the risk of generalisation, most sessions sound like whichever musicians have turned up that night. Although that’s not to say there aren’t the odd control freaks around. You can get the odd variation, when a flautist will pick up a whistle, or someone who doesn’t know the tune will play some percussion, or a fretted instrument player will vacillate between melody and accompaniment. That’s all that happens in my regular sessions.
If you’re in a band, apart from the efforts of the sound engineer, you can make changes to your sound by changing the instrumentation, not to mention the arrangements.

The people who strike me as being *particularly* able to change their styles at the drop of a hat tend to be:

(1) Extrovert natural mimics,
(2) People who began by playing pop back when it was good,
(3) And are still alive.

He’ll know how to bloody play the stuff, that’s the thing. He’ll know his instrument backwards, and the different ways people have used it. Re: Controlling your sound

The degree to which your band’s sound is a conscious choice is often largely determined by the degree to which one person is “in charge”. If you have someone who is serving as the ringleader, band director, and general instigator, and that person is accepted in that role (in other words, not just a self-nominated control freak) then that person can create a band’s sound that is something other than the sum (or average, perhaps) of the sounds of the people in the room. That’s a difficult thing to do well, but it’s a great thing to be a part of when it works.
If the band is more of a democratic affair, it’s harder to control things in that way. You can get great sounds either way, but if you want control, let one person act as the conductor, let them know what you’re looking for, and trust them to make it work. If it doesn’t work, you can always try something else.

That’s collective sounds. Individually, I find that I have a thing that I do when I play on my own, which I vary somewhat depending on who I’m playing with, and I’m partly in control of the variation and partly not. It’s the partly not that’s the most interesting, because it gives me the ideas I wouldn’t have thought of on my own. On the other hand, being in control of what you produce is very useful if you’re trying to construct a particular sound.

As a mandolin player, you can vary your sound by

changing instruments - a modern oval style sounds nothing like a vintage model A, which sounds nothing like a Model F of any year.

changing your instrument - tailpiece and bridge and strings make a big difference

changing your pick - this can change your sound, and it can change your whole style. I can’t play triplets with a big chunky Golden Gate pick, but it sounds neat for jazz standards

changing your posture - if you usually play standing, sit down, or vice versa. See what changes. If you strap over one shoulder, strap over the neck and see what changes. Biggest effect is whether or not the back of the mandolin is against your body. Since it’s a small instrument, this can make a big difference in the resonance.

changing your nose - as nicholas points out, the Lunny beak seems to have a big effect on his playing. Plastic surgery isn’t cheap, but it might pay for itself in the long run.

Re: Controlling your sound

Sure, you have to have the chops to make the sounds you want to make, but the word “control” strikes me as both a little too tightly wound, and overly optimistic. :-)

Thinking as a fiddler, with no small chunk of my life spent hoping to coax music out of a taut-strung wooden box with a lash of horsetail tied to a stick. If Rube Goldberg designed the uilleann pipes, then the fiddle bow was thought up by Kafka….

So the terms “bow control” and “bow management” (common in formal pedagogy) always give me an uneasy feeling. Yeah, I grok that music happens only when we can make the subtlest, finest moves of hair against string. Bowing is nothing if it’s not minutiae and nuance. As Grappelli liked to say, you don’t bow the strings, you caress them.

But I’m utterly convinced that this is something we can’t force. We can’t make musical bowing happen; rather we have to let it happen. The harder you work it, the more it becomes hard work, and that’s not musical, at least not for this music.

What helped me the most was losing my expectations and ambitions for bowing, ditching any pretense that I was in control, and simply settling into a state of completely relaxed intense focus. To me, this is what *listening* is. Genuinely hearing and integrating the sounds I’m making. Then gradually, gently playing with the possibilities until I’ve explored them all and they feel familiar. Not a short-term process. And there’s always something new to it. Especially when you play with other people.

(One of the best parts of playing in small sessions is discovering how to blend with other musicians, other instruments, other styles and sounds. Instead of always playing just like yourself. The more you can blend, the more musical you become. And the more you explore the possibilities, the clearer your own preferences become. So your sound becomes increasingly your own. Funny paradox, that.)

Sure, call it “control” if you want, but for me that term doesn’t help me to relax, doesn’t loosen me up to let the music happen, nudges me to steer the tune instead of following it. But that’s just what works for me. YMMV.

Re: Controlling your sound

I think variety is the key for a new band, not control. successful bands typically have about five members, several of whom are multi-instrumentalists. Listen to whole albums, or stage sets, and what should strike you is how you rarely hear the same instrumentation twice (and certainly never twice in a row). Each tune-set or song is arranged with different voicings, to keep the audience interested. If you can’t produce that variety of sounds, you’ll struggle to keep anyone other than true aficionados interested for more than a few minutes, no matter how good you are.

“So if Goldberg made the pipes and Kafka came up with the fiddle, who devised the button box? Riddle me this…”

The button box was invented by Leonardo da Vinci to drive the Belgians out of Tuscany.

Working closely with Sean O’Riada, he commented:

“On hearing the noise of this machine, the besiegers of the city from which it proceeds will either flee precipitately, or they will reason, ‘Surely the defenders are but peasants, incapable either of art or war, and we shall make a speedy finish of them.’ Thus disarmed in mind, the besiegers will be routed easily by a sudden and determined sally.”

The relevant diagrams by Leonardo were held incommunicado by the Pope for hundreds of years but were eventually leaked. This is why Italy has long excelled in accordion manufacture.

Nobody knows what happened to the Tuscans and the Belgians.
