Delicate question

I’m English. I grew up in a Manchester neighbourhood that gave me lots of justification to become endeared to the Irish people and their culture. But I left the U.K. and came to live in Australia when I was 14 (1965), and there is a question that I’ve always thought about since I started following this site. We have had a couple of really great and nostalgic video clips recently of old sessions in London. And then there was a more recent one in my own old hometown. But here’s my question:- From anybody who was there at the time, how did the I.R.A. bombings, mainly throughout the 70’s and 80’s affect the popularity of Sessions in England? I also ask this question with my current Muslin friends (and relation) in mind. I hate prejudice as much as I hate terrorism. Please lets not get political, but any objective knowledge would be welcome.

I was on the point of going to Australia then as well, Gobby -- as I recall, I needed £200 to be allowed in, and I had £190 when I was laid off, despite the gaffer promising that he would keep me on. By the time I found another job, I’d spent half my savings, so I never did become your neighbour.
As far as sessions went at that time, I don’t recall any adverse reaction locally. The IRA attacks were targeted mainly in the south, where I rarely ventured, and aimed at more political or military locations. The social scene didn’t change, except possibly close to a recent bombing. I think the main difference with the current muslim situation is that then the ‘enemy’ was known, and their whereabouts fixed, rather than the disparate, rootless perpetrators we are facing now.
There is the story, if you haven’t heard it, of the bouncer on the door of an Irish pub stopping someone at the door -- “I’m afraid I can’t let you in with that great bag under your arm.”

Re: Delicate question

I remember when we went to Ireland in 1974, we were told not to put the Australian flag on our backpacks, because it has the Union Jack in the corner. The usual choice was the oval white sticker with the black kangaroo.

We never ran into any problems or anxiety in sessions in England or Ireland, although I was sized up by a chap in a session in Dublin. He asked me what I did, and I responded that I worked in electronics. Ah, he said, timing devices, that sort of thing. I agreed that that was the kind of thing I could do, and he asked me if I was interested in doing some work “on behalf of a political organisation”. I demurred and he drifted away. What I don’t know is if he was recruiting, or sussing out people who might be recruitable.

One night in England, we went to a library and stayed till late closing time. As we were travelling in a camper van, we decided to just stay there across the road from the library for the night before going back to our usual haunt. In the middle of the night we were woken by heavy banging on the door and the nervous words “Police here, what is your name?” When I said “McGee” I heard one of them say “shit!” We came out and we and the van were searched. Turns out that the rather ugly building we were camped outside was the telephone exchange, and that the IRA had blown up the exchange in another nearby city the previous night.

Back in Ireland, we’d been at a session in Spiddal with, among others, concertina player Paul Davis. We offered him a lift afterwards. As we left the area we came upon a Garda road block, and as we slowed down, Paul started playing BABABA on the conca. The Garda was very unimpressed and again we got searched. It turned out that there had been a breakout of IRA men from Mountjoy prison. Fortunately, I’d met Paddy Glackin’s father Sergeant Tom Glackin at the previous Fleadh, and we were on our way to camp in their backyard, so I dropped his name unmercifully! After a few radio calls, we were allowed to proceed. Conversation in the van was subdued for a while.

So, thinking about the above, I guess there was an undercurrent which we didn’t let bother us.

Re: Delicate question

Your story about the guy approaching you in a Dublin pub, Terry, sounds to me like someone from the British forces trying, as clumsily as only us Brits can, to suss out whether you were a terrorist.

I experienced no issues whatever in sessions in Ireland, England, Wales and Scotland throughout the 70s, 80s and through to the present day. I used to drive around Ireland in the late 70s and I always displayed my GB sticker, and all I met with was hospitality. I did once have a fella try to pick a fight with me in a pub, but it was nothing political and nothing to do with me being a Brit, and the landlord, an ex-boxer, literally threw him out.

As for issues in sessions in England and Wales, I was living in Birmingham during the bombings there, and experienced no change in the atmosphere at Irish sessions, even in Digbeth. I was also living in South Wales at the time of the Newport pub bombings and again, as far as sessions were concerned, there was no noticeable effect whatever.

Re: Delicate question

I’ve lived and played in Manchester for 25 year and was in the very spot the day before the big 1996 IRA bomb took out part of the Arndale shopping centre. Ironically, I think the bombing actually led greater cohesion, with the Irish community distancing themselves from the event and the local council taking steps to promote a positive multicultural and inclusive agenda. In particular one councillor, Pat Karney, who was also responsible for the city centre, did a big job in restoring confidence; and Mancunians joked that the IRA had done more good for the city in rearranging the Arndale centre, than generations of planners. And though multiculturalism and Karney have their critics they seemed to do the job. But it is also significant that no one was killed or maimed.

The council funded initiatives,together with the Irish Government, and big sponsors like Guinness; the Irish festival got bigger and Irish community care got better financial support.

The mid 90’s was the height of the faux Irish pub scene and Youth culture was bouyant, post Madchester, and the session and gig scene was very lively. This is the environment in which Mike McGoldrick, Dezzie Donnelly, JonJoe Kelly, Ed Boyd and so many others developed their music. In 1997, Club Cheoil was funded, partly by the council, to make a recording of young Irish trad musicians called “In Safe Hands” (which is listed in therecording section of this site). The Central Library also assisted with the creation of a Trad music archive for the City.

Riverdance, the Celtic tiger, the peace process etc seemed to maintained quite a positive image of Ireland and sessions were in demand in pubs. Interest from publicans only really wained in the late 2000s. Now we seem to be back into good times again for sessions in Manchester.

Re: Delicate question

Long ago (ca 1990?) a friend in Belfast drove me through town and, pointing to the right, said there used to be a great Irish session there. He was pointing to a gap between two buildings: the pub had been blown up. Any idea where this was?
November 1981: flew from Heathrow to Belfast with a troupe of touring Okinawan musicians (I was lecturer/interpreter). Scary: 1) No carry-on baggage allowed. Why? “Security” – no further specific reason. 2) Between Belfast airport and hotel, scary-looking machine-gun-toting military types lined the roadsides. 3) The hotel itself was surrounded by a wooden barrier, and our luggage was searched at an entrance gate. (Barrier still there two years ago.) It turned out that this was the day of the funeral of Robert Bradford, a Protestant member of the UK Parliament who had been assassinated by the IRA in Belfast. - That afternoon I took a taxi to the local BBC to promote our concert on radio. I mentioned to the driver that I found this a rather frightening city. “Don’t worry,” he re-assured me with a smile, spotting my North American accent, “we’re not after you.” Thanks, buddy; would you mind circulating my photo to ensure I’m not targeted?
