Re: Bodhrán

As corncrake says, it is an instrument like any other, with its particular capabilities and limitations. Personally, I do not feel any great compulsion to play the bodhrán (although I enjoy playing together with a well-played one) but I imagine those that *do* enjoy complementing the tunes in a constructive way, much as any backing musician does, and allowing their own personal expression come through in subtle ways, whilst working within the limitations of the instrument. Perhaps more than any other instrument in Irish Traditional Music, playing the bodhrán is about creating synergy between musicians, since it is not, as a rule, used as a solo instrument (although many melody players too – including myself – are drawn to that aspect of Traditional Music).

I am sure there are some that perceive the bodhrán as an easy route to participating in traditional music, that being their primary motive for taking it up. But anyone with access to a good traditional music scene is surely quickly disabused of that, either giving it up, going on to pursue it in earnest or taking up a different instrument.

I’ll ignore the rather trollish nature of this thread and point you in a couple ways about why drumming can create pleasure, as well as help your brain.

Drumming can actually make you smarter, as it helps engage all parts of your brain, and is especially good at strengthening the links between the right and left hemispheres of your brain.

Drumming releases endorphins, enkephalins and Alpha waves in the brain, which are associated with general feelings of well-being and euphoria.

Playing bodhrán with other musicians is a social interaction that can give you a better connection to life (like the rhythm of a heartbeat) and can give you a better sense of self worth, much like playing a melody instrument in this music can… That’s a good thing. And some people are more naturally drawn to percussion than melody, and that is also a good thing.

For myself, I quite like a well played bodhrán. Can a bodhrán have a negative effect on the music, absolutely, but no more than any other poorly played instrument…

Living here in California in the 1970s there were few opportunities to see Irish music live.

I went to a concert around 1977 and there was a lovely little band consisting of a fellow playing uilleann pipes, a fellow playing bodhran, and a female singer.

He was of the opinion that one had no business accompanying any tune if they couldn’t play the tune on a melody instrument. I know this will sound wrong to many, but I think his point was a valid one, that knowledge of the tune was important, whether he was backing the tune on drum or chordal instrument.

Re: Bodhrán

For me, I started out as a drummer in grade school and played drum kit in rock bands through high school and my early 20s. I liked the sound of ITM but wasn’t really into playing it till a friend introduced me to the bodhran.
Bodhran became the “gateway drum” to the music for me, and how I learned the different tune types and how to listen to the countours of the tunes as an accompanist.
I already sang a bit, and later picked up the harp, whistle and mandolin as I went along. I particularly like playing the harp as a solo instrument and, at least before Covid, had a nice second income as a harpist and singer, but when I get the chance to go to session I always find my way back to the bodhran.

What do I like about bodhran as opposed to other percussion?
Its flexibility. The ability to play rhythm and rolls one handed, and change the pitch and resonance with the other means you can get a pretty good range of sounds and textures with a single drum. While it may not have as wide as palette as a drum kit with cymbals, it does pretty well and even outside a session does a great job accompanying small acoustic groups with a tone and range I personally like better than other kinds of hand percussion, such as a cajon or djembe. Plus, it’s a heck of a lot easier to carry around than a drum kit.

What do I like about bodhran as opposed to the melody instruments I can play?
Well, out of the various element of music, rhythm, melody, harmony, dynamics, timbre, etc, the one that’s always spoken to me most strongly has been rhythm. Not that the others are any less vital, but that’s the particular place I find my feet. ITM has a rhythmic sense that really appeals to me, and being able to lock onto that and amplify it is something I find more satisfying than playing melody. Of course, there’s the social element too, melody and accompaniment work together in a way that’s greater than the sum of the parts, and it is a lot of fun to be part of it when it’s really rolling along.

Re: Bodhrán

A post above asks for a definition of “bodhran”, I think? The answer is quite simple.
The bodhran is a percussion instrument consisting of a frame and a head. The frame can be of varying diameters and can be shallow or deep or in between; it is made of wood or some kind of plastic, and may or may not have jingles of various types. The head is always made of goatskin or some other material. The instrument is played with a stick, called a “tipper”, or with bare hand(s).
The bodhran is used to accompany Irish Traditional Music, or Irish Nontraditional Music, or Music That Isn’t Irish At All.

Why would the motivation to play bodhrán necessarily be so different from the motivation to play any other kind of drum? Generally speaking, in European (and European-influenced Amerrican) music, drums share the same role of providing rhythmic accompaniment to melody (often not the case in, say, parts of Africa and Asia). Certainly, there are particular nuances to Irish Traditional Music and the role of the Bodhrán (and other percussion) within it that make it unique, but broadly speaking, it fits the same mould. So, whilst it may not be answering the question directly, comparison with other European musical traditions and musics is not irrelevant.

↳ Posted by Kenny on August 10th, 2021.

Re: Bodhrán

“It’s just that, rightly or wrongly, the bodhran is often regarded as an easy ”entry instrument“ into Irish Music…”

Well, there is undeniably one respect in which it’s any easy instrument. You need some familiarity with the dance rhythms, but you don’t have to “know the tune” like a chordal backer does. You’re not likely to be playing anything much different on the third tune in a set than the first one. There are no pitfalls for unfamiliarity like there are for chordal backers because it’s dance music, where the hallmark is consistent rhythm.

In contrast, pity the guitar backer who has never heard Kid on the Mountain before, and after hearing a bit of the A part, thinks “Ah ha, it’s in E minor” and starts chugging away, followed by a crash on the B part when the tonal center shifts, and then shifts again and again. Or who dives into the Gravel Walks never having heard it before when it gets to that last part. Or who doesn’t know that essential F note is coming in Chief O’Neill’s hornpipe. Meanwhile the bodhran player will be happily thumping away through the shifts in the tonal centers.

There is skill in playing the bodhran in a way that supports the group, but at least they don’t have to deal with this kind of thing. It’s easier to play on a tune you’ve never heard before without crashing the session with your backup.
