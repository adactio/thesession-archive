Re: Tin Whistle Tips

A tip to add ornamentation to faster tunes would be to add them while playing slowly, then it will start coming naturally when you speed it up. Take the fast tunes slow at first. It’s all about them baby steps. One of my biggest mistakes when I first started playing was wanting to jump in and play them fast instantly. We all have to crawl before we walk. Get that rhythm down first because ornamentation is based to compliment/emphsize the rhythm. Usually rolls are put on the dotted note. Short rolls or breath marks on a single note. Just listen to some great players and see where they put in cuts/taps/rolls/etc…Like Llig put, Mary Bergin is a great place to start. Also:

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K0Xf1Ume0zA&feature=results_main&playnext=1&list=PLFA9EDE38B53CC6D4

This is a good place to start to get the feel of the ornamentation. There are also many others on youtube. Good luck to you. I hope that was helpful advice.

Re: Tin Whistle Tips

Here are a few:
When you have two or three notes in succession that are at the same pitch, use an ornament instead to avoid having to tongue the three notes to separate them.

When you need to take a breath, use an ornament to disguise the fact that you need to stop for air. Skipping a note or using syncopation is good for this, although not technically an ornament.

Use cuts or strikes for notes that start or end phrases.

In places where you use an ornament, vary the ornament or sometimes don’t do the ornament to create a variation each time the same phrase is played.

Listen to recordings of flute and whistle to hear what other players do and adapt some of it to your playing.

Make sure you know how to do rolls, cuts, strikes, smears and crans before you try any of this. What I have provided is a starter for figuring out the “when.” The “how” must be researched and studied by you.

Nothing is a necessity. Listen to as many different ways with the instrument and the music as you can and make your own decision on things, and, if possible, chase up living influences, learn from others directly. Print, on paper or on screen, is barely scratching the surface. There are also some decent book/audio and DVD things out there that can help, and, like Seamus Creagh’s “Tunes for Practice”, they don’t necessarily need to be whistle or wind related…

https://thesession.org/recordings/3225

There’s also Geraldine Cotter’s “Traditional Irish Tin Whistle Tutor”

If some snake oil salesman tries to convince you something is necessary, don’t trust them. There are those with all the tricks and flurries who can’t keep a steady beat, have little understanding of rhythmic and dynamic subtleties and some are cack players, while there are those that are simple and direct and a damned good listen, you can’t keep your feet from moving. But there are some impressive acrobats too, jaw dropping. The only thing that might be a truth is that it can be over done, O.T.T., the melody steam rolled to death, beaten flat with roll after roll after roll…

Micho is one of those charmers of subtlety, much of it from learning his music from his family, a lot of concertina players, and emulating their playing and their pauses in his style of whistle playing. A known truth was how you could date his tune acquisitions. The newer ones he’d attempt some version of a roll, while his older tunes, from his past, were roll free on the whole. His use of silence is wonderful, and he never sacraficed a melody for the sake of putting on an exhibition of acrobatics…

Re: Tin Whistle Tips

In my opinion, there are very few “ornaments” in the playing of most traditional Irish whistle players, that is, using the definition of “ornament” as something that is superfluous to the melody, which is added for decorative effect.

Long rolls are NOT “ornaments” but the very building blocks of traditional jig and reel playing on Irish wind instruments.

I could not begin to play reels and jigs on the whistle, flute, or pipes without rolls. To do so would mean creating artificial versions of the tunes, quite different than the way I heard them played by others, and the way I’ve always played them myself.

For me, rolls are not “ornaments” but rather a way of articulating the tune.

Prescriptive limitations ~ :-/

“Long rolls are NOT ”ornaments“ but the very building blocks of traditional jig and reel playing on Irish wind instruments.” ~ Richard C. Cook

Sorry, but while some swear by this as some kind of truth cut in stone ~ it’s B.S. There isn’t a melody that can’t survive WITHOUT rolls, or an instrument that can’t be played without them either… Yes, they can be fun, but, like with other things, they can also be a crutch, considered by some, as here, as a ‘necessity’… The subject of ‘articulation’ is an important one that exists outside of that added possibility and choice of using any of the many ways of executing a roll. Rolls, famously, can also muddy articulation,flatten the lift and rhythm of the music, the dance in it…

While there has become a kind of ‘norm’, a ‘standardization’ of such things, there are many different types of rolls, but they are not a ‘necessity’. A decent musicians can still lift and drive the music without them, and some do as a matter of tradition and/or choice…

‘Long, short, tongued, double tipped, etc., etc., etc. ~ rolls ARE ornamentation, decoration, like the baubles on a Christmas tree - a matter of choice - not necessity. They can and are often abused, abusive, but they can be fun and add interest, ’variation‘, in all their many forms. Tunes where the rolls are prescriptive, fixed, there’s where a problem exists - inflexibility, a limitation to imagination, to understanding, a ’dis-ability’…

Nice one gam…

Q. How much ornamentation do most people actually use?
A. Use as much or as little as YOU like - and fits the tune and what you are trying to do with it.

Q. Any tips for adding ornaments to the faster songs.
A. Listen to other players doing versions YOU like and get inspiration from them. In a while you will be “doing your own thing” and it will be completely natural IF you keep your ears open to the sounds you are producing.

This element, as much as the much-vaunted lilt, is in my opinion what makes Irish music so satisfying to play, to dance to, and to listen to. It has a freedom that the pibroch does not -- every note (and I mean *every* note) is to be played as written.

How complex or simple is your choice, my advice is to let the tune speak to you, and listen carefully. You will know if something doesn’t sound right, but only time and practise will enable you to know how to fix it -- unlike pibroch, Irish music is not written in stone. Which is why I prefer it.

The analogy to a cloud does not hold up. If you want to make a comparison, a cloud is to form as notes are to melody. It may be the same cloud, but change the form and it is visually different. Eight notes scale an octave, but alter their form and it is audibly different. Carrying on with gam’s analogy, you could play a tune and gradually change to another tune but it is no longer the same. The notes are the same, just as the cloud is the same, but it is essentially different than it was.

Re: Tin Whistle Tips

One of the more frustrating things about listening to and learning music is learning to keep under control one’s brain’s remarkable ability and propensity to “fill in the gaps”.

The human brain has evolved to try to extract as much meaning out of as little stimulus as it possibly can. It stores information and when presented with a lack of a complete picture it fills in the gaps with the most likely evidence from previous experience. It’s a very clever survival technique.

However, when presented with an overload of stuff it’s not familiar with, your brain finds it hard to open itself up … instead, it closes down and rather than making the effort to understand new things it likes to fall back on its survival technique. It blanks the new stuff out and subconsciously replaces it with stuff it is already familiar with.

Every effort must always be made to circumvent this propensity. It’s not hard. All it takes is a bit of concentration.

(and here’s another thing I cannot overstate more.)
