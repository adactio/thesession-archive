Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

Hi everyone!

I’ve heard it said that if you make a start on learning Irish fiddle music, having spent many years playing classical music (and usually being well classically trained), then you need to “un-learn” some habits.

This has me puzzled - I’m well aware that Irish fiddling is very different to (eg) a Handel Sonata, but what needs to be un-learned? Can any of you folks fill me in on this please? Thanks!

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

Traditional music is a different aesthetic from classical music. That’s pretty much the whole of the explanation.

If you were handed a piece of sheet music without bowings, dynamics, etc, you have a “default” approach to how you would play it. That “default” is very different from the choices a trained fiddler would make.

Unlike classical music, the traditional music aesthetic is not often taught explicitly and in detail (although this is changing). Instead, players are taught the rudiments of the music, and then expected to listen for the detail.

So it’s not so much a case of “unlearning”. It’s a case of knowing what not to do, like visiting a foreign country and knowing how not to antagonise the locals.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

Anne - I think the idea of “un-learning” anything you have learned as a classical musician is a complete nonsense, and based on myth and ignorance. If you’ve been well classically trained, hold on to that, above all else!

You *may* need to learn some additional things in terms of bowing patterns, bowing ornaments, left-hand ornamentation, etc for playing in the Irish traditional style, but all of this will be *in addition* to what you already know/can do. “Un-learning” is simply the wrong mindset! I’m sure that’s based on bad experiences from those listening to a minority of classical violinists who put a “tune” on the music stand and play it exactly (and only) note-for-note. Any musician of any genre would know how disastrous that could be.

Learning to play fiddle in an Irish trad style has its parallels in learning to play in any new genre. It’s different in terms of technique, but the level of technique required to play it is pretty low, in comparison to the requirements of (eg) jazz violin, or bluegrass fiddling, or the big beast of “classical”.

That said, I’d strongly echo what Dan Conway said about listening as much as possible, and just ‘getting into’ the style, playing with other musicians at sessions, etc. The same would apply to any new musical genre.

I think that Dan’s advice is sound (no pun intended).

Just for the record, Anne, as you’re new here, the 90% business that Dan referred to includes (among other things) a large amount of people who gave quite strong and very specific opinions on various aspects of fiddling, and who didn’t actually play the instrument :)

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

I have no classical violin background myself (although I do have some backround in classical music), but took up the fiddle as an adult, and am sometimes envious of the technique (tone production, bow control, command of positions…) that seems hardwired into those that learned violin early in life. There is certainly much for a classical player to learn in order to play traditional music, but I would question the idea of ‘unlearning’. A good classical training gives you an extensive tool kit; many of those tools are rarely if ever needed for playing traditional music - and playing traditional fiddle requires its own special tool kit - but there is bound to be a considerable overlap. As long as you keep your ears open, and strive to imitate what you hear, you should be able to work out for yourself what tools in your existing kit you can use to achieve that and when you need to acquire new tools. The main thing you need to avoid, I think, is using tools just because you have them.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

Here’s what I’d keep in mind…

1.) Don’t think of sheet music as the authoritative version of the tune. It gets you within grenade range.
2.) Consider shortening the bow stroke, especially at faster tempo.
3.) We tend to slur into downbeats more than you may be used to. So instead of marking the downbeat with a downbow, maybe we’re continuing a downbow we started a half-beat earlier, and giving it a bit more bow pressure on the downbeat, instead of a brand new bow stroke. Or we’re continuing an up-bow. When you learn a new tune, look at some bowings and maybe do some things that don’t make it sound too “squared off.”
4.) Relax!
5.) Lose the vibrato. You might add a little back later sometimes. But for now, ditch it.
6.) Foot-tapping is ok. in fact, it’s held in high regard.
7.) Get a few lessons from a great trad player. Randal Bays, James Kelly and Patrick Ourceau I know all teach via Skype. There are others, of course. Each of these players can help address some of the more idiomatic things with your playing, specifically, and take a lot of time off the learning curve.

It’s a bit like taking a Ferrari down one of those unadopted roads in Clare, you’re fantastically well equipped but you’d be better off in the HiLux.

A good place to start is by imitating everything else but the fiddlers. Because you’ll have such a deep skillset on the violin there’ll be a tendency to reference everything you hear to that and my experience is you try to ‘do it better’ at first until you realise that’s not better. If you listen instead to the flutes and pipers, trying to imitate them (learning the execution of the tunes by ear) you’ll probably listen more to the essence of what’s happening.

In terms of playing slow airs you’re much better off as long as you leave the vibrato in the fiddle case. Maybe listen to pipers & singers to get those.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

I think the important distinction is between the technique of playing an instrument and playing a genre of music.
There are specific techniques in trad, like bowing triplets etc… but mostly its a question of adjusting things like bowing choices, speed and pressure to gain that trad ‘sound’ (or in fact one of the trad sounds - its not necessarily that monolithic). This is where the listening and learning by ear come in - you’ll have a hard time internalising the trad sound and thus eventually producing it yourself without lots and lots of listening and playing with trad musicians.
Many of the bad habits of classical cross-over musicians aren’t all that great for well rounded classical musicians either or necessarily well regarded in all classical circles. A reliance on endless vibrato to hide your actual tone and intonation or relying on the fourth finger to avoid whistling e string noises wouldn’t be reccomendable in any genre.
For me, the key is to initially focus on rhythm. Get familiar with the pulse of the different tune types. Find ways to emphasise that pulse beyond the immediately obvious. Bowing jigs in sets of three or reels in pairs or sets of four is kind of clunky and can become boring if it’s the only approach. Break things up by bowing differently. Slur into downbeats maybe. Learn to separate notes with a cut rather than changing bow direction. Learn to rock back and forth across the strings on one long bow. Once you have a range of techniques to accent the pulse you’re better placed to find the balance between the phrases and the predictable rhythmic chunks of a tune.
A note on ornamentation. Ornaments are brilliant! They sound cool. They can be a springboard into finding little variations of phrases. At some point you’ll want to woodshed rolls and trebles and maybe a few other things and stick them into tunes everywhere. (This is a stage almost everyone goes through - I’m not sure I’m personally out of it yet.) But don’t get into the mindset that they magically make things Irish. The differences are simpler but deeper.
In general this is a music of small details. The nature of the tunes and practicalities of playing them with other people in an authentic way limit drastically what you can do. So it’s tiny variations in phrasing and rhythm that people go for. Tiny touches. Changing a note here or there. Adding a little double stop. Lingering on a note on one occasion and playing it as a bowed treble another time and slurring a run of three notes another time.
And enjoy yourself! Music should be social anyway in my opinion, but this music definitely should. Sit with people you like and play and chat and eventually make solid friendships. Otherwise what’s the point?You won’t make millions at it or become famous outside of a very narrow circle.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

Historically speaking Scottish fiddle music has been heavily influenced by Italian violin and european classical music for hundreds of years so the crossover is far more accessible to a classical musician than irish fiddle and pipe music, ( although in the last few decades it has been influenced by the advent of tv and radio and recordings ) but it is still stylistically worlds away from Scottish fiddle .
Far easier IMO for a classical player to approach jazz or other more typical western music than Irish fiddle where far more importance is placed on more ethereal aspects of music , the spirit and its function for dance than mere technical trickery .
Its not a style that lends value to technique as an end in itself rather as a means of expression, great value is placed on imagination and variation , rhythm drive and lift .
expressive intonation was certainly an aspect of the older generations that has become less prominent and is something that could well be reintroduced . Once you can understand that concept then things will be clearer.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

So you are classically trained & want to know what you should leave out of your training to sound Celtic. First of all get a very short bow - you don’t need the rest. Second, try to draw you bow diagonally across the strings. Third, play as many open strings as possible. Fourth, never play in a position unless you have no other option. Fifth, no vibrato. Sixth, bang your foot as loudly as possible -- preferably in time. Seventh, do not hold the ‘fiddle’ or bow in the classical way -- reinvent the wheel. Eighth, don’t worry if the ‘fiddle’ is slightly out-of-tune -- it makes it more traditional. Ninth, like the one-breath flute players, slur as much as possible.

Good luck!

I think it is fair to say there have been unschooled traditional musicians who have learnt music without the benefit of a formal pedagogy and they played w/multiple “bad habits” {at least from the perspective of better trained musicians}. Yet their informal music has contributions which, while far short of perfect, are still significant
(not everything, of course) and it is worth listening & learning trad from them. Sometimes the only way to hear what tradition players are playing these days is to go to their source. Go to the well, listen and get in their head
(the old players). It may be a matter of starting a new experience from a new beginning, or you might click on the particular style of one traditional musician. If I had to describe a method it might be a willingness to un-learn what you’ve been taught. Obviously no one willingly gives up the techniques they have worked so hard to perfect. And I doubt you can unlearn them. But take everything you’ve learnt & forget it just long enough to hear something new, except it’s only new to you. Traditional music is timeless.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

The concept of ‘ beginners mind’ is that as experts we already know it all and therefor may well have a resistance to new ideas new ways of doing things different approaches.
By maintaining a beginners mind it means bring open to these other ways , other approaches ,other standards.
So for example , we have the classical approach to intonation which has been adopted by most of us, myself included, because it’s the standard by which violinists and fiddlers are judged, but the more I listen to older players the more I realised we’ve actually missed something .
That being certain notes were modified for effect like highland pipers used to play certain notes that stood out against the drone. While nowadays we mostly aspire to harmonious tuning, because our ears have been taught to recognise certain sounds as “ good” and other sounds as. “Bad”
Of course fiddlers also play out of tune due to bad habits, laziness etc, but when we listen to the old masters there are certain things they had in common that we don’t really hear these days.
That’s why I mostly listen to the older folk I mentioned above because I actually enjoy their playing more, it has more flavour so to speak.
So what’s happened is the older traditional approach to intonation has been subsumed by a modern culturally homogenous approach as learnt through the medium of recordings and electronic communication.
So if you listen to the old guys thinking that the modern approach is right and they are wrong, you’ve no chance of ever getting to understand the depth of the music because your mind is already filled up by your knowledge.
To have a beginners mind is to have an empty mind, no judgement , no preconceived ideas just open to learning .

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

Will Evans nailed it. If you want to play trad listen to trad…a lot. Then listen some more. Sing (lilt) the tunes. Listen to the ornamentation. Listen to the places where the tune “breathes”. Listen to the tunes played on a variety of instruments. Did I mention listening? Listen to the great players Will mentioned and after a while listen to the not-so-great. Before long I suspect you will have developed a pretty good filter to help you understand what you are hearing. Then equally important, listen to yourself. Hear the difference? (I don’t mean the difference between personal styles, I mean the way tunes just sound “Irish” or not…hard to define). When you can answer that question with a resounding NO then you can take a seat with the great ones.

Seriously listening is the most important skill to develop. Most, I believe anyway, players of any instrument that have a hard time transitioning from one genre to another don’t really listen. They mostly listen to the new genre only when they are playing it so they never really catch on to the sound they want. Many teachers talk only about how to play and make short work of the importance of listening. Once you “get it” it’s not all that hard to pull out skills you already have, or learn some new ones, to play the (new) way you hear. Trad, like anything else, isn’t hard, just different, and if you can’t hear it you’ll never be able to play it. In all fairness I’d have to say it’s equally difficult for even the best Trad players to switch over and come great classical players for the same reasons. Bottom line: don’t over-think…listen.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

“Bowing,” would be my primary answer here. You’ll be well served by intonation and any dexterity you’ve developed in the left hand, but bowing in Irish music can be pretty different, and it’s seldom actually documented in sheet music. Primarily, when to slur notes. For further detail, I’ll refer to this: http://www.fiddle.com/_mndata/fiddle/uploaded_files/Spr.%2006%20-%20CaoimhinOR.pdf

This best encapsulates the sound I’d like to emulate most that I also find hardest to do consistently.

My second answer would be “ornamentation.” You’ll be familiar with the various ornaments from a classical perspective, but not with the requirement to determine for yourself when to deploy them. Again, seldom documented in sheet music, and for good reasons.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

I don’t think it is a matter of “unlearning” anything. The techniques obtained through classical violin study will serve you well. (My daughter plays both Irish fiddle and classical violin. She will tell anyone who asks that her classical training gives her the technique to be free to do what she wants with her Irish music.)

I think it is a matter of learning new skills and techniques to play Irish music well. Learning to listen closely and attempting to imitate phrasing is the most important thing. Next would be learning the “rules” of ornamentation and variation so you can make the tune your own while doing it within the tradition. Irish tunes (I mean the notes), for the most part, are very easy for a classically trained musician to play but playing the notes is a very minute part of playing Irish music. The phrasing, variation, and ornamentation are what make the tune “Irish.”

Those things can only be learned by immersing yourself in the tradition with a lot of listening. Listen to the “masters” past and present (not just fiddlers), go see ceili dances to understand what the music is made for, sit in on sessions (play along quietly if you have a few tunes but mostly listen and be cognizant of the rules for session playing and the “flow” of the sessions you attend), attend some workshops, and talk to people playing the music (most are extremely nice and encouraging if you present yourself as a learner).

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

Understand that you need to listen to the masters so you can get the sound in your head.

Remember that Irish music is dance music. Classical music can be dance music too, but, it’s not the same. For jigs and reels, the rhythm carries the music and this is why people love it so.

The best players accent beats 2 and 4 in a reel. This gives the tune a lilting quality that draws the listener or dancer in. Makes it more fun to listen to. When playing an ornament, it’s good to accent it. Doesn’t matter if it’s a right or left hand ornament. That way, it really jumps out at the listener. That’s all I’ve got. Study the masters. Each one has a different take on playing. You’ll come up with your own unique style. I play classical music and TIM. Each music form is entirely different from the other. Don’t use a classical vibrato. In Irish music, vibrato can be thought of as an ornament. Use it sparingly. Experiment with a slow vibrato on airs. It’s a long journey to learn to play TIM well.

Re: Coming from a classical background to fiddle music

One more thought: The bowings are going to be difficult to internalize. I mean, bowings that flow and sound good. I recommend Kevin Burke’s instruction videos. There are two. The second one is more advanced. He delves into bowings. So important! Many TIM books have terrible bowings indicated. Many others have no bowings indicated. A very few have decent bowings. That’s why you’ll want to learn about the concepts behind what makes certain bowings sound great, and other bowings sound choppy and awful.

It’s best to learn the tunes by ear listening to the best players. Tune books can be used for reference.
