Re: Time signature

Danielle, reels are in cut time (alle breve), with two beats per measure, not four. If you play reels in 4/4, they will sound very choppy. The music is written the same as 4/4, but there are only two pulses per measure. Reels are often written in 4/4 and people who know Irish music instinctively play them properly, but if you play in strict time, the tune would be completely wrong.

Re: Time signature

The exact definition of ‘air’ is debateable. To me, it means more or less any tune that is not a dance tune, so it would include any of O’Carolan’s tunes. *Slow air* is a bit more specific, but the term refers as much to the manner of playing as to the tune itself - it’s the instrumental equivalent of sean nos (old style) singing. Most airs do have (or can be assigned) a time signature, but they are not necessarily played in strict time.

The short answer is, there is no generic time signature for airs or slow airs.

O’Carolan’s Quarrel With the Landlady, as notated here https://thesession.org/tunes/11675 is in 6/8, but it would be played much slower than a jig, on account of all the 1/16-notes (semiquavers). It could have been written out in 3/4, with all the note values doubled and bar lengths halved, but being notated in 6/8 suggests that it might have been intended to have the feel of something like a slow jig.

Re: Time signature

Just to follow up on Ailin’s comments, as she stated reels are in alle breve ( 2/2 or “cut” time), and the time signature is often notated as a C with a perpendicular line through it’s centre. This is despite the widespread use of 4/4 for reels. The difference between 2/2 and 4/4 is that 2/2 is two groups of 4 quavers(eighth notes) and 4/4 is 4 groups of 2 quavers. I recall the last time I stated this on this website I was accused of being deaf, although it was never explained to me the relationship between a belief in alle brevenism and deafness. To confuse matters many excellent publications list their reels as 4/4 e.g “Compositions Of Paddy O‘Brien”,“Irish Session Tunes”(Brid Cranitch/Sheila Garry), but in both cases group the notes in 2 groups of 4. Allan’s Irish Fiddler uses alle breve for reels but common time(4/4) for hornpipes. Vol.2 of Bulmer and Sharpley’s “Music For Ireland” doesn’t bother with time signatures for its reels, jigs,hornpipes,or marches except 1(King Of Laoise), but curiously includes time signatures for slip jigs and slides. Vol 4 includes key signatures. O’Neill’s 1001 Fiddle Tunes uses alle breve for all his reels and hornpipes and I’ve always followed his use of time signatures. Slow airs are a bit more problematic as they often don’t have a regular/rhythmic beat.

Re: Time signature

“FWIW I understand ”air“ as something different than a ”tune“, when I’m talking to my session mates. And they understand the terms as being for different things. ”

That’s fine. O‘Neill used the term “air” for what might be termed “song airs” (airs-songs), but he classified Carolan tunes as “O’Carolan Compositions”. Petrie used the term “air” basically as synonymous with “melody”. Some of his “airs” are jigs or reels etc (set at fast tempo), and Stanford used the term in much the same manner. The thing is, nobody “defined” air, they used the term in their own particular way. It’s just that now, many people use the term in the way they’ve seen it, or heard it used by others. If you need a definition, look to a dictionary, because there is no standardisation. It is generally understood to mean something similar to a “listening tune” in Shetland music. Others might use it in the way, for example, Fleming-Williams and Shuldham-Shaw used it here:

http://www.cdss.org/product-details/product/popular-selection-book-2-traditional-dance-airs-for-country-dances-of-today.html

It’s pointless looking for an“ITM” definition, because it’s more like a (somewhat loose) terminology akin to jargon.

So Danielle, putting aside for now whether a tune is 2/2, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 6/8, 9/8, 12/8 or a totally free-form air with no clear time signature at all, the rhythmic pulse of the different types of tunes can be thought of in the following ways:

Reels sound like “Alligator, alligator” or “Salt and pepper, salt and pepper.”
Jigs sound like “Edible elephant, edible elephant.”
Hornpipes sound like “humpty, dumpty, humpty, dumpy.”
Slip jigs sound like “artichoke, pineapple, Mexico.”

Hope that helps. It’s just another way to listen for the cadence of the rhythm.

Re: Time signature//Slow Air

From “Traditional Slow Airs of Ireland” by Thomas O‘Canainn, Ossian, 1995. Dr. O’Canainnm , former head of the music department at University College Cork (succeeding Sean O Raida) and uilleann pipes player for Na Fili: Here is a word from his introduction to the above book with the conventional notation for 100 slow airs. This is, I think, about as accurate and scholarly as one can get.
"Introduction:
Many of the airs have come to us as the music of sean-nos (old style) songs. An acquaintance with singing is clearly a help, though not a necessity, in the playing of an air. I have included songs on the enclosed recordings, so that the change from song-air to slow-air might be more easily understood. There are 4 good songs here which have their corresponding slow airs in this collection.

Some of the airs are by Carolan, while others are unconnected with songs, as far as we know, but have become established as purely instrumental pieces.

Generally speaking, if a tune has been around for a considerable time and has been transmitted orally, I would call it traditional, whatever its original source.

As your air playing improves, you will realize that there are no unbreakable rules involved."

So in summary, this agrees with much of what is said above. Slow airs are part of the “tradition”.
Slow airs can be songs or can be tunes which have been handed down thru the years purely as instrumental pieces. And there is much room for variation in ornamentation and phrasing.

Dr. O’Canainn continues in this excellent book emphasizing that there is great room for individual artistic variation from the standard air thus reinforcing what has been said on this subject. He passed away this year after a life time of study and performance of ITM. The tradition would be much poorer without him. I think this would be a great addition to the library of any ITM musician fortunate enough find the slow air beautiful and irresistible.
