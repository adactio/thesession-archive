For me tune writing often comes unplanned. I just am minding my own business, practicing, then veer off into some catchy little doodle that I realize has potential. It’s sort of like free writing. For fun I start to follow the idea and sometimes, before I know it, the tune pops out, more or less fully formed. But…then I have to try to remember the goofy thing. That is often the challenge. Sometimes the main theme is easy to hold on to, but interesting little passages that just popped out in the free writing phase are harder to remember and I lose them. Years ago I used to do some song writing and it was the same. I would often be driving someplace for work, the inspiration would hit - tune, words, everything - and I would sing it over and over again to avoid losing it. I finally (duh!), bought a cassette recorder for my work truck.

↳ Posted by flyrodandbrush on March 12th, 2025.

Re: What are you struggling with as a tune-writer?

Tune creation tends to fall into three phases:
1) content creation
2) development
3) analysis/editing

The left brain analyst needs to be mostly turned off and held at bay while indulging in the first two phases, especially phase #1. Otherwise it can make everything extremely difficult!!!!!

Open, active, universal creativity, and accessing the subconscious, as well as using instruments, whistling, humming, hearing an inner musical activity, need to flow freely. I’ve experienced songwriting groups, music and writing guides that almost exclusively rely on phase #3 to sell their ideas. They sell you lists. Such guides typically haven’t done phase #1 and #2 themselves, it’s beyond their experience; they’re academic libraries, not creative artists. They can’t coach you, guide you, demonstrate to you, how it’s done in endlessly diversified ways. In Phase #1, you aren’t trying to fill a prescription or follow a format or take dictation, you’re radiating free flow creativity, totality, perhaps loosely influenced by something, but you are like a kid building sand castles, NOT an intimidated student taking a math test. Phase #1 is personal experience, immersion, exploration, play, concentration, persistence, expression, etc. There is no judging, there are no judges. Whether alone or in a group, Phase #1 is the most essential component of creating anything new. No copying is going on. Get lost, go hiking musically, PLAY, PLAY, PLAY, and play is in part pure wildness, pure liberty. Extreme liberty. You select, edit, organize, reconsider………….LATER! LATER!

Learn to separate Phase #1 from Phase #2 and Phase #3 and you’ll gain immense momentum with creativity.

Phase #2, Development is taking your raw materials and exploring how they can be further developed, organized, purposed, mixed, isolated, colored, all the aesthetic considerations come into play, exploring possibilities, finding and creating opportunities. Not to finalize, but to find options, explore ideas. Phase #2 can involve going back to Phase #1 for finding more possibilities, because sure enough when considering how to develop, you can run into questions, problems, awkward situations, obstacles, etc., when considering how to complete an entire song or piece or solo or suite, but that’s a natural part of the process, and you’re creative productivity needs to always consider your ability to generate more raw content as part of your creative tool kit. In Phase #2 at some point you can get a notion of what the song could be like when finished, but you KNOW you still need to explore many options, you do not short-circuit the process, so you don’t, you stick to your creative process!

Phase #3 is the left-brain part of things where you are closer to fine-tuning, to move towards an idea of what your work will be when finished. External concerns can come into play, such as sticking to a style, balancing parts of the song musically and lyrically, song length, what instruments to involve, and any recording and performances concerns. “I can’t get the whistle to play that high note cleanly, so I have to finish that phrase some other way, but it’s still going to be whistle.” Try things out. Don’t rush it.

If ANYONE starts off with Phase #3 and has been thinking that Phase #1 and Phase #2 are secondary or childish, they’re likely to find themselves stuck in or avoiding the process repeatedly, because they are basically just trying to plug in numbers to pre-existing mathematical formulas to create an audio version of a mathematical description of a shoe box. Nashville and FM radio are FULL of predictable, mechanical “music” like that. People who are drawn into copying, imitating or excessively use repetition, are missing the point if they think they’re going to be creative geniuses by refusing to be creative geniuses.

What’s difficult? Trying to get people to get music flowing OUT of them, when their culture predominantly thinks “music” is an external opioid that they need to repeat as perfectly as it’s written in sheet music or like taking out the trash at exactly the same time every Tuesday. Worst case scenario; parents and music teachers who know none of this, aren’t musical, and think the world and experience of “music” is having their young children mechanically repeat on a keyboard what is aligned as black dots by someone else they’ll never meet, as some kind of social status progress report. Countless children have had their musical spirit and sense of joy in life, destroyed by parents who are militant about that. And expect to be ridiculed as being mean, if you point out that there’s money involved in entrancing children with machines. I had two music teachers. The first one was friendly, musical, very astute and encouraged me to get through the challenges, and he watched for them and explained them! The second one was a social degenerate relying on academic theory, rigid routine, and who was adamant that no other way of experiencing music ever resulted in anything of value. What’s difficult? Well, sometimes, you have to BE YOURSELF! It’s not difficult, but situations can arise where you’re challenged on that topic. You have to say NO to people sometimes, or you’re likely just the next victim of backsliding.

You’ll always get them whether you need them or not.

My own view is that I don’t see the point of composing them just for the sake of it BUT if a musician is naturally inclined or motivated to compose tunes then they should do so. Even be encouraged, if that’s the case. For the rest of us, we can just carry on playing existing tunes as long as we enjoy it and compose something now and again if and when the mood takes us.
However, one of the reasons many people want to compose new music, including songs too, is the “royalty aspect”. Not that this is as quite relevant to tradional music as the potential gains are generally much lower.

However, it doesn’t really matter how many tunes either old or new are in existence or doing the rounds. Only the better ones usually survive the test of time and become session standards. Others, of course, may always be worthy of revival from time to time either on a temporary or permanent basis but the rest get forgotten.

The opening poster is an excellent and prolific tune composer but he is also highly respectful of the tradition. So, it’s a “gain, gain” for the rest of us. Re: What are you struggling with as a tune-writer?

My partner, who has written some fine tunes, has nonetheless expressed the opinion that on balance it would be better if nobody wrote any more tunes, and if furthermore an annual cull was instituted in which the weakest members of the herd were ruthlessly erased. There are just so many of the damn things, they have no natural predators, and the invention of sheet music means that they are effectively immortal.
I don’t agree myself. I take an epidemiological view. In the Darwinian breeding-ground of a predominantly aural tradition, the most potent ear-worms are the most infectious, burrowing deep into the brain of the unsuspecting host. Once established, like the Cordyceps fungus they drive the host to new behaviours - in this case, learning instruments and playing them in public so as to spread the infection to new victims. But it doesn’t stop there; the tunes then breed and mutate inside the host’s brain, forming new tunes that are released into the wild to perish or thrive. When a new strain emerges to which there is little natural immunity, it can spread like wildfire.
So from my perspective your free will is irrelevant in writing tunes. You only need to relax and let them use you as a stepping-stone in their parasitic lifecycle. You shouldn’t even worry about whether they’re any good, just let natural selection do its work.

Re: What are you struggling with as a tune-writer?

What I’d like to share in this reply may not be strictly a response to the original question, but since some other replies have raised the question of how to develop a tune, and since the approach I’m going to describe, though not using Irish style tunes as an example, can apply to developing tunes in the Irish as well as other traditions, I hope people will find it of some interest. With that preface, anyone who might be interested in the following somewhat technical discussion, is invited to read on.

I’ve composed several tunes in various styles by starting out with a traditional scale, fooling around with the notes in that scale until I get something that sounds like music. Since I don’t really play any instrument except in a very basic sense, what I sometimes do stick labels on the keys of a keyboard identifying the notes in the scale, and let my fingers wander over combinations of those keys until it starts to sound like a tune.

An example is my composition Sunset Meditation, an arrangement of a tune I composed in this way using the traditional Japanese Insen Scale; see the Wikipedia entry at:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insen_scale

I haven’t gotten this piece performed yet, but a software created demo audio is available at:

https://soundcloud.com/jon-corelis/sunset-meditation

The brief introductory passage is just the scale itself.

This technique can of course be applied to any traditional scale, many of which can be found on the internet. A particularly useful source is:

https://www.pianoscales.org/major.html

Re: What are you struggling with as a tune-writer?

“it’s hard to come up with a second phrase that reaches the level of that first explosion of inspiration…and nearly impossible to compose an entire second part to the tune that doesn’t just sound like a filler.”

Exactly.

What pops into my head is just a fragment. Sometimes it’s an entire 8-bar part, then as you say it’s a struggle to write a decent 2nd part.

But sometimes the fragment isn’t even a full 8-bar part, it’s just a motif, a “hook”, which I struggle to flesh out into a complete 8-bar part.

On the other hand, I’m used to, as a matter of course, exploring various melodic paths in trad tunes I play, so as long as I have something to work with I can come up with plenty of twists to it.

There was one tune where a complete 8-bar part popped into my head and just by playing with it I quickly came up with a three more parts. But that’s rare for me.

I have to get inside the tune, where I’m living in the world of that scale or mode and that rhythmic underpinning. As long as I’m in that place an almost endless number of pathways present themselves.
