Is It Still Dance Music?

This is off-shoot of some fascinating ideas on this thread:

https://thesession.org/discussions/17893

The question then is posed to all of you: Is it still dance music? If no one is dancing to it, is it still dance music? If barely anyone dances to it anymore, is it still dance music? If it is most commonly listened to instead of danced to, is it still dance music?

If so, then are the two still connected? How?

If not, is that a problem? Why?

I’m sure this has all been done before, but it’s always good to dig out the old ball and give it a good kicking up and down the pitch for the benefits of the new fans. Game on!

↳ Posted by Will Harmon on May 28th, 2008.

Re: Is It Still Dance Music?

But this brings up the question (as was sort of addressed in the other thread) of whether you play it the same when you’re playing for dancers. I’m curious to see if people think that the dancing and session traditions are diverging.

For me, playing for an organized dance (a ceili, set dance, or step dancers) is a pretty different thing. You generally need to play at a particular tempo. You need to provide a stronger unified sound, a la ceili bands, to help the dancers hear the rhythm, etc. So you might play fewer variations. You need to play specific tune forms, sometimes in a specific order. You might accent the down beat a bit more. A lot of things that don’t come into play very much when you’re playing in sessions.

Now, having said that, we occasionally have set or step dancers show up at our sessions, and in that case, we generally just play, and they choose when they want to dance - although, we will often extend a set of tunes, so that we’re not ending in the middle of a solo dancer’s step, or whatever…

So, to some extent, it’s a “tree falls in the forest” kind of question.

Re: Is It Still Dance Music?

I think traditional music is only dance music if you are playing for dancers and know whether your music is “doing it” for them

(as a trad dancer myself, albeit with little knowledge of Irish dancing - a little bit of set dancing and a fondness for sean nos - maybe I’ll learn it some time)

I have commented before on my theory that ITM has largely divorced from dancing (although they have joint custody of the kids, and they get along quite well). To illustrate my point, some forms of dancing have evolved distinctly from the tradition (viz many dancers on youtube complete lack of good taste and ability to dance *with* the music) and several styles of playing, have, by choice or by accident evolved to no longer be danceable.

And that’s fine. (Except for the step dancers who have no taste or rythmical subtlety, but hey, live and let live eh.)

I would compare this situation with what happens in Britanny, where the Pipe Bands (bagads or bagadoù) still play some dance music, but quite frankly I don’t enjoy dancing to it. I think there, the bagads are divorcing from the dance tradition (or rather the music they play)

Yet another situation is traditional music from the rest of France, where it is very rarely to be found in the absence of dancers.

Pros and cons (assuming that I am right in saying that a lot of ITM is no longer dance music - not forgetting the part that never was):

+ In much of France, you would go to an ITM concert, but very few people would attend a French TM concert. I think it’s healthy and good for professional musicians to be able to play their music without being “slaves” to dancers

+ Sessions and the divorce from dancing go hand in hand. Irish sessions are now a de facto standard (maybe linked to the number of “Irish pubs” throughout the world). French sessions are few and far between. (because there is no room for dancers and no one comes to listen)

+ Irish instruments get to be nice and quiet. Quiet subtle playing is hard to come across in FTM - not that there aren’t people capable of doing it, but accordeons and french bagpipes make a hell of a racket.

+ Irish music has a lot less buggering about with “accompaniment” whereas you can’t shift for the left hand of a button box which helps the mediocre dancers and players get along. (although celidh bands do have some of that oompaa from the piano, banjo and percussion - imagine all sessions being like that though)

+ Irish musicians are not hostages of the “folk revival” and “salsa/tango/rock” type of dancers, who would rather they played a variety of dances none of them master and in which none of them have a traditional style, than wall to wall reels for example.

- Fewer and fewer musicians and dancers know how to benefit from the presence of the others

- In France, there has been a *huge* variety in the evolutions of trad music - new dances, very rich harmony, rythmical variations, novel arrangements. The gate-keepers of this evolution are the dancers - if enough people can dance to it, it’s “accepted” (with a lot of grumbling of course).

I think this last point is crucial. Dance music is dance music so long as people can dance to it and we have a really interesting musical scene here in France, covering a wide spectrum from yer very traditional through the not traditional at all but danceable up to the not traditional or very dancable, but good enough music that people still dance to it.

Re: Is It Still Dance Music?

Oh, the music and the dance have definitely diverged. And neither is really a focus of general community life the way they once were. They’ve been replaced by iPods and tv and the internet.

So nowadays the music is more often played as “listening” music, with less emphasis on the qualities required by dancers. So as Rev says, you probably play differently for dancers than you do in a session or for a gig audience.

Personally, when I’m not playing for dancers, I enjoy sessions that are less “listening” music and more “participating” music--tunes played for the sheer joy of playing them, which is different from playing tunes to captivate some other listener(s).
