What do you need the music for?

I’ve come across this comment on more than one thread and the inference seems to be that learning a tune by ear is preferable to learning a tune from paper. Certainly the skill of translating what you hear into specific notes on the instrument of your choice is valuable to a session player but I can think of reasons where the inability to learn a tune from paper can present its own problems. We’ll skip merrily past the bit where you’re sitting in a session next to someone who has learned the tune wrong, learned a different version or is trying to wind people up and go to the bit where you click on a thread in this forum and find a wonderful tune you don’t know played solo by a brilliant player. Which bits are the tune and which bits are improvisations, embellishments or whatever? Surely if you can find the tune in an old collection like O’Neil’s or Roche or, of course here, you can be sure you are starting in the right place and not passing on faulty goods or demonstrating your ignorance when you play.

Re: What do you need the music for?

The main problem with O’Neill’s or any of the older collections is that quite a lot of the tunes have evolved since they were originally written down. There are also clear errors in transcription while many tunes have gained extra parts, etc, etc, etc.

So, while the written music is invaluable as a source, it should NEVER be taken as gospel. The way in which the tune is played in a session is the ‘correct’ way. But then again, it may only be ‘correct’ for that particular session!

Bottom line? Hang loose and keep your ears wide open!

Re: What do you need the music for?

This will turn into the usual spat, but before it does, I’ll dive in. I could never have become able to learn tunes by ear unless I’d started with tunebooks and transcriptions, but nowadays I learn more by playing along. The tunes as set down in O’Neill’s are versions of what was being played at the time, and not necessarily correct even then. When I’ve heard a tune I want to learn, I’ll print off lots of versions, and many of them are just variations sometimes not very well set out. There’s no fixed point, but having got somewhat to grips with a tune, I find on looking back at the transcriptions that there are phrases where the printed version is really helpful. I also skim through tune archives and stumble upon tunes I’ve never heard, and learn them anyway. The Scottish group I attend have dance sets written out 30 years ago, and there are tunes that you’d have difficulty finding as recordings. So learning them is a combination of listening to them being played, struggling to keep up, and then going away and working on them from the handwritten copies. This is something that has enhanced my life, and if I hadn’t bothered to learn how to read music (just about adequately) it would have been denied to me.

Re: What do you need the music for?

Theirlandis, a transcribed version of any tune is still not the “official”, authoritative version of the tune. For example, I have a book of Junior Crehan tunes that was published with consultation from members of his family. I’ve listened to recordings of Junior himself playing those same tunes, however, and he didn’t play them as written. Sometimes he didn’t even play them in the same key! So which is the “original”? And how about the way Bobby Casey played the tune? Or Kevin Burke? Or the fiddler down at your local session?

My own preference for sorting this out is by listening as much as possible to as many sources as possible, and then seeing how people play at my local sessions. If I’m still in doubt about a bit in a tune, I’ll find a really good player and ask them how they play it. You’ll rarely find one definitive answer. Even if you ask good players from Ireland, you’ll likely get different answers depending on where they’re from. The Sligo players usually have different versions from the Clare players, and the Donegal players are even more different from that. Eventually you have to make a choice on what you think is best---you just try to make the best educated choice that you can.

Re: What do you need the music for?

>> We’ll skip merrily past the bit where you’re sitting in a session next to someone who has learned the tune wrong…

This is part of where the problem lies, I believe. We want to believe that there is some de-facto “right” way a tune goes, and everything else is wrong… Who’s to say it’s wrong? O’Neill? You? Me? The person who wrote it?

A lot of times, even the person who wrote a tune won’t play it the way they wrote it. So are they then playing it “wrong”? Part of the beauty of this music is that it is not rigid like that.

It is helpful for you to have what you would consider the “base setting” of a tune. But think of it as *your* base setting, not *the* base setting, and that will help you get away from thinking other people are “wrong”.

And I have evolved the way I think about the tunes over the last few years. I used to think of a tune as being a “base setting”, that I could then “vary” in different ways. But that leads you into thinking of variation in terms of “improvisation”, which can quickly lead you down a path where you’re playing things that don’t fit within the music, at least in a session environment, where everyone is playing the same melody. (The case of performance is different, because you can do variation that doesn’t need to fit with the group consensus - either as a solo, or as a pre-determined thing. But that’s different).

Instead of thinking of the variation as “improvisation”, I like to think of it as “expression”. When you start thinking of a melody as being a “musical idea”, instead of just a sequence of notes, then you’re free to express it in different ways. If you use the language analogy, it’s like saying the same thing, with different wording, cadence, emphasis, etc. And it makes it very easy to play it different every time, and every time through!

If you think about the melodies this way, then it really blurs the lines between “right” and “wrong”, and the “tune” and “variation”. And from there, you can sit in the middle of a session, and revel in the fact that you’re part of a chorus, engaging in musical conversation, with a common theme, but still plenty of room for individual expression.

Re: What do you need the music for?

O.K. I’ll alter the question. Can the melody of a tune be written? Yes.
Each tune has a melody divided into phrases made up of notes. Sometimes a note is written down as a single note repeated, when actually it is played continuous with interruptions, except when it is not played as continuous; or as just the one pitch.
While it is possible to write melody it is nowhere near as easy to transcribe the articulation. I wouldn’t go so far as to say impossible. Though, almost always incomplete. Also, it depends on your instrument as to how & where you articulate. The important thing to remember is articulation tends to emphasize the melody, & the rhythm, rather than actually adding notes. You hear some harmonics & other interesting sounds* during articulations. Even these have a tendency to draw attention to the melody while keeping your ear interested.

*I wouldn’t be so generous as to call each of these sounds a note. It’s something like saying Pluto is a planet.
Pluto isn’t a planet. It’s a cosmic articulation.

Re: What do you need the music for?

I feel that the key to what makes a good tune is the intervals (i.e., the jumps and descents from note to note). They are the structure of the tune. When they are right, individually or in their overall arrangement through the tune, the tune sounds somehow right and satisfactory. I compare them - though without having pondered this - to the struts and rafters in some fairly complicated wooden roof, which placed properly are firm and hold each other up and also look good, and if not properly fitted - well, don’t fulfil these requirements.

Of course, weak or bizarre or tongue-in-cheek intervals can be part of a good tune, and be there intentionally, to “spice it up”. But a tune where there is overall weakness in the interval structure is likely to sound fairly wissy.

So - Someone trying to find “the real tune” in a nimbus of dots or recorded or session versions might do worse than take note of the intervals, to see which ones seem structural and which might be adapted to be made so. I think experienced players do this all the time.

Re: What do you need the music for?

Somewhere above the OP asked for the relative merits of learning from the dots and of learning by ear. Well….

(With a focus on *this* music, which is predominantly an aural tradition)

Pros of learning from the dots:

- They are less ephemeral than live music. You can come back tomorrow and look again at the sheet music. So they’re a handy storage device (particularly if you don’t have a sound recording).

- If you understand the mechanics of how to read the dots--***and you have enough listening of this music in you***--then you can recreate the tune, without an mp3 player, tape machine, or other mechanical device. You can sight-sing the tune right from the page. Or you can play it on your instrument. No batteries required.

- Notation (especially abcs) can easily be shipped, via mail, email, txt, etc.

- Notation allows us to visually pore over the structure and elements of a tune as slow as we want, with no need to hie to the actual tempo of the piece if it were being played. For detailed analysis, this can be handy.

- Similarly, notation allows us to quickly compare/contrast two or more settings of a tune simultaneously.

Pros of our ears:

- Music is wholly and fundamentally aural. The more we use our ears, the better we get at it, and the more adept we become at listening to and making music. The musician who does not listen (a lot) is no better off than a person who cannot hear. We cannot play music without functioning ears and the aural centers of our brains. (Yet plenty of people play music just fine without functioning eyes.) For the aspiring musician, time spent really listening is never wasted.

- Learning to listen accurately, comprehensively, and with genuine understanding is how we grow the synapses and neural pathways that shape our brains into truly musical brains (instead of, say, painter brains or smell-connoisseur brains). The more you listen and play, the more musical your brain becomes, the better a musician you become.

- Learning by ear is expected in this music. ***ALL*** of the experienced players of this music can give you tunes by playing them. Many of them can’t or won’t write them out for you. Learning by ear is integral to this tradition. Sight reading notation is not. Becoming adept at learning by ear is like earning a membership card to the club. It gives you credibility. It enables you to take advantage of nearly every encounter with veteran players.

- Learning by ear leads to playing by ear, which is how this music works, whether in sessions or in performances. You cannot fit in with other traditional players unless you can play by ear. Unlike in formal music where a player or ensemble follows a score (and often a conductor), this music is played without a script. In this music, the one essential skill that enables us to “play well with others” is an ability to listen and play responsively to what we hear.

- When you’re good at it, learning tunes by ear is far quicker and more complete than any other way of acquiring tunes.

Cons of learning by dots:

- Notation cannot capture all of the qualities and nuances (timing, pulse, tone, articulations, phrasing, etc.) of music that make it music and not Muzak.

- Notation often contains mistakes. It also shoe-horns the music into bars and note lengths and other conventional formats that do not accurately represent the actual music.

- All the notation and reading skills in the world add up to nothing if you do not know *how the music is supposed to sound.* YouTube is chock full of examples of alleged musicians who’ve learned a few Irish tunes from the dots yet who clearly have no idea how this music actually sounds. (Yikes! http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7w65438PCGM)

- Good sight readers often make the mistake of relying on their visual skills to the neglect of their aural abilities. This does not a musician make. Take up painting, graphic design, or sculpture if you want to pursue a visual art. The mere presence of notation encourages the visual bias. We are a strongly visual species. Visual stimulus to the brain tends to override our ears. That’s exactly wrong for learning music.

Cons of learning by ear:

- In the beginning, it feels difficult (especially for people adept at sight reading). But complaining that it’s “too hard” before you’ve spent any time to nurture and develop the skill is absurd. Learning to listen is no harder (likely easier) than learning to decipher the arcane, near-mystic hieroglyphics of notation.

- Learning to listen tends to turn people into aural sponges. It becomes harder to ignore the bombardment of canned crap piped throughout malls, grocery stores, radio, etc. The solution is to avoid those places and make your own music.

I’ve said nothing yet about how being able to read notation gives a person access to all the many written sources for this music--all the old written collections, and now the innumerable online sources for sheet music and abcs. It’s true--being able to read music opens up vast libraries of tunes. ***But please don’t confuse that access with the real ability to well play those tunes.*** Consider this: being able to read books gives us access to loads of information and ideas. But being able to read does little or nothing to enhance our ability to speak. Music works in much the same way.

For me, it boils down to this: It’s okay to use notation and know how to read it. A handy tool and skill, for certain things, in certain circumstances. But neither is necessary for playing this music and playing it well. Using your ears *is* necessary.

[Disclaimer - I am not anti-dots. I learned to read notation at the age of 7, some 45 years ago. For some genres of music, being able to play from notation is essential. In general, sight reading is a handy skill for musicians. Learn how. Just not at the expense of developing your ears. And not as your point of entry into *this* music.]

I can learn a tune by ear and often do. I have to record it and play along with it. The 3-times-through standard at are session is sometimes enough for me to sort of get it on the third time, but I won’t remember it.

However, the work of transcribing it from the recording (I like dots better than abc) helps me memorize it faster then I could without transcribing it. I’m a fast transcriber, and I doubt I could play a tune say, 30 times in the time I took to transcribe it. I actually get the tune into long term memory better by transcribing rather than by playing it 30 times.

Often I transcribe a tune and never look at the transcription again. I have to practice it a lot more, but I don’t have to look at the dots while I do it.
