The flute appears to be a generic, anonymous 19th. Century Blackwood and Ivory flute of German manufacture. These flutes were often high volume production models of questionable quality, sometimes being poor players, with problematic intonation and variable pitch, depending on their date of manufacture. Occasionally you get a good one, but it’s the exception rather than the rule. The vendor may have bought it from someone in Geneva, but I doubt it was originally made there. Definitive proof would require a makers stamp on the instrument, and I don’t see one in the photo’s on this example. If you are interested in using an antique flute for sessions, then one of English or American make would be more suitable. Compared to your Casey Burns folk flute, the combination of multiple keys and Ivory head joint, will make this one feel extremely heavy in the hand. As you currently play a modern Irish Flute tuned to A=440 Hz., I would try some antique ones out to see how you fare with them before making a purchase.

Re: Thoughts? Old Swiss keyed flute with ivory headjoint

It is a typical German-style old system flute. Thousands of these were made c1840-1940.

In Germany they preferred the tone of the old 1-key Baroque flute, with uniformly small holes, and they continued making those, but adding more and more keys, up until WWII.

Ivory heads were extremely common, not a rare or unusual thing in the least, but in the majority.

They aren’t favoured for ITM due to the quiet dark tone.

In London by the 1850s they had increased the bore and fingerhole and blow-hole size and doubled the volume. It’s these c1850-1880 wooden London flutes that modern “Irish flutes” are based on.

Anyhow these small-hole German flutes are a dime a dozen, few people want them nowadays.

Re: Thoughts? Old Swiss keyed flute with ivory headjoint

“We take it for granted that one man out of ten plays the flute” was not a retrospective estimate, but a quote Robert Bigio presents in his Readings in the History of the Flute, taken from a review of W. N. James‘ Flutist Magazine, that was published in 1829. Although it is impossible to fully verify the claim, it should be noted that the flute was the single most popular instrument of the time, and that most major newspapers regularly carried flute related advertising. I personally believe that the figure of 45’000 when applied to flutes themselves is not far fetched. Rod Cameron estimates that the firm of Richard & Henry Potter manufactured around 20‘000 flutes during its existence. The serial numbers for Monzanis had reached around 2900 by 1829, and the Clementi Nicholson models would have been very close by this date. Adding these estimates together pushes us beyond halfway towards 45’000 instruments, and that is just from three companies. Looking in the New Langwill Index, I count around 25 makers who were active in London leading up to 1829, and that’s only counting those with the initials of A-C.

Re: Thoughts? Old Swiss keyed flute with ivory headjoint

About my statement a dime a dozen, that’s a saying not literal, but I see them all the time in antique shops for well under $100, on Ebay when I was looking at flutes several years ago they were on Ebay every day, nobody bought them. I’ve seen a half-dozen sitting in a single booth, all with ten or more keys, all with ivory heads (usually split) and nobody wants them.

Thing is, a person serious about ITM doesn’t want them because they don’t have the tone and performance usually associated with Irish trad fluteplaying.

A person serious about historical performance of Baroque or Classical (1750-1825) music doesn’t want them because they’re NOT a Baroque flute nor are they a Classical-period flute- they’re flutes made in the period when orchestral music was being performed on Boehm flutes in France and the big-bore big-hole London 8-key flutes in Britain. They were dinosaurs or “retro” when they were made originally, in the 1840 to 1940 period.

So, they have very little market value for serious fluteplayers. Nearly 100% of the time they’re used for décor, not for musical performance. I have seen hundreds of them decorating pubs and restaurants, never seen one being used for a professional performance.

About the flute being the “pop” instrument in the early 19th century yes it is true. I know that we’re used to guitars having that role, to every other person having a guitar in their flat and knowing at least basic chords, and most of our pop music over the last half-century being guitar-based.

But try to set aside our modern situation for a moment and go back to the early 19th century. The flute was extremely popular and widespread, for one reason you could take it apart and put it in a coat pocket. Very inexpensive one-key flutes were made in vast quantities out of cheap wood for amateur use.

It’s hard for us to realise how inexpensive. My grandfather’s fiddle was made in Chicago, they cost $24 a dozen. Yes he played a $2 fiddle.

And top fluteplayers were the Rock Stars of the early 19th century, playing to huge public audiences and touring constantly.

“We take it for granted that one man out of ten plays the flute, and knowing well how much each of us, the musical ones in particular, adores his own image, and the image of his own employments and thoughts, we arrive at the conclusion, that the work whose title is affixed to this notice, may, on general principles, expect a wide circulation. In it flutists will find all that is useful to be known as to the passing matters of interest in the little world to which they belong by sympathy of flute playing.”

Possibly James makes the statement elsewhere and the writer is reflecting it in this review? Contrast also the “one man out of ten claim” with the “the little world to which they belong” barb in the second sentence. A sly dig?

The negative tone towards the end relates to the interpersonal backbiting that pervaded the magazines of the time. It does get pretty overbearing. But everything in these magazines tended towards the excessive. Even the erudition - how often do you come across words like “emendation”?

Like many other professors of the time, James sold his own “Improved flute”. While he claimed to have them made under his inspection and tuned by him, they were of course flutes “made for the trade” by other cottage-industry makers. He got into some hot water with the quote from Tulou - Tulou later protesting that he only ever played flutes by Monzani.

Yes, Wyldeman Fluter. I can imagine the constabulary were under some pressure at the time in regard to “flattening the curve”!

And no, I don’t think I’ve ever come across a James Improved in the flesh. Nicholson’s Improved of course - I have two of those, one of which is definitely not improved! I also have a T. Lindsay’s Improved, a B&S Dulcet Improved and an “Improved, London” Bb fife. Pask also made a Ribas Improved.

You can see why I dubbed this period the Improved era - it was all the go. Who would be satisfied with Potters Patent Flute when you could have an Improved one!

Pratten went one better of course with his “Perfected”, which was really just Siccama’s 10-key flute downgraded to the usual 8. I think it was also the marketing-mania period!

I’m reminded of the portrait of the young man from Middlemarch, Fred Vincy, whose flute playing did not arouse boundless admiration:

“Oh, I say, Rosy,” said Fred, as she was passing out of the room, “if you are going to the piano, let me come and play some airs with you.”

“Pray do not ask me this morning.”

“Why not this morning?”

“Really, Fred, I wish you would leave off playing the flute. A man looks very silly playing the flute. And you play so out of tune.”

“When next any one makes love to you, Miss Rosamond, I will tell him how obliging you are.”

“Why should you expect me to oblige you by hearing you play the flute, any more than I should expect you to oblige me by not playing it?”

“And why should you expect me to take you out riding?”

This question led to an adjustment, for Rosamond had set her mind on that particular ride.

So Fred was gratified with nearly an hour’s practice of “Ar hyd y nos,” “Ye banks and braes,” and other favourite airs from his ‘Instructor on the Flute;’ a wheezy performance, into which he threw much ambition and an irrepressible hopefulness.
