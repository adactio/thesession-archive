Folklore or Spirituality in traditional music?

Folks, I definitely want to be careful with this conversation, but here it goes:

I was drawn earlier to a live video of Tony MacMahon playing “Port na bPucai” that was circulating in a Facebook group. Eventually it got under my skin enough to find a video of Tony playing this tune for a documentary, where he also talks a bit about the playing of Slow Airs, with a bit of a twist. I’ll quote here in case folks don’t want to rewatch the whole video, although of course it’s well worth the time: https://youtu.be/gXTvikUGr0E?si=ktIqMZxnCsp94UNh

In it he says: “ In the modern world, one doesn’t play a slow air which tries to touch the pulse of an individual who is maybe a bit lonely going through life.
And this tune comes from the great Blasket island. It’s a tune which was brought to us - brought out of the island, by one of the last fiddle players who left there in the early 1960s, and is called in the Irish Language Port Na pBucai, which translated means “Music of the Ghosts”. And it’s a little piece of music which in a sense shows that in Ireland, in this part of Europe, we’re not governed by laws and mechanics, that we have the old religions, the old feelings, the belief that rocks and rivers and mountains are inhabited by spirits - and they’re not just shapes. They’re three dimensional beings (inaudible) - and I think that tune in a sense is a lonely way of bringing that out, and that’s the reason I like to play it.”

So - what I want to ask is - do you find any interest in “the old ways of thinking” (whether those be spiritual or folkloric or however you define that) has anything to do with your interest in traditional music?

The Irish slow air is the most ancient and atavistic form of Irish traditional music. It is the instrumental component of unaccompanied sean nos singing. Many of these airs are thought to date back centuries, perhaps longer. The late Tony MacMahon was a master of the slow air on the accordion. It’s not an exaggeration to say that he took slow air playing on the box to the level of the great Irish pipers. In Tony MacMahon’s last years his fine-motor faculties degenerated due to a neurological illness that was perhaps Parkinson’s. His last recording was a collection and documentation preserving his slow air playing. Its double-meaning title was “Farewell to Music,” at once the title of an ancient slow air, and perhaps a benediction for Tony MacMahon. There’s lots to absorbe in that recording.

Not everyone sees life or reality as a rigid either/or dualism between spiritualism and pure empiricism. Tony MacMahon was a deeply educated man who was literate in science as well as art, and actually left medical school to pursue spirit and music. Many of the ideas he lived, played, spoke, and wrote about intersect fascinatingly with quantum physics theory--classics in this vein are “The Dancing Wu Li Masters,” by Cary Zukav or “The Tao of Physics,” by Fritjob Capra. The complete works of Hayao Miyazaki also come to mind.

I remember discussing Tony MacMahon with my teacher, who came from a musical line from Ireland’s rural West Coast. And I recall him saying that Tony MacMahon offended and generated controversy in some quarters of the traditional Irish music community, “but when you hear Tony MacMahon play a slow air, you are hearing the soul of Ireland.”

RIP Tony MacMahon.

We also have a “repeat offender” group of non-player audience members who are regularly in attendance at our venue (a local hotel’s patio during the warm seasons and at the same location’s pub-like indoor space in the colder, wetter ones). Though none are players many have suggested tunes they’d like to hear and we players do our best to oblige. What gets added to the repertoire and played regularly is a collaboration of the entire gathering.

At glacial pace it has become a small community of listeners and players who know each other and our “traditional music” very well. Some wag once even dubbed it a wine club with a music problem.

After reading through large parts of this thread and listening to Paddy Jones expand on Draoicht vis a vis the Sliabh Luachra tradition (a music I learned from a native of Sliabh Luachra and still play with another group of friends) it occurs to me that a piece of what is being explored here as “folklore” or “spirituality” is also something that seems to emerge if any community of musicians plays together long enough.

This undefinable essence—Draoicht—comes and goes as regularly in my decade-old non-ITM sessions group as it does when I get together with my Sliabh Luachra music crew.
Are we not just describing and experiencing a fundamental (primal?) element of what makes both playing and listening to music such a powerful part of the human enterprise generally?

Whatever it is it is elusive. It’s not always there. It can’t be forced out of hiding and does not respond well to impatience. Which are, of course, all points Paddy Jones made crystal clear and far more eloquently than I.

If one wants to experience Draoicht—or its equivalent—in any musical tradition the more time one spends immersed with a close knit community of traditional players the more likely it will be to reveal itself.
