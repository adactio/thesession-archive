These pipes were built to sync with drones - not A=440. That’s not dysfunction. That is BEC thinking.

Re: BEC (Before Established Canon) tunes and pipes that were made with them in mind

Uilleann pipes aren’t now, and never were, designed to play in Equal Temperament.

Nor are any species of bagpipes I’ve seen, and there’s a reason: the scale of the bagpipe chanter is heard over a fixed drone.

That drone emits the Harmonic Series, an entire series of notes.

Since the chanter is heard over this series of notes, you have two choices: match the pitches which exist in drones, or clash with the drones.

Using a set of pipes in D as an example, the drones produce this sequence of notes, starting with the lowest note:
D
D
A
D
F#
A
C (natural)
D
E
F#
G
I’ll stop at the 11th Harmonic, you get the idea.

These notes are NOT in Equal Temperament, which is an invention of humans.

This scale, the Harmonic Scale, is not the invention of humans, but produced by the laws of physics, and has always existed in our universe. Pythagoras put numbers to it thousands of years ago, numbers with can never change.

For the key of D, it differs from Equal Temperament like this:
D 0
E +4 cents
F# -14 cents
G -2 cents
A + 2 cents
B -16 cents
C (natural) -31 cents

Re: BEC (Before Established Canon) tunes and pipes that were made with them in mind

A kind of grass I suppose, a rush.

Though nowadays many people use pipe-cleaners and guitar strings.

Whether or not the chanter is rushed, the Regulators are. There’s a little knob at the end of each Reg that is attached to a metal rod that goes a bit into the Reg bore, the rush (of whatever material) is fixed onto that rod.

This allows the piper to pull out or push in the rush inside the Reg bore without having to take the pipes apart.

You can carve away a portion of the rush that’s adjacent to one of the tone-holes on the Reg to sharpen that note relative to the others.

Or if your rush is a guitar string you can put a blob of wax on the string adjacent to one of the Reg’s tone-holes to flatten that note a bit relative to the others.

Likewise with the rush you shove up into the chanter bore you can add material or take away material to flatten or sharpen the chanter note adjacent to that area on the rush. It’s how chanters were tuned prior to the invention of electrical tape.

Another old-time tuning thing was to put a tiny bit of wax inside one of the chanter’s finger-holes to flatten that note a bit.

Bottom line is a piper is going to do whatever it takes to get the pipes in tune. The chanter might have a combination of rushing, finger-hole wax, and tape. (The advantage of wax over tape is that the wax stays put forever, while the tape eventually gets gooey and starts sliding around while you’re playing, not good.)

Equal temperament really only becomes useful when you have chromatic instruments wanting to play chords and multiple keys.

Re: BEC (Before Established Canon) tunes and pipes that were made with them in mind

As bagpie points out, the current tuning of the Highland pipes, where every note is tuned exactly to the Harmonic series (which as I pointed out existed before humans were on the earth) only became adopted around the 1970s.

The old tuning, which we have numerous old recordings of, and Seumas MacNeill subjected to scientific analysis in the 1950s, was the same for most notes of the scale but used a “neutral 7th” (when fingered “open”).

The oddest note was the 4th, which was tuned 20 cents sharp of Equal Temperament (22 cents sharp of Harmonic Tuning). When I started piping, in the mid-1970s, most pipers still played that way. The pipers in the military, who were used to playing along with Brass and Woodwinds, seemed to have been the first to bring the pitch of the 4th down to a Perfect Fourth.

Re: BEC (Before Established Canon) tunes and pipes that were made with them in mind

As others have pointed out, the pitch center you use and the temperament/intonation you use are independent of each other. You can have equal temperament in A435 or A415 or A392, and just intonation in all of those as well.

Historically, the other issue that you have with what I think you’re *trying* to say is that the uilleann pipes were/are not a “native” or even really a “Gaelic” instrument. Evidence for bagpipes played before the 18th century in Ireland points to instruments much more like the Highland pipes, and the uilleann pipes are descended from pipes that were also found in England and Scotland, and there is some debate as to the ultimate origin of those designs. A significant number of prominent players of the uilleann pipes in their early existence were Protestants and Anglo-Irish, many living in the Pale and other not-particularly-Gaelic areas of the country. Even the name is relatively new, only attested around the turn of the century, and probably more related to the Gaelic Revival than to a longstanding name for those pipes.

I would therefore caution reading much into the specifics of piping technique as some kind of expression of an ancient Gaelic musical tradition. And as others have pointed out, there has been plenty of “native” work here to push the music into more easily defined/contained boxes without any help whatsoever from the folks across the Irish Sea.

Re: BEC (Before Established Canon) tunes and pipes that were made with them in mind

“I’m talking about how Irish music was pressured to conform to central European theory once colonialist structures took root.”

In one local session I played in for years, the session leader who was a very good fiddler would sometimes call out the key of a tune we were playing to help a struggling guitar player. And she sometimes shouted the wrong key. Not that unusual in my experience for melody players in this music, who are just playing the tunes and not necessarily thinking in terms of Western musical theory.

Besides, many of us are still playing diatonic instruments that don’t easily fit the 12TET regime. I avoid using the C natural key on my flute because I like the slightly sharper cross-fingered Cnat -- the “Piper’s C” or “C Supernatural.” It just seems to fit the music better. And I’m not even Irish!
