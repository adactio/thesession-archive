http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_traditional_music_session

There is no improvisation at all. The ‘togetherness’ you see is because they are all playing the same, ‘Traditional’ tunes that are known all over the world, wherever the Irish have gone and made homes.

Certain tunes are played with other certain tunes according to custom. Local sessions have their favorite sets of tunes as well. Sometimes the names of tunes to be played are discussed prior to a set, sometimes they are not and they are selected spontaneously on the fly by the musician who began the set.

“General meaning of word ‘session’ is that musicans (who may do not even know each other) gather and play music - one after another, or improvising together.”

Mostly there tends to be a core group who insure it happens on a regular basis, usually weekly. That can be a few friends or a known band. They basically establish what it is they want to happen and work to direct it that way, ideally not to dictate but to keep it focused on their ideal. Others come as they choose, and yet others drop in out of nowhere because they heard there was a session going at so-and-so, usually a pub, and since their in Gadansk for the weekend they thought they’d stop by.

Each session is it’s own entity, like a living thing, dependant on the core group that keep it chugging along, from minute to minute, tune to tune, person to person, and from week to week.

Everyone, usually, is welcome to start up a tune, and others are welcome, usually, to join in with it.

Drink, yeah, musn’t forget that, for some that can also be integral.

There are also ‘exclusive’ sessions, basically invitation only, or they can feel like that. Mostly though they are social and welcoming, at least so long as you respect it is their session if you are just visiting. It helps to scope things out, to listen and watch, before adding your two bits. However, quite a lot of sessions won’t let you alone to quietly observe from the sidelines, and someone might ask you to make a contribution ~ music wise…

There are others on site here who can say it better than me, that was a bit of a quick stream of consciousness ramble. Maybe it will make some sense, hopefully…

Re: What is session?

The best way to see a session if you’ve never been to one is to look on YouTube and try to find some videos. There is a major technical aspect to a session. There is also a lot of etiquette rules that people are expected to follow. To simplify it, there is always a session leader. This person kind of controls the session. Usually it is the person who starts the most tunes or sits a bit apart from the others. As far as tunes go, the players usually go in a circle, each starting a tune. To join in a foreign session, it is traditional to wait to be invited to join. There’s many more rules that exist, but that’s just the basic ones. If you noticed, I used the term usually a lot in there. The reason is that Irish Traditional Music is an oral tradition. Seeing as it is passed down from generation to generation by mouth, this has allowed many different styles and customs to evolve. No two sessions are alike, just as no two people play the tune the same way. For example, 1 tune might have 10 different names. These differences are very important to consider when playing ITM.

Re: What is session?

Brequas, I think the various explanations above tell you all you need to know. However, I found this Blog piece on the Comhaltas site which may help to complete the picture.

A session involves a group of musicians who come together to play and experience good traditional music primarily for their own enjoyment, although in most cases it also proves highly entertaining to non-participants. Examples of sessions include pub sessions, sessions in hotel lobbies and street sessions at fleadhanna cheoil.

Instruments you’ll find at a session range from the humble whistle to the uilleann pipes and in rarer cases the harp. Most common instruments include the fiddle, flute, button accordion, banjo and concertina. You’ll sometimes come across mandolins, piano accordions and of course the ubiquitous bodhrán!

There many types of session varying from the “closed type” where musicians perform in a gig like manner and visiting musicians are not encouraged to join in to the “open type” where all-comers are made to feel welcome. The latter type of session is, thankfully, much more common and what you will normally find at fleadhanna cheoil, festivals, etc. There are also “slow” sessions where the emphasis is on learning new tunes and musicians new to the session scene are specifically catered for.

Much of session behaviour (or etiquette) is common sense Many pages could be written on this topic but for now we’ll just look at some of the main considerations.

While to the uninitiated, one session may look very much like any other, it will be found that this is not the case and most do have their own individual ethos. Visiting musicians should therefore always try and identify this and go along with it as closely as possible. In many cases there may be a session leader who starts most of the session sets or invites people in the group to start a tune at some stage so that a visitor who muscles in and takes over this role would not be very popular. In most cases the best course of action is “wait to be asked”.

I hope that with all the posts on this thread you now have a reasonable understanding of what a session is. As for starting one, I’d say go ahead - if you can find another two or three musicians you’ll be in business. Surely in Moscow there has to be any number of musicians playing classical who will be highly competent on their violins who could quite easily be introduced to the art of fiddle playing. All you have to do is introduce them to the tunes most of which are available here on the Internet (Youtube, Comhaltas, etc) and away you go. I can’t recommend it too highly as you’ll get great enjoyment from it and once you’re hooked, it’s a thing for life!

Re: What is session?

Mostly, it’s about learning how to play your instrument. You do that on your own - a session is not the place to practise! The idea is, you come to a session able to play some tunes well with the others - or else, you sit and listen.
If you play whistle, try and get a good tutor (instructions book) with a CD. It is good both to learn to read music and to pick up tunes and techniques by ear. Some say that using a written tutor is not “traditional”, but it helped me and I recommend it. Without tuition you could spend a long time not playing properly, and not knowing why.

(There are good and bad whistle tutors: the good ones start with simple tunes, have a lot of detailed notes on technique, and progress to more and more complicated dance tunes; the bad ones just have simple pieces all the way through.)

Gradually you’ll get the techniques, and learn more and more tunes. Then you may meet other players, and find a pub to play in. These things sometimes happen by chance.

I would like to dispute one point made by Rob Handel

“…there is always a session leader. This person kind of controls the session….”

This is something that has been discussed many times here. Here is my summary: Sometimes there is an official, appointed leader, or a small ‘core’ of musician that take a leading role (often, these receive some payment). Sometimes a particular player will be a naturally dominant personality and will take on an unofficial leading role. Sometimes, due to the balance of personalities, there is no need for a leader, and all players will have equal control over which tunes get played, in what style, at what tempo etc.

Re: What is session?

About leadership at session- I’ve seen gracious, inclusive session leaders who try to get everyone involved and quietly try to muzzle a player who trys to start every tune in order to give the more reserved players a chance. These leaders always join in as soon as they recognise the tune, which is the unspoken signal for everyone to join in.
Then there are unwelcoming sessions which are dominated by a clique whose goal is to play their favourite tunes with each other and who don’t want any unwanted tunes or players to disturb them. If someone outside their clique starts a tune, they will all sit staring at the ceiling, or put down their instruments, or go for a pint, even though they know the tune perfectly well, and even if the tune happens to be one of their favourites (which you discover when they’re playing that same tune later). It’s a lonely feeling, to be left “twisting in the wind” in that way.
Most sessions I’ve been to are somewhere in between these extremes. Various people start tunes. Some start more than others. The session may enourage someone to start a tune if they’ve been quiet. Subtle snobbery may be displayed if a person starts a tune which most people at the session don’t like or if the person’s playing level is subpar.

I’ve often wondered about sessions in Moscow. There are plenty of expats there and I bet there are some Irish sessions in house parties rather than pubs. I have noticed some websites and advertisments in the “Irish pubs” for local Russian groups playing “Celtic” music too.
I usually take my mandolin when I visit Moscow but have never played it there except in my hotel room.

I have a superficial impression of Russian musicans (distorted and prejudiced) that goes like this:
A lot of ordinary people who can play simple guitar accompaniment for Russian folk songs or satirical topical songs.
and then - highly trained musicians whose standard is so high it scares me a bit. I have a (perhaps unjustified) feeling that they would look down their noses at a simple plucker like me.

But if you like I’ll give you a call next time I’m in Moscow.

As for session rules and the like - the fact is, you don’t need any rules if you’re just going to play with your friends. All a session really is, is a gathering of friends to play music in a relaxed social setting, with joy and spontaneity (hopefully) .

When you have it in a public place, and open to strangers, then I suppose it helps to have a framework that is broadly understood

I’ve been to sessions all around the world and never seen or heard any mention of “rules” except on sites like this. But I suppose there are “unwritten rules” that most people have in their heads - don’t join in if you don’t know the tune, don’t play non-melody instruments so loudly as to drown out the melody, don’t talk too loudly when you’re not playing, but don’t get too precious if others do that while you’re playing - it’s a pub after all, get your round in, relax, be happy, be friendly. Don’t get too stressed if things go abit wronmg now and then

Think of your session as a travellers’ crossroads rather than an isolated outpost under siege. Musicians will come, and play with your tunes, and maybe bring some tunes of their own to swap. You will get some idiots, you may get many idiots, but if it gets a reputation as a place where people can go to play Irish/Scottish/ “Celtic” or whatever music in good company and a relaxed and friendly atmosphere, then you will be surprised at some of the great musicans that will drop in.
