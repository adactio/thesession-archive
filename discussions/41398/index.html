Re: Tune Names are they important?

Most of them are just “handles” and convey little meaning.

Of course, some names are specially chosen by composers but these don’t ncessarily convey anything about the style or substance of the tune.

In fact, it’s debatable that a tune name ever does and it’s really just your imagination working overtime… “Rolling Waves”“ sounds like waves? ”The Butterfly" flapping and weaving around the room and so on.
Of course, some tunes are deliberately written with “effects” to represent something or other but I don’t think this is true for most of your “bog standard” Irish and Scottish tunes and they could really be given any name.

Re ‘I have no name for that tune’…

Yes, there’s so many that we can’t possibly remember them all and we learn many without knowing their titles. However, it’s also the case that some of the older more cantankerous players will deliberately be a little evasive or vague when you enquire regarding the names of tune titles. Possibly, because they consider playing to be more important or are just weary about newbies asking too many questions! :-)

Re: Tune Names are they important?

Tune names are important, but the tune is more important. If you have the name, but not the tune, that’s not super useful. If you have the tune, but not the name, you can carry on.

Tune names are important to me for a variety of reasons:
1. They help me remember the tune (at least it gives me an edge)
2. They reflect feeling or meaning from the composer (if the name was given by the composer)
3. They give you a sense of their journey (especially for tunes that have many names of people attached to them)
4. They’re very useful in tracing versions and variations through recording and publications
5. It’s often more efficient to discuss tunes using their names

Re: Tune Names are they important?

“Every tune we play today was written by someone or evolved from a tune written by someone”

The second half of your statement is much, much more convoluted than it seems as written, especially the more you look into the origin of tunes. Let me first clarify that I agree with you - respecting the composers’ name(s) is my preference. But the reality is that for many of the tunes we play, the evolution has transformed the tune into something completely (or at least sufficiently) different as to be a different tune. And often many different tunes.

Let’s take Lark in the Morning as an example - what is the “original”? What should the “correct” name be? There’s a huge family of tunes that are all related. Here’s an example: which of Lark in the Morning (the typical 4-parter), Lark’s March/Geese in the Bog/Lark in the Bog (usually 5 parts now), Galway Tom (5-parts, in O‘Neill’s), Galloway Tom (from O’Farrell’s), Cooley’s Lark in the Morning (2-parts), The Fair-Haired Boy (2-parts) is the “original”? Or is it something else entirely.

I’m not trying to be argumentative - I find all of this incredibly fascinating and love nerding out about it. My own operating method is to use the tune title of the composer where the composer is known, and to use the name used by the source from where I got the tune otherwise.

Re: Tune Names are they important?

For me, the tune names used to be pretty intimately connected to the tune itself. I have often described it as a subconscious mnemonic device that would attach the finger pattern of the first few notes to the name of the tune. And that still happens up to a point. However, a few years into my playing, I was finding that I was struggling to play a tune until I could remember the name. I think the biggest part of that was that I was expending more brain power and focus on trying to remember the name of the tune than I was actually playing the tune. So I started forcing myself to focus on playing the tune and less on the name, which got rid of that problem.

However, this act of letting go of the tune name, combined with the fact that I was starting to pick up tunes on the fly in sessions has put me in a state where I struggle with names for tunes for a lot of what gets played. Most of the time, when I start a tune in a session, I know the name of the tune. But if someone else starts something, I will play it without knowing the tune name more often than not. (And a constant barrage of questions of “what tunes were those?” after every set gets annoying!)

Another interesting thing is that I have a pretty good idea of the names of tunes I’ve learned, but they’re not necessarily connected to the tune as much anymore. So if you say “Do you know xyz?”, I can usually tell you whether I’ve learned it or not, but I can’t necessarily start it without someone playing the first few notes. And then there are times when someone asks if I know a tune, and I’ve never heard the name before, but I actually know the tune. And that phenomenon is most likely a combination of tunes having multiple names, and me learning tunes without ever actually knowing the name.

Having said all that, not knowing the names of tunes is a fairly constant source of frustration for me. And I DO think that knowing the names of tunes is important. It’s part of the bigger whole of the tradition, in which you try to remember where you learned a tune, who you first heard play it, who composed it (if known), when it was composed, and maybe a story or two about why it was named that way. And you can pass that information on to other people. Is any of that stuff as important as knowing the tune itself? No… but I think it’s still important…

Re: Tune Names are they important?

I don’t think it’s fair to blame polkas specifically - I think the musical culture in Sliabh Luachra placed a much less heavy emphasis on the name of a tune.

I think there are two forces at work:
1. They were often more interested in telling you the history than a name: “I got this from Johnny, he got it from Denis, who learned it from Padraig” or “this was learned back in Ballydesmond”, so whoever wrote it down (or recorded it on an album of the Chieftains/Planxty/etc) called it “Johnny O’Leary’s” or “The Ballydesmond” or whatever. This happened to slides, jigs, hornpipes, reels, and not just polkas. Polkas and slides get the blame specifically because the larger repertoire of this tune type held on predominately in Cork & Kerry, and not as much elsewhere. A counterexample is that we often do have tune names more often for polkas from places like Sligo.
2. They recognized that “a rose by any other name would smell as sweet”, or in other words the tune name doesn’t change the tune, so call it whatever you want. This is why you see tune names being christened by the source or collector (Music of Johnny O’Leary, Sliabh Luachra on Parade).

A third possibility is that they (the musical culture as an anthropomorphized entity), simply recognized that they didn’t know the original name OR that they’d changed the tune sufficiently (there was a LOT of recycling) that the original name didn’t need to apply anymore.

At the same time, they certainly did keep names to tunes as well, so who knows the real thought processes of the actual musicians…

Re: Tune Names are they important?

I’m happy to help! The problem with the database is that discography tune names go to the tune with the lowest index number (look up any recording with a “Tommy People’s” or “Paddy Fahy’s” and you’ll see what I mean). So, while you probably CAN find the Din Tarrant’s or O’Keefe’s you want (if it’s in the database, and you add at least a tune type and possibly the key), going in the opposite direction doesn’t always work (unless of course, there’s only one tune with a certain name, and no risk of confusion - e.g. Never Was Piping So Gay). This “backwards” search also shows inaccurate statistics, as in this Cobbler example:
“There are 121 recordings of a tune by this name.”

We can be pretty sure that it’s NOT on 121 recordings, but the other names associated with it (O’Keeffe’s, Padraig O’Keefe’s and Padraig O’Keeffe’s) do.

I must say I’m very perplexed as to what logic has to do with anything, especially since naming tunes after people has been part of the tradition for a very, very long time.

That said, my recollection was wrong (Cronin wrote Roger O‘Neill’s for the Chief’s son, and Catherine O’Neill for his daughter, so please forgive the momentary lapse). From O’Neill’s Irish Folk Music:
"When Edward Cronin, a new star of uncommon brilliance, first appeared on our musical horizon, great interest was manifested in the long-forgotten treasures which his sweeping bow introduced.

That which first claimed attention was a hornpipe of smooth and easy rhythm, a stranger to us all. Not a single air or tune having resemblance or relation to it has yet been discovered. Although learned in his native Tipperary, Mr. Cronin never heard it named, but to make amends for the deficiency he christened it “Chief O’Neill’s Favourite”.

On account of its peculiarly Irish tonality, some people refer to it as “The Brogue Hornpipe”. Regardless of the name, it is a prime favourite with dancers, and its permanent popularity is assured."
