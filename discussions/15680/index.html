The traditional way to learn tunes is by ear, without ornaments.The bones of the tune, bar by bar. Re: Ornaments, Part of the Tune or the Music?

>>1. playing tunes faster with no ornaments
>>2. playing tunes slower with ornaments

I think you have to do both when you are new to this. Option 1 when you are playing with the session and option 2 when you are practicing at home.

Also, I think some combinations of notes, at least on the instrument that I play -- the flute -- are not possible without using ornamentation.

Take a tune like Dinky’s reel. Those octave jump parts. What are you going to do? Tongue the notes? No, you really have to do some kind of ornamentation technique as the means of articulating the notes or else it’s just not right at all.

However, if you are a beginner like me, you won’t even play the octave jumps at all in the session. It’s just too difficult and too fast.

If you’re a beginner like me (who never listens to the scoffers on the mustard board telling you to stay home) you’ll find ways to play the tunes without playing every single note. I learned this from a fiddle class I took long ago. The suggestion was if you could even just play the first note of each measure you could contribute and learn at the same time. You kind of learn the tune from building blocks.

Still, when you are at home, you practice playing all the notes so your fingers will learn.

Re: Ornaments, Part of the Tune or the Music?

Consider a proposition of the form:

Ornament X belongs at the end of the Nth measure in tune A.

“Belongs” doesn’t mean “is necessary”. Tune A is still tune A, and is still ITM, even if you omit X.

(Though, if you omit X, change the form, tempo, rhythm, key, add a bit of rasgueado and play it on a stylophone, it almost certainly won’t be ITM or even tune A any more)

“Belongs” means, you should usually play X if you want to play the tune as it is traditionally played. If that is your objective (or if you want to participate in sessions where that is the objective without getting dirty looks) you’d better play X.

It’s not my objective, but I don’t have a problem if it’s yours.

Re: Ornaments, Part of the Tune or the Music?

First, I have to define the word “ornament”, which, in music, refers to something superfluous to the actual melody and which is ADDED to embellish it or give a decorative effect.
Given that definition, there is very little “ornamentation” in Irish traditional music.
Cuts, pats, and rolls are devices of articulation, and have little decorative effect, the cuts and pats being of such short duration that they are not heard as pitches or notes but as seperations.
Even crans, it could be argued, are rythmic and not decorative devices.
Only the occasional F sharp trill employed by pipers and some flute and whistle players (in imitation of the pipes) falls under the idea of “ornament” in the normal musical sense. Also decorative in effect are double-cut rolls, which few people play. I’m talking here about the older traditional Irish instruments (pipes, flute, fiddle) and the traditional style.
Cuts, pats, and rolls are absolutely integral to the melodies of jigs and reels, they being the means by which jigs and reels are articulated. Removing all of this “digital articulation” would render the tunes rather non-Irish sounding.

Re: Ornaments, Part of the Tune or the Music?

To bolster my comments, I point out that the “New College Encyclopedia of Music”, under the head word “ornaments”, lists the following: trill, mordent, appogiatura, arpeggio, turn, and springer.
None of these appear in traditional Irish music (on traditional instruments in traditional style) except for the occasional F sharp trill as I mentioned above. The classical turn is quite different than the Irish roll, likewise the classical appogiatura has a different intention and sound than the Irish cut.

Re: Ornaments, Part of the Tune or the Music?

I agree with you there, Jim. You reiterated what I said a bit more clearly. Ornamentation per se is not “the” music and surely we’ve all heard someone over ornament a tune to the detriment of phrasing and rhythm. The ornaments you are taught to play are devices facilitating the types of phrasing and rhythm fundamental to Irish music. Do you need to play a long G roll at the beginning of Jimmy Ward’s jig? As in, is the only “correct” way to start this jig is a G roll? No, but the first bar of that tune has a distinct rhythm and you do need to play something that will create the necessary rhythmic effect. G rolls happen to work very well for that, but so do other combinations of notes. You can backstitch, or you can play a G and then have a rest, or really anything that maintains correct jig rhythm and phrasing.

At the same time, you wouldn’t make every single instance of a G in the tune a long roll because that would mess up the rhythm and phrasing. That’s my view of ornamentation -- devices that help you articulate rhythm and phrasing.
