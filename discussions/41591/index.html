Technology - How Has it Affected the Irish Session?

Hi! I am a graduate student in Music Education, and am currently taking an ethnomusicology course in Irish music. I am writing a research paper and preparing a presentation about Irish sessions, and I would like to discuss how technology has impacted sessions. There are of course some articles I can read and review, but I would like to hear from those of you who are regularly participate in sessions. How have you seen sessions impacted by technology? (This website certainly counts, as do apps, recording devices, etc.). I will likely quote you anonymously in my presentation if you don’t mind. Thanks in advance for sharing your experiences!

Re: Technology - How Has it Affected the Irish Session?

People now use the word “technology” to mean only electronics and you seem to be doing that. I don’t use the word that way and a lot of the answers here show that I am not the only one.

Electronics has not made any hugely good impact on sessions because electronic tuners, for instance, serve the same purpose as tuning forks and pitch pipes so their introduction was not revolutionary. People who take tune lists in on ipads could do the same thing with a paper list. I still turn up with scruffy bits of paper. Recording sessions noticeably inhibits some players and telling people who are chatting next to the microphone to shut up does not go down well so the results are often so poor that it is impossible to learn from them. There have been, as people have mentioned, more bad effects than good. It is the rare session now which doesn’t suffer from pop music leaking from another bar, phones going off or big screens. Pop music is so ubiquitous that there can be a terrible cacophony and only the musicians seem to notice that there is a problem. You also get occasional electronic instruments turning up and they can be a menace. Bass ukuleles are my pet hate.

It is outside the session and in learning to play that there have been greater effects on the music. The online Fiddlers Companion, the session web site and other ABC collections have largely replaced the endless searching of books which I used to do to try to find a tune which was going round in my head but for which I had no title. I used to copy tunes out by hand into a big, blue book (which I still have), just as people had been producing music manuscripts for four hundred years before me - but now the tune can just be printed out. A lot of people learn from videos or via skype. Again there are pros and cons though. It is very easy to waste the time saved by spending too much of it contributing to online discussions. Searching books and copying music by hand is oddly soothing, whereas keyboards never are.

Many people’s attitude to noise seems to have altered as a result of it being everywhere all the time. I recently went to the Music Library in the Central Reference Library in Manchester where I used to collect both songs and tunes and was saddened to find that two pianos and a full drum kit had been installed in it. As one piano was being played and someone with no sense of rhythm was banging the drums it was completely impossible for me to read the musical scores. Concentrating on reading words was difficult enough. I didn’t stay long. I blame an electronic world for the idea that there should be background noise in a library.

Re: Technology - How Has it Affected the Irish Session?

I’ll chime in as someone outwith Ireland and the UK (I’m in the USA) who began playing this music in the mid-1970s, before the internet, digital recorders, mobile phones, and all the rest.

Learning this music out in the diaspora was very difficult. The locals who got good at playing it usually did so by moving to Ireland for a year or more. When they returned their style and repertoire was completely transformed.

If you didn’t go “over there” all you had were the few available vinyl albums, some books, and whatever players you happened to meet here. Your impression of the music was shaped by whatever players happened to end up on vinyl albums which (more or less by chance) made it over to these shores.

Instruments were also very difficult to find. The only “Irish flutes” available were those made by American makers such as Ralph Sweet. For me, taking up the pipes, it was much worse. I got a practice set by an English maker who (as I realise now) didn’t really know what he was about. For the first five years or so of learning the uilleann pipes I only had a working reed around half the time. I’d play for several months, the reed would die, and happen upon a reed somewhere several months later, and be playing again for a while. (In 1982 I acquired the reed I still play today.)

The only positive thing about acquiring Irish instruments at that time was that horrid Pakistani copies were yet to make their appearance.

Obviously technology has utterly changed the lot of the diaspora beginner. Now they can go on YouTube and hear a wide range of genuine Irish traditional players both playing and teaching. They can take Skype lessons from teachers anywhere on earth. They can see and hear and purchase instruments from makers all over the world.

So that’s all about the process of getting to the point of being able to attend a session!

About sessions themselves, travel is much less expensive than it was in the 1970s and people today attend festivals tiolonoil etc in distant places regularly.

During a session everyone has their mobile phones out, which I’ve seen used in many ways:

-electronic tuner
-storage for tune-lists
-recorder (audio and video)
-TunePal
-and, in Michael Eskin’s case, the instrument itself.

Personally I pick up tunes the fastest if I can see a player’s fingers (auditory + visual) so I’ll record a video of the person playing the tune and at home I can learn it in a few minutes.

Re: Technology - How Has it Affected the Irish Session?

“with varying tape speeds they were usually anything from a quarter-tone to semi-tone flat…”

Around here, back in the 1980s, myself and many other session players used tape recorders that had a half-speed setting.

The purpose of the setting was to allow you to use half as much tape (if you recorded and played back at half speed).

But if you recorded at full speed and played back at half speed the music was half the speed and an octave lower, so that you could play along in tune. That’s how I learned all my tunes back in the day.
