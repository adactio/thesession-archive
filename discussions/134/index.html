“What would a session be like with no guitars, pianos, bouzoukis etc.? It would be at a great loss…”

Well.

I’ll start with the fine print first: I truly enjoy good accompaniment, especially at sessions, and guitar, bouzouki, piano, etc. can add tremendously to the music.

BUT…

Some of the best sessions I’ve heard or participated in had NO accompaniment instruments. In fact it’s a blessing to finally be able to hear all the nuances and the special blend that fiddle, whistle, flute, concertina, button box, etc are capable of.

If Zina and I keep quoting from Barry Foy’s book (Field Guide to the Irish Music Session), we’ll soon have plagiarized the entire thing, but the following is simply to clear accurate and to ignore:

Page 44: “Irish music was in fine shape before it began to be swaddled in lush, melodramatic harmonies, and it would be in equally fine shape if all chord-playing instruments (including accordionists’ left hands) were to disappear tomorrow. Irish dance tunes were designed to stand on their own, without accompaniment, and they still do so quite admirably. In the final analysis, every accompanist must acknowledge a cruel but indisputable truth, which is that what she is doing is, let’s face it, superfluous to the music. Not that guitarists should let this news get them down--it’s too late to turn the clock back now. They should, however, let it inspire a little humility and discretion.”

Amen.

Just so’s you know, if you’re going to play DADGAD, you’d better have the thick skin of a banjo player. *grin* One of the more polite and kind jokes I’ve heard about DADGAD players is that they play DADGAD because they don’t want to know anything about the actual tune or the actual chords that might go with it…

You almost never hear piano at a session, rather unsurprisingly, since they’re hard to tote in your handbag. And anyone bringing a keyboard to a session without being specifically invited to do so is just looking for a little punishment. One of my favorite accompaniment comments is the one on Pat O’Reilly’s 2000 years of Irish traditional music for 1909:

First ground breaking recordings by a group of post-structuralist Irish pianists including Eileen O’Shea,
John Muller, Ed Greoghan, Ed Lee, Herb G. Henry. Accompanied by an unreconstructed fiddler from
Sligo, Michael Coleman, the new art music is widely misunderstood.

That said, you often see a piano accompanying the piano accordian or other solo instrument at a feis. Dancers need a nice strong beat and hope for a musician with lots of lift to help them along, and the piano is about the only thing that can stand up to the piano accordian and actually be heard enough to add something to it. I have great respect for good feisianna musicians, by the way. How many other people can play with lift and great vigor for eight hours or more with only three or four short breaks? The ability to deal with the boredom alone is worthy of respect!

We just had Andy Irvine in for a concert and workshops through our company The Cuirt House. It was lovely, and inspiring as well. But it’s important to know the roots of anything you do, not just for the plain fact that it’s history, but also because it makes you a better whatever-it-is-you’re-doing. Andy himself knows a great deal about the history of Irish traditional music, and he thinks about what he adds and doesn’t to it -- he also knows the difference between the Slavic stuff he does and the Irish stuff, as well as the commonalities. (He also commented that he is NOT an accompanist -- he adds in around the tune melody and the accompanist, was how he put it. He said he doesn’t really think of the bouzouki as an accompanying instrument, nor is it a really good solo one since it’s not loud enough.) An Irish trad musician who can only deal with Riverdance, Lunasa, and Enya isn’t much of an Irish trad musician. (Not that I’m accusing *you* of that, Bloomfield!) It would be like the rock musicians who don’t realize what a debt they owe to J.S. Bach, for instance. It’s important to know when and where things like those driving rhythm sections came from. If I was listening to one of the pure drop albums that have come out recently and an Irish trad musician from the MTV generation thought it was boring because there was no driving rhythm section, I’d automatically assume that they were either complete and utter tyros or had terrible taste or any of the above. It doesn’t make them bad people -- just not good Irish trad musicians. It doesn’t make Riverdance bad music, just not traditional Irish music. Being a musician is only partly about whether you can play your instrument. Otherwise, you’re just someone who plays music.

Last Night’s Fun by Ciaran Carson and Barry Foy’s Field Guide to the Irish Music Session should be required reading for all Irish traditional musicians who do not reside in Ireland! (And some that do! Heh.) :)

End of rant. *grin* My suggestion for you, GoldenKeyboard, is that you start a thread with a specific subject, like “Chording voices” or some such, rather than something as general as “Accompaniment for Tunes”. You’ll stand a better chance of not having us solo players butt in and hijacking your thread then! (No guarantees, though. *grin*) I’d love to see a thread for guitarists and how they choose their chords, though, because I’m often in the awkward position at our slow session of having unexperienced guitarists who want to know what chords to play and I have little idea of what to tell them!

Re: Accompaniment for tunes

This dialogue is a perfect example of how new ideas are introduced into a tradition, tried out, mulled over, judged as worthy additions or as trash, and incorporated or thrown out. You can see the opposing forces so easily: the ones who are eager to try out the new, and the ones who want to keep the tradition is it is. (And of course, most fall somewhere in between.) And both viewpoints are of value to the tradition: those who incorporate new influences can add a sense of adventure and prevent stagnation, while the ones opposing a change add stability, a sense of judgement on whether or not a new element is worthy of the tradition, and they also prevent loss of the values of the tradition.

So where does that leave us with the accompaniment issue? Exactly where it should! Both camps have done their job, fulfilled their function. If I attend a session with a really good guitarist/pianist/bououkist/etc, great! Or I can go see Lunasa (who, in my humble opinion, rock big time.) Or if no accompaniment instrument shows up at my session, great! We’ll have a great time regardless, the ensemble being complete with or without the guitar etc. Or if I show up (or better yet, Matt Molloy shows up) all by myself/himself and plays a few tunes, great! The soloist ethic of IrTrad decrees that the ensemble is complete with only one person. (And Matt Molloy, IMHO, rocks big time also.)

In my Non-Irish musical life, I play a very non-trad instrument; it’s called the Chapman Stick and was invented about 30 years ago. (My obsession with this wonderful instrument makes me want to explain all about it, but I’ll refrain, except to mention that those interested can learn more at www.stick.com. Check out the links and listen to some sound files of it!) When I started playing IrTrad, because my daughter started at an Irish Dance school and because I just plain liked it), I thought I was going to do an new thing: play Irish music on the Stick. As I learned more, I stopped that effort, got a tinwhistle and started on that. (And now I have a low whistle and a M&E flute is on the way to my door, gawd help me. Not to mention having bought more CDs these past few months than I did for the last 5 years.) My intention is to continue immersing myself in the IrTrad musical tradition, on traditional instruments -- while continuing my study of the Stick, which is getting REALLY hard to find the time for, darn it! --it’s SO much easier to pull out the whistle and start toodling away). I’m looking forward to my first session - maybe in 4 years or so - and making more musical friends.

And who knows? Maybe after years playing my flute and whistles in sessions, with hundreds of tunes under my belt, maybe I’ll walk in, no accompaniment instrumentalist will have shown up for the 6th time in a row, everybody will be in a mood to try something else, and I’ll pull out my trusty Stick. And then?

Be afraid. Be very afraid.

Re: Accompaniment for tunes

Good rhythm is the key to good accompaniament. What most people don’t realize, is that it is a much bigger challenge, requiring more skill, to play good rhythm, than it is to play good lead. It literally takes 10 to 20 years to develop the rhythmic skills to play expert rhythm.

At first glance, it seems simple enough; just play a steady beat….. ..even if others are not… with an incredible amount of accuracy.

How accurate?… well, at 120 beats per minute, playing 4 notes per beat, we are down to 1/8 of a second. That is the length of one note. We want the beat to sound at the beginning of the note. The human ear can easily detect the difference between straight eighth’s and swing eighth’s. That difference is equal to 1/6 of the length of the note. So we are down to 1/6 of 1/8 of a second, which = 1/48 of a second. If we play with this accuracy, we are still leaving enough room to muddle the difference between straight 8’s and swing 8’s.
Even if we cut this error in half, we still have some pretty sloppy playing. If we cut it the error to 1/4, then we have an error of 1/4 of 1/48 of a second which is about 1/200 of a second, which is equal to about 5 thousandths of a second. Absolutely incredible, and this has to be done 120 times each minute, including chord changes, etc. I know of no way to test this assertion, but that is likely the accuracy range that is needed for the music to flow, so that spontaneous toe tapping among listeners takes place.

So how does one learn rhythm… marry a metronome, or a good hard shoe dancer!

Re: Accompaniment for tunes

Jeff, this may only seem at first to have only slight relevance to what you’re talking about, but stay with me, here; have you ever seen a dancer dance the slip jig? It might tell you a bit more about the tune and what it’s trying to say. Irish stepdancers consider The Butterfly something of the penultimate slip jig, although, due to it’s deceptive simplicity, it’s often played for beginners.

I love the slip jig, it’s my very favorite dance. But it’s the hardest, in my opinion, anyway. It is literally, when danced at regulation feis speeds, twice as long as the single jig. By the time you get done with two steps, you’re about ready to be done, and if your teacher still makes you do three steps, when you stop you’re hanging off the barre from lack of breath. It’s necessary to flit about the floor with as much height, grace, elegance, and acrobatic skill as you can manage, for almost twice as long as your reels. For an easily obtainable example, “The Countess Kathleen”, while not particularly traditional in any way, is a lovely, lovely slip jig to dance to, although I can’t say I care too much for the step that Jean Butler does in the first version of Riverdance.

For a dancer, the key words for the reel are power and grace. For the light jig, military precision and bounce is key. For the single jig, it’s bouncy and perky you’re aiming for. For the *modern* slip jig (emphasis very much on purpose), it’s all of those things. You aim for height and power and grace and precision and a certain perkiness that doesn’t overrule the grace. It takes excellent physical condition to dance the slip jig really well.

A dancer prays for a musician that inspires them to find the dance in the music. It can make all the difference in the world. When a dancer bows to a musician after their steps (which generally happens, when it happens at all, after his/her bow to the judge), s/he is saying, “thank you for the honor of allowing me to dance to your music, for letting me be your music in physical form.” There is no way to really describe the difference between dancing for a musician who has the lift and drive that a dancer can’t always find words for, and one who doesn’t. It all sounds very esoteric and airy-fairy, but there’s really no other way to describe it. It’s to do with music that makes you want to tap your toes and music that doesn’t.

Re: Accompaniment for tunes

Jomac, I agree that the rhythm comes from everyone, but…..
if the rhythm is not as accurate as the best lead player, then it weakens the session. A good rhythm makes everyone play better, and a weak rhythm brings down the level of the session.

Yes, everyone shares responsibility for the rhythm, but if someone else handles that job, it frees the lead players to focus on getting in sync with the rhythm, playing their tunes with lift and interpreting the tunes. By the time that a player has developed the skills to play lead and manage the rhythm, the player is no longer interested in playing in situations where he/she has to manage the rhythm, because it detracts from the artful performance of the music. Hence ---- closed sessions.

Managing the rhythm, usually means something like playing with a heavy emphasis on the beat, to draw everyone together. There is nothing wrong with this, except that sometimes it is nice to play with subtlety as well, to express other aspects of the music.

Having said all that, my timing has plenty of room for improvement, and for some reason, improvement in that area is very slow for me.
Perhaps that is one of the reasons that I am always posting about timing. It takes so long to develop it. Start now!!!!!! *grin*, and have fun.

ScottyElFiddlero: Agreed with all of your points. I think I’ll start a new thread about rhythm because I’ve got some questions about it.

You know, in the ooolllddd big bands, they used to apply the “everybody is the rhythm section” ethic, working hard on having the whole band play in exact time - except the drummer! They would have him play (ever so slightly) ahead of the beat, or behind the beat, depending on what effect they wanted. They got incredibly skilled at doing this without making the whole band rush or slow down, and it has a subliminal effect they took advantage of: You had the drummer play ahead of the beat when you wanted the audience to get up and dance, and you had him play behind the beat when you wanted them to sit and eat dinner, or just listen, or whatever. Wonder if anybody in IrTrad plays around with this sort of thing?

Going way off topic, I always liked to listen to the bands Chicago, and Tower of Power, both horn bands. In Chicago, the horn section almost always plays behind the beat (while keeping the tempo going), causing a laid-back, cool-jazzy sort of feel. While the TOP horns always played right! on! top! of! the! beat! with great precision, giving them their ultra-phunky vibe.
