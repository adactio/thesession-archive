Not-the-same spots vs ear conversation

First, my disclaimer: I realize spots vs ear is a contentious subject, and that the overall consensus (at least with ITM) is that you should try learning by ear and with a teacher. If no teacher, there are CDs, MP3s, YouTube, etc. Find ways to slow down the music (if needed), but spots should be a last resort.

Overall, I completely agree that ear training is incredibly useful (regardless the instrument) and the sooner you get away from sheet music, and over to playing by ear and memory, the faster you’ll be enjoying the process and being able to convert just about any tune from one that’s heard to one that’s played, without needing to see it written on paper (mostly).

While I’ve been learning ITM, I’ve slowly been encouraging myself (more and more) to try to find ways to listen and learn. So I’m working on it! And being an American, I often practice by using common American tunes and songs that I remember in my head (sometimes Christian hymns), and just try to play them without looking up the sheet music. It’s also not unheard of for me to try converting a Disney song into a tune.

That being said, I grew up in a “classically trained” sort of way. Played violin when I was a kid, saxophone as a teen, guitar, bass guitar, and various other instruments over time, which almost entirely rely on having decent sheet music, and putting in the practice of playing what you see.

But I personally find great value in being able to do both, reading and hearing, in order to learn a tune. It opens up a lot of possibilities, especially outside ITM, where good recordings for certain tunes, historic and almost-lost tunes, might be sparse, but the sheet music exists. And I 100% recognize (and agree) that to play good ITM, and to be able to add your own flair, ornamentation and style, that it’s crucial to get away from the training wheels once the tune is memorized.

SOOOOO, without getting into old debates, a slightly different question.

--

For those who can read sheet music, do you find that you try to avoid talking about it in this sort of forum? Not necessarily that you feel bullied or anything (but maybe you do?), but maybe to keep the debates down and to avoid arguments. Or do you have no problem sharing that you read sheet music, and sometimes use it as a tool? Do you have a limitation on how much you’ll say on that topic? Like maybe you avoid sharing that you’ve learned an ITM tune from sheet.

In other words, how do those people who can read sheet music feel about the topic of sharing that you can read sheet music? It kind of seems like it’s a mixed lot, and that some people here tread very lightly if they talk positively about reading music. But maybe I’m reading the environment wrong.

And also to those who can’t read sheet music and have spent your life ear training (which I think is astounding, personally) or have come up with your own method of notation, do you feel that those people who read sheet music are disadvantaged and that reading might become a life-long crutch? Or do you feel like they’re maybe a bit more advantaged than you, but maybe you don’t like the feel of someone using your lack of reading against you, or their ability to read music as a means of lifting themselves up?

I guess this is sort of a deep look into personal feelings, experiences, and so on, and how readers vs listeners may have made each other feel over time. This might end up being a touchy subject, but maybe a chance to air grievances, or just give your opinion and share your experiences? Who knows.

But what are your thoughts?

NOTE: If this is a bit of a sensitive subject for you, I hope we can all just find ways to express ourselves respectfully. Bad experiences often influence us in negative ways, all of us, so lets validate a bad experience if we see one and move forward on finding peaceful resolution. After all, we’re all musicians (or at least music lovers) who share an appreciation for something that really seems to bring in people around the world. Bless the Irish for their contributions to music! So I think we should remember that, and enjoy the fact that this is a sibling(?)hood, and that we’re sort of a big family. Thanks everyone. :)

Re: Not-the-same spots vs ear conversation

I think any advice you read here should be viewed with healthy skepticism, including this post. :-)

It’s really nobody’s business to tell you how to learn.

Figure out a system that works for your brain and then do that.

I’m classically trained since childhood. I can read tunes from notation at full speed like I’ve played them my whole life.

That being said, I exclusively learn tunes by ear through a multi-day process of playing along with slowed down and then progressively faster recordings using Transcribe!.

Why? Because that’s what works for me to really own the tune.

If I’m sitting next to you at a session and you start a tune and play it well, I could care less if you learned it from your teacher, a recording, dots on a page, a crappy MIDI file, or some magic tune-conferring peat-based enema you bought from a shaman.

You do you. Don’t let anyone tell you that what you’re doing to learn tunes, if it works, is wrong.

Now, if what you are doing to learn tunes isn’t working, then try another approach until you find the formula that works for you.

When you do, hopefully you’ll find, as I did, that following the formula takes all the effort out of the process, and it becomes an absolutely repeatable and satisfying recipe for learning and ultimately owning new tunes.

One of the “dirty little secrets” of trad is that many of the famous old teachers and players used some form of musical notation fairly regularly. Padraig O’Keeffe, for example, had his own distinctive notation that he’d use to teach his students.

So, don’t worry about using notation. Don’t necessarily rely on it totally, but don’t listen to anyone who would cast you out into the wilderness for using notation every now and then. I doubt they’d do the same to Padraig!

I don’t avoid talking about that I can read music, in this forum or at all. I have noticed it does almost feel displayed as a badge of honor for some musicians to be able to say they can’t or don’t read though, in lots of traditions, not just ITM. I do remember being very impressed at Dolly Parton having such a long songwriting career without ever needing or bothering to read - decades of melodies all in her head. After hearing that & similar from others I was inspired to not *need* to have sheets & make extra effort to get to know the tunes in a way they could never be taken from me if I didn’t have that one book, internet, name, etc.

I don’t feel shamed if I say I learned a tune mostly from sheet music versus a recording, but I do always give a quality recording first before a link to the dots as a reference when asked, because I was taught that is a superior source for appropriate pronunciation, akin to the mother tongue approach. That all learn to speak their native tongue quite fluently from absorbing it constantly before learning to read or write - why would music as a language be any different? It was presented to me very strongly that way since music is primarily experienced via the ears, not eyes. When I was taking lessons after the memorization piece was in place my teacher would say, “ok good now we can get on to the music making part”, which was quite helpful in instilling that it’s so much more than getting the pitch & rhythm “right”. We should be using any & all senses at our disposal to learn tunes the way we’d like to play them - smell, taste, whatever works for best results. :)

When I was first coming into learning Irish tunes, I took very seriously the oft-repeated advice to learn them by ear instead of from the dots. But I didn’t know if it was better/worse or slower/faster, just that a lot of folks were saying it in one way or another. I even heard one fine & famous fiddler tell a workshop there was no possible way to get the essence of the tune from sheet music so not to bother! They said it might even stunt your own full expression so was best avoided. I interpreted this as an emphatic nudge to at least never stop with the sheet & think that because I could play the notes I had really absorbed & could deliver the tune in a compelling way.

At that time I had several Lúnasa recordings which I listened to almost exclusively for the better part of a year, then some Kevin Burke, Liz Carroll, Martin Hayes, Danú. I took in those tunes aurally over many months & because it happened in my car, at work, while doing chores it almost felt like no extra effort at all.

For newly discovered tunes I still prefer to learn from recordings for what I perceive as efficient use of my time, but will look up on here if a passage is so fast I can’t make it out, or I need to make a distinctive effort to play in a particular setting for a particular session or person. I never ever start learning a tune from sheet music without hearing it first at least a few times since for me personally it feels like a waste of effort until it’s at least singable from my mind. I could read it yes, but how can I begin to play well what I don’t already know in my head or better yet have soaked into my bones? I’m sure I’ve missed out on some super tunes written out in books (or flashed at sessions I didn’t catch titles of) that I haven’t come across on recordings though, which is a shame.

The circles I play in are generally open & enthusiastic. No one seems to mind how the tunes are learned as long as they are openly shared. I don’t prefer a session where time is spent finding the correct page in a book. I do get excited when a friend has spent time reading through a book to find the most interesting ones to learn & bring around, and trying to pick a new tune up after a round or two is one of the most exhilarating parts of the evening!

Re: Not-the-same spots vs ear conversation

This is less about any individual person and more about the music itself. The fact of the matter is: Irish traditional tunes are simple enough to learn by ear that sheet music is hardly ever necessary. This is by virtue of them being simple melody lines. This is one of the reasons why backers and accompanists tend to have such a difficult time picking up the music, because they don’t deal in melody lines. They deal in harmony lines. So they not only have to be trained by ear, they also need proficiencies in composing/improvising chord structures.

With that being said, as an ear musician, I never avoid discussing my ability to also read sheet music. I’ve worked hard to cultivate both sets of skills, why be ashamed of either one? If you want to learn anything efficiently and to a high standard, it is important that you utilize all exploitable resources necessary. When I’m learning sheet music, I’m still listening to the music, comparing the things I’m hearing, and using the same skills I use in ear training. And when I’m learning by ear, I’m still using visual cues and imagery to develop a secondary understanding of what I’m hearing. And if the music is complex or difficult enough, I will study it by ear, with sheet music, and a tutor! But to bring it back around; Like I said a moment ago, it’s just rare that Irish music ever really takes all of that.

There are a few other points I would like to make.

First off, most of the ear/dots brawls on this site have generally come down to a distinction that I think is important to make. You can’t learn to play Irish music from the score alone. You can learn Irish tunes that way, but you need a good understanding of how the rhythms, nyah, and lift make it sound Irish. So a lot of the people being adamant against using sheet music are doing it in response to people that are trying to learn the music, and are doing so with sheet music because they are intimidated by learning by ear. When you learn this music by ear, not only are you learning the melodies, you’re also hopefully learning how to play them ‘properly’. But in general, once you play this music well, nobody cares how you learned a tune as long as you play it well.

I will admit that I do believe that people who are ‘paper trained’ (and don’t learn by ear) are at somewhat of a disadvantage. Of multitude of tunes that I play (~1000 or so), I would say that close to half of them were learned in sessions. When you have a good ear, it’s not uncommon to play a tune that you’ve never played before just from having it played a few times through. In this case, there’s a difference between ‘play’ and ‘know’. But after playing along with the same tune a few times in sessions, I can add it to my ‘known’ category without ever even knowing what its name is… (I do try to learn the names, of course).

The other point I will bring up is that during the many discussions we’ve had about this over the years, many people have said that they feel like they don’t retain the tunes that they’ve learned from the dots as well as tunes they’ve learned by ear. For me, playing music is exclusively an auditory process. I process what I hear similarly to speech, and when I play, I am probably expressing the music at least partially through the speech center of my brain (which makes it difficult to talk when I’m playing, although, that’s something that I’ve been working on for 20 years and have gotten a bit better at…) The woman who first introduced me to this music could utilize both ears and dots for learning music. She used to sight read a tune for me to learn by ear, and then have me teach it to her by ear so that she could retain it…

↳ Posted by DonaldK on December 8th, 2021.

Re: Not-the-same spots vs ear conversation

“In my opinion, there’s nothing special about Irish (or traditional) music in this respect. I would contend that you can’t learn to play any type/style of music from the score alone. There’s nothing in a classical score to tell me to play the notes with (what to me) seems excessive vibrato.”

There *is* something special about classical music (read ‘European art music’ – Baroque, Classical, Romantic etc.) in this respect, however, in that staff notation evolved concurrently with it in order to serve it. Every symbol in its inventory (at least, those regarded as ‘standard’) exists primarily to represent a feature of classical music; many of them happen also to be applicable to other styles and genres. But yes, even then, the performer’s interpretation of the music goes beyond the information that can be got from the page.

Re: Not-the-same spots vs ear conversation

To elaborate on what DonaldK posted, it seems as though a good number of people that come to Irish music from another style come from Classical music. This makes sense, because playing music for people often starts in school, either in Orchestra or Band. These people generally learned to read music at the same they learned to play music, so it’s all tied to the style that they were learning to play. So sometime later in life, when they’re deciding to branch out into other styles of music like Irish, the natural inclination is to get some sheet music for Irish and play it. But a lot of these people don’t necessarily have much knowledge of what it should sound like, and seem to think that playing a bunch of Irish tunes is all it takes to play Irish music. So steering these people toward ear learning, especially at the beginning, is a way to help accomplish multiple necessary steps that they need to take to become proficient Irish players. (1. Listen to a lot of Irish music. 2. Start to internalize the rhythms. 3. Learn to put some flexibility in their music. 4. Learn to pick up tunes on the fly…)

When I say ‘flexibility’, I mean that different people play the same tune in pretty different ways. For me, that was a real struggle early on if people weren’t playing a tune the way that I played it. So I had to learn to be flexible with tempo, rhythm, and even melody. The good news about learning flexibility is that it helps lead you toward being able to play with variations as part of your expression of the music, which is a place that people that are reliant on the dots often seem to struggle with.

I have always maintained that the 2 skills - playing from notation and playing by ear - are complementary, and if you have both, it is a huge bonus. The things you don’t find very often in written down trad music are such things as dynamics, rubato, phrasing, pauses, etc: this is all left to the interpretation of the player, and probably rightly so, though unfortunately you do hear some tunes being played just as a string of notes with no interpretation at all, when people have just learned said string of notes off an un-embellished score. Such players would probably do better learning by ear from a recording of an established player who does put some feeling and expression into the music. (without necessarily being a slavish copy of the original recording.)
