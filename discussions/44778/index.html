Which Type Of Preferred Flute, And Why……………?

As a flute player, I am interested to know how other flute players on the forum feel about their preferred type of instrument specifically for playing Irish music, and most importantly……………..why ? The first two main categories I suppose being modern and antique, with the further subdivision of Rudall and Pratten style. Not to forget tone hole size, which generally fall under; small, medium and large. Other factors playing a role, such as embouchure size and shape, material used for construction, keyed or key less etc………………. I thought it would be interesting to find out which factors mattered and work for individual players. I would like to add that I personally do not see any “right” or “wrong” type of flute, as luckily the amount of choice out there is nearly as varied as the players out there using them.

Re: Which Type Of Preferred Flute, And Why……………?

My answer is probably going to be different from everyone else’s.

I started playing “Irish flute” in the 1970s when everybody I saw or knew of played antique London-made 8-key flutes because at least around here (California) there wasn’t anything else. At that time I never saw in person or saw a photo of heard a description of anyone playing a new-built “Irish flute”.

There was no internet so the players I saw in person were the ones on tour, like Cathal McConnel and Mick Tubridy, and the vinyl albums available here were few, and they all played antique flutes.

For many years I played a Rudall & Rose made at their 1827-1837 address. It was boxwood with ivory rings and sterling keys. It was a lovely player, so responsive and tonally flexible and easy to fill, yet there was something about the power I heard out of the Prattens other people were playing so I bought one of those.

It wasn’t made by Boosey, but another well-known London maker named Kohler & Son, around 1860. It was Cocus. In its bore and fingerhole arrangement and performance it was identical to Boosey Prattens. It looked uncannily like the D flute I saw Matt Molloy playing. I sold the R&R and played this Pratten for many years.

What was impressive about that flute was the tonal evenness- every note of the low octave from Bottom D on up were equally strong and clear. Passages involving Low E amongst the other low notes popped out more clearly and powerfully than on the R&R. That flute took a bit stronger blow but it was by no means inefficient, you got what you paid for. It gave more power and clarity but compared to the boxwood R&R wasn’t quite as responsive and flexible.

So when people talk about modern flutes styled, based, copied, or inspired by Rudall & Rose I have no opinion or comment because I have no way of knowing what these things play like. I only know what an actual c1830 Rudall & Rose plays like.

Ditto modern flutes said to be “Pratten style”.

BTW the thing I did of playing a R&R early and later switching to a Pratten was something I observed with some other players.

Re: Which Type Of Preferred Flute, And Why……………?

I swabbed the flute after every playing session and oiled it fairly regularly- besides the moisture in a flute is mostly condensation. It’s not like you’re dribbling spittle into it. So no saliva from 100 years ago or an hour ago.

If Hawkes & Son were like other 19th century London makers they would have made a wide variety of flutes over the years, starting with strongly conical small-hole 8-key flutes in the early 19th century through Nicholson and/or Pratten gentler-cone larger-hole 8-key flutes in midcentury to cylindrical-bore Boehm/Carte/Radcliff/etc etc mechanised flutes later in the century.

19th century London flutemaking is a fascinating subject due to the almost infinite variety of types of instruments. In France the Boehm flute was quickly and nearly universally adopted (though with certain fairly minor modifications) while Germany held fast to the tone of the small-holed small-bore wood flute well up into the 20th century. In England players were caught in the middle, recognising that one or more of the various independent design changes that together made up the Boehm flute were improvements, but wanting to hold onto one or more of the features of the traditional wooden conical bore 8-key flute. Thus there was endless experimentation with flutes combining old and new features, and the same London flutemaker might offer 20 or more different models simultaneously.

Re: Which Type Of Preferred Flute, And Why……………?

The issue of cross contamination of pathogenic organisms between players in the same room does not apply to antique instruments which haven’t seen use for considerable years. Protozoa and parasites purposely develop outside of the host, often spending a sizeable part of their life cycle in stagnant water. Their pathway into a host usually relies on coming into direct contact with said water, or by being bitten by a vector (insect) which contains it. So, unless you dredged your latest old wooden flute out of some black lagoon, these organisms can be ruled out. Pathogenic bacteria, (the prime source of infection from touching a contaminated object) again are happiest in their hosts, where it is an ideal 37 degrees C. for replication and log phase growth. Outside of the host, there are two environments in which bacteria can survive; a reservoir, where conditions are good enough for the bacteria to replicate, or a fomite, where the organism can survive, but not replicate. An old flute, which hasn’t seen use for decades, is without doubt a fomite, as there is nothing that the organisms in question can feed on, and the environment is largely hostile to such organisms. On average, the vast majority of these organisms can survive in a fomite for no more than a few weeks, and many not more than a few days. Viruses face the same limitations on surfaces, and those with a none enveloped structure (such as Covid 19) can be deactivated by strong soap. The most long lived infective particles would be spores of fungi such as Aspergillus, which can survive and remain viable for up to 30 years. We all breath in a handful of Aspergillus spores each day, but fungal respiratory infections nearly only take hold in the immuno-compromised. It is therefore highly unlikely that you’ll catch a life threatening condition from playing an antique wooden flute. In fact you go go as far to say; “The older, the better.”

I have a Siccama-system flute, c. 1850. It is like an 8-key, but has two extra keys, one over the third hole for each hand. The holes these keys cover are placed where they should be for intonation, but are thus out of range for the fingers to cover without the added keys. The holes are large and the power and tone of the flute are, imo, without equal. I got the flute to help with a hand condition I have. It serves that purpose well, so the fact that it is a superior instrument is an unexpected bonus. Robert Pratten owned one and loved it. Terry McGee has an article on his website, for the interested. They are not easy to come by. Mine is by William Hall & Son, New York. It is cocus with Sterling silver keys and embouchure.

Ailin, I have to agree that Siccama system flutes have an even response and strength of tone that is unmatched by any other simple system flute. I don’t know if you’re aware of this, but the Siccama system flutes marked William Hall may not have actually been made by him. Hugh Cottier was a New York based flute maker who won awards for his “diatonic flutes” (the exact same term used by Abel Siccama for his original design). William Hall was the primary agent for Cottier by 1855. Cottier was awarded a gold medal for a flute that was described as “superior to any flutes we have ever examined” by the judges.

Re: Which Type Of Preferred Flute, And Why……………?

After 4 years, I’m still a flute newbie, but I’m so thrilled with the instrument that I can easily get lost daily for 3 or 4 hours. It’s a breath instrument like your voice.

I have two flutes, a “small-medium holed Rudall-style” Firth, Pond and Co from about 1860, and a John Gallagher “large holed Rudall”, a replica of a mid-19th century English flute. My FPC is more-or-less similar to the replica Terry McGee makes.

I bought the cocus FPC 18 months after I started flute, and was challenged by the embouchure for a good long time - like the next year! While frustrating, I believe that the effort to learn good focus was ultimately to my benefit. My tone is finally much more consistent, clean and clear. This flute is in extraordinary condition with few surface mars, crisp holes and no cracks that I can see. It has some of the typical 19th C quirks like a low E that gains clarity by venting the Eb. But the C-foot pewter keys work perfectly and it is very easy to get a strong C/C#.

I bought the blackwood Gallagher six months ago, and absolutely love it. As a close replica, it has some of the same quirks as the 19th C antique flutes. Whenever you change to a new flute there is a learning process, but after six months, suddenly everything has come together with solid, loud, rich tone from low C through 3rd register Eb & E. I think of the tone as dark-chocolate, and then when you push it, it turns into molten chocolate tart. In other words, it just keeps giving…

Comparing the two flutes. I have to be more precise and focussed to get decent volume and tone from the FPC - the flute embouchure just isn’t as generous (easy). You can’t push the volume like you can on the Gallagher, but the small holes makes it very nimble. The low C is amazingly strong and easy-to-play.

The Gallagher is definitely much louder and more useful as a session instrument, with an addictive rich tone that can be sweet or reedy. It is easy to play loud, but it takes good focus to control the notes. Surprisingly, the low C on the Gallagher is not as solid as on the FPC, and I couldn’t even get the C to sound at first. This improved after I put effort into playing in the 3rd register.

Re: Which Type Of Preferred Flute, And Why……………?

Looking back on 15 years of Irish flute playing, I bought and sold way too many flutes. Odd, when I was fine with one baroque flute for about the same length of time, but I was looking for the magic flute, and I had the means.

My first 5 flutes were all Rudallesque flutes by modern makers, mostly keyless. I lacked the patience to develop the embouchure needed to play them well, even though I had flutes by McGee, Byrne, Grinter, Cornia, Murray, so there was not a problem with the quality of the flute. My first non-ludallesque flute was a very fine, and in my opinion greatly under-rated, flute by Paddy Ward based on a Hawkes flute. I liked the larger holes, and larger bore sound of that flute.

Next I headed in back in the Rudall direction and got a very good original. Tuning was more of a struggle, but there was something very appealing about the sound. I ended up buying two (2883 and a 4000 number) at different times. The second was described by Pat Olwell as “one of the magic ones”, and indeed it was so. I kept the second one for a decade, and only moved on when I retired and downsized my collection after we moved. This was the end of my exploratory period.

By then I had realized that I liked larger holed, well-tuned flutes with interesting tonal character. At that point I tried a run of great keyed flutes. A Glen Watson (large bore), a Tom Aebi (large hole, medium bore), and a Stephane Morvan (Prattenesque). In my defence for the constant searching, I was selling some flutes to found purchase of uilleann pipes which is an expensive business. All of these flutes were superb by any standard, but with different ratios of strengths from tuning, sound, flexibility and responsiveness. In real life any of the flutes I tried in my first ten years would have been fine (meaning that I wasn’t being limited by the flute!). If at that point I could have picked any of the flutes, it would have been the Glen Watson. That said, after I sold the Morvan on, I bought it back when it came on the market 2 years later, and I use it as my main performing flute.

On a whim, because I liked the sound quality of period flutes, I bought a John Hudson Siccama. For me, this has been then end of the journey, and I will (I swear to my wife) never buy another flute. Easy blowing, great sound, great tuning, expressive, it is a joy to play, and people comment on the great sound. It would be my performing flute but has required a series of repairs, some rather time-consuming.

So now I am a 3 flute guy: Hudson Siccama, Morvan (both focus), and Forbes Delrin for travelling. If I had known in 2005 what I know now, I would have bought a Glenn Watson.

Hugh

Re: Which Type Of Preferred Flute, And Why……………?

So to go back to TootinTwang’s original question, for me I don’t think in a priori terms about bore design and hole size and blow-hole shape or any technical stuff. That’s up to the makers.

I pick up a flute and I play it and it has magic or it doesn’t.

What is the magic? For me a big part of it is what might be called efficiency, how some flutes pour out large tone with ease.

Another is responsiveness, how some flute immediately respond to anything your breath does.

And there’s what could be called tonal evenness, how when you go up the entire gamut every note is solid. Of course it all has to be in tune.

I also covet a powerful Bottom D, strong low notes, and easy sweet high notes.

Another- and this is getting strange I know- is how some flutes seem to sing in your hands, like the whole thing is vibrating. Now everyone is going to say that science has proven that this can’t happen. Whatever. It’s something certain special flutes have. The best ever in this regard was a flute by Casey Burns. It was made of some unusual wood IIRC that grows in the arid Southwestern US. That flute came to life when I played it! (But I needed a keyed flute, so some other lucky player bought it.)

An electronic tuner does work when clipped on a wooden flute, but they’re very sensitive to vibration. They have to be, to work on a guitar headstock so far away from the soundboard. My unscientific view is that what we feel with a “lively” flute (and I do feel that) is probably 95% air column vibration under our fingertips and 5% body vibration. I only notice the vibration with most of my fingers down, and I don’t think I’m feeling much if anything from my RH thumb support or LH side-finger support on the flute.

Anyway, to the original question -- I’m playing a Thomas Aebi 8-key flute in cocus wood, a Rudall larger hole type. I previously played and first learned on a very nice Windward keyless flute in blackwood, but this one suits me better. It’s easier to play in the second octave, needs a little more effort to get a solid, hard low D than the Windward but it’s there when I want it. Not quite as loud as the Windward but loud enough for sessions. I love the look and the historical association of the dark reddish-brown cocus wood, even though it’s a recently made flute, maybe 15-20 years old (not sure, I bought it secondhand).

I can’t say if it’s easier to “fill” than the Pratten-ish Windward. It sort of feels that way, but I can’t tell if it’s the flute design, or just that my embouchure and breathing has been improving over the years so it seems easier now. I like this flute well enough that I’m not looking for another one in D, and I sold off the Windward. I might pick up a Bb flute just for fun one of these days, but I’m set for now with this Aebi flute. It’s one of those “better than I am” instruments that I’ll always be able to grow into as a player, in the years I have left.
