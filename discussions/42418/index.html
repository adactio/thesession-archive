Re: Name that (show off) tune and recommendations for big show off tunes in general

I don’t see anything… just like Bernard.

In any case I think four distinct things are being discussed:

1) tunes that are extremely fast

2) big tunes (tunes with more than two parts)

3) difficult tunes

4) tunes that impress The General Public

About #1 any tune can be played fast.

I’m here in the USA and it’s strange to me how so many American folk bands that dabble in Irish tunes (as opposed to bands dedicated to traditional Irish music) will play The Irish Washerwoman, playing it over and over maybe ten times, starting slow and getting faster and faster. I don’t know why it’s so ingrained over here. They think it impresses the audience… well it doesn’t impress me, because when they get it too fast it’s no longer musical or played cleanly but just a mush of sloppy notes.

About #2 and #3 I don’t think there’s a connection between how many parts a tune has and how technically difficult it is. Many 2-part tunes have tricky passages and many of the longer tunes are technically straightforward.

About #4, it’s odd which tunes The General Public responds to.

Other musicians might be impressed if you play a difficult tune but The General Public can’t tell a difficult tune from an easy tune.

A little group I’ve played in for decades has discovered that The General Public over here really likes Lark In The Morning. We have no idea why. So whenever we need to make a bit of a splash we play that. Reels, which one would think would be more impressive, aren’t.

When I’m asked to “play a tune” to showcase the uilleann pipes I’ll always choose an air. The General Public loves it.

Re: Name that (show off) tune and recommendations for big show off tunes in general

> What’s the evidence for “The Atholl Highlanders” not being Scottish, please ?

Somewhere in Donaldson’s Highland Pipe & Scottish Society - unfortunately it’s been missed out of the index (which has quite a lot of holes in it) so I can’t find it immediately, but I think it was in or around the chapter on the Ossian phenomenon.

More to the point - Colclough was published in 1840, Mackay’s Piper’s Assistant in 1844 and comparing the two settings it’s hard to conclude that Mackay didn’t lift it setting directly. Intriguingly, Glen published some sixty years later a tune described as the “old” Athole Highlanders, which is a 2/4 march bearing no relation to Mackay’s 6/8.

It’s nice to rail about traditional tunes being appropriated, and that certainly did happen, but if we start looking at the early major sources of highland pipe music, like MacDonald, it’s very clear that a lot of appropriating was being done in the opposite direction. Vast chunks of MacDonald are lifted directly from the major violin collections of the day and some I suspect had never gone through a chanter before they hit the page.

> To me it’s a bizarre concept that there is a “correct” interpretation of a traditional tune - even in highland piping.

Quite. It’s very rare that I’ll see or hear a tune written gracenote for gracenote the way I’d play it, and I think that’s true for most. Yet at the same time, I have a very, very clear sense of what is appropriate or idiomatic for any given tune, and most pipers would come up with something similar for the same set of dots.

Re: Name that (show off) tune and recommendations for big show off tunes in general

For whistle (and flute, I guess), my list would include:
The Deil’s In the Fish (Northumbrian: Charlton Memorial Tunebook, p54) is great, but there are many other spectacular show-off hornpipes from that part of the world: I suppose because the closed fingering of the NSP chanter mostly requires only one finger at a time to move, making very fast arpeggios possible.
Any Billy Pigg tune will work your fingers and impress the crowd: try the Carrick Hornpipe with its spectacular triplet variations in the C and D parts.
The Star Hornpipe (Kerr’s Merry Melodies IIRC). Full 2-octave range, lots of flashy arpeggios and triplet runs. I suspect it was written for the squeezebox.
The Rusty Gulley family of 3/2 hornpipes always goes down well, as does the Souters o Selkirk, whether in Matt Seattle’s wonderful (and challenging) variation set, or - my preference - in the version Selkirk Burgh Band plays at the Common Riding played with a bit more speed and swing, as though for dancing and not casting a 6‘x4’ flag around your head. (See https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tv6tMLwrk1I)
Some of Scott Skinner’s tunes are hard to do but spectacular, if you can dodge them into 2 octaves: e.g. the Duke of Fife’s Welcome to Deeside.
And of course the old Scottish favourite, Deil Amang the Tailors (either folding up the last phrase of each part on an A whistle, or playing in A on a D whistle - very tricky g sharps, but doable with half-holing, time & patience).

My argument says that I have spent quite a lot of time reading and learning about the light music culture of the GHB in the early 19th century and I am not aware of any documentary evidence.
