Tunes that lose bad guitarists

I’m talking about the mediocre three chord guys who somehow get the impression they’re perfectly welcome. It’s not so bad when there’s just one of them, but when there’s three or more it just muddies up the session something awful.

I find that slip jigs tend to confuse them, and encourage them sit out for a while.

I don’t know of any reels or ordinary jigs that throw them. Can you think of a few?

Re: Tunes that lose bad guitarists

Yes slip-jigs for sure… they can’t find the “1”.

Ordinary jigs, reels, hornpipes, etc they just perceive them all having a 2-beat or 4-beat so they’ll usually hang with it more or less. (BTW I’ve seen non-trad musicians write out jigs in 4/4, each of the four beats having a triplet. They can force most things into their preconceived 4/4 universe if they try hard enough.)

The sure-fire thing to confuse most people (not just guitarists) is to play Bulgarian dance music. There’s one Bulgarian dance tune, Sandansko Horo, that I’ve played a recording of to many good musicians: jazz musicians, orchestral conductors, Hollywood composers, etc and not a single person has been able to figure out where the “1” is, despite the recording having a drummer beating out the pattern with absolute clarity and regularity, and with no syncopation happing anywhere.

Yet a roomful of people can dance to it, feeling the underlying rhythm without any difficulty.

Re: Tunes that lose bad guitarists

@ Tony O’Rourke, re The Golden Eagle
I believe that in the first bar of the B-part the first chord (in the first half of the bar) should be Bmaj, followed by B7 in the second half . The first reason is that B7 in the second half of the first bar is a modulating chord between the Bmaj chord and the Emin of the second bar. That modulation would be weakened if the whole of the first bar were B7, for there would be nothing clearly to modulate from. The second reason is that Bmaj in the first half is a strong and bright chord, whereas B7 is a naturally quieter chord that contrasts well with the Bmaj as well as leading into Emin in the next bar.
Analogous reasoning applies in the third bar to the contrast of Amaj followed by A7, which nicely leads the modulation to Dmaj in the fourth bar.
What it boils down to is that using 7th chords in the whole of bars one and three weakens the tune.

Re: Tunes that lose bad guitarists

Getting serious (just for a minute, promise), I don’t think trying to outrun a phalanx of inadequate guitarists is ever going to work. Everybody else will feel more pain than they will.

I think you first need to discuss with the other melody players what they would like to happen - it might be that they are equally frustrated, or that they are not the least bit frustrated. Assuming they all or mostly are, you now have good reason to do something. But you have to work out what.

Is perhaps part of the problem that you have more than one guitarist at a time, and they are not always playing the same chords? Perhaps you need to consider a rota system? I’ve been to sessions where there was such a rule.

Or would your melody players just prefer some unaccompanied time? Time to explore their natural collective rhythm, rather than have one impressed upon them. Maybe you build in one or more melody-only breaks?

Or is education the answer, in which case it might pay the group to run some accompaniment lessons for a while. You could even try an experiment, where you bring along chord charts for a particularly interesting tune, and, using the excuse “I just want to see if these chords really work” show them that some tunes just need a lot more thought than others.

Essentially then:
- determine if collectively you have a problem
- define your problem
- decide what a solution would look like
- devise humane but effective ways to introduce your solution
- monitor results and fine tune.

I remember a folk festival in Sydney, circa 1975. Old Tim Whelan, a great whistle player was there, a thousand miles from home, bent on enjoyment. But whenever we started a session anywhere, a dozen assorted percussionists would descend upon us and join in, as best they were able, and as loud as they could. There was every form of percussion you could imagine - bodhrans (some no doubt purchased earlier that day), spoons, lagerphones, bush basses, bones, you name it. Old Tim put up with it as long as he was able, and sitting near him was enough to incur sunburn. Finally, after a day or two, he cracked and let them all have it. They fled in all directions, and were never seen again. We had some grand sessions after that.

“Beware the anger of a patient man.” Best if you can avoid that kind of stress building up!

Re: Tunes that lose bad guitarists

I might suggest we cut the backers a little slack.
Backing an ITM tune, IMO, is harder than playing the melody straight up.

ITM is essentially melodic and the tunes may not lend themselves well to harmonic interpretation.
Tunes may be rendered quite nicely without any backup but “good” backup can drive a tune.
But “good” backup requires more than just knowing the tune. Rhythmic sense of course is primary (know the difference between a reel, jig, slip jig, etc.,) but also a sense of chord structure and what works with this or that modal tune. (Not always easy to ascertain and can differ from say a guitar to a zouk or other.) A good backer also has to know when less is better and can use that sense to add interest to a tune.

As a melody player, and a bluegrass and classical guitar player in other genres, I might suggest a chord here or there if asked but I cringe whenever I’m asked to back an ITM tune! ;-)

Thank you FIDDLE4, for posting that -
The backing is, IMHO, spot on.
They are well matched, the backer clearly knows the tune cold,
and is filling out the playing without crowding the melody.
And it never got too “busy” for me. I like simple.

I know that, at a session, everyone should feel able
to whale away a bit and enjoy themselves, guitarists included.
Does it seem that many guitarists, wanting to cut loose beyond 3-chord tricks,
get a little carried away and quite unintentionally wind up clashing instead?
I cannot believe it is mostly from insensitivity, ignorance, or incompetence,
(not that anyone here is saying that they are)

I mean, we are all musicians and we all want a good sound.
However, trying to add some variety as a backer does not always
work out quite as they envisioned.

Just speculating.
