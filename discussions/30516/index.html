(I am perhaps handicapped by actually knowing what I am talking about here… This post is a bit off topic for this board, but anyone who spends significant amounts of their life out in the night life environment (e.g. pubs) can profit from paying attention to some of it.)

Despite what I say in the rest of this post, the only martial art that you might actually do long and seriously enough to get any good in is the one where you actually _enjoy_ the process of training. So try them all out. A good trainer is usually more important than what they are teaching.

Will’s advice on what is necessary to build a comprehensive and effective fighting style is pretty good. One very true saying is that what you train is what you will do when push comes to shove. So, if you train with a lot of kicking/punching, then that is what you’ll try to do in a street fight. Or throws, groundwork etc.

Generally, fights are characterised by distance. There are three distances:
1. The opponent cannot hit you without taking a step towards you. Make sure any talking is done at this distance. This delays any attack and is safer than:
2. The opponent can hit you without taking any preparatory steps. This is very dangerous and you need to be a skilled kickboxer or similar to survive at this distance for any length of time.
3. The opponent is too close to hit you effectively - grappling techniques will usually decide the fight here, e.g. locks, biting, gouging, strangles, etc. Once at this distance it is usually very hard to break away again safely. (Note; zone 2 for the head butt and elbow is usually zone three for most other striking attacks.)

The distances will vary depending on what your opponent is trying to hit you with, e.g. feet, hands, knife, sword, head. A difference in weapons or reach may lead to you being in zone 2 for your opponent, but him being in your zone 1. i.e. he can hit you suddenly, but you must move to hit him/her.

Further, and more important to the person looking for survivability in streetfighting is some consideration of the differences between reality and training. Here are some of the points that I listed in the notes to a course that I ran called “self defense for martial artists”.
1. Martial artists often overestimate their skills and sometimes are too quick to choose the fight option.
2. No one says “start” or “Hajime” or whatever before a real figth starts and the first blow that lands often decides the issue.
3. Know who you are dealing with - the guy in front of you or him _and_ his three mates standing behind you too?
4. Can you see their hands? Is there anything in them? Most knives are used before they have been seen by the victim. I have no experience with guns.
5. Where are the exits? Do you check this automatically whenever you enter a room?
6. Don’t go round blind corners closely - swing wide.
7. Carry a torch at night.
8. Think about possible scenarios BEFORE you find yourself in them.
9. Listen to that little voice whispering in your ear saying things like “I don’t really feel comortable here…”.
10. Improvised weapons are dangerous - e.g. bottles,glass ashtrays, table tops, bar stools. Don’t disregard them, both for yourself and your opponent. (I have “bottles” made out of insulation foam for training… The wooden iPhone is not far from the truth.)
11. Think hard about the legal situation long before you end up in any situation and be clear in your mind about how you are prepared to deal with that. Run away? Attempt to secure witnesses? Something else?
12. Nobody chooses wilfully to fight on the ground (broken glass, dog sh*t etc.) , but you will probably end up there anyway so you cannot ignore it in your training.

The end result of most of the above is that most of the martial artists I know are fairly unlikely to get into a fight.

Two ways:
1. Through increased knowledge and awareness, you are hopefully less likely to stumble into an avoidable fight. And most are avoidable if you are not concerned about pride.
2. Should you end up in an unavaoidable fight then your chances of being relatively healthy at the end of it are increased. And in many cases, the damage to your attacker may also be less in a quick and cleanly finished fight.

Fights may be unavoidable for a number of reasons, including, but not limited to, a lack of suitable avenues of retreat or the need to protect/assist someone else who is in trouble.

There are plenty of places in Scotland where your build, your face and/or your accent can get you in serious unprovoked trouble . However, with some knowledge, you can avoid or flee those places most of the time.

In college I acquired a black belt in Moo Duk Kwan/Tae Kwon Do. Training was fun, there was great camaraderie, and the overall context was to look at it as a sport, with bouts governed by AAU rules, with gloves and other padding, timed rounds etc. There was also considerable focus on the spiritual/artistic side with poomse (analogous to karate’s kata). And also a focus on board and brick breaking and techniques for killing people. My head was full of Bruce Lee movies and I thought it would be cool. By the time I earned my black belt I’d hospitalized three people, and been hospitalized once myself, for injuries sustained in full-contact bouts.

So I got my black belt, graduated college, and gradually started growing up. I lost interest in martial arts as I became more involved with real life—a family, career—and more involved with real art: design, writing and music.

It’s hard for me to say ‘don’t study martial arts’, as I did and had some fun. But there’s definitely a dark side to it and you have to figure out how much you want it to define who you are.

I think it’s a bit like music in that if you want to be good at it and stay good at it you’ve got to spend a lot of time doing it.

Life’s too short and ultimately, it seemed to me to be a waste of precious time.

So now, if I ever tense up when I’m playing, I will often use techniques from tai chi to help me relax.

Tai chi is what we would term a “soft” martial art; it is about using your head, evasion, or "letting your opponent have their way. It is very accessible, but no less useful as a method of self defense than any other art.

Incidentally, I also have MASSIVE confidence issues, which are also expressed in my playing a little more than I’d like. I find tai chi helps with that, too.

I’ve written more about it here: http://blackfiddle.blogspot.co.uk/2012/10/martial-arts-and-music.html
