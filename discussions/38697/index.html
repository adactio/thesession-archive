How is the ‘glottal stop’ ornament/articulation produced?

What do flute players actually do when they make a ‘glottal stop’, bearing in mind that different players may produce this articulation in different ways? In addition to playing ITM on a wooden flute, I am a retired professor of linguistics and have taught and done research in the areas of articulatory and acoustic phonetics although this was not my primary research area.

(1) Is the glottal stop articulated with the tongue?
Seamus Egan in his ‘Mad for Trad’ flute tutorial writes that the glottal stop ‘is achieved by making a guttural “K” sound at the back of the throat’. What is being described here is not actually a glottal stop but is a palatal stop produced by raising the back of the tongue to close off the air stream. (If one pronounces the series ‘key’, ‘Kay’, ‘ca(t)’ and then contrasts that with one’s pronunciation of ‘coo’, ‘coa(t) [Irish or Scottish pronunciation, _not_ what is sometimes called ’RP‘ (Received Pronunciation), i.e. the kind of pronunciation which used to be heard on the BBC and which people learn in grammar schools in England], ’caw‘, it is the second set of ’k’ sounds which Seamus Egan is writing about.

(2) Is the glottal stop an actual glottal stop?
A glottal stop is produced by closing the vocal cords (bands of tissue across the larynx which are set in vibration when producing voiced sounds such as ‘z’, ‘m’, ‘n’, vowels, ‘b’, ‘d’, ‘g’ and which are uncontracted and loose when producing voiceless sounds such as ‘s’, ‘h’, ‘p’, ‘t’, ‘k’). A cough will produce this sound as will the informal negative ‘unh-unh’ and many speakers pronunciations of words such as ‘bottle’ (using a ‘?’ to mark the location of the glottal stop, this would be [bo?l] and most speakers pronunciations of words such as ‘mountain’.

(3) A diaphragmatic pulse?
Majella Bartley discusses using this in one of the lessons of her ‘Fleadh Tunes for the Irish Flute’ series on the OAIM, although she describes it as a way of helping in the production of a strong bottom D and of strengthening the stomach muscles rather than as an ornament.

(4) a combination of (2) and (3)?
This is mentioned in a discussion on thesession (7395) of why northern (Irish) flute-playing is ‘bouncy’:
’Part of the reason why what typifies “northern” flute playing is
bouncey is because of its pulse punctuated rhythm. Because of
marching band flute playing the articulation is mostly diaphragmic
pulses and glottals so you get a very pulsey bounce to the rhythm.
But then you also have northern players like Tara Diamond and Cathal
McConnell that play in a relatively smooth style.’

Are there any other ways of producing this articulation?

I would especially appreciate hearing from those who have participated in workshops led by players like Conal O‘Grada and Kevin Crawford (and possibly also Catherine McEvoy) who are known for their advocacy and use of ’glottal stops’. Did these players give any exercises to develop this technique?

As a linguist you well know that nearly everyone who isn’t a linguist is unaware of points of articulation, and if asked to describe something are going to describe it wrongly.

In addition many traditional players have a degree of self unawareness regarding their own technique in general.

For example I attended an uilleann pipe workshop taught by a “big name” piper, a guy with loads of albums out and so forth. As he was describing and demonstrating his fingering and ornamentation I could clearly see that what he was actually doing was different from his description. Likewise one of the teachers on an NPU instruction video plays many things in a quite different manner from how he describes them. The booklet which comes with the video has sheet music which reflects his notions rather than his actual playing. I had to write out all of his tunes for confused beginners, so that they could have sheet music which corresponded to what they were hearing and seeing in the video. It seems to me that, when asked to teach, many traditional players develop a construct of their playing which may or may not reflect how they actually play.

So I would take traditional musicians’ descriptions of their own points of articulation with a huge grain of salt. I would guess that some who speak of a “glottal stop” wouldn’t have a clue how to produce one if asked.

One example was a lecture given on the history of Irish fluteplaying, which I didn’t attend, but I have a tape of it. The lecturer plays an example of an Irish fluteplayer who clearly is doing “pushes” with his diaphragm (yes I know that’s not anatomically accurate) but the lecturer calls it “tonguing”. Well it sounds nothing like tonguing, but I’m guessing that the lecturer simply doesn’t have any other word to describe articulation.

In short, as a linguist you can listen to a fluteplayer and probably have a much clearer idea of what they’re doing than they themselves do.

Thanks to everyone who has shared their knowledge on this difficult topic. Bearing in mind Richard’s point that it is very easy to think one is doing something when in fact one is doing something completely different, it appears that it is possible that there are at least two, perhaps three (see below) ways of doing this thing. Note that all have one thing in common: they are ‘cleaner’/‘neater’ than the tongued ‘ta’ or ‘ti’ of the classical flutist.
(1) Seamus Egan and Fintan Vallely may use a back ‘k’ sound (probably a little further back than the ‘ke’ of the classical flutists double-tonguing (te-ke or te-ke-te))
(2) Conal O’Grada (and probably Kevin Crawford) use a strong glottal stop
(3?) Since posting this I’ve remembered a couple of discussions which Steph Geremia gives of the glottal articulation in her Flute Basics lesson series on the OAIM. The first is in the discussion of articulation in the six free lessons (lesson 4) and the second is in the 10th lesson (the one with The Humours of Whiskey). In both discussions she makes the point (and illustrates it) that the glottal articulation is more legato (less explosive) than a tongued articulation. And in both discussions she gives (away from the flute) the example ah-ah-ah-ah (a back ‘a’ vowel with rapid opening and closing of the glottis (the vocal cords) as in admonishing a child. And she gives examples with the flute of doing this in bursts of 4 (lesson 4) and in jig rhythm in lesson 10 (The Humours of Whiskey is a slip jig). These examples show how it is possible to produce a glottal articulation at good speed, although I’m not sure if it is the same articulation as the stronger ‘cough’ articulation of Conal O’Grada.

Hi cac ,

The glottal stop is an “Irish” technique. But it is wrong, wrong, wrong. To properly play a flute the throat must always be kept fully open and never constricted such as in a glottal stop. Yes, the same effect can be achieved by proper technique, much more simply and much faster.

Breathe with your diaphragm, beginning way down low in the depth of your belly. Leave your throat wide open, always. And then a selective “push” with your diaphragm can do all of what a glottal stop can do.

Better yet, that method of breathing then leads to much faster embouchure articulation, in all ways.

Re: How is the ‘glottal stop’ ornament/articulation produced?

What sounds like a glottle stop comes from the diaphragm. To learn the technique imagine that as you are exhaling and controlling the airflow diaphragmatically (as you should be) you are expecting somebody to thump you in the solar plexus. The natural reaction when you recieve the blow is to immediately tighten the diaphragm and the airflow naturally stops by the throat closing to stop you being winded. The only effective description orally is hu‘h (using the ’ as the stop) which occurs naturally as well. You will find when you practice the technique that there is a slight increase in air pressure a microbeat behind the stop and if you cut from the note above the note you wish to achieve the stop at (particularly in the upper register ) within the microbeat you have an incredible little gem of an ornamentation.
I have taught this technique (and other ones)to many over many years.
Interesting to hear from people from a classical background! I trained as an oboist when I was very young.
I hope this helps.

Re: How is the ‘glottal stop’ ornament/articulation produced?

I deliberately did not mention the controversy over the glottal articulation. I know there are a few players (Grey Larsen and Niall Keegan for example) that tend not to be wild about it, but a large number of players I really like (Catherine McEvoy, Step Geremia, Kevin Crawford, Conal O’Grada) use it and I think their reasons for doing so are very sound (less explosive or obtrusive than tongued stops, cleaner than cuts or taps (not that these don’t have their place), etc.).

I don’t think it is possible to obtain the necessary speed of articulation with a punch or push from the stomach muscles. As an aside, on the Boehm flute I was taught to use this push to speed up the air stream to aid in jumping octaves, etc. I was taught to use the lips as little as possible for this (my teacher was a pupil of Aurele Nicolet who was a pupil of Marcel Moyse; btw Nicolet passed away last month a week after his 90th birthday -- may he RIP). This is not what most teachers of ITM advocate, but that is not relevant to our discussion here.

To answer the question about the ‘gargle’: the area behind the soft palate (if you curl your tongue backwards along the roof of the mouth you will feel a soft area) is traditionally called the uvular region and gargling (and the back rolled r of some dialects of German and French) is called a uvular trill (the little flap of tissue called the uvula vibrates). There are also stops produced in this region and they are found in many languages (most dialects of Eskimo, many of Quechua, most dialects of Arabic, etc.). It is not easy, however, to produce a rapid sequence of uvular stops, so I’m not sure they are used by anyone in ITM. At least no one has claimed to that I know of.

Re: How is the ‘glottal stop’ ornament/articulation produced?

One can never do a proper glottal stop from the diaphragm. It lacks the “caw” that a proper glottal stop has. Bear in mind that, in Irish music, the glottal stop is less of a technique than it is an effect or ornament. One never needs to do a glottal stop. You do it specifically to get, not only a punch of air, but a sharply articulated start to the note that the diaphragm cannot provide. The diaphragm provides the push of air, but not the caw.

o’muirgheasain, I have to put this bluntly: What you are writing is uninformed rubbish!

I’m saying this as a flute player since 25+ years, having also studied musicology and discussed various flute playing issues (tone, embouchure, rhythm etc) with both classical and “traditional” flute players.

Glottal stops are no “abomination”, but rather a means of articulation sometimes used in flute playing in Irish music. Other alternatives for articulation are e.g. “t”, “k”, cuts and taps.

Glottal stops have nothing whatsoever to do with getting a good tone from your flute. They are just a short break in the air stream, just like a “t” or a “k”, but different.

Blowing more down into the flute is probably not an innovation that comes from traditional Irish players. It was also used by “classical” flute players at the beginning of the 19th century.

I have compared embouchures with “classical” flute players and also experimented with different embouchures myself. I can say with confidence that they cannot get the same sound as an Irish player without changing their embouchure to be more “Irish”.

So, unless you have some very good stuff to back up what you are saying, I suggest you stop making sweeping statements about things you half-know.

Re: How is the ‘glottal stop’ ornament/articulation produced?

Ireland’s finest classical or traditional players, I wonder?
That apart, bearing in mind the post that started this discussion, a little linguistic knowledge helps to place things in context: one of the ways of articulating a consonant in speech is called a stop (or “plosive”). Made at the alveolar ridge, it’s called an alveolar stop - /t/. Made at the soft palate it’s called a velar stop and sounds as /k/. When the point of articulation is between the vocal folds (or “vocal cords”), we call it a glottal stop.
My point is that the three are all versions of the same thing: they are all voiceless stops. (By contrast, /d/ and /g/ are voiced stops.) If tonguing a /t/ or /k/ is permissible on the flute, then why not the third type of stop? The suggestion being made is that a glottal stop closes the throat, thus marring the tone. But, if effectively executed, it does this for only a fraction of a second.
And, to make explicit what I implied in an earlier post, traditional Irish flute players are playing dance music most of the time so rhythm and volume are more important than purity of tone anyway.

Re: How is the ‘glottal stop’ ornament/articulation produced?

David50 put it very well. A push from the diaphragm is in no way similar to a glottal stop. A push from the diaphragm increases the flow of air; a glottal stop does not. A glottal stop has more in common with tonguing as an articulation option than it does with a push of air from the diaphragm. You can demonstrate this for yourself and you’ll find you would never use a push from the diaphragm in the same place you would use a glottal stop. The sound would be profoundly different. As I said many posts back, you lose the “caw.” You also get a possibly unwanted plosive of air. Although a glottal stop can be accompanied by a bit of increased air flow, it does not do so by necessity. It’s up to the player. They are simply unrelated techniques. Both are valid, but neither replaces the other.

When I first saw this thread title I didn’t open it because I assumed it would be quickly answered in a few responses. After all, anyone who can hold their breath has just done a glottal stop. After reading this lengthy discusssion with its moments of productivity, I realize that not everyone holds their breath the way I have from childhood. It is done below the throat and when stopped fully, no breathing can be done from the nose or mouth. It can be done more softly to let some air through, and it can be done with a pulsating rhythm, whether inhaling or exhaling.
I feel that it is a technique that cannot be duplicated by other methods, having a different rhythmic feel and sound than diaphragmatic breath pulsing or tounging and, happening below the throat, having no effect on embouchure.

For me the articulation of notes to give them a pulse, or to make them sound separate eg: fdd edd in the third part of the Gold Ring, is done by the throat thing, a little cough as was quoted above ( paraphrasing Catherine McEvoy ).

You can see Conall O’Grada’s throat action here:
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SYWp2T1AOSA
( 2.09 minutes in ). You can hold a G and feel your throat pulsing ( on each side below the glands ).

I find as I prefer melody over rhythm that I have to intentionally think about my throat to remind myself to do it. I can’t find any other satisfactory way to articulate two consecutive notes of the same pitch. The front of the tongue sounds all wrong in this music for this effect.

I don’t care what its called, but in my experience works well. This along with fingered cuts allow something like parts 3 and 5 of the Gold Ring to pop out at speed.
