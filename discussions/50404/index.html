Allowable Accompaniments

Not long ago I was at a session that turned out to be more of a singaround. There was a mixture of songs and instrumentals, but then one guy switched on a laptop, that was connected to a kind of karaoke machine, and proceeded to sing with a full orchestral accompaniment from the internet. I almost fell off my chair, but most of the regulars didn’t seem to think there was anything strange about this kind of performance. I’ve got used to people singing song texts off their mobile phones, but this seemed like a new low. As a guest, I didn’t feel I could object, but after several songs performed in this way I was feeling pretty fed up. How would you react to this kind of performance? Is it allowable in a session?

Re: Allowable Accompaniments

> so how can I get a session going without sheet music?

This is a good question and probably deserves a thread of its own, but I would suggest you start where you are. Presumably people who want to play in these sessions know at least _some_ tunes, so start by playing those. If this means that you have a lot of solo sets, this is actually not such a bad thing, and is quite traditional - in fact, in my understanding, this is the older tradition of “session playing” - itmight not have been called that at the time, but I mean the habit of playing trad music with others, not for the benefit of dancers but merely for the tunes themselves. Reg Hall’s compilation “Paddy In the Smoke” is a good example.

It is likely that in the course of this, people will discover that they have a few tunes in common, which is a good start, and people will hear tunes that they like or which sound familiar, which they can either learn offline, from books or recordings or other sources, or (possibly more fun) which they can teach each other. Over time, the result is a consensus repertoire which will come together organically over time, and which will be unique to your session.

Re: Allowable Accompaniments

> I like it where everyone gets a turn to start off a set .

This is a tricky one. I appreciate the desire to include everyone, and I definitely get what you’re saying about sessions dominated by the loudest, most forceful players being less fun. On the other hand, it can be a bit of a drag to go around the circle in a sort of mechanical way, and it does take away some of the fun, sort of like a conversation which proceeds in a fixed order, and you have to say your piece when it’s your turn or wait. There’s a lot of downsides to that approach.

The temptation is to say that the “session leader” should just deal with this, and it’s possible for a sensitive and respected leader to do a lot about this - for example, inviting the quieter players to come in with a tune, or calming down some of the more excitable players so they’ll leave a bit of room - but for me this is really about the session culture, by which I mean the ways people engage with each other in this session over time. We all shape the culture of the sessions we play in, whether we do it actively or passively. If you want there to be room for other players to get in, you can do your part to shape your local session to be that way, through your participation. How you do this is probably a topic for another thread, but we can always make a difference, and the differences we make by the examples of our actions and our behavior are always more enduring than the differences we make by imposing rules.

Or, as the hippies like to say, you should be the change you want to see at your session.

Re: Allowable Accompaniments

An efficient way to build a common repertoire among local players is to bring them together to swap tunes in your kitchen. Anyone who can play a tune can teach a tune, preferably by ear. Do this once a week, separate from your regular pub session, and you’ll soon have a rake of tunes in common to play down at the pub. Also, you’ll have weaned those “very good musicians” off the paper so they’re fully listening to one another. That makes it easier for them to pick up tunes (and variations, different settings, pulse, degrees of swing, etc.) on the fly.

If the “by ear” aspect meets resistance, sure meet them half way and allow dots in the beginning. Also encourage them to record the tunes to play along with at home. Keep reminding them that music is aural, and the goal is to use your ears every time you play together.

This is quite common these days especially in small pub performances, cabaret in day centres, old folks‘ homes etc. I’d say there is no real harm in this as long as it is the performers’ own work and it still sounds natural.
