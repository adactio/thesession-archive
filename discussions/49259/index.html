Depth?

I’m definitely going to start a war with this one… I’ve listened to a lot of Irish music. Not as much as most of the folks here, but enough to know I don’t really know where the depth is. Some people say it’s in the way the tunes are played different each time or the overall arc of a set, but I’m blind to it. I do not see the complexity here. I don’t get where the emotion or the vehicle for self expression is. Playing it for too long just bores me now. I realize at this point that I used to be a huge fanatic of this tradition, but I’m starting to figure out that I was merely using the tradition as a template for how to create. How do you get into it? Is there some sort of secret I’m missing? It seems like there is. Playing it doesn’t help. It’s fun if you’re playing with others in a spontaneous setting, but otherwise I find just listening to it a bit shallow right now. I’m hoping someone can change my mind, but It doesn’t seem that deep. Tunes are just musical phrases that repeat, there isn’t a lot of variation with those repeats or changes to the phrasing. Maybe a longer note here or a triplet there but nothing substantial in my eyes. I don’t see the subtlety and I’m used to listening for subtle details. I mean I guess it’s fun simple music for when I don’t want to listen to something massive, but I can’t help but think more can be done with it. It might be an ignorant view. I hope some folks here can enlighten me.

Re: Depth?

I think Irish music has a lot of scope for emotional depth, particularly in the variations in tunes between sharp and natural etc, and in the beauty of songs and slow airs.

Certainly if it doesn’t grab you and you’re bored, don’t bother - but it could just be that sessions you attend have become show-off places, ‘let’s see if you can play this as fast as I can!’

A tune played too fast becomes blurred and shallow, particularly if played at the edge of a musician’s competence. Emotion in music comes out via the dynamics - soft or louder, which notes you emphasise, dwelling on notes or phrases - but there’s no time for that, if you’re struggling to belt it out as fast as you can. Though there again, ‘too slow’ can be a problem too, making a tune sound pedestrian, with the listener dying of boredom.

But honestly, Irish Traditional Music is so rich, so vital, so packed with nutritious protein - there’s loadza ‘depth’. Why not try listening to fiddlers like Martin Hayes & Kevin Burke, who get that ‘aliveness’ into their tone whatever they’re playing - slip jig, reel or slow air?

I hope you can find the magic again!

Hi Kellie! I’m surprised nobody else has said this, so I’ll take the chance… is it possible that you’re just not that into Irish trad anymore? Perhaps it’s served its purpose as a lens for creation, and you’re no longer a match. In fact, some of your other posts make me wonder if classical music is really your deepest love, since you seem to view things through the lens of it. If so, all of that is fine - none of us stay the same forever, we’re always growing and shifting, and there’s always the chance that you’ll fall back in love with trad sometime in the future.

If you do still love it but feel that you’ve lost some part of the connection, sometimes changing variables helps. It’s the equivalent of jiggling the connector cables or rebooting your internal system. You could start by changing your environment: Go to different sessions. Play with different people. Maybe pick up some new tunes that you’ve always wanted to learn. Play your favorite tunes differently (maybe slow them way, way down and see how you can keep life in them at that speed). Identify the sorts of musicians you usually listen to, then start changing variables: If you usually listen to older folks, try some young ones; always listen to folks from Clare? Try Kerry or Cork or the north. If you only listen to whistle players or fiddlers, try box or concertina players…. and so on. Maybe give yourself the gift of taking a break, totally guilt-free, and coming back to it.

Maybe, somewhere in that search, you’ll find something new that resonates with you but didn’t before, or maybe it’s a familiar “friend” that has taken on a new sparkle. Either way, the most important thing is that you’re happy and doing the sort of thing - hopefully music of one sort or another! - that brings you joy, whether that’s Irish trad or not.

Re: Depth?

It’s happy music. It induces jigginess in the listener. The “depth” is in the simplicity. It is friendly to the ears - you don’t have to be Irish or immersed in the genre to simply feel uplifted by the melody and dance rhythms. It is social music - in the manner of its learning, playing and listening. It is spontaneous, energetic, joyful, engaging.

It’s NOT an art music. It doesn’t require 7 lifetimes of study and virtuosity just to discern the simplest facets. It’s not disguising a musical idea with multiple layers of harmonies, counterpoint and exploration. It’s not excessively instrumented or “produced” as might modern pop. It doesn’t live life to the beat of a metronome nor is it orchestrated or conducted. It isn’t music for the educated and discerning few.

Irish trad isn’t “missing” the complexity. It’s like Lego or Minecraft in that simple folk construct pleasing explorations with the simplest of building blocks. The absence of your so-called “depth” may just be where it derives its purity and soul.

It is the antithesis of the complexity you think you are seeking. You can’t write it down.

If it’s boring you - it may be time for a break, a long one. Swim in the oceans of endless depth elsewhere. Take your personal inspiration where you are engaged, challenged, inspired. But I suspect you’ll return to the shallows of satisfaction when your wanderlust is sated. As Jackie Daly said in a recent session - if it was any better, it wouldn’t be right.

Re: Depth?

For me it is, to put it far too simplistically, in nuance. I think when you focus on the nooks and crannies you begin to hear what you’re calling depth: the pitch, the bends, the rhythm displacement (off the grid), the accent, the ornaments (there are hundreds that don’t go by formula),the scratch in the tone, the color variation… I think when you begin to focus on several tunes on this level of precision, you begin to develop your own style and you begin to recognize the difference between players with deep roots and those that simply play well. For me, all these elements are a composite of the living tradition that go back several generations and are a continuum of local music culture. We may only be able to imitate the sound but I think it is possible to create the illusion that you are more directly connected to the tradition - you begin to feel you are a son of the tradition - have become adopted. You begin to take the music more personal and become pissed when it is played carelessly.
I play next to musicians that are very talented but there seems to be a lack of care for the music. I play with inferior players and have a great time with them. I feel their years of playing, listening, devotion, and love for the music despite a lack of technical skill on their instrument. The music is in their head and I can hear the bits coming out in the performance - despite the holes in the overall sound I can fill the blanks.
If you need further evidence that the music has depth (it is an ocean) it is in the simple fact that the tradition is so strong across the world. This would simply not be possible without the greatness that exists within the music. I admit that it was not always easy for me to hear it. I think I got that from careful examination of the nuances I discussed earlier. So, in short and a rather harsh statement of your initial question, the problem is you. Do not expect the music to come to you - it is up to you to find it. Expect it to come with a lot of work and with persistency over a long period of time - especially if you did not grow up around the music as is my case. I think the journey has been well worth it and I remain excited to stay on the same never ending path of discovery. I certainly am only just beginning - it is an ocean.

It’s an interesting question

There are some very negative remarks here that suggest only an idiot would ask such a question, but I think it’s a good question. We all get bored with musical genres from time to time. I think it when that happens, musicians should be honest about it… and then they should ask themselves why they’re getting bored and what might change that. Re: Depth?

To me, the heart of Kellie’s question is a plea for help from those of us who *aren’t* bored by this music. He’s asking the obsessively passionate among us to explain what elements of this music draw us in and hold us in thrall. He writes:

“How do you get into it? Is there some sort of secret I’m missing? It seems like there is. Playing it doesn’t help. It’s fun if you’re playing with others in a spontaneous setting, but otherwise I find just listening to it a bit shallow right now. I’m hoping someone can change my mind, but It doesn’t seem that deep.”

The futile part is that Kellie’s asked this same question before, in various ways, but he seems unable or unwilling to take on board the generously proffered answers. So when I bother to post on one of Kellie’s threads now, it’s with the hope of providing some thoughts for other readers to chew on.

If Irish traditional music wasn’t a vital part of your family and community, then you likely won’t have much of a clue what to listen for in the playing of the music. Some people bring experience in other genres to Irish trad and process what they hear through those criteria and filters. Others may have little to no musical experience and may be unaware of even the most basic musical elements to listen for.

***In either case, you won’t begin to understand Irish trad until you immerse yourself in it and hear it for what it is.***

I’ve played this music for 50 years, and I’ve been fortunate to play with many masterful musicians. Based on that experience, here’s where I find depth in this music. Here are the elements to listen for, to watch for, to open your mind to.

- Foremost, this music is about sharing a personal soundspace with other people, face to face and immediate. Among players who’ve lived inside this music, their version of a tune is theirs alone—a deeply personal and dynamic (ever-changing) sense of a tune’s contours and feeling. But coming together over tunes isn’t just about the music. It also includes craic, stories and backstories, laughing, the angle of a piper’s knee and the easy flight of a fiddler’s bow hand, shared appreciation for drink and other vices, sometimes food, and a heady mix of relationships, a community, communion. You cannot truly immerse in this with a recording or written notation (though once you viscerally know what musical communion feels like, a recording or even notation can evoke a fair simulation). And you won’t have an inkling of the depth of it until you’ve lived it for years.

- The tunes themselves, like any good music, are about depth and scope of emotion and expressing that emotion. A straightforward tune like The Mountain Road may sound one dimensional—happy, or foot-tapping—until you hear someone who’s lived inside that tune for a thousand sessions and countless hours at home exploring its possibilities. And they play it wistful or yearning or pulling up an emotion we don’t have words for.

- The depth of the emotion, of what you feel the tunes expresses, is about actually *playing* the tune. It’s in the infinite ways of articulating the notes (each of which has a start, middle, and end), tone, loudness and softness, phrasing, pulse, silences, and so on.

- Which leads to infinite variations—in ornamentation, timing, rhythm, phrasing, tone, interval and melody choices, implied harmonic structure, etc.

- And then you get to slide seamlessly into another tune, continuing a theme, or perhaps expanding on it, or maybe diverging into another feeling altogether, and let that tune lead you through more choice of how to articulate it, how to vary it, how to make it sing the way you hear it in your head.

Imagine doing this not just with one tune or a handful of tunes but hundreds of tunes, thousands of tunes. Imagine building a life’s work of tunes, and knowing each tune’s provenance, the friendships and loves and arguments and laughs that each tune carries, having at hand the tools to express each tune countless ways, and knowing that you’ll hear a fresh take from the next musician you meet, and pick up yet another new tune as well.

Ideally, you absorb all this by sitting among brilliant players steeping in sharing the music with their neighbors. If you don’t live in an Irish trad community, you have to search out the nearest tradition bearers and dive in. If you think, “I can’t do that,” what you really mean is, “I have other priorities.” Which is fine, but they won’t lead you to living inside the tunes.

Find a session of fierce players and go every week. Learn to play. Learn a shedload of tunes. Read Ciaran Carson’s “Last Night’s Fun.” Play Out on the Ocean until you run out of variations. Play it in four other keys. Play it till you can’t stand it any more and then play it for another two hours.

If you soak yourself in this music, you’ll spend many hours alone, but you’ll never be lonely. For companionship you’ll have the tunes and all the people associated with them, and you’ll have ready session mates around the world.

But wait, there’s more…

…As I learned more about dancing, not even actively practicing but just exploring their world by indulging in “dancer” media, I learned a lot about tunes; particularly, how articulations and ornamentations work. Learning the basics of dancing, and intentionally learning about Irish music in a dance context, markedly influenced my playing. Matter of fact, I would go as far as to say that learning dancing taught me the things that would eventually lead to me finally understanding how playing this music works.
