A casual survey.

This thread risks re-raising two old contentious issues;- (a) dots versus ears, and (b) whether or not so-called ‘ornamentation’ is considered simply a part of the tune or else something separate. Myself, though I can basically read music, I am strictly an ear player, and to me ornamentation is simply just the tune and how you come to play it. In other words, although I constantly practice the techniques that are so-called ‘ornamentation’, those techniques just allow me to play the tune as I hear it and want to play it. In other words, I see no separation between ornamentation and the tune itself. I am hoping that this will not become another argument as to which of these two matters is the right or wrong way. What I am interested in is if there is any correlation between those who read the notes and see ornamentation as being a separate thing, and those who play by ear and see ornamentation as simply part of the tune. So please treat this as a simple survey and tell me how this relates to you personally. Discussion is, of course welcome.

I learn tunes in a variety of ways, including by ear, from written notation, and often these days by osmosis - e.g. I find I can often play a tune from one of the records I listened to in the 1970s, but have never actually played before.

I never learn ornamentation, and rarely teach it. In my own case, ornamentation was something which just happened over time, and just happens when I play. You know, I never understood why so much of a distinction is made between people who choose to learn by ear and those who choose to learn by sheet music. But I say this in a completely general sense of course. It goes without saying that one has to have an understanding of how Irish music sounds to deliver the music, but does the fact that someone reads sheet music mean that they have a completely different listening experience from someone who learns by ear? It seems to me that whether or not one reads sheet music or learns by ear is independent of whether or not they feel that ornaments are part of the tune or separate. Learning a tune by sheet music doesn’t take away all of the ornaments you hear in other people’s playing, or in recordings. Learning a tune by sheet music doesn’t dictate how you’re going to play the tune for the rest of your life. What if I told you, that you could copy a tune down on paper, and then rewrite that tune a dozen times with ornaments and variations, changing it every single time, while maintaining the tunes integrity? How is that so different than just playing it a dozen times, but differently every time? You could also transcribe your favorite recording, with every single ornament and variation. And then learn it, in companion with the recording.

For me, playing by ear and sheet reading have always been apart of my life. I’m naturally better at playing by ear, so that’s how I learn most music; But I’ve always just seen them both as 2 methods with the same purpose: Understanding the music. I don’t feel that they are so different that we think differently from each other. Even the skills themselves are arguably on a spectrum. I mean, how many people don’t use their ears in some way to learn the music? If I’m learning a piece of music by sheet, when I make a mistake, I don’t think “That looks wrong”, or “That doesn’t match the sheet music”. No, I immediately think, “That doesn’t sound right.” Because we’re all connected to music aurally in some way, even if we don’t have formal ear training.

So to answer the question; No, I don’t believe that there is a correlation. Because from all the books I’ve seen, recommended and suggested ornamentation is included in the sheet music already.

2) I see ornamentation as separate. Making an analogy: if a person were to go out to a party to dance, then the person would be the tune and their clothes, jewelry, etc. would be the ornamentation. How will people remember the dancer? As a combination of the person and their dress. Unless they’re specifically thinking about the different items of adornment, people will simply visualize the dancer as the person + their clothes/jewelry/etc. But the person is not actually the same as their clothes. Same with tunes; we tend to think of them as having such and such ornamentation (rolls, cuts, maybe slides, whatever), but really, those things can be stripped from the tune and you still have the tune.

Thinking about tunes this way helps one to adapt to different settings as someone else said above. Now, I do tend to learn a tune along with ornamentation (especially when I learn by ear, but even when I learn off of sheet music), but the better I know the tune, the more I can separate the tune from the ornamentation and add different ornamentation.

Re: A casual survey.

I learn exclusively by ear. And for me, the matter of ornamentation is more than a black and white “are they part of the tune or not”. When people are first learning, I think that learning ornaments as part of the tune can be very valuable, because it provides them context for practicing the ornaments. And there are certainly some tunes where there are rolls/triplets which are pretty well baked into the structure of the tune.

However, to be truly musical, I believe that you need to put some personal expression into a tune each time you play it. As Will Harmon used to say, I don’t believe that ornaments are something that you hang on the tune to make it pretty, like hanging ornaments on a Christmas tree. A big part of their function can be to help convey something in a phrase (which is hopefully something slightly different than the last time through) as part of your expression of the tune. If you think about it like language, it’s like telling a story with a different emphasis, which can ultimately change the underlying meaning of the story, while still being the same ‘plot’.

I have met a number of pretty good players who play tunes exactly the same every time (every time through the tune in a session, but also at every session when that tune comes up…) To me, that’s a sign that people have learned a tune, and then just regurgitate it like a parrot that has learned to speak a phrase without actually understanding what the phrase is all about. Once you’ve learned a tune well, that should just be the first step. The next step is to get more intimate with it, by figuring out different ways to express it. And even simple changes in your ornamentation can convey a different feeling.

So I encourage my students to learn some ornamentation as part of a tune, but also to play around with the ornamentation as their first step in becoming eloquent in their expression of the tune.

Ha, JNE, I wasn’t referring to you in the parrot comment, and I’m surprised to learn that you consider yourself unable to do variation without mucking up the tune!

To be able to start adding variation into your playing, you just have to do it, and not worry if a few tunes get harmed in the process! For me, the progression toward being able to add variation to a tune really did start with the ornamentation. Like taking ornaments out, or adding them in places… Then I started working on simple note dynamics (sliding into the note, emphasizing the note more or less, cutting the note off, like taking a breath, playing a double stop, etc). And then melodic variation started by learning specific ones (a John Carty class where he taught 4 different variations in the same spot in the tune Sunny Banks was a great start, and then finding melodic variations in recordings that I liked, and learning them…) Melodic variation can really be as simple as changing one note by playing it in a different octave, or playing a harmonic note instead, or using a higher pickup note instead of a lower one, etc.

Eventually, you get to the point where they start happening organically in your playing - somewhat controlled, but really just manifestations of your desire to express the phrase with some feeling.

There’s no time like the present to get started! And next time I’m in town, we should get together and play some tunes in private, where it matters less if we muck a tune up here or there… ;-)

Gobby, you say “So improvisation comes under the heading of ‘ornamentation’ does it?”

That’s not how I’d say it. Variation can include ornamentation, but it can just be different notes, too. And variation doesn’t have to be improvised, but it might be.

So let me use “Donnybrook Fair” (AKA “Joy of My Life”) as an example, because I think most people are likely to be familiar with it. And please note that this is my understanding of the tune, other people’s almost certainly differs in key points.

So the first two beats are, in my mind, a sort of superposition of a G roll and the pattern GFG (ABC notation, all F’s and f’s sharp), and an A roll and the pattern AGA. If you asked me to play the tune without ornamentation, I wouldn’t hesitate to play the second versions there as my default pattern. If I’m playing it normally, I will freely shift back and forth between the two patterns, occasionally throwing in other ones.

The next beat I learned as Bee -- there’s a lovely loping variation for that I got from Sean Cleland which goes Bge. When I play it, it is a variation without ornamentation. It certainly isn’t an improvised melodic variation, it’s something I learned and pull out fairly frequently because it gives a nice pop to the tune there.

Then if you look at the beginning of the second part, in my head it is very solidly gfe fed|~e3, BUT I know it works nicely to sub a g roll or a f roll for the first or second beat, and if pressed to do without it, I might play the e roll as ege. It would be rare for me to roll all three beats, it would be rare for me to not roll at least one of them.

Does that make any sense?
