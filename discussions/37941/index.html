Fiddling In A Fake Irish Style

The recent “Singing with a Fake Irish Accent” thread got me thinking about a similar thing, which is playing Irish Trad tunes on fiddle when that is not the only style you play in.

Is this acceptable? Let’s assume that, generally speaking, the players are very good at what they do.

With the exception of those who simply ‘play the notes’, use over-long bowing, and are not really from a fiddling background, and thus that deficiency can be spotted by ‘the layman’, what’s the feeling on good musicians playing Irish music on fiddle?

I’m thinking of players like Aly Bain, Alasdair Fraser, Peter Knight, Dave Swarbrick, Mark O’Connor, Giles Apap, etc ..

What do you think?

Re: Fiddling In A Fake Irish Style

‘Irish’ covers a huge range of styles. And most fiddlers from any tradition also have a wide spectrum of style, so there is always a huge overlap (certainly when it comes to fiddlers from other Celtic traditions, there may be less overlap for a Klezmer or Cajun fiddler). So to sound vaguely Irish a Scottish fiddler only has to use a certain part of his normal style, he doesn’t need to imitate an Irish fiddler - there may be differences, the Scottish fiddler might use more bow articulation where an Irish play might use more left hand, but the overall effect will be vaguely Irish sounding.

To see how musicians of the calibre of those that Jim mentioned deal with the situation just watch Transatlantic Sessions. When an Irish singer stands up they don’t all start trying to be Martin Hayes, they keep playing in their own style, but in ways that blend with the music. The one exception to that on Jim’s list is Alastair Frazer, who does try to play in specific styles and even tries to emulate specific old masters. But to my ear his playing always sounds contrived - like a classical violinist wanting to play trad and trying far too hard. The elements he thinks are important in defining the style get over emphasized and you land up with a sort of cartoon version of the style.

I think possibly if you are not an Irish fiddler but want to ‘play Irish’ the trick might be not to try to copy an Irish fiddler or use 5 note rolls and things that you would never normally do, but just play within your normal style while concentrating on not doing things like chord chopping that sound distinctly un-Irish.

Re: Fiddling In A Fake Irish Style

There’s a difference between a non-Irish fiddler playing Irish tunes attempting to play them in their idea of an “Irish style”, and a non-Irish fiddler playing Irish tunes but using the fiddler’s normal native style.

I’ve heard Jerry Holland play Irish tunes and they sounded pretty much like Jerry Holland playing his usual Cape Breton tunes. I’ve heard Appalachian fiddlers play Irish tunes and they sounded pretty much like their usual native Appalachian tunes. A language metaphor perhaps is somebody from Appalachia reading aloud a book printed in Ireland using his normal Appalachian accent. It might never occur to him to put on a fake Irish accent when reading such a book, or a fake New York accent when reading a book printed there. Likewise all the tunes he plays are expressed through his native Appalachian style, wherever the tunes originally came from.

Such people aren’t failing to “sound Irish”.

About Americans making that attempt, a very good whistleplayer I know told me once “we Americans are always in search of ”the sound“. Irish players HAVE ”the sound".

When I was playing Irish flute all the time, 20 years ago, I was learning tunes from a variety of sources and played each tune more or less how I heard it. An old Irish fluteplayer who moved here, born and raised in Clare, whose entire repertoire was old Clare tunes played in the local Clare style he grew up hearing, found my playing oddly marvelous. I would play a tune I learned from a Matt Molloy album in an approximation of that style, followed by a tune I learned from a Micho Russel album in an approximation of that style and so forth. Such a thing was unthinkable to the old Clare-man. Of course it was nothing but my musical immaturity, not having integrated my various sources/influences into a mature personal style. Yet both of us had, in reality, learned precisely the same way: learning each tune as we heard it. The difference was not in how we learned our tunes, but in the source of the tunes.

Re: Fiddling In A Fake Irish Style

@jjw

He probably has heard Irish fiddlers, and what he plays there is probably what he personally hears.

When someone comes to Britain and learns English as a second language they learn by listening and repeating, so you would expect them to land up speaking exactly like the natives they learn from, but they don’t. Someone from France or Germany almost always speaks English with a discernible French or German accent. Part of the reason is that they listen to our language with ears that have been trained to listen to their own - there is no ‘th’ in French or German, so when we say ‘the third thing’ they might hear and repeat ‘ze sird sing’ - they hear the nearest equivalent sound from their own language. The same thing happens with the Japanese with ‘L’ and ‘R’. In Japanese the ‘l’ and ‘r’ sounds are different accents of the same phonetic, so they hear the two as being the same and find it very difficult to distinguish between them. And I think the same thing happens in music - a classical violinist will hear an Irish fiddler referenced to what he knows, and actually won’t hear the subtle nuances that aren’t part of his first language.

Re: Fiddling In A Fake Irish Style

@ Frank LeHouillier

Why should you strive to get the “Irish sound”? Who told you that? I really don’t get this obsession with wanting to be Irish, either in singing or playing. ‘Sounding Irish’ is such a wide target it really doesn’t give you anything specific to aim at. So instead of starting with that as a target, I think it is far better to start at the other end by thinking ‘how can I make my fiddling better?’ and move upwards in small steps from there. If the music you like and listen to is Irish then making your fiddling ‘better’ will inevitably mean drifting slowly in that direction, but without the need to copy an Irish fiddler note for note. You’ll probably never be considered ‘authentically Irish’ but who cares? you will have a style that is authentically your own. When it comes to playing with others in session or wherever it really doesn’t matter if your playing come from the pigeonhole marked ‘Sligo’, ‘Donegal’, ‘Cape Breton’ or no pigeonhole at all. All that matters is that you can play in a style that fits and blends with what others are doing. You don’t have to play exactly like them, a variety of styles just adds colour to the music.

Re: Fiddling In A Fake Irish Style

I feel compelled to add my tu’pence worth.

I’m Irish and playing fiddle now about 3 and a half years. Does my fiddling sound Irish? I really can’t say.

I’ve learnt a load of tunes over the last few years - probably about 150 at this stage - it’s been quite intense but thoroughly enjoyable.

Sometimes I’ll learn a tune and play it very frequently for a few months, so I can really enjoy playing it, and stop thinking about the left hand, and then think about the right hand, and then stop thinking about the right hand also and then really listen to it and really play it. A few times I’ve done this and due to my haste, eagerness and “reckless” approach I can find myself listening to someone playing it - and realise - oops, its supposed to sound a bit more like that (accents in wrong place, flow is off in places, or even on occasion just a blatantly wrong note or two - sometimes even a critical note) - No big deal - lesson learnt - just fix it as best I can and keep going.

So do I sound Irish? I think sometimes I do - but mainly only when I’ve full unconscious control of both the left and right hand - these are technical things - if my bow isn’t in the zone or my left hand is not where it needs to be then - I probably don’t - but the again maybe I do… maybe I sound like a poor standard Irish fiddler. When that unconscious control is present then the tune can emerge and evolve - without it, your running through repetitive patterns of notes.

I think one can’t really say that there is such a thing as faking a style - you can either play in the style or not.

I listen to plenty recordings of tunes and then try to imitate the phrasing and melody (obviously) that person is playing - not because I want to fake their style - but because, provided they are good fiddlers and I like their approach to the tune, then imitating that persons style gives a starting point (in fact often the only place to start) in relation to how the tune is played, i.e. their specific technique for that tune is a certain way in which the tune can be produced from the instrument. It doesn’t mean its the only way it can be produced but it is one way which is true to the tune.

I don’t think anyone should then persist in imitating a particular individual style once the tune is learnt - all our arms are different sizes and our wrists are different and our fingers are different and our hands are different weights and sizes etc. So that sweet spot in the bow in terms of movement, pressure and angle are also different.

I believe once the tune is in a persons head and heart they should play it how it feels to them - that’s the truest expression of the tune. I think that can only occur when the unconscious part of technically playing (mentioned above) takes over - but once that happens then just let the tune emerge - Don’t worry about whether it does or doesn’t sound like Paddy Glackin or Frankie Gavin or Michael Coleman - those guys just play the way they play.

So just play the way you play - that’s the Irish style as best I know it. If what comes out doesn’t sound like the widely held consensus (not that there ever really is such a thing, but I think you know what I mean) of how the tune should sound - then you’re just playing it a bit wrong, because in your head its a bit wrong - so see where its wrong and change it a bit.

For instance - doing a Georgia Shuffle on reels is probably going to sound wrong - or doing conscious bowing patterns will probably sound wrong - most likely because it messes with the phrasing, disrupts the unconscious control and so ends up not really being the tune intended - and its easy to get used to something that sounds wrong and so start believing it sounds right - so all of a sudden you are intending to play the tune wrong and believe it sounds right.

Anyway - I know that my reply is a little abstract - but I suppose in the context of a thread intended to be about fake fiddle styles which is largely about dutch accents I suppose its not so bad a contribution.

In summary, I don’t believe there is such a thing as faking a fiddle style - you can play with style or not.

And - imitating a particular player’s (or region’s) style is a good thing to a point - to enable you explore technique that may be required to play with that style - but should not in my opinion be treated as the definitive way a tune should be play, as attempting to play note for note, bow for bow and ornament for ornament what another player is playing is akin to treating the music as a complex classical piece without sheet music - i.e. play the tune with personal unconscious technique as opposed to with clinical repetition of someone else’s version of the tune.

While I’m sure that some are Irish “for real”, and some have been recorded by musicians inside the tradition, it’s still the same dozen of tunes that most people from outside the tradition play (typically Bluegrass players, old-time players, Swedish folk musicians, and that group called beginners).
