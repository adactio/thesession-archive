Mary, this is purely anecdotal, but I’ve played fiddle for around 25 years now and definitely notice some decline in my hearing, especially in the left ear. Higher pitches seem most affected, and it’s at its worst when trying to separate out the signal from a lot of background noise--listening to one person talk in a crowded party, for example.

I have no way to confirm that this is from fiddling, but I can’t think of any other chronic exposure to loud noise I’ve suffered over the years, nor many acute loud noise “injuries” I might have been exposed to. And I’m only 45--I worry about what my hearing will be like in another 15 years.

I’ve considered wearing ear plugs when playing, but I’ve never done it. In the last 10 years or so, I’ve made a habit out of playing fairly quietly unless volume is needed (some gigs and sessions). And I’m thinking of having my hearing tested in the near future.

Re: Hearing loss

Virtually everyone gets some kind of hearing loss as they get older - I know I’m getting it. Often, as in my case, it’s no more than a loss of sensitivity to the higher frequencies (higher than 10KHz, say), which doesn’t, or shouldn’t, affect enjoyment and playing of music, but can sometimes cause problems in conversation. There are however one or two advantages - you can ignore the advertising about audio systems having frequency responses up to 20KHz because you (and everyone else except bats and dogs) will never be able to hear that high, and it’s background noise and hiss anyway. To put it in perspective the top A on the piano is less than 4KHz. Go two octaves above that and you’re well in the range of hiss and noise.

I’ve known some elderly musicians who have gone quite deaf and wear hearing aids, but are able to continue playing in orchestras. Here again is another advantage - you can turn off the hearing aid if you don’t want to hear the conductor’s comments :).

Tinnitus can be the biggest problem, but not necessarily so; and there are treatments to alleviate some types.

The most important things for a musician are rhythm, timing and intonation, and these are unlikely to affected by all but the most extreme forms of deafness. Even absolute deafness needn’t be a bar - witness Evelyn Glennie, one of the world’s great percussionist players. I heard once about a viola player in a professional symphony orchestra who completely lost her hearing in her teens from an infection but was able to continue her studies to a professional level. Apparently, she is able to “hear” the notes she’s playing from the vibration of the instrument and bow, as well as getting visual cues from the orchestra.

Trevor

Re: Hearing loss

If you think about it, the sound level of session music in a pub bears no comparison with that of a large symphony orchestra on a roll. On Monday I was at a pub session with about 15-20 musicians. At one stage in the evening I went to the other end of the pub, about 40 feet away, where the bar was fairly clear to get another drink. The sound of those 15 or so players was hardly above the level of general conversation around me. I stepped outside for a minute to start the drink, and the music was virtually inaudible.

Many players in professional symphony orchestras, who are playing several hours a day in rehearsal and concerts for 6 days a week most of the year, do end up with hearing problems of greater than normal severity. The viola players tend to be most at risk because they are sitting in front of the heavy brass section. In fact, some orchestras are now placing acoustics screens between the viola section and the brass. Over the years, symphony orchestras have been getting louder because that is what the public want, fuelled perhaps by the unnatural sound levels of film music in moderna cinemas, and the general increased noisiness of modern living. So orchestral string players are encouraged to play louder, doubtless having their instruments set up to attain this end, and the brass instruments now have larger bores to increase the sound.

This modern orchestral sound level is one of the two reasons why I no longer play in a large symphony orchestra and therefore restrict my classical playing to a chamber string orchestra and a chamber symphony orchestra (of 18c dimensions). The other reason, of course, is to give myself more time for ITM.

Regarding audio systems, the best one available is free and is within your brain, if you take the trouble to train it.

Re: Hearing loss

Beware of hearing tests in the UK.
The standard test consists of listening through headphones to beeps of different volume and frequency, and you are required to press a little button if you hear the sound.

The doctor then makes an assessment and bases your hearing acuity on the results of this simple test. The results are often inaccurate.

Why?

The hearing test as described does not reflect the type of sounds you hear in real life, and you rarely hear sounds that close to your ear (ie through headphones). The main problem that people with hearing loss have is understanding speech. The test does not use speech at all.

I’ve been through all this, and the test results clinically indicate that my hearing loss is not enough to justify a hearing aid, even though I sometimes have trouble with speech, if the speaker quiet and unclear. I don’t have any problem with music, except maybe hearing it at a slightly reduced volume.

I don’t know what audiology tests are like in the U.S. or anywhere else, but they are certainly far too basic in the U.K. Out of the plethora of variety of sound, they choose a beep? Whats going on?

Any audiologists out there?

Re: Hearing loss

>>“I don’t know what audiology tests are like in the U.S. or anywhere else, but they are certainly far too basic in the U.K. Out of the plethora of variety of sound, they choose a beep? Whats going on?”

The beep is the simplest sound, a single frequency, so by testing you with beeps at different frequencies they can plot how your hearing responds across the spectrum, which in turn tells you how it will respond to all the more complex sounds in real life. So it is actually the most accurate way of assessing your hearing, if they used a more complex sound how would they tell if you were hearing the whole sound or just some frequencies?

CMO, yes the high frequencies are the most easily damaged, if you look at the Flecher Munson equal loudness curves you see that for two sounds to seem to be the same loudness a high frequency sound needs to be much lower sound pressure than a low frequency one. And the more sensitive the more easily damaged - if you look at those curves there is a distinct dip at 3.5 - 4KHz - that’s where the sibilants that make speech intelligible live, so we’ve evolved to be more sensitive to those frequencies. The first sign of hearing loss is very often a loss around 3.5-4KHz. But the frequency of sound causeing the damage doesn’t matter, sound at any frequency hits and affects all the hairs in the cochlea, not just the ones that respond to it.

People have also mentioned single sided hearing loss. In most of the world loss of hearing on the left (which people here are attributing to fiddling) is most common, but in Britain, Australia, Japan and India loss on the right is more common. Why? Because we drive on the left and a major contributor to single sided hearing loss is driving with the window open.
