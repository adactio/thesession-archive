I’m breaking out in warts; lots of warts

Our mate has been playing sessions with the very fast players. Good news ~ Bad news.
He comes to play with us & it is like he’s an Irish dance-tunes juke box. Plenty of speed, which is crackworthy. Good new is, I’m doing calisthenics & getting into tip-top shape, there’s plenty of tunes, & my session mates are giddy. Best news is when all this energy creates good sounding tunes & it *is* dance music.
It’s not all good news, though. With so many tunes, at such a high velocity, mistakes are made. A few is O.K. Actually I like the fact he’s not super human. But, what’s a few? When is it tunes & when is it a cheese grater on your ears? I crave melody. Not saying it has to be perfect, god no!
Here is the question for discussion, “How far do you push a tune when you’re leading off?”

“”How far do you push a tune when you’re leading off?“”

However far the person starting it wants to go. Anyone interested or capable will follow suit and join in… others might sit it out if it’s too fast or they prefer to play slower. When other people start a tune at a slower pace--that should be respected and not expected to take the same fast pace. If the people having the session are all playing at a fast pace with every tune then you might want to just listen instead. There are many factors to consider before determining how to respond. It’s all good.

Our session varies the pace a fair amount, letting the tunes and mood suggest the tempo. It’s nice to mix things up so that fast sets sound up tempo, not just another set at the “standard” pace.

Re: I’m breaking out in warts; lots of warts

Occurs to me that the blanket answer to Ben’s OP question is that it’s rude and not very constructive to launch a tune too fast for anyone else in the circle to cleanly keep up.

On the other hand, if a session has been crawling along for a few years, not quite reaching the higher energy pace of typical dance speeds (say, 108 to 122 bpm), then it’s not a bad thing for a more seasoned player to drag them into the next gear. Upping your game make take a few weeks or months, and the process may be sloppy at first, but growing comfortable playing at dance tempos is a *good* thing.

Having played a fair amount of bluegrass in my younger years puts most dance tempos in perspective. Bust out a breakdown at 160 bpm and nothing under 130 bpm (which would be high for most any set or step dancer) feels all that fast anymore. You can learn to make the music breathe and lift at 126 bpm, say.

Re: I’m breaking out in warts; lots of warts

Jack, if I understand you, you’re saying that good players shouldn’t necessarily be constrained by the limits of the other musicians present.

I can see that. Sure, it’s nice to cut loose once in a while. And it’s wonderful to hear a great player go for it without a lot of extraneous noise. But how well it goes over would depend on the situation of the session at hand, and how frequently the top dog revs things up. Maybe it’s more of a round robin, where everyone takes a turn. (Not my cuppa tea.) Or a couple or three good players occasionally toss in a set above the other’s heads. Absolutely. Folks can learn a lot from listening, or just kick back and enjoy hearing the music played at a high level.

Too much of that, though, can derail the session for others. When those “others” include regulars at that session, yer headed for frustration and trouble. Once or twice during the evening would be all right, but to me, repeatedly playing above *everyone* else smells of showboating, and that’s rude (unless it’s your paid gig and everyone else is just a hanger-on). I think it’s more sociable and polite to try to include others. Particularly if the other players know the tunes, but just can’t match the pace of the lone ranger(s).

Similarly, when we play a tune no one else knows, it may not be required, but it seems like common courtesy to follow it with a more widely played tune in hopes of drawing participation from others. Or at least end the set with something the present company is likely to know and join in on. Any seasoned player will likely have dozens of apt tunes to choose from. Or, at the very least, let other people start the next 30 minutes of tunes and play along at their pace.

Which all speaks to the type of sessions I most enjoy--toward the inclusive end of the spectrum, making room for different levels of ability and experience, while also creating space for high caliber playing. I don’t think it has to be an either/or dilemma.

If you’re *too* open, friendly, and democratic, every beginner who is desperate to play all night and djembe player for miles might appear and your session risks becoming one of those free-for-all percussion ensemble things. Some people enjoy those and if that’s what a particular session is intended to be, cool, but I won’t go to that.

There are sessions which have successfully set those boundaries. They feel open, friendly, relaxing, but the musical standard is still pretty high. I like those sessions.

My other pet peeve: Visitors who plonk themselves down at your session, without any introduction, any acknowledgment of the regulars, and proceed to play loads of sets. I really wish people wouldn’t do this. When I go to a strange session, especially one where I don’t know anyone there, I sit back for a while, assessing the types of tunes being played, the general level of playing, the whole social dynamic of it, and usually start a set when asked.

Re: I’m breaking out in warts; lots of warts

This has been an interesting discussion, unlike a lot of the prior “speed kills” type of threads.

A couple of different things are being discussed here, including speed for the sake of speed, etiquette in whether or not to try to always include players who can’t handle the speed, people trying to play faster than they can, starting sets with warts, and lack of variety in a session. All good things to think about!

I will say that I don’t automatically think of “speed for the sake of speed” as being a negative thing. The music takes on some different qualities when it is played fast. The twists and turns in the melody start providing the musical “surprise”, which is one of the things that makes this music interesting to us. I liken it the difference between a nice train ride through France, and riding a rollercoaster. Each has its purpose, but rarely does the French train make you burst out in spontaneous laughter, whooping and hollering because it was so much fun. At speed, the twists and turns become more abrupt, or violent. You may have ridden the rollercoaster a 100 times, but you still get a thrill from the ride. To me, fast tunes are like that.

I agree with Will’s statement that the music should feel effortless, but to me, it shouldn’t necessarily *sound* effortless. I like sets that have momentum, where it feels like you’re speeding up the whole time, even though the tempo is staying steady.

But… I sat in a session in Trim a few years ago with some fabulous players. It was maybe the best music I have ever been part of. But it was also hard-driving tunes all night, without much variety in the tempo, or tune types. So it did wear on me after a while.

My sessions vary in tempo and tune types quite a bit. A session that I “anchor” has a wide range of playing ability amongst the people who attend, and we do encourage people to start sets, so we get a wide range of tempos just from that. But I also believe that it’s good to play stuff faster than some of the people can handle. For one thing, you give them something to shoot for, and you can also “drag them along” if you’re not playing too far out of their range. For me, that was a really key element in my progression as a player, and I got better by being “dragged along”.

And pretty much everybody starts sets with “warts” from time to time. Unless you’re really used to it, playing by yourself in front of other players can be difficult, and a lot of players (myself included from time to time) really don’t feel comfortable until other people join in, and then you can relax. What happens sometimes, though, is that players end up starting sets slower than they really wanted, because of that discomfort. So then you either play the set slower than you wanted, or end up speeding it up. Neither of which is particularly good. So the other option is to start it fast, with warts, and then let it settle into a comfortable, good set once everyone has joined. But it’s not a performance (Sorry Jack), so the warts are just part of it, and don’t really matter much.
