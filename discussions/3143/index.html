Where’s the joy?

I’ve been browsing through the forums here for a while now, but after a point I really must ask that which is the topic. From your descriptions of circle etiquette in this country (USA), and from things I’ve read about sessions out here on the west coast, it seems like the whole concept of a session is a closed circle playing the latest and greatest reel, slip jig, etc. - With no more than one bodhran, heaven forbid any bones. People shouldn’t dare step out of line to play along with a song they don’t know, nor become so enthralled in the music that they lose track of the metronome in their heads?

Where’s the joy in playing? Where’s the joy in music? I can understand exactly how annoying ‘outside’ musicians can be, how intrusive and how frustrating when they do stupid things. Yet it happens inside the circle, too. From these threads, I’m to the point of thinking that everyone involved in ITM is so frustrated with everyone else as they’re willing to excise -just about- everyone else to find the groove with the one or two players who are of the same mind.

But then, isn’t that just a band by another name? Should a ‘session’ have a rulebook, steering committee, and sheet music?

Now that I’ve got your collective attention, let me back off a tad; Most of the ‘sessions’ I’ve been to have been very informal, with players of common interest and great musical depth getting together to play music as they might. Whether they were semi-organized, occurring on a set night or two during the week, or whether they were spontaneous, erupting around a campfire or anywhere else, they were very fluid.

Now it’s true, I’ve heard far more bad music, both Irish and otherwise, than good-- And it’s true that I’ve sat out a great deal of time rather than trying to play along with a song I didn’t know, or even wasn’t interested in playing.

But when I look for some place to play in the SF bay area, and can only find ‘sessions’ or ‘slow plays’ that have page after page of rules, I have to wonder how much ego is involved here, and how much music. It’s hard enough to find something outside of San Francisco and Oakland proper, and even then it all looks far too organized for me. I’m just here for the music, not a popularity contest or anything of that nature.

It’s been so long for me that I’m willing to put up with all the ‘rules’ and ‘etiquette’ just to have someone to play with once more, but I would love for someone to tell me that that the impressions I’ve drawn from this board are wrong, and that not all US sessions are comprised of snobs. One thread was entitled “Who owns the session”; I read it and don’t understand; Don’t the musicians?

Any rate, I apologise if I’ve offended anyone (Or everyone! :> … I’m an equal opportunity offender ;>)- It was not my intent. Take my words at face value and understand that I really do just want to know that there are players out there who are there for the music and not for the social games of the human-animal.

Just a whistler,
and a dragon,
-Kysh

Kysh, perhaps you’re skimming over the joy in the majority of comments here. Sometimes we get to slagging each other, and taken out of context or taken seriously at all, it would come across as harsh, baiting, and hard nosed. Most of that is actually meant in fun, to be laughed off. Some of us have been posting here for three years or more, and have an extensive common backhistory.

Another possible explanation for what you read here is more serious overall. This site is different from a real session in that we don’t actually play any tunes together. Which leaves lots of time to talk, analyze, disagree, digress, etc. It’s also something of a ‘safe’ place to commiserate over the problems we encounter in 3-D Land. So someone who rags on and on in these threads about spastic bones players and bombastic piano accordions and two-hour off-key sean nos songs probably isn’t as cynical and elitist in real life (though I’d hazard a guess that a few of our members are actually more tolerant here than down at their local--and you know who you are :o).

In other words, the confines of communicating online tend to skew the conversation toward nit picking. And stories session struggles are by nature more interesting than stories ad nauseum about session bliss.

You might also note that some of the uglier threads feature only a handful of commenters--the rest of us stepped over it like a mud puddle in the road.

Finally, there is no small shred of truth in your criticisms. I keep a session running here in Montana, and so far we’ve avoided posting rules, and it’s been years since we used the ‘hanging tree’ (I’m kidding!). But there have been times when I had to weigh in on the side of preserving some sense of what an Irish music session is. Or at least what the core players wanted our session to be.

In the clearest example, we had to ask bluegrass boom-chuck guitarists to consider coming to the monthly slow session to learn more appropriate backup (only after 5 guitarists showed up one night and all played--different chords--at the same time, over every tune). And we pointed out that the bluegrass jam was across the street on another night of the week (run by someone who also regularly plays at our Irish session). Problem solved.

In other words, for those of us who want to play Irish trad music, in a session setting (as opposed to a band or a world music free-for-all), sometimes we have to ask newcomers to listen and learn, to bring some respect to the session. No less than you’d ask of someone new, shouting in English, barging into your weekly pub ‘conversational gaeilge’ chat group. I don’t think that makes me an ‘enforcer.’ More of a caretaker, really, an advocate for something special that deserves a safe place in a sometimes hostile world.

To me, the fact that this stuff comes up as often as it does on this site suggests not that us trad players are intolerant music bigots and machiavellian rule mongers, but that the problems of musical assualt by the incompetent or malicious are fairly widespread. Do a search for ‘river rocks’ or ‘tambourine’ and you’ll find several funny examples.

Maybe you’ve never had the experience of enjoying a session, and then witnessing someone come along who takes away the joy by imposing themselves on the session. The person who imposes them self isn’t doing it consciously necessarily, and they usually have the best intentions. It’s much harder for people who are new to ITM and sessions to understand this. I am as guilty as anyone else when I first started out. In Ireland people are for the most part taught about sessions by having a good example, but the further you are removed from Ireland -- your example will often be like the blind leading the blind. People elsewhere will become experts at what a session supposedly is, but it will be based on a local example that might have been invented by the locals. That doesn’t mean it’s not valid; it’s perfectly acceptable for that specific group of folks, but that model can’t be implied where ever they happen to go.

Ireland has deeper roots with the source of such a social phenomena as a session, and they’ve had a lot more time to develop a style that is truly Irish. Some of us that have been playing ITM for quite some time, and have made visits to Ireland, are inspired by what we saw there in regards to sessions. We try to import a little of it to our local sessions and we’re usually met with resistance and suspicion. So I guess what I’m trying to say to you Kysh, is that “joy” is in the eye of the beholder. If a newbie comes to our session, and they’re polite and aren’t trying to learn their instrument while we’re trying to play a few tunes -- we always ask them to suggest a tune or two. We let them set the pace, and we join in and keep the pace they set. When we ask them what tune they’d like to play, we ask if they have any tunes they would like to follow it with. This way we help them make the change, but the whole time we keep their pace. They seem quite happy with this approach and return from time to time as well.

There are many ways to include newbies to the session without spoiling the fun for everyone else; Trevor’s example is another really good one. You could always send the newbie off to the “slow session” but they might be more interested in the craic of hanging out in a regular session more.
