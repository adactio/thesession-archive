Re: Can u make a living playing Trad?

There are common misconceptions about making money in the world of Irish music. I think the overall misconception is that occasional big money is enough to sustain you; it’s not. The people I know who really make a living off music do it by teaching lessons and workshops all week long, on a pretty solid schedule, not by performing alone. It’s not unlike craftsmen like concertina makers: although a decent concertina will cost $3,000 or something, the makers actually pull in most of their business doing repairs, tuning, and tinkering, since they can only produce new instruments so quickly. Long story short: it’s work. Hard work. Long hours. Not a lot of pay. Work work.

With great difficulty, most very successful bands need a singer, instrumental dominant bands are all about the arrangement of the tunes, it has to sound like more than tunes in a pub, accompaniment and production are what sells the music to more than just musicians, ask Andy Irvine about Mosaik and that’s the answer you’d get, he felt only musicians appreciated it.
If you’re a tune player, how do you balance playing tunes for fun with knowing that you need to be paid well each time so that you don’t cheapen yourself by playing for free?
At the fleadh, a 60 odd year old guitar player called Gerry was lamenting how difficult it is and how hard nosed you need to be to make sure you don’t get short changed.

Even as a fiddle player of 9 months, my mentality is that I can’t provide something to a pub for absolutely nothing, even one free pint or some food or a cheaper price for musicians or playing to support a friend’s gig makes all the difference - if I think I’m benefitting a pub and not getting the acknowledgement from the bar I hesitate to go back. So I can’t imagine what it’s like for someone playing fiddle 9 years! Same when I sing a song, if I don’t get what I want out of it, I’m not bothered singing again - at the very least, applause, preferably somebody verbally telling me they liked how I sang it or asking for another song or a free pint from the bar or something, I won’t “perform” in any context unless I get something for it.

Re: Can u make a living playing Trad?

Short answer: yes.

Long answer: yes, but it’s hard. And can sometimes get boring (but hopefully, if you’re with the right people, this will happen once in a blue moon).

Longer answer (in question form): Can you play music even when you’re feeling sick, tired, or when you just want to hang out at a venue without playing? Like Danjo and others have said, making a living playing trad, only trad, is hard feckin work. Unless you’re god’s gift to whatever instrument you play (and not just in your own head, haha) you’ll need to be extremely conscious of your finances, work well with others, be proactive about getting gigs and practicing yourself, etc. Actually, even if you ARE god’s gift to your instrument, you can sabotage yourself with poor conduct or a lack of the aforementioned skills. Being humble helps, too. Sometimes you have to work your way into peoples’ spheres by playing for pennies or by volunteering--if you’re good and pleasant people will remember you and start asking about you. Psychology, man…

Expect poor pay for awhile, small venues, unappreciative audiences, all that jazz. If you can manage to make a decent living solely through musical means then fair play to you, because the rewards of sharing music with others, traveling, and improving yourself musically are totally worth it if you’ve worked hard enough and the fates aligned.

Like any job, there’s a great amount of blood, sweat, tears, and luck involved. The people I know who’ve succeeded have a good head about them and are extremely passionate about what they do. Hurray!

Re: Can u make a living playing Trad?

Had to come out of hiding for this one, because I did try to do exactly that. I wanted to make a living playing trad (despite being seriously underqualified) or at least “something like it.” And there’s the rub.

The market for Irish trad is very, very small. If you can impress a large percentage of those sophisticated listeners, and are willing to travel a lot, you might make a living.

But if you are anything less than a god of trad (and very few us are) you have to add a bunch of extra stuff to make it appeal to a wider audience, because serious trad fans will not be interested. And then it’s not really trad anymore, is it?

So I guess my answer is, “Yes, for the very lucky and incredibly talented few.” Just like all the other musical genres, only more so.

I think the main issues are summarized nicely above.

As a young fellow I came under a bit of pressure to go full time from the guy I duetted with. We had a great circuit that supplemented my income nicely and I loved the playing opportunity that came with it. For my pal, he had other gigs but essentially my supplement was equal, more or less, to his total income. We did a mix of STM ITM and other related trad etc depending where we were.

Then a rash of places started asking for ITM. My pal was very well placed to oblige and me, being his regular sideman, I was more than happy to play. Eventually this work load reached critical mass and decision time loomed large for me. I was late 20’s had a mortgage, and a well paying job. But I was bored and gagging to play at every opportunity. I thought I’ll give it a few months.

So with the words of my one time semi pro father ringing in my ear: “don’t give up the day job”, I set off on a 2 week tour as a “full time” player with no other commitments. By the end of it I was convinced that it was the wrong move for me. The right move for my pal but the wrong one for me. My decision was one of economics. Due to the fact that I realised I wasn’t enough of a player for it to be viable, so I had to be realistic and honest with myself. By that time I’d met several players who did earn a living from music. The thing that marked them out from the rest was the fact that they had other skills which meant that the days they didn’t gig they would teach or compose on commission perhaps tour with shows etc, in short a musical day job to fill the diary blanks. These were skill sets I didn’t have, well not on tap. I needed an alternative day job which used the work skills I did have.

It was the first reasoned adult decision that I’d genuinely thought through. It killed me to admit it to myself but I had to keep the roof over my head, bottom line. That decision put a lot of strain on the relationship with my pal at the time, but it was the right one for me, 15 or 16 years later we’re still the best of pals and I’m sat typing in the comfort of my own home.

I think if your a young player or otherwise without commitments I’d say give it a go but keep in mind; playing alone isn’t enough to provide you with a reasonable living. The kind of living that can keep house and pay for the inevitable family type stuff, you need something else to pay for that. But without commitments it’s a different proposition and if you can get a few years non stop playing under your belt before the eventual life pressures get in the way, why not. My pal grew into a quality player, even though he saw sense and settled down to start a family, he wouldn’t have reached the standard he did playing a regular session each week and practicing in his bed room. So if you’ve the passion plus the ability to keep it going get out playing and use the money you’ll earn to fund your playing, as thats all it’ll do: fund the lifestyle, period (provided you can keep off the deoch and persian rugs).

My girlfriend earns a living from STM music. I don’t need to break her income down to know that it’s by a “country mile” that her non performance work is what makes her living. She doesn’t make that much directly from playing gigs rather it’s her very well honed related skills that provide the secure base that enables her to go out and perform.

Re: Can u make a living playing Trad?

Kess’s question should probably have been ‘Can you make a living playing traditional music in Ireland?’ since that’s where s/he is from.

The answer is most definitely “No, you can’t” unless you happen to fulfil at least one of the following categories:

1) you’re a member of The Chieftains;

2) you hit it big in the 1960s/1970s/1980s/maybe even right up to 1995 and have an extensive back catalogue of recordings which accrue a decent annual sum in royalties;

3) you’re touring with one of the numerous ‘Riverdance’ or ‘Lord of the Dance’ troupes - but that’s not exactly playing traditional music all the time;

4) you’re regularly called upon by a TV company (RTÉ, TG4, BBC Ulster, the company behind ‘The Transatlantic Sessions’, etc.) to participate in or front a show (and that’s only going to happen if you’ve already made your name);

5) you have an extensive touring schedule (solo, or in duo, trio or band format) backed up by a new CD to hawk at gigs (and that’s only going to happen if …. see 4) above;

6) you’re prepared to play every paid session under the sun (and be one of those remunerated) and attend every festival going and be paid to teach at each (and those’ll only cover you for a few months);

7) you get hired to play a residency in a European pub, probably in The Netherlands or Germany;

8) you have an agent who books you for lucrative overseas tours;

or, lastly,

9) you’re in demand for lucrative studio session work.

Other than those who fit into my first category I can only think of a few musicians in Ireland who make their money entirely from traditional music. One of those is an uilleann piper from Kess’s own city, but he has a reputation built up over almost thirty years and a full-time gigging/touring schedule which might drive others to the wall.

So, in conclusion, I’m sorry to offer you negativity, but if you’re patient and wait for the next ‘Titanic’, ‘Riverdance’ or ‘The Quiet Man’ you might be in luck.

who me? I can play music for its own sake at home, or in my home with friends, or virtually anywhere that doesn’t make somebody else money. For right now, music earns me money, even if it’s spare change - sometimes I used that to buy pints at my favourite sessions so music is a means to being able to enjoy more music. But when I start working, I will still be mindful of not allowing musicians to be exploited, I understand the value of a good session to a pub and have been involved in such a session that dramatically boosted business for a pub - if they get paid the musicians should too. If the musicians get paid and I visit their session regularly and add something they need to the session then I expect they’d reciprocate some of their earnings towards at least a pint. If I visit a session and sing a song, unsure of how it’ll go down, of course I want to know that it was appreciated, otherwise I naturally have mixed feelings about singing another one, maybe they don’t like songs, maybe they don’t like that type of song etc
