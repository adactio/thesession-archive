Identity

What does Identity mean to a tune?

Are we simply asking for the tune’s name?
Or is a tune’s identity merely its notes, performed aurally by some great player?
Or is a tune’s identity as complete a history of its origins and outings that you can compile?

I’m interested in the fluidity of it. The subjectivity and the objectivity. The confusion, the cousins, the identical twins, the distant relatives, the in-laws: The blood line and the mere association.

I’m interested in how easily they can be recognised, and yet how impossible they are to define.

Re: Identity

All identity, whether of persons or things, is a conventional imputation; everything, whether person, thing, idea or cultural artefact, is empty of any identifiable, permanent essence. (Madhyamaka 101, one might say.)
A tune is a particularly clear example, although the same applies to things like France, me, love, or a Boeing 747. The boundaries between what we might agree, albeit tentatively, to call a “basic tune”, its ornamentation, variations on the tune, different settings, different versions, related but different tunes, vaguely similar tunes and so on: these boundaries are useful for discussion, but always to some extent arbitrary.
In short, a tune, like *everything and anything* else, has no true identity. It is empty.

Re: Identity ( or tune dialectics)

Of course many tunes have one name and were composed by such & such and might be named after a place, friend, or what they had for breakfast, but some tunes have a more colorful history that can be interesting to anyone who cares.

Take the Gneevgullia reel for example. The first two parts of this tune are actually a tune called, “The Pride of Rathmore” and the third part is the A-part of another tune called, “The Girls of Farranfore.” The way I heard it was that these tunes used to be a common medley played in a certain region, but at some point the second part of the “The Girls of Farranfore” was forgotten and the A-part of that tune became the third part of a new 3-part tune given the name, “Gneevgullia.” This is one of the few anecdotes I can offer when this tune comes up that people will actually take an interest in. I think it’s clearly part of the tune’s dialectics or identity.

Re: Identity

Ted Hughes’s use of “And” to start a sentence is a continuation of a literary usage of venerable antiquity. If you look at the King James Bible you will see a large number of sentences starting with “And”. If you go back a millennium and a bit to St Jerome’s Latin translation (which is an important basis for most succeeding translations of the Bible down the centuries) you will see an equivalent large number of sentences starting with “Et” (Latin for “and”). Jerome was translating literally the Hebrew and Greek of the original documents.

It was common in written forms of those ancient languages to start sentences with a conjunction or similar marker to indicate the start of a new train of thought, or to highlight a contrast. It is significant in this respect that punctuation was unknown, as were spaces between words.

In Classical Greek, in particular, there is a whole series of little words, known as “particles”, which are used to combine sentences and clauses and partly to give emphasis to certain expression. The rules governing their usage are complex and have continued to puzzle students of the language for centuries.

Essentially, the particles in Greek, and similar constructions in other ancient languages, were the written equivalent of the hand-waving and changes in tone of voice you get in conversation to convey subtle (and not so subtle) shades of meaning and emphasis.

Re: Identity

Right, let’s get on to the business of ending sentences with prepositions, and split infinitives while we’re about it.
For many hundreds of years up to the mid-19th century Latin was the second language for most educated people throughout Britain and Europe. In particular, Latin had a pervasive influence on written English from the 16th century onwards. With some English writers it wouldn’t be at all difficult to translate their prose into Latin, such is the close correspondence between their English and the Latin of a classical author. It is hardly surprising, therefore, that some grammatical pedagogues decided that some parts of English grammar should follow the Latin example. Two obvious ones are,
1) The split infinitive. The principal infinitive of a Latin verb is a single word (e.g. “ludere” = “to play”), so cannot be split. The English pedagogue therefore says that an English infinitive should not be split, and so would not allow “to boldly go” - a famous phrase which would completely lose its impact if the “rules” were followed.
2) Ending a sentence with a preposition. Again, in Latin, a preposition always immediately precedes its noun, and in Latin prose the verb is almost always at the end of a clause or sentence. Two inapplicable reasons why the English pedagogues tried to squeeze the Teutonic-based grammar of English into the strait-jacket of Latin grammar.

Re: Identity

My take on the above, not very conclusive I admit:

The Greeks asked, “what is it to be a man?” Human existence itself
remained a cause of wonder. Writers came up with some answers, as to how a man should behave to others, organise himself in cities, avoid the wrath of the gods, face death in battle; etc. The Greeks were gifted with an extraordinary poetic imagination, and also kicked off Western history, philosophy, mathematics, art (sometimes starting from elements of other cultures). Those in the Classical cities (men, that is) grew up and exercised together, taking in their culture while knowing that any time they might have to fight some other state. But this - and war experience - gave them a keen edge when they took part in political debate.

The Romans asked, “How am I going to survive and keep my dignity in this society?” By the time Roman literature really kicked off, Rome was already a megalopolis, whose indefinite continuation could be taken for granted. The ordinary citizen could not influence it; prominent men close to a capricious Emperor could be bumped off if they said the wrong things. So the private sphere was a valued retreat.
The surprising thing about ancient Rome was how long it lasted with so many ghastly people at the centre. Its administrative etc. institutions were very durable. The soldiers, civil servants and farmers (let alone slaves) don’t get much of a look-in, in the documents, compared with the metropolitans; but a strong sense of duty and public-spiritedness seems to have existed in the outer bits of the Empire.

I haven’t read the works of the Founding Fathers of the USA, but imagine that of all the countries in the world the US has been free-est to cherry-pick from Classical precedents in devising its constitution and government. However, the Greek city-state - an autonomous small town - isn’t really in the running as a model today.

Re: Identity

A lot of the same things could be said about a joke. One time it’s a banjo player, another time it’s a bodhran player, then it’s a blonde or a bass player, but it’s the same joke because the punch line is the same. Or maybe the punch line is a little different but the same idea, sort of. But then when I tell it, nobody laughs. Is it still the same joke then?

It’s interesting to think about this stuff, but hard to talk about it. So okay, Pete, I’ll see your “ephemeral” and raise you “ineffable”.

But, wait. There are legal consultants who specialize in musical copyright cases and they seem to have some kind of semi-quantitative way of comparing two tunes and concluding that they are, or are not, the same. Or same enough to warrant legal action. I wonder how many of us would agree with their algorithm.
