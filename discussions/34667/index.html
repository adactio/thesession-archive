Re: What does ~ mean?

In math, when it appears before a number, it means ‘approximately.’ Which is similar to what it means on an Irish tune--things may vary somewhat. It basically means that, since Irish musicians are impatient, and get bored when holding longish notes, that they are going to throw in something twiddley. It might be a roll from a fiddler, or a triplet from a banjo player, or a swoop up into the note from a flute player, but in dance tunes, rarely is a note longer than a quarter note played without some making and mending being involved.

Re: What does ~ mean?

The ABC standard v2.1 says that the ~ character indicates an “Irish roll”

Converters that use abcm2ps (i.e. most of them) represent:
~ (“Irish roll”) as a tilde
!roll! (“a roll mark (arc) as used in Irish music”) as semi-circular arc with the open end at the bottom
!turn! (“a turn mark (also known as gruppetto)”) as a standard turn mark.
!mordent! (“same as !lowermordent!”)
!lowermordent! (“short /|/|/ squiggle with a vertical line through it”) as er, well, a squiggle with a vertical line through it.

I can’t be bothered just now to check what MIDI converters do, but they probably all sound horrible.

Looking through, Breatnach ditched both the tilde and his funny little loop symbol in CRE2 and instead wrote out the notes to be played. I don’t find that particularly helpful to be honest …

… and maybe Breatnach didn’t either, because by CRE3 the loop is back, he is now using mordent signs in some of the places where he used a tilde in CRE1 and he’s combining that with writing out the notes in some other places.

By CRE4 the loop is still there, but both the mordent sign and the tilde seem to be absent and there is much less notation of ornamentation altogether.

CRE5 has similar ornamentation to that of CRE4. It is worth noting that CRE4 and CRE5 are not directly the work of Breatnach himself, but are posthumous volumes drawn together by Jackie Small from Breatnach’s record cards on which he kept notations of tunes.

Frankly, I find the best notation of all of the CRE books - at least for me - is that in CRE1, which appears to be a facsimile of very well-written out, handwritten staff notation. Re: What does ~ mean?

Yes in mainstream music a symbol that’s not on my keyboard indicates a ‘turn’. I suppose a tilde “ ~ ” sort of looks like it, but not really.

People, when notating Irish music, have long used the turn symbol to indicate a roll, though of course a roll, though it might or might not have the same notes as a turn, is timed differently. Just a symbol of convenience, really. BTW in the Baroque period a turn was not necessarily timed evenly, and could sound a bit more ‘Irish’.

Breandan Breathnach’s symbol was the symbol called a “tie” in mainstream music.

The turn symbol and the tie symbol perform the same function, letting an Irish traditional musician know that the associated note can become a roll or any other idiomatic thing that can traditionally occupy the same position (play a triplet, take a breath, etc).

“I’ve just spent some time Googling the tilde in music notation. For anyone interested, I suggest you do the same. There is no uniformity.”
Ben Hall is correct here, and it’s not restricted to the tilde. The history of the nototation of musical ornaments is as thorny as a Schoenberg string quartet- the symbols varied from region to region, decade to decade, and in some cases from composer to composer. Several of Rameau’s harpsichord music collections were published with ornament charts not unlike Breandan Breatnach’s. Other composers were not so thoughtful. As you might expect, the notation becames somewhat more standardized over time,and of course composers started writing out ornaments more and more-until the 20th Century, when composers started creating their own “special effects” employing unorthodox techniques for which they need(ed) to create their own notation.
As for the ~ in ABC in my experience it usually is used to indicate a roll-with the understanding that in Irish trad the specifics of the ornamentaion are up for grabs-so, “do something here, maybe.” Or, as Sebastian said,“it doesn’t make much sense to transcribe explicit ornamentation (here you have to play a cran, here you have to play a triplet etc.) anyway. It seems a good question to ask would be this: “What would be an *incorrect* execution of the ”~“ in trad music?” If in ABC it indicates “do something here,” as @Ben said much earlier, is there any “something” that would not fit? One could then identify the range of motion, so to speak, of the indication.

I also think one could argue that the ~ is a bit androgynous between the mordent and the turn—even depending on font, egad. Above all, though, if one posits a *relationship* between classical music’s and trad’s use that symbol, it’s still true that the function of such ornaments in classical music is not only widely variable but also has a less variable aspect, which is a particular syntactical relationship to harmonic conventions. I’m new to trad and old to classical (!), but everything I have inhaled about the latter suggests that the “something” in trad does not have the same function as in classical music. (In fact, I do not see how they could, given the way harmonies function in trad—and indeed, curlicues and appoggiaturas are something I miss in trad, as much as I love it, and as brilliant as the nuances are on their own terms.)

I have been very impressed with Grey Larsen’s book (from what I can evaluate), and he emphasizes the distinction between ornamentation and articulation. It sounds right to me—but I am eager to hear more or otherwise—that, as he says, many of these figures are not embellishments so much as articulations—indispensable to the identity of the music (even if they vary). Whereas classical ornaments are, relatively, more ornamental. I’m intrigued at how Larsen’s point of view syncs up with the way nuances are configured and valued in *honkyoku* (solo shakuhachi music), where it is even more strongly emphasized that the elaborations are **not** ornamental but are articulation (the tongue is not used) and, again, part of the identity of the music.

In a way (many ways), it does not have to matter if the trad symbol is what @Ben calls “an approximation”—though that does imply a genetic link rather than coincidence or even emulation. Thing is, with either/any, trill or mordent, the way they would be used, like I said much earlier, has extensive variation as well as intricate syntactical effect. Even something as simple as this: when the classical “early music” boom arose in the 70s, there was increased recognition that a trill in the Baroque would have been started on the *top* note. That’s a hugely important different within the syntax of classical music.

Am I right to guess that while the ornaments/nuances in trad are equally intricate (and quite virtuosic), with certain established conventions, there would not be, say the expectation of a certain contour at, say, the penultimate chord/measure of a section of a tune to enhance the resolution effected by the perfect authentic cadence? If the established options and individual variations work as I suspect and have been told, then any similarity would be only very superficial, and graphical resemblance would not necessarily suggest syntactical or causal relationship. Sorry for the unsophisticated language. ;-)

Does anyone have more information on that more aesthetic/performance practice-related aspect?

Wikipedia: Ornament (music) See the actual listing in Wikipedia for additional examples of musical ornaments and examples of how they are to be played.

Turn or “Gruppetto”

A turn is a short figure consisting of the note above the one indicated, the note itself, the note below the one indicated, and the note itself again. It is marked by a mirrored S-shape lying on its side above the staff.
The details of its execution depend partly on the exact placement of the turn mark.

The exact speed at which the notes of a turn are executed can vary, as can its rhythm. The question of how a turn is best executed is largely one of context, convention, and taste. The lower and upper added notes may or may not be chromatically raised.

An inverted turn (the note below the one indicated, the note itself, the note above it, and the note itself again) is usually indicated by putting a short vertical line through the normal turn sign, though sometimes the sign itself is turned upside down.

<<<For Tartini the turn (and the inverted or lower turn which, like rising appoggiaturas, he found less useful) involved taking as little length and emphasis away from the main note as possible. This produces a rather different-sounding ornament from the evenly measured note groupings described in French and German sources. (Although Tartini did not specify that the three initial notes of the turn should come before the beat, it is difficult to follow his instructions without that being the effect.)

Tartini’s coverage suggests that Italian practice in the application of these graces did not always conform to the rules enunciated elsewhere in Europe. Neumann argued from a large number of music examples that it is *****impossible to be dogmatic***** about the length and placing of small ornaments (i.e. whether they occur on or before the beat and how much, if any, of the value of the principal note they account for).

******

The ‘single relish’ was variously a turn and a trill, though Mace (G1676, p.107) asserted that the backfall of the latter ‘would always be performed very strongly, and smartly’, implying a perceptible dwelling on the appoggiatura. The ‘double relish’, like any other compound ornament, involved a shake; the term was used by Playford for Caccini’s gruppo. The two constituent elements of Locke’s ‘Fore-fall & Shake’ were sometimes separated (ex.39), allowing the shake to define the pulse and preventing the bar from going astray rhythmically. These are not to be confused with shakes with an ascending prefix taken before the beat (ex.40) or starting on the beat, the latter unknown in England before about 1725. The ascending prefix must also not be confused with the termination of the ‘Shake Turn’d’, which required a discernible lingering on the final main note (ex.41). Purcell’s term ‘Plain Note & Shake’ (renamed ‘backfall-and-shake’ by Howard Ferguson: see Purcell, G1696) makes it clear that the initial note is not to be regarded as a backfall proper, but rather as a standard italianate appoggiatura taking half the value of the note being graced (two-thirds when it is dotted). Any addition of a tie between the plain note and the first note of the shake is unwarranted.
