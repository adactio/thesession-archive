Re: One and 2 row box intonation

When centered on C, tuning systems ameliorating the poor major thirds that characterize ET place the greatest compromise on what have become the black keys on a modern piano keyboard. A black key will either be tuned to the flat or the sharp, and there are numerous examples of early keyboard instruments where such keys are mechanically split to provide both (each with its own strings). If a conventional keyboard with seven white and five black keys per octave is tuned to just intonation, the white keys would also need to be split. By definition, if the octaves are tuned pure, the fifths OR the major thirds can also be pure — but not both. (In mathematical terms, this is a direct consequence of there being no positive power of two that equals any power of three.)

In vocal performance, octaves, fifths, and thirds can all be pure, but the pitch level will then wander according to the sequence of those intervals. On instruments with some pitch flexibility — say a fiddle with fixed-pitch open strings but free finger placement, or a wind instrument where the fingerholes are tuned by the maker but the actual fingerings can be varied to play “in tune” with other instruments — there are specific (often instinctively employed) techniques for juggling pure thirds and pure fifths. Accordions are commonly tuned with deliberately controlled pitch discrepancies between nominally identical reeds in different registers. This masks compromises in the underlying tuning system with a “wetter” or “dryer” pulsing effect.

There is an iPad and iPhone app named “Temperaments” that readily demonstrates all this on a simulated piano keyboard (https://apps.apple.com/us/app/temperaments/id994751711). If set for just intonation, it tunes the D a “beat free” pure fifth above the G. This is a different D than the one a pure fifth below A, as heard in the clearly beating interval between those notes on the same keyboard.

I’d be curious to know the source of the belief that Ryan Molloy somehow gets around this on his album, “Tempered.” If anything, I would have taken it to demonstrate how elegantly the pipes accommodate harmonically elaborate accompaniment on an instrument that is firmly anchored to ET. (If anyone is interested, I will gladly provide links to video discussions of the effects attendant to just intonation mentioned above.)

There’s not a word about just intonation with regard either to the pipes or piano. The cited 18th-century tunings present different approaches to providing the purest possible fifths and major thirds. The jumping off point for that sequence of developments was the quarter-comma meantone temperament first described in the early-16th century (pure thirds, suboptimal fifths). It was still regularly employed by concertina makers in the 19th century, and figures alongside other comma-fraction meantone temperaments in present-day discussions about alternatives to equal temperament for tuning such instruments.

Re: One and 2 row box intonation

The battery of so-called historical temperaments developed in the context of art music — in the good old days when G sharp and A flat were separate notes, and long before the button box or uilleann pipes were even on the drawing board. That doesn’t make them one jot less relevant to the instruments in the present-day ITM lineup. The equal temperament to which these are commonly tuned was known both to the theoretically and practically oriented authors who contributed to the massive body of earlier writing about tuning systems. However, with the exception of on instruments with fixed frets, it was literally the last thing any of them felt worth implementing.

In just intonation, the E derived by tuning a series of pure perfect fifths C-G-D-A-E and subtracting two octaves, is not the same E that is obtained directly by tuning a pure major third above the C. The interval between the two E’s is termed a “comma” and has to be accommodated one way or another in any system used to tune a fixed-pitch instrument. (The same applies to every other note that is tuned a pure third or a fifth from another.) It is possible to select either of the alternatives on the fly in vocal performance but the comma still doesn’t go away. It simply causes a different, perhaps even more significant, musical problem.

I attached a link to a video explanation of the practical consequences of all this to a previous comment in this thread, repeated here for convenience, and would urge anyone with an interest in the topic to watch it before returning to the following exposition and whatever further dialog it might trigger.

https://youtu.be/XhY_7LT8eTw

The process of dealing with the comma when tuning instruments is called temperament. “Quarter-comma meantone temperament” was the first such method documented in Western Europe and remains in relatively widespread use. It narrows each of the fifths in the C-G-D-A-E sequence by a quarter of a “syntonic” comma. This brings the E down to a pure major third above the C, and is otherwise characterized by pure major thirds and pretty crummy fifths. Equal temperament divides the same comma into eleven parts, evenly distributed among the twelve fifths between C and B sharp. This closes the circle of fifths, making those two notes identical (“enharmonically equivalent”). This is a major musical convenience but the system includes no pure interval other than the octave. Other systems juggle the quality of the fifths and thirds in perceptibly differing ways. (The one with a pure fifth is referred to as “Pythagorean tuning” and its major third is borderline intolerable.)

One of the points that has been consistently overlooked in this discussion is that, although just intonation provides chords that are glaringly more in tune that those of equal temperament, when melody is played on a fixed pitch instrument the situation is pretty much the opposite. An instruction book for the “Pastoral or New Bagpipe” (the immediate predecessor of the uilleann pipes) published in 1743 (https://www.musicologie.org/Biographies/g/m/2110292319.pdf), says this “Of the Flats and Sharps” in an explanation of the fingering chart for the chanter:

“…it must be observed that over D sharp, the Note E is marked flat to signify that they are both performed in one and the same manner, likewise A flat is performed in the same manner with G sharp and it must be noticed in the Scale that over G sharp in the place of A there is a mark thus ♭ to signify that A flat is the same with G sharp.”

This equivalence is definitive of equal temperament and provided by none other. It might be argued nonetheless that enharmonic equivalence can be shoehorned into other tuning systems, but just intonation is as far away from having that property as any such thing can be.

Re: One and 2 row box intonation

The term temperament designates a method for subdividing an octave into a number of smaller intervals. Tuning, say, a keyboard instrument without help of an electronic tuning device requires the temperament to be set by assessing intervals and chords, struck both simultaneously and sequentially. That sequence can also be extended to a proper melody, which can also be described by the sizes of the available intervals.

A temperament does not have separate horizontal and vertical attributes. However, instruments that afford their players latitude for making on-the-fly tuning decisions produce melodies that cannot necessarily be associated with a single temperament. If we take this a step further to the full intonational freedom enjoyed by vocalists, sure, temperament can be foregone entirely and just intonation used both horizontally and vertically.

This comes at the musical cost of pitch wandering throughout a performance and precludes the use of conventional instruments together with the voices. We might ignore that latter factor in a discussion that doesn’t involve instruments, but the present one is anchored in them. Just as the first video link in my own contributions to it was to a carefully structured demonstration of pitch shift, many attempts have also been made at crafting melodies that cancel it out.

The second link illustrates how this has been regarded as impracticably utopian by composers, performers and theoreticians over the centuries that it has been a musical concern. I’m at a loss to know how otherwise to drive the point home. All these videos we’ve been shown relate to keyboard-centric art music, which isn’t what we are playing. All the temparaments named are keyboard temperaments and (apart from ET) they don’t apply to other instruments. The guy in the video makes the point that ET was not a new invention, it was known about in the renaissance and baroque but they chose not to use it, because it wasn’t the best system for the music they were playing. Our music relates to music of that period more than to fully chromatic art music. Our music sticks to one half of the circle of fifths (a semi-circle of fifths if you will) and once you do that it doesn’t matter if JI gets enharmonic towards the end of the cycle, because you aren’t using those notes. And if you add a drone comma drift instantly disappears (Instruments like the organistrum were built specifically for this purpose. Even today fiddlers playing alone naturally gravitate to JI, but their pitch doesn’t drift, because the open strings keep them anchored.

Going back to the topic of one and two row boxes: a one row box is diatonic, it can only play one side of the circle of fifths. A two row box has one row covering one side of the circle, the second row covers the other. The best way to tune it depends on how you are going to play it: If you mainly play with pianos and guitars you could use an electronic tuner to put it in ET, but if you mainly play alone or use it or vocal accompaniment it will sound far sweeter if you don’t. The general procedure for tuning diatonic instruments is to get it into the ballpark by tuning round the semi-circle of fifths to give JI. If you are only ever going to play single note melody, or melody and drone it will sound sweetest if you leave it at that. But if you want to play chords it will sound better tempered. The tempering process involves tuning the beats out of the triads, starting with the most important, and not touching a note again after it has been tempered. so on an instrument in C you would tune
E and G to the C to give a beat free C triad, then tune B and D to give a beat free G triad and tune F and A to give a beat free F triad. On a 2 row box you can carry the B across and follow the same procedure. On chromatic instruments like lutes and citterns you can extend things a bit - by tuning F# to make the D triad sound true and Bb to make the Bb triad true you can now play in C, F or G. But if you try to go further than that things start getting noticably enharmonic. If a cittern player needs to play in any other key he needs to swap to a different size instrument.

Here’s a clip of Rob MacKillop playing an C18 Guittar with a tempered fretboard. I couldn’t find a comparative clip, but I think you’ll be able to hear how much sweeter this sounds than a modern ET instrument. https://youtu.be/N3YaFJxWCXk?feature=shared

In its earliest form it was played in ‘noter drone’ style, with the melody played on one course and two drone strings strummed at the same time. just like a bagpipe with chanter and drones. On these early instruments the frets were only under the melody string, and were diatonically spaced and were positioned in JI. JI is still the sweetest sounding fret spacing for this style of playing. At some point they started making the frets go right across the fret board, so that they could finger the drones to play different chords. At this point, although still diatonic the fret spacing moved to something like 1/4 comma, and this is the for the instrument took for about 180 years up to the 1980s, playing Irish and Scottish derived music on a tempered fretboard, even when playing in consort with other ET instruments (but importantly, only in the ‘celtic’ keys). It is only since the 1980s that people have started making and playing dulcimers with chromatic, ET fretboards.

I’ve been engaged in the study of musical tuning systems from both practical and theoretical perspectives for over sixty years. This has involved both absorbing and contributing to the literature about it, and countless academic and workbench discussions with others in the field. However I might be ranked among them, my familiarity with the attendant terminology is extensive.

The term “temperament” has been used from the outset to designate a type of tuning system that balances the purity of major thirds against the purity of perfect fifths, by slightly detuning both. Temperaments are defined in rigidly quantified terms. Equal temperament is not in a class of its own by being “the result of a mathematical formula.” Those mathematics operate by subdividing a comma, and are similarly applied in the descriptions of all other historical temperaments. Prior to the advent of the electronic tuning device, practically useful systems also included procedural instructions for their implementation. Quarter-comma meantone temperament has the advantage of being the easiest to “set” on a keyboard; equal temperament (aka eleventh-comma meantone temperament) is at the other end of that spectrum.

Instruments with fixed pitches are tuned by their makers. Otherwise, to the extent that there is latitude for doing so, instrumentalists tune according to their own musical judgement. It is generally assumed that keyboard players commonly modified whatever temperament they were nominally applying to the extent that it became an unnamed variant of its own. The same goes for setting up instruments with moveable frets such as lutes or viols, and the ubiquitous taping of fingerholes on woodwind instruments. The term “sweetening” is now commonly used to designate such tweaking, but also goes back to the early days as a synonym for “tempering,” and in the general qualitative assessment of temperaments. (I go further into the comparative analysis of tuning systems in a journal article available at https://loopholes.blog/wp-content/publications/Karp-Analysing-Tuning-1984.pdf .)

I’m not arguing with your definition of ‘temperament’, just pointing out that ‘temperament’ and ‘tempering’ are two different words. ‘Sweetening’ may have been a synonym for ‘tempering’, but today amongst string players it has a slightly different meaning - adjusting the tuning of the open strings to suit a particular key, without tempering the frets.

Your talk of tweeking is important. All your named tempaments are really only relevany to keyboards, which themselves are not really relevant to Irish, Scottish, Breton, Cajun or most other trad genres. Tempering (or tweeking, or sweetening if you will) is important to us, but your temperaments aren’t.

Consider this: JI has an error of a comma over the whole chromatic scale. ET divides that error equally between all twelve notes, all notes are equally out of tune. But for diatonic music, which only uses seven of those notes, why not keep all the notes you use in tune, and put the whole comma into the side of the circle of fifths that you don’t use? That is the basis of tuning non-chromatic instruments - you put the error into the notes you don’t use and keep the ones you do use as sweet as possible.

You also need to understand how difficult it was put an instrument like a whistle or accordion into ET before electronic tuners came along - with no fret lengths to measure it was a case of counting beats per second and comparing it to a table. Not a task for your average folk instrument maker, it was much easier to just play the intervals and tune by ear. Reading about temperaments and actually tuning instruments are two very different things. I trained as a luthier fifty years ago, then made lutes and baroque guitars with the late, great Stephen Barber. I spent a lot of time studying lute tunings and temperaments, measuring the fret dents on the edges of the fingerboards. I’ve built replicas guittars with both ET and tempered fretboards (including one which I originally built as ET then replaced the fingerboard with a tempered one, so that I could make a direct comparison.) I’ve re-tuned English concertinas, starting by putting them into ET with an electronic tuner, then spending several hours touching up reeds to bring it out of ET and into tune with itself, to make it sould pleasing to the ear. In a world that isn’t dominated by keyboards there is a lot more to tuning instruments than just putting them into ET.
