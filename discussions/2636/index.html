Critique of Grey Larsen’s flute book

While it may be impertinent of me to believe I can offer comments of any worth on the subject of Grey Larsen’s new book “Essential Guide to Irish Flute & Tin Whistle”, well, it’s in character at least so off I go!

I’ve been waiting for this for a while. Pre-ordered in October and pestered Grey about it for the next two-and a-half months, until it arrived just recently, and the time since has been spent poring over the 500-odd pages within. While it doesn’t quite embody platonic ideal I’d conjured up, it comes as close I think as any instruction manual could.

What makes it an important contribution to the world of trad, flute and whistle-playing in particular of course, is not the how-to bits. How to roll, how to cut, how to breathe, how to practise, etc. Those parts are presented brilliantly but are nowhere near as valuable to me as the ‘new’ vocabulary Grey has come up with to describe ornamentation, which he then uses to dissect and analyse the playing of a great many significant flute players (which makes for very interesting reading as one follows the transcriptions while listening to the performace - certainly for me deeper insights and appreciation have resulted).

This new way of marking up rolls, cranns, cuts, strikes, slides etc, is available for download in pdf format from Grey’s Website (though his analyses aren’t), and goes a way to giving the idea of being able to accurately transcribe ITM performances a fighting chance.

In the time that I’ve spent reading the book, one thing that has given me more pause for thought than anything else is Grey’s view on the use of tonguing in ITM flute and whistle playing. He seems to be a keen advocate of the many different kinds of tonguing techniques available to the flute player (single, double, triple, different consonants, etc), almost but not entirely to the exclusion of throating, which is the method that’s been drummed into me by my teacher and to an extent this forum. He writes that throating (glottal stop articulation), in his opinion, does not offer the same range of expressiveness [I’m paraphrasing without the book in front of me, I believe this is an accurate reflection of what he meant] as tonguing.

When you hear Grey Larsen play, you can understand what he means. His technique is undoubtedly superb, he’s arguably a grandmaster of ornamentative and tongued articulation and his playing is delicately and precisely nuanced as a result. But then listen to Conal O’Grada, who very rarely uses (and actively discourages, if I’m not mistaken) the use of tongued articulation. Glottal all the way. To me, at least, his playing is not delicate and precisely nuanced at all, it’s roughly hewn and deliciously raucous.

This is further complicated by my flute teacher (a former O’Grada student - ah, the plot thickens!), who has forbidden me from tonguing a note in his presence for the next three months!

Now I’ve still got a touch of rebelliousness left in me, so everything anyone tells me gets a pinch of salt taken along with it [subject to review], and I’m not sold on either extreme, (I appreciate listening to Grey’s subtlety, but I dig listening to Conal’s vitality). My argument though is that Grey, while not actually bashing the raucousness of other ways of playing, favours a more introspective approach and this comes across in the text.

I know, I know, it’s fininglinly mininkisom of me to harp on about, and doesn’t reflect the truly epic result of Grey Larsen’s many years of work, research and writing. I’ve only written all this because I care so much about the subject, and I was up till three this morning staring at my ceiling, thinking about things. Let me be explicit: the book is invaluable, it’s the most useful and thought-provoking literature about Irish flute-playing I could have hoped for. I like it so much I wish there was more.

Re: Critique of Grey Larsen’s flute book

I had a look at Larson’s book this weekend and it looks great. I’m especially am interested in the historical research he’s done on different flute players and styles. Fascinating.

That being said, I think it’s important to consider a few things:

1) You can overanalyze things. Umnnn… it’s FOLK music. That doesn’t mean that you can’t think about it or study it in depth or even attempt to codify it (though, I think you’ll always fail at that, IMHO). But I think it does mean that the music should have a certain naturalness. If you think this book will be a substitute for playing with good players and getting a feel for the music through real players, you’ll be sadly mistaken.

I can already hear the pr1cks at workshops telling their instructors, “but that’s not how Grey Larson says to do it.” People will think they know everything because they read about it. They’re the same pr1cks who sit in an “advanced” class and clean their $3000 flutes every two minutes because they can’t f3ckin’ play them.

2) Which leads me to my second point. Just because it’s in a book doesn’t mean it’s right. But it’s not necessarily wrong just because it’s in a book. There are no easy answers. Figure out what you like for yourself by playing with others and listening, listening, listening.

3) Grey comes from a classical background. That’s not a criticism, but consider that. He’s a fantastic player, a generous, nice man (as far as I can tell) and very knowledgeable about what’s trad and what’s not. He knows his stuff much, much more than I do. But he has a style that I think is very influenced by his roots. Which is ok. It’s great. Trad music is always being influenced by things. It’s part of the tradition. But if he says something should be done that’s not normally done by trad players I think it’s wise to consider where he’s coming from. And then decide if you want to do it. He might be improving things, giving us more tools for our toolbox. Maybe.

I know Grey doesn’t say that his way is THE way to do things. And I don’t think that he’d encourage the attitudes I’ve described above. But I know that a bunch of people will take it that way.

Post-industrial society is used to giving priority to written information. We don’t tend to believe what people say - we believe what’s written, what’s recorded. But with a folk tradition those rules don’t apply. Don’t try to just learn about the tradition; be a part of it.

All that being said, what a great resource. And worth much, much more than what he’s charging. A valuable resource from a great player. If you know what you’re doing, or even if you don’t and you keep an open mind, this book will do you much, much good.

You know, while I like Grey’s playing, it’s not my favorite playing either. But I once had a very interesting lesson from Grey when he was in town. Yes, yes, I’m a fiddler, and Grey, while he plays, doesn’t consider himself a fiddler, but I sort of figured, well, I wonder what I can learn from him? Anyway, my first teacher is a fluter, so I was used to learning from a fluter.

I was actually amazed at how much I learned from him. We took apart a tune I already knew, and he helped me really work phrasing and understand the arcs of phrase that run through every tune, from both the simple and easy division of the phrase through to a more advanced melodic (and melodic phrasing is Grey’s strong point) standpoint of phrasing.

Teaching stepdancing, I know that some people (especially adults) need to have things broken down and analyzed, where some students do better if you just show them what you need, get them to try it, and then get them closer and closer to what has to happen. Sometimes a student will need both approaches depending on what it is they’re learning and how easily they learn it. It is useless and a waste of time to argue with this fact of life; if you happen to need things broken down and analyzed, then you do, and if you happen to do better by a demonstration and then doing it and getting the bad way of doing it to the good, then you do, and a teacher would have to be very bad not to teach the student as they need to be taught.

So tutors and breaking down and analyzing what you do is useful and necessary, and so is relaxing about it all and going out and playing with those better than you so you can also get better. Everything in moderation, including moderation.

Heh, I think Ottery just woke up on the wrong side of the bed this afternoon. He’s not his usual light-hearted self.

I work with writers of all kinds, and I’d say it’s a rare bird who imagines his or her book is the be-all, end-all encylcopedia on the subject (whatever it may be), regardless of the hype on the cover. Most authors are simply trying to pass on what they’ve learned, and they painfully understand the place their book holds in the greater scheme of things. Can’t remember who said it, but “Being a writer in a library is like being a eunuch in a harem.”

On being overly analytical: It’s the nature of a how-to book to take something apart and put it back together again, explaining the bits and pieces along the way. To criticize a book based on that alone is akin to complaining that a dictionary “lacks narrative tension.” It misses the purpose of the book. Fluters who can jump into a session and honk tune after tune don’t *need* a how-to-flute book. Could they benefit from reading one? My hunch is probably, if they came to it with an open mind.

In teaching music for years and years, I’ve noticed that many people who resist anayalzing the music and their playing also tend to play in repetitious, predictable patterns and ideas. They just don’t realize it. I’m not saying this is bad--most music, and certainly Irish trad music, is *founded* on repeating predictable patterns. You can be a good player without ever really thinking about what you do. But I would argue that the more aware you are of those patterns, the more influence and control you have over them, and the more likely you are to keep exploring other possibilities.

Consider Kevin Burke as an example. In his early years, he almost always used a particular bowing pattern for string crossing phrases like the ones found in the a parts of Drowsy Maggie or the Tap Room and a million other tunes. 99 times out of 100, he’d bow any phrase like that the same way. Other fiddlers would pester him to explain that bowing and he’d get a little defensive: “I don’t use bowing patterns. It’s different all the time. If you’re thinking about the bowing, you’re not *doing* it.” But listen to him now and you’ll hear 20 (or more) different approaches to the same phrase. And in his lessons he demonstrates a very thoughtful analytical approach to exploring the possibilities. Is he analyzing all the time as he plays? I don’t think so. That work has already been done. Playing is simply following the melody as it unravels from your fingers. But the range of expressiveness and spontaneity, ironically, come from a thorough understanding of all the elements. Burke faced up to that in his own playing, and IMHO at least, he’s light years ahead of where he was back in his Sweeney’s Dream days.

When people speak they use the tongue as a form of articulation. That’s how accents, dialects etc., are formed, and why some people are easier, and maybe more attractive, to listen to.

But it remains the same language.

So, with traditional music, bowing and tonguing styles are a form of expression, surely neither right nor wrong!

I’ve been lucky enough to play with hundreds of flute players, and having a tune is the important thing.

Just as some people are gifted at impersonating people, if you have control of your instrument, you can impersonate other flute players! It’s quite fun really. I sometimes use this as a teaching method, to impress the importance of listening to what is taking place.

If anyone is interested in such style analysis, for a comparison there was a book written by David Lyth in which he broke down the bowing styles of Coleman et al. It was pretty amazing stuff. He simply slowed down the recordings and worked out which way the bow was travelling. Not exactly rocket science, but it did allow people to work out why the music was so attractive. Re: Critique of Grey Larsen’s flute book

Will [Jerball, et al]:

I appreciate and agree with the points you make in your most recent comment, but with one disagreement.

I would certainly agree that slavish imitation is something which has become much more prevalent since the advent of audio recording, then portable audio recording, then slow-down recorders, then hard-disk slowdown, etc., etc., and that it is a Bad Thing.

And I would agree with Jerball’s beef about players in workshops (or on the ’Net, for that reason) who have all the written citations at their fingertips and will argue them ad nauseum, when they should be practicing. [This seems to happen more in some instrumental areas than others, maybe due to the presence of printed methods for some instruments or the absence of same for others].

But those slavish imitators aside, I would offer the contrary viewpoint that careful imitation of recorded master players, if used in the right way and for the right reasons, has historically been a very useful and by-and-large constructive means of assimilating a musical tradition.

You say:
“Ever since someone first cut a groove in vinyl, players have worn out the disk trying to catch every note, every inflection, every flick of the finger or tongue, of some ”master.“ And losing sight of the tune itself in the process.”

In both Irtrad and other oral-tradition musics (I’m thinking of jazz in particular, but I can cite dozens of examples from within ITM), there are many very important players whose own individual, innovative, and deeply personal styles are a partial result of careful study and rigorous imitation of recorded models. A very short, fiddle-specific list might include Andy McGann, Kevin Burke, Frankie Gavin, and a host of other players specifically copping Coleman/Morrison/Killoran from recordings (yes, Andy studied informally with Coleman, but he also “wore out the Coleman records”, on his own testimony).

In many cases, the difference b/w these players, who started with careful imitation but moved on to become seminal influences in their own right, is that they didn’t *stop* with slavish imitation of a single model. Instead, they worked hard on mastering aspects of one player’s style, then moved on to another, then another, and so forth. The end result is that such a person is deeply rooted in the tradition, but is not exclusively indebted to any one model (in jazz, players who *are* so exclusively indebted are referred as “wearing [Model Player’s] dirty drawers”).

It’s been a real dilemma in ITM ever since the Golden Age that recordings--and sometimes a very small pool of recordings!--have become such a touchstone for slavish imitation. But I’d argue that close study of a model, when carefully and thoughtfully pursued, does not *automatically* lead to slavish imitation.

[FWIW, I don’t believe that we two are in fundamental disagreement. I’m just respectfully raising a point of order on one particular aspect of the discussion.]

best,

chris smith

Re: Critique of Grey Larsen’s flute book

Chris, I *do* agree--it’s important to understand what your musical ancestors played, and your contemporaries, and then to move on--which is what I meant when I said we should aim to develop our own personal relationship to the tunes. Yet I’ve seen some people get stuck emulating their heroes, but neglecting to emulate the developmental steps they took, including that big one of coming up with your own interpretations of the tunes.

One aspect of this music I find really interesting is how most experienced players can trace their influences across oceans and back through several generations. It’s like each of us can be part of a genealogical tree--I learn a tune from Cait Reed, who learned it from Martin Rochford, who learned it from Michael Touhey, who learned it from Paddy McNamara. And in the end, with a little luck, I end up playing a tune that Paddy McNamara would recognize and join in on, were he alive today, yet I play it slightly different than any of the previous players, as each in turn did. I like to think Paddy would smile, hearing the old tune in there, and enjoying the changes. That’s cool.

Oh, and the up side of having all these recordings floating around is that you can slavishly imitate all sorts of players these days, and end up sounding like none of them. In other words, we have more choices to learn from, and even if we’re losing the characteristics of regional styles, I think we’re gaining a remarkable range of distinctive individual styles.
