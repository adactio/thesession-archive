Pakistani pipes again

It’s been a while since the last thread about these and I am keen to see how things are moving on with input from people who are playing or have attempted to play Pakistani pipes. I am not interested in all the usual stories of dodgy seasoning and painted wood, etc, NOT INTERSTED HEAR ME. What I am interested in are genuine tales from those at the coal face so to speak not the opinions of non players.

For my part the first set of “highland” pipes loaned me by the band at the age of 11 were a converted set of Pakistani rose wood pipes, new bag, new reeds, new chanter and cleaned out drone bores- no problems at all and did me for 3 years easy.

Many years later I worked for a leading pipe maker for a while as they were developing a new instrument. My band were aware of this and when I turned up with a shiny new set of drones all singing sweet and balanced to my chanter they were keen to try and very complimentary, including the 3 guys who played with the local grade 2 competition band too. I waited until the end of the practice to tell them that our new drones were still some time off and the shiny much admired new ones were actually a set of Hakim Dins, cue much amazement.

What I’m saying is that with a bit of knowledge and a reasonable starting point my experience of Pakistani pipes has been fairly favourable, tourist shoppe wall hangings notwithstanding. I have Shepherd and vintage Henderson, GHB pipes, a selection of band and solo chanters, including a Sinclair which has been exquisitely fine tuned, a beautiful set of Garvie border pipes and Scottish smallpipes- I like and know my Scottish pipes. What I haven’t got is £3000+ ,or anything like it for that matter, for a set of uilleann pipes that I might not ever master. Hence my renewed interest in Pakistani pipes.

So folks who’s playing Pakistani uilleanns, who made them and what did you have to do to them to get them going? I’m fully expecting to have to junk the reeds, bellows and bag and make good all the joints etc, par for the course really that and given I already have the bellows still exceptionally attractive cost wise. Who if anyone is producing a reasonable instrument given that I’m not expecting to be joining River Dance or playing a duet with Jarlath Henderson any time soon?

Once again just people who have played, tried to play or are playing please. Yes I know I can get a “reputable maker’s ” half set for £1500 too, take a 0 off that and I’m in!

-Drone shutoff switch was improperly positioned (it protruded between the tenor and baritone drones) so that it would stab the player in the lower abdomen. The other was from a different, unknown maker, a practice set. Same problem existed with the chanter -badly out of tune with reeds supplied.

In both cases “out of tune” does not mean “off by a few cents here and there” but “nowhere close to correct intonation for an instrument of this type.” They were very rough tonally; the bores looked very rough and unpolished.

An experienced pipemaker I know used to rework these Pakistani instruments, but found that he was spending as much time re-reaming them and moving tone holes as he was making his own instruments from scratch, so there was no cost savings for him or his customers.

Re: Pakistani pipes again

“What is difficult about making pipes is they are required to play in two full octaves, of course in tune with each other. Making a reed is easy, making one to play in any given chanter, not so much. The combination is tough.”

It’s not quite that way round. Of course, the whole craft of pipe making is a high art, but once the design has been established, and a good stable and dense enough wood selected, the physics would tell you that the chanter is dependent on the accuracy of the particular article to the given design. The reed is the more variable aspect, and is worked on until it “crows” correctly, then adjusted in the chanter. The skill is in precision in reaming, hole placement etc and the aim is to achieve the correct dimensions. It’s no less or more of a problem than making an oboe. If the dimensions are accurate, the octaves should play in tune. The accuracy of pitch is also dependent on a well made and accurately adjusted reed. The finishing of the chanter involves correction of whatever discrepancy might have occurred in the machining, as well as undertaking those things that need to be done by hand - but still to a given design.

“I always feel that a chanter only sound, is most of the time, a bit too ‘naked’ to be an enjoyable one”

Sounds like something coming from the perspective of Highland piping. In uilleann piping it’s actually quite common to hear pipers play the chanter only. There’s a reason uilleann drones have a shutoff switch!

The uilleann piper who has travelled the world more than any other, played more places than any other, played for more audiences than any other, sold more albums than any other, is Paddy Moloney and he almost never uses his drones.

In the Bulgarian tradition also it’s very common for pipers playing in Bitov ensembles to not use the drone. Bulgarian pipers who do a lot of ensemble playing will carry around several chanters in various keys but they don’t even own drones to go with them.

Hard to imagine, I know, because Highland piping is so very drone-centric.

“one penny chanter, unknown who made it”

“Penny Chanter” is David Daye’s trade name for his uilleann chanters made of PVC pipe and brass tubing. I’ve played dozens of those over the years and every one has played very well: easy to hit both hard bottom D and soft bottom D, good octave tuning, good tuning overall, nice crossfingered C natural, etc. In short they do everything an uilleann chanter is supposed to do.

Do they have the warmth of tone of a $3000 wood chanter made by Alain Froment or Geoff Woof? Of course not. But David Daye chanters play better than the majority of the wood chanters by new makers I’ve tried. That’s the trouble: so many new makers have jumped into uilleann pipemaking recently and many of them really don’t know what they’re doing just yet.

Re: Pakistani pipes again

I’m coming to that way of thinking Will but I still don’t buy it that a chanter Uilleann, highland, border, whatever is that hard to make in the 21st century. Yep a master craftsman turning out a couple of dozen by hand a year may be the top of the game and able to charge at will but I’m talking a decent, playable, work a day instrument. With 21st century tooling, modelling and measuring equipment a chanter like everything else can be reduced to a sequence of manufacturing processes which if replicated exactly will bring a known result every time within acceptable tolerances.

Half the battle it seems to me is that most pipe makers use 19th century style, craft type techniques doing everything more or less by hand with the accompanying varying degree of applied skill. CNC machinery, computer design and analysis, 3d printing and modern tooling all have the potential to transform the process and produce credible instruments IMO. Look no further than the success of the GHB maker McCallum for the proof of that. The same McCallum who are now starting to apply these techniques to the uilleann pipe now as well. Me, I just wonder why it has taken so long. It may suit the mystique of the pipe makers, players and music sellers to keep the 4 and 5 digit price tags and years of waiting to get a sound instrument but to me it just shows how wrong things have gone and low base from which the piping tradition has been reborn that such a situation is accepted with equanimity.

Hi Steve T
This is a comment on a thread you started quite some time ago, so maybe you are no longer interested in a reply but I’m going to post this anyway since the comments on Pakistani pipes are rather controversial in the majority of posts. I started piping in 1977 on a Brian Howard practice set and, playing the right hand as my top hand had to rebuild the set to lefty. That way my experience with reworking pipes started quite early. For the same reason obtaining drones and regs without ordering them to have them built turned out to be a bit of a problem since, as in your case, money was an issue. In the late 70s a second hand lefty set was practically impossible to be had, especially in Germany where I come from. I played the practice set for some years but was never too happy with it having no drones, so they ended up as decoration hanging on the wall until I came across an internet ad offering a drone stock and drones by Roosebeck for € 450.-. I was well aware of the bad name Pakistani instruments had among serious musicians and in the vast majority of cases this bad reputation was well deserved. In the case of the Roosebeck pipes, however, I had seen a specimen of the instruments at the Frankfurt Musical Instrument Fair and had a fairly accurate idea of what to expect. The choice of material (ebony and brass) was what I prefer over the more common stainless steel and rosewood most often found in Pakistani Uilleann Pipes, so I ordered the dronestock, drones and regs to add to my Brian Howard set.
What I got is best described as a good basis for a DIY job. The varnish they put on the wood had to be taken off, the whole set taken to pieces, the bores smoothed some stainless philips screws replaced by brass ones, the keyes had to be reworked since they still bore the traces of rather crude filing and traditional hemping had to take the place of the cork these people had, for no apparent reason, set their heads on fitting their pipes with.
After reeding them with Eezedrone reeds it turned out, that they work surprisingly well. So as far as drones are concerned everything was fine. The regs, however, were a different glass of beer altogether. The keyes worked well and were airtight from the start but the rushes they tied onto the tuning pins could have been used as cricket bats, I’ve never seen the likes of it before (and hope I never will again).
I dont’t know what the chanters by Roosebeck are like but have read, that they can be made to work well without too much effort and a good reed.
So, all in all, if you are prepared to invest a few hours of work and know your way around pipes rather well the Mid East pipes are worth giving them a thought. Alas, even these are nowhere near the £ 150.- you said you were prepared on spending for a half set, it would, probably, add up to something like £ 500.- (reeds, bag, bits and pieces not told).
I hope this will be helpful to you or anyone looking for a cheap set of Uilleann Pipes.
Michael
