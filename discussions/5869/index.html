My question: do focus on becoming a solo virtuoso on your chosen instrument(s) and then take that skill to a session, or is all of your practice aimed just at being a good session contributor?

Or is there even a difference?

Re: Focus

Grgeo, some people claim that sessions, since their inception 50-60 years ago, have led to a “common denominator” approach to the music that emphasizes blending in over individual expression. They miss the good old days when a lone musician was expected to know enough tunes and be able to vary them on the fly to play for a 10 hour house ceili or whatever. Back then, counties and regions had their own distinctive styles, and players too. Listen to the endless inventiveness in Bobby Casey’s playing, or Morrision’s, Killoran’s, Seamus Ennis, Willy Clancy. All instantly recognizable as indiviudals as well as exponents of a particular style.

But I think today’s “virtuousos” continue that tradition (even if the regional style thing has blurred significantly because the music and people travel so much more easily), regardless of the effect of sessions.

In my own playing, I definitely notice the levelling effect sessions have on my approach to the tunes. Any more than 4 players and I tend to conform to the common setting of the tune, albeit with some variations that will blend well. Large sessions also tend to get stuck in tempo ruts--all the jigs played at the same clip, all the reels played at one pace. Ugh. I

But if just two or three of us are playing together, you can hear each other better and so play more spontaneously and creatively. Nuances stand out more, so I feel compelled to incorporate more of them.

I usually get only one session a week, so it’s not hard to balance that with daily solo playing, and time to sit down with just one other player. After this week’s session (with about 12 musicians), two of us went to a neighboring pub and played for another 2 hours, enjoying the freedom to set our own pace and feed off each other for musical ideas.

The up side of sessions is that the craic is fun, you pick up fresh tunes from other people, and you can either enjoy getting in the groove as an ensemble playing in tight unison, or sometimes venture off on your own experiments under cover of the group sound--so if you trip up, quietly, no worries, the music keeps going.

I’m back. Doc says it’s nothing a drop of the Islay dew won’t cure.

It occurs to me that I use both sessions *and* solo time to further me along the learning curve for any given tune. To begin, I let the tune soak into my head. Doesn’t matter whether it’s a well-developed version chock full of variations or a bare bones setting. Then I start playing the tune, building familiarity with it in my fingers and bow hand. Fairly early in this stage, the tune becomes sessionable--I can start it and play it at a session. For the sake of sessioning, I often noodle around a bit to find a few tunes that transition well into and out of the new tune.

If it’s a tune that some of my session mates also know, I’ll play it. If it’s one no one else knows, I’ll play it before the music starts, as a warm up, or at the end of the session, when everything’s winding down, just to put it out there and see if anyone says, “Nice tune, what’s it called?” Sometimes I’ll get together with one other sessioneer during the week and work it up together, to build critical mass so it’ll catch on among the rest.

During this stage, I tend to settle on a fairly firm “standard setting” that’s “mine.” And simultaneously play around with all the ways I can think of to articulate the notes and rhythm, vary the phrasing, etc. As that process goes on, I’m also trying it out at different speeds, in different styles (Donegal, Sligo, or Clare; driving or lyrical; sparse Sean Smyth or Martin Hayes, or bric a brac with rolls and triplets a la Seamus Connolly or James Kelly), and sometimes in different keys and modes. If the tune isn’t particularly flute friendly, I’ll make a point of transposing it to a more workable key so my fluter friends can join in (and still play it in the original key, especially if it just sounds right there).

Now all of this helps develop the tune for inclusion in the session, but it also helps me more fully understand the tune’s individual personality, which is crucial for performing it solo.

For me, that’s where the tension between session playing and solo playing comes in--frequently, sessions don’t allow much opportunity for personalizing a tune. When you’ve spent the time getting to know a tune inside and out, it can feel constraining to play a less developed version in a session. Or to play it anyway but know that you’re the only one hearing most of the nuances. I guess I consider that a reasonable price to pay for the fun and benefits a session offers. And I’ve still got six days a week to play it however I want.

The other thing that enters into this is the myth of always needing to vary the tune each time through it. Over the years, I’ve listened to thousands of tunes on cds, session tapes, and live recordings. Yes, most good players do put in variations as they repeat a tune. But even the most inventive players sometimes play a tune the same exact way three times in a row, or with the same exact sequence of variations in recordings several years apart. And it’s actually quite common to put variations only in the A part, say, of a tune and leave the B part alone, or to insert only one or two subtle changes--a triplet in place of a roll, or an unornamented dotted quarter note in place of a three eighth-note motif, etc. Even playing well rehearsed solos for recordings--when you’d think most musicians would want to strut their stuff--many good players do more with less.

Which works for both solo and session playing.

Okay Grego - I’ll try to simply answer your question and not put in too many value judgements.

I do focus almost all of my practise time on becoming a better solo player. Whether a virtuoso or not. I focus on becoming more expressive, inventive and precise, on learning tunes I love and crafting ways of playing them that please and move me (and hopefully my listeners).

As for then taking my skills to a session, Ceol Cairdeas makes a valid point, that this kind of thing is too rarely appreciated in sessions. It would take a real change of mindset for many to play a lot less and listen a lot more.

Evidence seems to suggest that players in the past didn’t expect to be able to play together at the drop of a hat, but were content to listen to the player or style that was unfamiliar to them, take something of what they heard away in their head and maybe later make parts of it their own. This has little to do with virtuosity, but much to do with patience and respect for the tradition and for other players’ settings and variations.

I have had a wide variety of musical experiences before I settled on ITM that included; rock, jazz, classical, baroque, medieval & renaissance, Middle Eastern, Andean and other South American styles, and the very thing that attracted me to ITM was the fact that it was complete no matter how many instruments were involved. In other words; the solo melody and style of the music had all the harmonic and rhythmical elements necessary, but you could add how ever many other instruments into the mix that you desired. Your taste and sensitivity to the style was all you needed to embellish and enhance whatever was already inherent in the melody.

The other thing that appealed to me was the social aspect of the music. The idea that you could go to a pub or a party and have something fun to do, like play tunes together, provided an additional attraction for me. I don’t sit around and practice for the session, but I do spend considerable time learning tunes my friends know in order to have more fun when I go. Sometimes the sound of being part of a lot of people playing is thrilling, and I feel like we’re all flying, other times it feels like we’re trying to swim against the current, but it’s always fun to go out and hang with your pals, meet new folks, and… well, you know what I mean ;-)

My personal musical goals are to be able to succeed at what I described in the first paragraph, and in doing so being able to contribute to the ensemble playing better. I have no ambition to be a “soloist,” but I do endeavor to be able to play satisfactorily as a solo instrumentalist when the occasion arises. I have a “band” that attempts to put all these aspects of the music together to the best of our ability, but the session is more like the “after party” for me… I go there to relax and have fun.

Re: Focus

ITM sessions are a fairly recent phenomenon in my area. Twenty years ago, I don’t think I could have found one. The sessions we had drew from American trad folk music, mainly Appalachian and New England (New English?) and whatever bits of Irish, Scottish and English material anyone happened to bring in.

It was mostly a round robin sort of thing, with each person or couple or whatever contributing one tune or song in each round. Others generally wouldn’t join in unless invited, which was the case more often than not. If someone played a fiddle tune, it would have been considered presumptious for another fiddler to jump in. We were more interested in hearing each person’s style and approach to the music. Later in the session, as people ran out of special things to offer, it might turn into more of a mass jam.

Today, sessons like that seem to be less common. The fiddlers and banjoers know a lot more of the same tunes now because they go to the same festivals and music camps. Now the typical old-tyme session is more like an Irish pub session. I’m not complaining (much), but now the general song-and-tune-round-robin session seems to be left mainly to the folks who aren’t highly accomplished on an instrument. There’s a sort of de facto segregation happening.

I suspect I’ve just rambled, but I’m gonna just submit this without re-reading it.

Re: Focus

Sessions motivate me to become a better solo player. I can’t seperate the two. I don’t take lessons and I don’t enjoy learning tunes from CDs or consciously dissecting the style elements of renowned players. If I didn’t go regularly to sessions that are way out of my league, my playing would never improve. While I am there, I am paying very close attention to what the people I would like to sound like are doing, and at the same time I’m picking up their repertoire.

In other words, the session is where I learn to play, and what I learn there makes me sound much better on my own. I came from a place where I had *one* session every two weeks, I knew all the tunes, and I was closer to the top that the bottom as far as the quality of my fiddling was concerned, and moved to a place where I can (and do) go to at least three sessions a week where 3/4 of the people there play way better than I do. With that change, my ability as a solo player has increased to the point where I’m twice the player I was when I arrived under a year ago. (Although I don’t really know how one quantifies such things - I’m pulling these figures out of the air.)

The session is also my reward for all the effort I’ve put into learning how to play. Relaxing with some friends over a couple of beers and *not* having to think about “virtuosity” a few times a week nicely balances the time I spend in my room thinking *only* about “virtuosity”.

At the sessions I go to, though, there is typically so much solo playing that the people who come expecting an ensemble experiece complain that everyone is just “showing off”. I personally don’t think this is the case, as anyone who can keep up is welcome to join in. The complaints come from people who either don’t know the tune or don’t have the ability to play it well enough or fast enough, and they would rather the entire session be reduced to their own level of ability than listen and appreciate the solo playing, at least half of which I would actually *pay* to see.

But to each their own. I understand that most musicians go to a session to play, not only to listen - that’s part of the beauty of it - but I don’t understand why they would complain if they find themselves out of their depth. For me, it inspires and motivates me to “catch up”.
