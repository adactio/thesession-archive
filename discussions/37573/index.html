Which Came First?

Fiddle Aunt’s question about repertoire got me thinking about a question better given its own discussion. The question is, which came first for you, the desire to play a certain instrument or the desire to play a certain genre? I was always drawn to flute, no matter the genre. What put me over the top was the playing of Ian Anderson of Jethro Tull. However, when I discovered trad, I really took off. I also play Early Music, jazz, folk and pop. The one essential for me is that it be on flute. I can’t imagine finding music that I like and then having to wonder what instrument I might play it on, but that’s just me. What say you?

Re: Which Came First?

The OP’s question is interesting, and the responses show that it’s different for different people.

I was taken aback, some time ago, when some kid called me to start pipe lessons. I asked “which type of pipe?” and told him about the differences between the Highland pipes and the uilleann pipes. The kid said something like “I don’t care, either one.”

Wow, somebody is going to go to all the time trouble and expense to learn pipes, but doesn’t know or care anything about them? So, I told the kid “go on YouTube and do a load of listening to people playing both types of pipes, and find out what kind of music you’re drawn to.”

Because it seemed to me that the kid was putting the cart before the horse. As Anthony Baines wrote: “A musical instrument exists for the music played upon it, and for that alone.”

Thing is, as has been pointed out, there are instruments like the violin which are musical chameleons and can superbly play music from a large number of genres. I once saw an Indian ensemble with sitar, some sort of Indian drum, and a guy playing a violin. The violin was held upright in the guy’s lap, more or less like a cello, and sounded utterly “Indian”. Were somebody to hear it, and not see it, they would probably imagine it was some sort of Indian folk instrument rather than an ordinary orchestral violin.

Then there are folk instruments which have a particular musical style and set of performance practices and repertoire so associated with them that it seems impossible to extricate the instrument itself out of the matrix. This seems especially true of bagpipes. Just listen to Bulgarian piping, uilleann piping, and Highland piping to hear this in action. When it really jumps out at you is when you hear somebody playing one of these bagpipes who never learned the associated style and performance practices… the instrument just doesn’t sound like itself.

Re: Which Came First?

In my case I have two answers to your question. Firstly I started out on guitar,and it was definitely the instrument that had me fascinated rather than any particular music genre. I was, and remain to this day, a complete guitar nut, and I enjoy many styles of guitar playing. But having started attending Irish sessions in Melbourne back in the 1980s it became apparent to me that whilst the guitar was suitable for backing and concert performance, it was not suitable for participating in playing tunes in a session- it simply didn’t fit tonally and got swamped aurally. So I decided to play something else at the sessions, and as luck would have it John Carty was touring at the time and playing mostly banjo. Having heard him convinced me that the banjo was the ideal instrument for me to play at sessions, so in that case it was the type of music that dictated my choice of instrument. So I not only ended up being a musician but also a banjo player!

Re: Which Came First?

“I have recordings of people who are a hell of a lot better than me playing bluegrass, blues, Handel, all sorts on the uilleann pipes (like one or two tracks of otherwise normal Irish albums), and yep, it still sounds Irish.”

I will point out, that if it’s Jerry O Sullivan you’re thinking of, he’s playing a variety of types of music but retaining the complete set of stylistic devices/performance practices traditionally associated with the uilleann pipes: he’s doing crans, barks, hard bottom D, bending notes, and so forth, exactly as he would in ITM. As I said above, it’s hard to parse out a quirky traditional instrument like the uilleann pipes (or Highland pipes) from its associated performance practices.

When you do hear it, and I’ve heard it (Highland pipers trying to play uilleann pipes using GHB fingerings and stylings, sax players trying to play uilleann pipes as they would a sax) it sounds very strange, and quite unlike uilleann piping. Only the timbre remains.

Re: Which Came First?

Interesting that so far (unless I’ve missed someone), no other flutist has commented. I had played the guitar (flamenco and classical) and the lute (Renaissance) for many years when a desire to play an instrument on which a note didn’t simply die away after being played but could be ‘worked with’ came upon me. (With the guitar or the lute there are constrasts of loudness/softness, crispness/sweetness that can be made, but once a note has been plucked one can only let it die away or replace it with another note.) I chose the flute partly because I loved its music and partly because I loved the sound of particular flutists (Rampal, Nicolet and later Moyse). I played Baroque flute and silver flute. I also tried the violin and the fiddle (classical and Appalachian) for the same reason. I was drawn to the music of Britain and Ireland by the music, however, and after working for some time with the fiddle, back trouble led me back to the flute, on which I’m enjoying playing the music tremendously,. But the music is first and the instrument second., as it was with my first love, flamenco (where if I could have done so, I would have preferred to be a singer to being a guitarist).
