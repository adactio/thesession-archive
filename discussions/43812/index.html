>>>: “So my question is: what would be the elements that would make up your session from Hell?”

I have made a list, many elements of which are probably what would also define a session-from-hell experience for you as well.

#1: Somebody who sings johnny cash at your session. This happened to my mother when she was hosting a session in my pre-banjo years when I was a little kid.

#2: A fiddler or whistler who refuses to tune, has no idea what they are doing, and tries to tell you, the experienced musician, that you are full of crap. Other instruments can fall into this category, but the fiddle and whistle stand out a bit more. Pipes are not included in this category, cause the pipers never seem to be able to keep their instrument in tune, even though they constantly try to do so.

#3: A bluegrasser invasion. For those of you who do not live in the parts of the USA where ITM lives in the shadow of bluegrass, a bluegrasser invasion is when a bunch of bluegrassers come to your session and refuse to play anything other than bluegrass. Such events are often characterized by drunken singing and surprisingly good banjo playing.

#4: A highland piper indoors.

#5: Sheet music. Though, I hear that it makes good kindling for the fire…

#6: Shakey Eggs. These small, egg-shaped torture devices are the punters weapon-of-choice against ITM sessioners who refuse to play that one tune from Lord of the Rings. One activates this weapon by shaking it a lot, pulling a pin stuck to the end of it, and throwing it directly at the musicians.

#7: A guitar or piano player who plays chords on the downbeats.

#8: Noodlers. They are the worst.

#9: Excess singing. A few songs are okay, but too many get annoying.

#10: Overcrowded sessions. This is when 20 or more people are in the same room. You cannot hear yourself play, and there is zero room for cases, so you can only bring one instrument. That’s why I really like smaller sessions; everybody can hear each other and mandolin players don’t have to worry as much about volume.

@AB: “How often does this sort of thing happen? I’m beginning to think it is a widespread, common experience but it’s not getting better. Perhaps even worse.”

I wouldn’t draw too many conclusions from the posts here. We’re talking about worst-case scenarios, and it will depend on location and how many years you’ve been attending Irish trad sessions. I’m in the Pacific Northwest USA, and have been attending sessions for only 12 years. That’s nothing, compared to what others here have in their experience of session disruptions.

In that 12 year period, in spite of the prevalence of fretted string botherers from the local OldTime and Folk Song contingent, I have only personally witnessed that one time when we had to dis-invite a banjo bass player. I heard second-hand about another episode where an octave mandolin player was ejected from another session for never learning tunes and “improvising” counter-melody (which wasn’t) because he didn’t want to learn tunes at home. I was at a session a few months ago where a cello player sat in, and was good on some tunes and terrible on others. If that person shows up again, something might be said about recognizing when it isn’t working and then sitting out the tune.

But in general, my personal experience has been that sessions in my area are almost always good fun, with only occasional hiccups that have to be dealt with.

The alternative is a by-invitation house session, which I participate in sometimes. But there is an unpredictability about public sessions that I enjoy. There is nothing better than having someone, maybe even a guest or newcomer sitting in that you’ve never met before, strike up a killer tune you haven’t heard and now MUST learn. That doesn’t happen when sessions get too inbred.

Most of the above.

However, one caveat here is that the majority of us here are thinking in terms of the typical Irish(perhaps Scottish and/or some other tunes) session which have the usual and accepted mix of instruments and an ideal number of competent players… i.e. somewhere around 4 to 8 people would be my preference.

However, as we know, not all sessions are like this but may still be acceptable and enjoyed by many. It really depends what the regulars want. Basically, it’s when people and practices which don’t really belong in the regular and accepted format start to upset the the proccedings that problems arise and the “hellishness” begins. Of course, it may also be in the eyes of the beholders and any session which isn’t to their liking or follow a different format from which they are used to experiencing may also fall into “the session from hell” category. :-)

Re: The Session from Hell

Isn’t the player Richard describes doing what we (should) all do in sessions? That is, if you don’t know the tune well enough to join in robustly on the melody, then discreetly play along with the notes you can pick up, gradually increasing your knowledge of the piece until the shape and line of it become familiar. After all, going to sessions is one of the main ways of learning new tunes, and you have to start somewhere with the previously-unknown ones. I don’t think this is the noodling referred to above: that’s an altogether different business, and simply not to be borne.

The main thing is to play along discreetly - i.e. quietly and judiciously, so as not to spoil the thing or annoy other players. Back to session etiquette.

But as to hellish sessions . . . have you come across the box player who, by clever fingering, can create all sorts of chords for which the instrument was not invented? Very inventive, of course, but clashing horribly with anyone who’s playing a more conventional harmony/accomp part.
