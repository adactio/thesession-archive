There’s also the accordian. If you play a bit of piano already, a PA seems quite a logical step. Many would argue that the button box is best for Irish music but there’s many good PA players too.

Re: Best beginner-instrument(s)?

I am an adult beginner. First picked up guitar at age 46 (4 years ago) and added bouzouki a year later. Fingering at first was a nightmare. The key has been to practice, practice and then practice some more. It can be done. My wife began at the same time and plays hammered dulcimer (HD) and tin whistle. She does not have to work as hard on her instruments. So from that perspective, I would say both of her instruments are easier to get started on. (Mastery or becoming really good on them is an entirely different matter.) However, HDs tend not to work well at sessions if that is your goal. But they are great for solo work and in bands.

IMHO, pick an instrument that you want to play. Just picking one that is “easier” (is there really such a thing in the end, I mean if you really want to be good at it?) may result in you playing something you really don’t enjoy. Motivation to work at it will come easier if you select an instrument you really want to play.

Your experience on piano will definitely help. I played trombone, tuba, piano and bass guitar at various times until I was 18 and then did nothing for almost 30 years. The music fundamental that I learned earlier in life have been invaluable and speeded progress despite the fact that none of the technical skills were of any help.

Re: Best beginner-instrument(s)?

Do you plan to invest in lessons? If not, then you need to know that some instruments are harder than others to learn on your own. Starting from scratch, fiddle is very hard to learn without help; mandolin is less hard; whistle, still less.

Are you able to cope with the frustration of working for months before you can stand to hear yourself practice? If not, forget the fiddle; you have to crawl through lots of thorns to get to the roses.

Are you able to borrow or rent instruments to try out? You may find that you take very naturally to an instrument, or you may find, as I did, that some instruments simply cannot be played (e.g., the concertina - they do it with hidden electronics) and save yourself some expense and frustration.

My advice would be – listen, choose something you like to hear, rent or borrow one and take some lessons. If you don’t like it, at least you’ve provided a little income for some poor starving music teacher. :-)

Re: Best beginner-instrument(s)?

I’ll also disagree with Kerri and BegF on the time & shortcut issue. The shortcut is to make use of transferable existing skills. Having finger strength and dexterity before you start is a shortcut. Having years of familiarity with hearing and playing tunes before you start is a shortcut.

Mechanical instruments like concertina and accordion also provide shortcuts (compared to fiddle & flute, for example) in their design in that you don’t have to spend years working on intonation and tone. Push the button and work the bellows and you get an unobjectionable sound - first time, every time. Subtlety takes work, but we’re talking a year or two, not five, at least for an experienced musician.

Re: Best beginner-instrument(s)?

OK, ceolina, you have thought of three possibilities. Great! The next step might be to think what you will most likely be doing most - playing in sessions or solo? In sessions, you might find the harp frustrating because it can be a bit quiet, difficult to haul around, and not as well suited to fast session-paced jigs and reels as it is to mellower O Carolan compositions, songs and airs. (Although it is an absolute delight to play alone, and works very well if you’d like to get together with other players away from the sessions to do mellow duo and trio stuff).

Concertina and button accordion are great for sessions but tricky - you have the finger dexterity but you’ll have to learn a less logical progression of notes than on the piano, as well as co-ordinating the bellows with your fingering.

Piano accordion would probably be the “easiest” for you, but would not be the most welcome sight at most sessions. If you go that route, it pays to listen to a lot of button accordion and concertina albums and try to get that sound - generally you’ll need a lighter touch and to think a lot about the space between the notes, which a piano accordion tends to obscure by design.

Chris, I cultivate low expectations wherever possible hoping it will inspire people to prove me wrong. I’ve seen lots of people give up music altogether because nobody ever mentioned it takes *time* to get good at it. I consider that useful information.

And Gary, I wouldn’t call YEARS of playing the piano and learning musical skills that are applicable to other instruments and styles a “short cut”. It’s more of an asset and an advantage, that one has worked for and spent a lot of time on.

Re: Best beginner-instrument(s)?

Actually, as a piano player you know quite a bit about harps. left-hand plucks bass notes / rhythm, right hand treble / melody, they are tuned in a particular scale, meaning that if you just start on one string and play every string after it you are basically playing a piano with no black keys, one key after the next.

If you want to play in a different key, they have “sharping pegs”, meaning that if you want to play in D major instead of D Dorian you can take all the Fs and Cs and flick a little switch that changes them to C# and F# fairly easily. Your piano training will help you with that part as well.
