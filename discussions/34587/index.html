What/how should I be practicing?

A typical practice for me is scales, left hand exercises, right hand exercises, work through some tunes, then work on vibrato. Is this a good practice to continue, or do y’all have other suggestions?

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

I use vibrato often … but very little. If you see what I mean. You’d hardly notice that I use it, and it’s completely different from classical violin vibrato. You could easily go your whole fiddle life without vibrato at all, and I’d strongly recommend forgetting about it completely. Period.

I’d suggest ditching the scales - they’re just not worth it if what you want to do is to be able to play trad. Concentrate on a few tunes at a time. Six seems to be a manageable number for a lot of people. Always practice them very slowly. If there’s a part of the tune that you can’t quite manage, play it very very slowly and over and over and then go back to trying it in context.

If you work through six tunes per practice session slowly, you’ll find that an hour passes in no time, and your playing will soon come on.

Good luck! :-)

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

It won’t do any harm to do a few exercises for a minute or two before you ‘work through some tunes’ as you put it. But I would caution against this mental attitude to the music -- ‘the tunes’ are not a series of items, to be acquired like stamps.
When you are playing, concentrate on the rhythm, the lift, the expression. Imagine people dancing, smiling, having fun. Get their feet moving.
Look for the natural phrasing, try different stresses, alter the swing from one extreme to the other with ‘dry’ or ‘straight’ in the middle, and note how it affects the way you think of the tune.
Try playing very slowly with maximum feeling, and gradually (over months if need be) speed up while trying to maintain that feeling.
Vary the length of bow from all of it to a couple of millimetres (literally!) and see what happens to the spaces between the notes, which are very important in Irish trad. Forget the vibrato and concentrate instead on steadiness and accuracy of tone.
You are off to a good start, since you are aware of some of the things you need to fix. It helps also to listen for good things and to learn to reproduce them at will. Good music is not just about getting rid of the mistakes.

FledglingFiddler,

Good for you for studying fiddle. I’ve been learning cello about the same amount of time, but with a teacher.

I’d drop the vibrato as others have said. Also de-emphasize the scales. . .i.e. don’t practice them for their own sake, but use them to work on bowing or breathing. I only practice scales for their own sake (i.e. getting the notes right) when I am learning a new song in a new key. ITM uses the same scales over and over.

My big suggestion would be to go to sessions as much as possible. Especially the one where you want to play in a few months. Listen, listen, listen. What key are they playing in? There is one session in my town where they play in only one key! What tunes are they playing (take paper and pen)? What tunes do they play every week? How do they group the songs (maybe learn 3 songs that they group together so that they can follow when you debut or so that you can follow them)? How fast do they play? What instruments are there already? Other fiddlers? Picture yourself there. Get to know the players. Even sit in the circle/at the table if it is a quiet night.

Maybe you already do this?

I would also work on ear training . . .lots of places for this free on internet. Maybe you have a good ear already to pick up what key the song is, but then it would be great if you could follow a tune you’ve never heard, pick it up.

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

A few cautionary tales from my own experience:

Don’t worry about speed. You’ll hear the music played mostly at speed, but that doesn’t mean you should practice it that way. I quote a reliable source in Derry: “Speed comes with how well you know the tune.”

It can be helpful to keep in mind that the people you hear in groups or see on the net who were raised “in the tradition” are like the 1%. In other words, respect the tradition but don’t get too caught up in Celtic mysticism or Pub-crawler mystique. At the end of the day, it’s music that is a blast to play and share.

The repertoire is big, but don’t be intimidated by this. Learn whatever catches your ear and tickles your pickle. A record of some old guy from Clare with loads of tunes on it often reflects a lifetime of music learning, not a few years. So if you are stressing *adopts Jamaican accent* CHIIIILLLL MAAAHHN!

^Related to above^: It can be fun to see what tunes you have, and don’t have, in common with other players you might meet along the long mad road of fiddling. It might tell you something of their personality, their preference of drink, where their wallet is located…it’s late so I’ll stop there.

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

If you’re a decent drummer you have a huge advantage, just don’t be distracted by the fact that your fiddle can play loads of different notes, think of it as another rhythm instrument. Sure the notes, and intonation, are important, but rhythm is both crucial and the killer. You could make up a three note jig or reel and play it with good rhythm and it’ll sound good. Struggle through a tune with ornamentation added, and weak or stumbling rhythm and it’ll sound like total rubbish.

Put it another way, you have two instruments, the fiddle and the bow. The bow is the important one.

I don’t think at any point more technique is a bad thing. With more master of the instrument you will be able to more easily articulate what you hear in your head…so to that point I would suggest listening to a lot of different fiddlers.

When I started listening to this genre I would hear a lot of sweet and mellow toned fiddlers, a lot of fast and technically accurate fiddlers, and a whole bunch of scratchy out of tune “authentic” fiddlers.

I don’t think any of them are “wrong” per se…each had their moment and place and still do…in the tradition.

So to get to your question…what I do is vary it up. I play a lot of Scottish music. Do that end what I do is spend some time..sometimes weeks…reading through a collection.

Sometimes that is with the music I am reading through or listening to.

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

I would say listen carefully to other players and understand TIMING. Not just how many beats there are in a bar, and time signatures, but understand how some things are ‘swung’ and some aren’t… how a jig measure isn’t equally divided into six half-beats. And then try to emulate that timing using your bow. Timing is the most important thing for this music. If your timing is good, you can get away with blue murder on everything else. And we often do.

Second, HAVE FUN. This is a very enjoyable pastime… even when you’re in the practice room, picking scabs.

Concentrate as well on tone production. Play slowly and understand the changes you have to make when you play at the tip of the bow, in the middle, and at the frog. Experiment with how changes in bow pressure and the length of the stroke affect the sound.

Master the bow stroke… keeping the bow perpendicular to the strings, at all points in the stroke. Many people in the early stages wind up swinging the bow in an arc.

Get a fundamentally good left hand position. Avoid the collapsed wrist. Yeah, someone will always post some video of Martin Hayes or Liz Carroll playing with a collapsed wrist. But there are certain things they will never be able to do on the violin unless they make some technical adjustments, and the propensity to injury is much greater.

Try to minimize finger movement. Fingers should be positioned over the strings and not wind up getting raised way up. That wastes motion, increases margin for error and slows you down.

Consider having at least one lesson, early on, even if you can’t afford to take a lot of them. Early intervention can help you avoid a lot of bad habits. A teacher can also see whether a shoulder rest would benefit you, and help you set it up.

Are you getting fatigued at any point? What muscles are fatiguing first?

Don’t limit yourself. You can become a very good session player with only a limited amount of fiddle technique, if your timing is good. But why limit yourself? A few years from now, you may fall in love with the Bach Partitas and Sonatas. Or Beethoven. Or jazz. Or bluegrass. Or you may want to compose music, yourself. Build the technical foundation to do whatever you want.

Learn from everyone. All styles.

If it helps you to play scales, play scales. If it helps you to play arpeggios, play arpeggios. Both are necessities in other styles of music. You can get away without doing a lot of it if you stick to trad, but why limit yourself? You should know G, D, A and C and their relative minors inside and out, though.

Listen to the strings you aren’t playing. If you are in tune and you have a good fiddle, the whole violin should start to come alive in your hands, because the other strings are vibrating in sympathy. Scales are a very good way to suss this out, but there’s more than one way to skin a cat. You can do it playing tunes, too, once you get the knack of it, and you resist the temptation to play too fast.

Whatever you do, take your time, and start slow. Painfully slow.

I agree with others about the vibrato. You need it early in other styles of music but not in trad. If it is something you want to learn, you have to learn to control it. It’s like garlic… It’s great, and it has a strong flavor and can add a lot. But you don’t want it on everything, and you never want it to overwhelm the entree, which is the melody. Make it your servant, and not your master.

Feel free to post a vid or two here of your playing and I’m sure a number of us can help point out any obvious technical issues that risk holding you back.

Good luck!

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

As you can immediately see from reading through all these, there are varying opinions, often split along the lines of people who mostly/only play irish music and those who play irish music, among other things (and possibly have a classical or jazz background). There is not necessarily a “right” and a “wrong” camp, but there is a “right for you” and “wrong for you” - which you can probably not know now - so I suspect you-in-3-years will thank current-you for keeping a very open mind and trying a bit of everything.

For what it’s worth, me 8 years ago (or current me, if I were to take up fiddle - I play flute) would find the following advice “right for me”
- Ben Hall, gam, and fiddle4 usually have great advice, particularly concerning fiddle
- Try to find a real person who you trust to check that you are not picking up any *horrendously* bad habits that will be incredibly difficult to unlearn (this is just a quick sanity check)
- You will have loads of things to unlearn - keep an open mind and don’t stress about it too much
- Stay away from reels for the first year (I *was* given that advice by a former member of this website and it’s pretty much the only advice I actually followed - and did not regret following)
- If you have a thick skin and are not going to just ignore everybody’s advice, you could post recordings of yourself here and ask people what they think. The result may just be confusing, but Jerone ( https://thesession.org/members/70591 ) did that a few years ago and I believe it worked out quite well for him (other people have tried it and the results are more iffy)

As to your practice regimen, bear in mind that you are learning 3, possibly 4 things
1) how to play the sounds in your head on a fiddle
2a) how to play irish music on a fiddle and 2b) how to play irish music (it sounds like you have a head start on the latter - it’s basically a question of organising the sounds in your head into something that you can then play on fiddle - e.g. understanding what a cut sounds like/ought to sound like, understanding what jig rhythm sounds like/ought to sound like)
3) tunes (you probably have a head start on those to some extent)

With that in mind
1) Scales, irish tunes, but also *any* melody/rhythm that takes your fancy will be good here. Scales will be more boring, with payoff that (to me), wouldn’t be worth it. Try adding playing simple melodies (happy birthday, thrift shop, trad jazz, whatever you like) that you already know (in your head) to your practice regimen.
2) There are two schools of thought: you can’t play irish music without “ornamentation”, because it is as integral as bow strokes are to articulating the rhythms ; ornamentations are just decorations you can add on later. I’m in the former camp - and that’s part of the reason from staying away from reels. Lift, swing, articulation, phrasing - you can escape from none of these - tunes are more than just notes strung along one after the other.
Listen intentionally - what are fiddle players doing - when are they bowing, when are they ornamenting, how are they varying? Same with players of other instruments
3) Life is too short to waste on tunes you don’t like. (Some people will say that tunes are carefully chosen in tutor books to give you a nice progression and you should respect that and draw benefit from it, *I* say if you don’t like a tune, don’t play it). That means if the tune is a reel and sounds better with a roll in it, and you absolutely love it, ignore my advice, ignore people who tell you not to learn ornamentation, and play it!
Tunes are more than just notes strung along one after the other (I already said this, I know). In irish music, you can play *different* notes and it is still the same tune, or you can play different notes and it becomes a different tune - try to develop a feeling for how players vary while letting it stay the same tune.

Best of luck to you

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

Scales and Arpeggios!!!!!! Scales and Arpeggios!!!!!!
Playing scales and arpeggios trains both your ear and your fingers so you do a better job of tuning your instrument and placing your fingers. You may not notice small differences in pitch, but your instrument will become much more resonant when tuned and played precisely, and anyone can hear this.

I have played for more than half a century and periodically have adopted the position that I do not need to play scales because I will be playing all those notes in the tunes anyway, only to find after a short time that my playing does not sound as good.

Start your practice daily with 5 - 15 minutes of scales, then play the tunes. Scales do not take much time and do improve you sound

Re: What/how should I be practicing?

I do believe in scales and arpeggios. Especially arpeggios.

Yes, they are in the DNA of any Irish trad tune. But when you focus on the proper execution of a scale or arpeggio, you are focusing on different things than you are in the context of a tune.

Understanding how music is put together also helps immensely when, say, in a gig setting, you’re a fiddler and your up there with a singer/guitar player who does something you’ve never heard before. Or she says “I do it in Bb minor.” Or it could be something you’ve done many times, but the guitar player puts his capo one fret off and plays it in C minor rather than B minor. One half step apart, but an immense difference in fingerings, idioms and sounds.

Or someone asks you to play off a chart.

You should have an analytical and technical framework to guide you through the rough seas. Tunes by themselves won’t do that. The first time you fly on instruments, or pull out the compass and sexton to help you navigate shouldn’t be during the Storm of the Century! Indeed, you should be able to set sail on an azimuth and be confident in your direction even when you lose sight of familiar land.

People say “well, you don’t have to do this to play this music” but I have had to do these very things, many times, all within the context of playing Irish music in trad bands and occasional studio sessions. The first few times someone dragged me into Cm in front of an audience were humbling experiences, indeed, but now I rather enjoy the challenge.

I’ve posted this essay from the excellent classical violinist Lara St. John before, I think, but it’s quite valid for any musician at any stage of development:

http://www.ehrsamproductions.com/archive/lara/essays/scales.html

You have got to put in the time and the work. If you do, everything you do will sound better - and you will get more chances to play!
