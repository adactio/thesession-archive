↳ Posted by tradlad on February 1st, 2011.

Re: Guitar as accompanying instrument

Hi Ananya,

Tip 1:
If you want to ask about accompanying guitar on this forum, develop a thick skin. It is one of emotional subjects here, with some very strong opponents.

Tip 2:
Best start from AlBrown’s guide to accompanying the session https://thesession.org/members/7960.

Learn it by heart and implement.

Tip 3: Listen a lot, watch a lot how others do it on youtube (search for Geantrai clips, there are some good guitar backers there).

Tip 4: Find a melody player to work with. Playing only backing is much less fun.

Tip 5: Finally, mingle with Irish trad musicians and they will smoothen the rough edges of you.

I can testify that Sara’s earned her stripes at sessions around here - she’s made herself welcome at a lot of places, even when she brings the guitar.

One rule that I would add, though, is one that she demonstrated when she turned up:

e) be willing to take advice and suggestions from the other players.

(This may be why she’s in a position to give advice now)

Janek - my advice would be to read Al’s guide, learn it, and then forget it and listen. If you can, play with someone who’s willing to give you some hints about the overall key of the tune (but remember, they may be wrong - some tunes are deceptive).
You might find a fiddle teacher who’s willing to give you a “lesson” - you can learn a lot from just playing with someone, especially if they give you the key and play the tune in a simple sort of way.

Thing number one to avoid: learning “the chords” to a given tune. You’ll find “lead sheets” all over the interwebs - they’re useless. Learn to use your ears and you’ll do okay, after a few mistakes.

↳ Posted by Jon Kiparsky on February 1st, 2011.

Re: Guitar as accompanying instrument

I agree with much of what was posted.

Specifically, listen, listen, listen.

Work chords out quietly.

Don’t play at all if you haven’t heard the tuned being played.

Learn the chord structure and keys, so you have a good starting point for most tunes.

Don’t trust the chords in books or on the Internet; often, they are wrong.

Read Al’s guide, very helpful to a point. I read it, it gave me a good idea of the concepts, but I also needed to start to play and experiment. I did that at a slow session before I went to a real session. I learn by doing and with my ear, so the theoretical knowledge got a little dense for me.

Finally, have fun, get to know the people you play with, most musicians are very pleasant to be around.

Re: Guitar as accompanying instrument

Me, I think there is good bad and indifferent on the guitar front for sure. As to the value of the guitar in trad, I’d say it’s down to the individual. I certainly found playing guitar accompaniment a worth while exercise, and whilst I certainly came across some individuals who were indifferent to my playing or to guitars generally, they were the exception rather than the rule.

I’ve also suffered at the hands of ruiners of various kinds, playing both melody and accompaniment. My attitude is that we can’t all be tradesmen, and therefore we just have to take the rough with the smooth, it’s a hard old station playing sessions sometimes.

When out and about at sessions I’ve found that there is a 20% hit rate for good sessions, 1 in 5. Even regular sessions of a high standard aren’t fantastic every week.

Crappy attitudes are easy to find and even easier to avoid, so Tradguy, stick with it.

I think the fact that the guitar playing made no impression is a good thing - the job of an accompanist is to fill in the gaps without really being noticed.

Martin is one of the best guitar accompanists I’ve ever heard (and an even more accomplished piper). But it’s in the faster strathspeys and reels that his skill really shows. Unfortunately there don’t seem to be any clips on Youtube. The duo seem to prefer playing in sessions, but when they can be coaxed onto the stage they inevitably steal the show.

I didn’t mean to diss Alasdair - I was lucky enough to spend a whole weekend playing with him at the Celtic Connections “fiddle village” a couple of weeks ago. I don’t think I’ve ever learned so much in two days. But when it comes to style, he’s picked up traditional music and run with it. The end result is great music, but it isn’t really traditional.
