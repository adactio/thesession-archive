The Myth of Materials

Really following on from the discussion about ivory I confront my own prejudice with regards to the material which is used to create my own instruments. If you weren’t aware I play pipes, small pipes, border pipes and the highland pipe. All of these instruments are made from what is regarded as the quite traditional material of African blackwood.

Blackwood has fantastic properties for instruments because it is very slow growing, it is very dense and has a very straight grain. This means that it is a turners wood of choice and can be worked well and relied upon to stay quite stable when seasoned.

Here’s the contentious thing though, what it has not got, despite claims and hearsay to the contrary, is any particular acoustic merit. It is simply the material used to make a tube which is used to contain a column of vibrating air created by a reed. The pressure of that vibrating column of air is wholly insufficient to have the slightest effect on the wooden walls of the tube (drone/chanter) at all. It adds no resonance and no harmonic that is not simply the product of the dimensions of the bore itself. It’s nothing but physics.

Knowing this though I still insisted on Blackwood for my last set of pipes despite cheaper and greener alternatives. Completely irrational but quite common in the selection process with regards to musical instruments I’d suggest?

Re: The Myth of Materials

There is a lot of myth about materials in instrument making. But at the same time a lot of what can apparently be dismissed as myth using simple physics is in fact very real.

If you look up ‘sound’ you will find two separate definitions:

“In physics, sound is a vibration that propagates as a typically audible mechanical wave of pressure and displacement, through a medium such as air, and water. In physiology and psychology, sound is the reception of such waves and their perception by the brain.[1]” (from Wiki)

As musicians it is the second one, how the sound is perceived, that interests us - we don’t really care about pressure waves and vibrations, we care about what people hear. And that perception of sound is influenced by things other than just the vibrating air.

If you record video of a flute and a synth playing the same piece of music an ask poeople which sound they prefer they will usually opt for the flute. But if you swap the two sound tracks, they will still tell you that the video with the image of the flute sounds best, even though it now has the sound of the synth. Even things like the colour of the room and how comfortable you are alter your perception of sound.

So even if the material an instrument is made of doesn’t alter the physical sound of an instrument at all, it may well still alter the perceived sound which, at the end of the day, is all that really matters to a musician.

Re: The Myth of Materials

“what it (African Blackwood) has not got, despite claims and hearsay to the contrary, is any particular acoustic merit.”

This can be taken to mean that ABW doesn’t have any particular acoustic superiority over other tone woods (Ebony, Cocus, Mopani, Cocobolo, etc). I agree that ABW may not be the best wood for tonal purposes but is chosen because of its availability and ease of being worked and stability.

“It (ABW) is simply the material used to make a tube which is used to contain a column of vibrating air created by a reed. The pressure of that vibrating column of air is wholly insufficient to have the slightest effect on the wooden walls of the tube (drone/chanter) at all. It adds no resonance and no harmonic that is not simply the product of the dimensions of the bore itself.”

Now if the claim is being made that material has no effect on tone, it’s one of those things that engineers and physicists seem to be on one side and many experienced professional musicians on the other.

About bagpipes I don’t know, though many Highland pipers claim to be able to hear clear differences between drones made out of ABW, cocus, ebony, or cocobolo. Highland drones have rather thick walls so the material might well have a minimum effect.

But for flutes the differences between various woods is quite clear. As I’ve said I got to try around a dozen wood headjoints made to the same specs by the same guy, though made out of four or five different woods (it’s been several years, I have his catalogue somewhere and I could look up the specifics). I could have easily sorted the headjoints blindfolded by the tone and other performance characteristics, because each wood was clearly different from every other, in other words each wood performed in a clearly unique way. No engineer or physicist can tell me that I didn’t experience this.

Likewise professional Boehm fluteplayers experience quite clear differences between flutes made out of gold, silver, platinum, and nickel and likewise can easily sort these materials blindfolded by the way the flutes play; however even experienced flutists/flute teachers can have difficulty telling which material is which when somebody else is playing the flute, in other words the differences are mainly the sorts of things experienced by the player (like resistance and responsiveness) not things heard by a third party.

Trumpet players might bring instruments of brass, silver, and/or copper to a gig to create different tone colours. I was in the orchestra pit at one gig and I noticed the trumpet guys all had trumpets in silver, and with copper bells. They explained that the copper gave a dark tone which was preferred on certain pieces.

Re: The Myth of Materials

“Delrin makes some damn fine chanters that’s for sure, more resilient and easier to make uniform for band players.”

Delrin has many drawbacks, and therefore is rarely used, if at all, by serious competition bands. Delrin is more subject to alarmingly fast changes in heat and sun, i.e. the sun hitting the chanters on comp. day can send the pitch in very short time through the ceiling. Also, most serious competition soloists and band don’t care for the sound or the feel. I could be wrong here, and I’m no expert, but I’m not aware of any top bands, e.g. FMM, SLOT, SFU, etc. going anywhere near delrin.

I competed in a reasonably serious Grade III band. We tried delrin, and hated it. I also tried delrin for solo comps., and didn’t care for the feel or the tone.

For lower grade bands, delrin may make sense. Cheaper, and accident proof, among other reasons.

Apologies for winding this one up and letting it go, work got in the way. When I was involved in the piping business the band scene was most certainly dominated by if not entirely then as near as damn it by plastic chanters. Delrin & polypenco is the same stuff in a different bucket. Solo competition is dominated by wooden chanters as they have a tendency to have a more individual sound, not something you want when you’ve 20 plus sets of pipes to tune. Plastics are loads easier to work with a CNC lathe and milling set up which is why some manufacturers these days can churn them out on mass. A wooden chanter can be worked in the same fashion but is much more likely to react in a slightly random way to being worked in this manner. A tone hole could have a minute dimensional change as a result and there you go. Most top soloists will be fettling something on their chanter themselves for sure I’d suggest.

Richard the differences in your various flute head I’d suggest has far more to do with the slight differences in manufacture, from the same maker or not. They are hand made or finished and they are going to be different as a result. Different materials will work to different tolerances, I might just be convinced there is some differences as a result of thermal, conductive properties of the materials but I’d suggest that as we are only talking about an exceptionally thin plating on something such as brass for a wind instrument like a flute. I’d have thought that any thermal characteristics will come from the main body of material not the micrograms of finish.

Re: The Myth of Materials

“Different materials will work to different tolerances”

This is certainly true.

I had noticed for years that Ralph Sweet’s basic Irish flutes in maple consistently played better than his more expensive Irish flutes in rosewood.

One time around 20 years ago I was attending the national convention of The National Flute Association and there was the man himself! He had a booth selling his flutes and fifes.

Well well he had around 20 of his Irish flutes in maple and a like number in rosewood. When I tried them sure enough the maple ones consistently had a bigger sound. I thought “I wonder…” so I began pulling the headjoints off and sticking a finger in the top of the body joint to crudely measure the inside diameter and sure as shootin’ the maple flutes had bigger bores than the rosewood ones.

I bought this to Ralph’s attention and he was puzzled. “They shouldn’t be different… I use the same tooling and the same number of passes… hey wait a minute! Maple is softer, and I reckon the tool takes away more material!”

I found it incredible that a guy who had made more Irish flutes than all the other makers put together wasn’t aware of the bore dimensions of his flutes. I’m quite sure that that headjoint guy was a different story.

Since most of my piping consists of funeral services and graveside services, the Polys are easier to use at a church and then head to the graveside with, as they don’t seem to be affected by temperature and humidity as easilly.
