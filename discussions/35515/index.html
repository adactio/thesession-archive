Re: Concertina and Keyboard.

It has a bellows like accordions. It’s in the free-reed family. That was the wind-sucking noise you heard. It has 15 buttons on each side fit into the ends. Mine is black with white buttons.

As for under the end, or more like inside of it, is the button’s mechanism(which is very similar to that of a piano). You push the button, and a long lever under the end lifts up a little cover from over a hole that the sound from the reed comes through.

They’re very intimate instruments, you get your hands on one and you’ll
be able get a good idea of how it looks, cause unlike a huge piano, you hold this instrument in your lap. You can touch and feel every part of it. And yes, the really nice ones are very pleasant to hold.

↳ Posted by Piece on February 10th, 2015.

Re: Concertina and Keyboard.

I can explain perfectly Piece.

I’ve always favored stringed instruments over “air” instruments. This includes almost all the woodwind and reed instruments. The free-reeds are no exception.

More specifically why I’m not attracted to the piano accordion, the same reason why I’m not attracted to the church organ, too many overtones. Pianos have very concentrated(focused) timbres. Many of the piano’s overtones come from consonant harmonic resonance.

But accordions and organs have very fat and heavy timbres. Too many different sounds coming from one note. I can hardly tolerate bass accompaniment of piano accordions because to my ear, it’s dissonant and muddled. Like noise.

The irony…

Concertina, Smallpipes, and Uilleann Pipes. They have very concentrated and direct timbres as well. The drones of the pipes sound even and clean. Even though they are “air-powered”, you don’t hear the “airiness” like you would hear from a flute because of their concentration.

There’s also the responsiveness/attack. It’s too light and weak. Not strong and sharp like a piano’s responsiveness/attack. It’s moreso with button accordions but also subtly with piano accordions. Concertinas are way more responsive judging from what I’ve heard.

When I held a concertina for the first time, it absolutely fascinated me. When I got the opportunity to play a few, they stole all of my attention. The same thing happened with fiddle.

When I picked up my peers’ piano accordion it felt more like “Meh…” Even after playing my favorite single reel on it.

So there you have it! The complete analysis :-)

Oops, I forgot this thread. OK, Drowsy Maggie was a strike below the belt since the “away chord” is only there for half a bar but still very important. Also, the tune itself is played single, and thus there isn’t that much “room” to make up a line.

I’m not a piano player, but let us assume we have a basic pattern like this (for the A part):
|:Em Em|Em D|Em Em|D D:|

First of all, I’d rarely stick to that, even the first time through. Instead, I’d substitute the D with a Bm here and there, linger on the Em for who knows how long, play an A major as a passing chord the half-bar before D (or Bm), I might even play a G major (as you would). So, depending on the chords I’d use, the line could be smth like:
|:E | F#|G |F# D:|
or something busier:
|:E F#|G F#|E G|F# D:|
or:
|:E G|E D|E G|A B:|
or a combination:
||E F#|G F#|E G|F# D
E G|E D|E G|A B||

I’m pretty sure that some purists out there would call this jazz, but is something I could do if had a gun pointed at my head and I had to accompany this tune. Re: Concertina and Keyboard.

Hey tdrury, jeff, about the bass lines. By what I’ve been figuring out, both of you are right in a sense. Tdrury, you’re right to say minors don’t lend themselves to the *same* “walking”(I call them rocking since the thumb and pinky rock back and forth) bass lines. Jeff, yes there is “just as much” room for minor progressions as there is for major progressions.

What happens is that major progressions don’t work for minor keys because major progressions don’t “symetrically equal” their relative minor progressions of the same pattern.

Let’s look at a Pop chord progression in the keys of CMajor and aminor for example…

I, V, vi, IV: 1, 5, 6, 4(CMaj, GMaj, amin, FMaj)

If you tried to make a physically symmetrical progression, it would be: (notated in CMajor) vi, iii, IV, II: 6, 3, 4, 2(amin, emin, FMaj, dmin) which makes no musical sense. Even though the contour is the same(notated in aminor it equals 1, 5, 6, 4) it changes 2 of the chords which means it’s no longer “symmetrical”.

CMajor’s actual relative minor progression for the “1, 5, 6, 4” is: (notated in CMajor) vi, IV, I, V: 6, 4, 1, 5(amin, FMaj, CMaj, GMaj) which makes a lot of musical sense; The point being that even though this progression has a different contour, it still uses all the same chords, which means it “reflects” in a sense.

Not only are all of the chords the same for the Major’s actual relative minor, if you look at “1, 5, 6, 4” conpared to “6, 4, 1, 5” you’ll see that the latter simply starts at a different place and circles back around. They simply have different shapes.

Since we use all the notes of the scale in the bass line, and do “walking and rocking” technique, finding the progressions for minor tunes is a task cause they are much different from those that are in major tunes.

I hope all this makes sense. Maybe I’ll start a new discussion and call out to some backers to see how they feel about it.
