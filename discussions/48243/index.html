Flute mods for older players?

The discussion “Age maximum to play the flute” seems to suggest that flute players seem to start running into problems in their seventies, though some manage better than others. So it prompted me to think what would make aging with the flute easier? I suspect there won’t be one single answer (but if there is, then we’d better get on to it pronto, hadn’t we!). Let me invite you to think about it, and tell us what challenges you, and what you think would make it easier for you. Perhaps we can then challenge the makers and modifiers of flutes to rise to those needs.

(And yes, I am a serial “maker and modifier of flutes”, but at my age (75) I’m not really looking for dramatic new challenges. Just being able to knock out a few instruments and keep playing keeps me entertained enough!)

Like many players, the left hand stretch is probably my weakest point. Interesting to try this experiment. Hold your flute up to the lip and finger low D. Left hand feeling a bit stretched? Keeping L2 and L3 in place on their holes, let L1 slide forward down the tube to wherever it feels comfortable. Mine would like to hang out about 18mm (nearly 3/4") below the first hole. About where the C key cup is. That takes all the strain off the other fingers. That seems to suggest an L1 key might help. Nothing really new there, Boehm used one on his 1832 conical, and on his 1847 cylindrical, the modern flute. 190 years later, we’re still stretching….

Now, try this as an alternative. Keep L1 and L2 on their holes, but let L3 slide up to where it’s comfortable. An L3 key was Siccama’s approach, brought out in 1842. If that works for you, perhaps a Siccama flute is in your near future?

And some people find it increasingly hard to bring the flute up to near horizontal because of the twist in the left wrist. So how would a swan-neck head help you? Try holding the flute to the lip but drooping down to 45º instead of horizontal. Feel better? See an example at: https://www.flutelab.com/swan_neck/

The message seems clear. For every challenge, there is at least one solution. So, enough from me. What are your challenges? And we can discuss what approaches might help.

Re: Flute mods for older players?

Interesting about the chimneys used by Carbony, belayatron. We wooden flute makers do the same sort of thing with undercutting or even drilling holes at an angle, but on Carbony’s thinner walls that wouldn’t achieve much. And somewhere I have seen a chimney on an earlier instrument, but can’t for the life of me think where. Perhaps a Speaker hole on a clarinet, or oboe or by one of the more experimental period flute makers like Clinton, Ward, etc. Perhaps if I stop thinking about it, it will come to me!

There is a limit to how much you can move a hole using such methods without introducing tuning or performance issues, but of course if it gets the hole within your grasp, you might be very prepared to live with almost any side effects!

Another interesting aspect of the Carbony is the single hole near the bottom. We tend to think of flutes having either a Short foot (eg a French 5-key) or a Long foot (essentially an 8-key foot but with two open holes where the C and C# key cups would have been). And if a Short foot, always a pronounced flare in the bore over that last few inches. But flares are not really practical in thin-walled instruments, so they’ve got around the need by having the lowest note venting through a side hole rather than just the end of the bore. And if you have a flute with a long foot, and get someone to finger the lowest hole and even the end of the bore while you’re playing low D, you’ll see they don’t contribute much if anything. So Carbony have steered the middle course. Sneaky!

And yes, WhistlingFionn, thumb rests would seem very desirable for bigger whistles. They’ve been around for a long time on large recorders, clarinets, etc, so why not big whistles? But I noticed you specified the “top thumb”. I seem to remember that thumb rests on other instruments are more commonly used on the lower thumb. (Keep in mind that I’m in Australia, so my top thumb would be your bottom thumb….)

Which reminds me that we should include in our flute discussions the role of any similar additions that might assist flute players. I can immediately think of:

Boehm’s Crutch - a T-shaped handle that screws into a socket on the flute, such that the T-handle nestles in the joint between your left hand thumb and first finger. It’s job is to help support the flute, but also prevent it rotating away from your lip. Boehm copped a lot of abuse from Rockstro over it, so it never found much favour. You can see images of one about 3/4 down this page: http://www.oldflutes.com/boehm.htm#mod

Nicholson’s flattening around the fingerholes, his indent for the left hand pressure point, and insertion of some shark skin for the right thumb tip to press into. (Nicholson used the Three-Point Hold.) You can see the indent and the flattening of the right-hand holes in the first image at: http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Nicholson_development.htm

Cornelius Ward’s thumbrest. Again, a device to “lengthen” the thumb a little, while also anchoring it so it won’t slip. See near the bottom of http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/unusual.htm

They are the only period adaptions I can bring to mind. (Well, ignoring Captain Rebsomen who lost an arm in the Napoleonic wars but then got a maker to re-key his flute so he could keep playing. What a man! See: http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Ward_FE_3.htm) Anyone got any others?

I have previously messed around with a small chunk of Delrin in the shape of the letter C, that can snap onto the flute where the right thumb tip presses into the flute. Again it “lengthens” the right thumb while providing a flat surface that is less prone to slip than the flute’s curved surface. I should probably play with it some more….

Re: Flute mods for older players?

Terry, I have been doing this for years with my emphasis on smaller handed flutes with ergonomically offset fingerholes. These work well for older players with larger hands too.

It was Mickie Zekley who inspired me way back in 1986 to attempt to make a smaller holed version of the Pratten flutes. Mickie passed away in late October and I miss him deeply and think of him daily! The picture of him circulating laughing whole holding a long flute is usually cropped. My daughter took that picture and I was on the other end. I made for him something I called a “Courting Flute” and it was essentially a pair of Folk Flutes joined at the bottom with 6 fingerholes for each player and no vent holes. One had to finger G or higher to vent the bottom D of the other player. Trouble ensues when bit try to honk on the D. Its like what happens to Tim Conway’s conjoined elephants in this very funny video (Outtakes from the Carole Burnett Show): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3qqE_WmagjY

My Pratten copies back then were coined the name “Honker” by Richard Cook who taught Irish Flute at Lark Camp. Eventually I created a smaller-handed version and these were called and labelled “Honkette”. After about my 500th flute around 1990 I stopped numbering. However, I kept moving the holes closer and closer.

So now what I call my Large Holed Standard was at one point, my small handed “Honkette”.

There is more to making these flutes ergonomic than simply offsetting the 3rd fingerhole for each hand. One has to come up with a bore that supports the plasticity of hole placement. As far as the holes themselves, I keep the 1st and 4th holes set in place as far as scaling goes, and move the other holes up. Each hole has to be carefully sized to bring the 1st octave note into tune. The degree of undercutting is used to tune the 2nd octave. It doesn’t make as much of a difference on the F# hole but the C#, B and A holes are often severely undercut. Also I go for a deeper cork position around 25mm.

Also my flutes tend to be larger in diameter in the body. This may make the flutes heavier by an ounce. However it gives more chimney height for the fingerhole undercutting. The larger diameters are easier for some to grip.

And then there is the offset. Sometimes the G is rotated about .5 mm towards the player. In the left hand, I usually rotate the B towards the player around 1mm and away from the hand. The A is rotated towards the hand up to 2-3mm. Thus the holes are arranged in an arc. On the right hand, the F# is rotated slightly towards the hand (1mm) and the E is rotated farther (2-3.5mm).

Usually there is some offset, even on flutes for players who use the piper’s grip.

Finally there is the embouchure itself. One of my trade secrets and why my flutes play so well (I am retiring at the end of March so I can share these now) is to never play flute recreationally - thus my lips are weak. For years I used to beat myself up about never playing at sessions but I really couldn’t handle the second hand smoke. Then it dawned on me that by keeping my lips weak, I was forcing my flutes to do all the heavy lifting. It turned out that my laziness was actually genius!

Sizing the embouchure is important. A too large embouchure will require more energy. I size mine around 9 X 11mm. As I shape the embouchure with slight undercutting on the sides and as much possible on the target side, I aim for the best - and easiest - production. Usually the target side is less steeply undercut at the start, and then steeper farther into the bore, almost but not quite becoming parallel with the bore at the point of contact.

One would think that these easy to play flutes would become too easy to play for a professional player. Quite the opposite. I have a recording of Matt Molloy trying out my “Large Holed Standard” template. He picked it up, played a few notes and muttered “I could play this in concert!” and proceeded to play it for the next half hour as I collected data on his flutes regarding key positioning for the Bb flute I made him. Eventually he tried another 5 keyed flute that I made for him - but by that time he decided to stick with the Olwell that replace the one that got stolen. It was gratifying though to see both Patrick’s and my flutes played by him at the same concert. It just doesn’t get any better than that!

It astonishes me that very few to almost none of the makers have pursued making smaller-handed flute designs. I even witnessed some old school maker telling some young woman who found his flutes uncomfortable that you have to roll up some terry cloth and jam it between the fingers to spread them enough. They used the same technology to bind feet or flatten foreheads in the past. It was his booth at Folklife though - so I just had to keep my mouth shut as I cringed.

More on my retirement at my blog eventually (see laurentflutes.substack.com) or on my webpages. Basically I am no longer going to take orders and receive deposits soon, as soon as my schedule is booked. I will take Folk Flute orders through the end of this year. I will be doing some other things for income, including another Substack blog focused entirely on woodwind making. I will dive deeper into the topic of smaller handed flutes and what makes a flute “ergonomic” and how to keep it playing well.

Sgnorff!
Casey

And good to hear the philosophy behind your alterations, Casey. You’ve really pushed this into an artform!

It’s good for us to keep in mind that we are all making and playing flutes that balance stretch and performance; if we put the holes where they ought to be, none of us could play them! If you look at a wooden flute or even more so, a Low D whistle, you can see there are really two clusters of 3 holes. The gap between holes 3 and 4 being much greater than the gap between any of the other holes. Whereas if you look at a treble D whistle, it’s more of a relatively uniform spread of 6 holes. So it’s not so much a choice between ergonomic or not-ergonomic, it’s a choice of how ergonomic! Or Boehm!

I suspect that the problem with the Giorgi is that your air jet is running right into the pressure wave returning back up the flute, rather than being transverse to it. Aha, you might reasonably retort, but what about the tin-whistle - it’s in-line like the Giorgi and plays fine. Mutter grumble, I respond, yeah but in the tin-whistle, the jet is firing across a window made in the top of the tube - the pressure wave has to come right to the top and then bend upwards, where it comes under the jet and lifts it out of the window. It doesn’t confront the jet.

And it could well be that the Giorgi needs a different embouchure approach to avoid that confrontation. It would be great to hear from anyone who has made good progress on the instrument.

Re: Flute mods for older players?

The basic problem is that the transverse flute is the most unergonomic of the mainstream woodwinds (and most of the un-mainstream ones too).

On oboe, clarinet, whistle, smaller saxes, and even bagpipes the shoulders are level, both arms are at gentle curves, both wrists are relaxed and relatively straight, and the neck is straight.

On transverse flute everything is thrown out of alignment.

Of course many cope for a lifetime, but so many can’t that in the mainstream flute world vertical flutes are increasing in popularity. They solve all the flute’s ergonomic woes in one fell swoop.

Woah, Richard D Cook, I’m a bit stunned by the sharp right-angle bend in the flute head in the video. But listening to the sound, it still clearly works fine. The Flute Lab heads I referenced above make the bends needed more gradual.

And I’m not surprised, crackedfluteplayer, that the prices are high. These are niche items and both design and manufacture are not straightforward. I have made an experimental “bent barrel” as a first step, and it seems to operate without introducing any problems, other than twist. It’s clearly important that the body of the flute has to point directly at the lip, even though the head section runs accross the lip. Otherwise, the flute tends to twist in the hands because the off-centre pressure of the lip is rotating it. So a simple bend won’t work, we need at least two bends. Both of the Flute Lab models and the one in the video appear to pass the body-pointing-at-lips test.

It could be that an enterprising wooden head maker could come up with a design that could be adapted to work with all the flutes out there. That would be a welcome innovation.

And Casey, the Carbony people seem to be putting some effort into that: https://carbony.com/products/flutes/ I haven’t heard any experience of their approach.
