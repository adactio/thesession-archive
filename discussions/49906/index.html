Flute “bad days”

Please help me understand something. I sum it up like this:

How can I have a long string of great days, where everything is smooth and easy and comfortable… and then one day pick up the flute and can barely make a decent sounding note, the flute feels like it wants to fall out of my hands, and more of that? Or, similarly: How can I have a great practice session, everything’s wonderful, and then 25 minutes in, all that “bad stuff” starts to happen?

How can everything just fall apart literally from one minute to the next? I mean, in some cases this happens to me in the middle of a tune, without even taking the flute away from my lip… it’s all just suddenly gone.

Now, please note, I asked HOW. I understand THAT it happens, so I don’t need anyone to tell me “We all have bad days sometimes.” I appreciate the support, but I know that. And I also understand “The flute is a difficult instrument, it takes time/it’s a journey, etc.” I understand that also, and I believe it. I really do. But what I’m asking for is actionable advice.

I’m asking WHAT is happening that causes this. Physically, mentally, ______ally. Does anyone know (or hazard a guess) at what mechanisms are factoring in here? Because if I can watch for those things, I feel like it would be easier to work on avoiding them or cleaning them up.

And when I say “long string” of good days… I’m still a beginner, but I practice a lot. Every day, and most days about two hours, some days slightly less, but always at least an hour. But still, I know it’s a process and I’m a beginner. But by this I mean: a strong of 10-12 days where I pick up the flute, put it to my lips, and right from the first note it’s clean, and clear, and full without a hint of wispiness to it. And the flute sits in my hands like an extension of my fingers. And I can go like that for hours. And I mean it, a week-and-a-half straight of that… and then one day just nothing works. Like I just got my first flute in the mail yesterday.

So, yeah. I obviously know THAT it happens. I’m experiencing that. But does anyone know WHY? I want to know what to work on to get past this inconsistency. And do I continue to practice through it on days like this? Sometimes I do and everything gets great later in the evening. Just suddenly -- TA-DA -- back to normal again. But sometimes I do and it just continues to be a fight.

(Beginner = I’ve had a flute for about 4 years, but the first few were a little uneven due to work schedules and then some dental work. I’ve been at it solid for 9 months now, with the practice schedule I described. I have had a few lessons by live video conference, and the teacher was impressed with what a good sound I had, but she never really had an answer for me when I asked this question, and the lessons ended up not working out due to scheduling conflicts.)

Re: Flute “bad days”

I understand the advice you’re looking for, so I won’t waste your time with commiserating with you.

Here’s the deal: there are many forces at work when you play your instrument. Energy, mood, weather, muscles, nerves, fatigue, breathing, etc. The relative harmony of these forces is in constant flux. What separates them as do from them as don’t is the ability to recognize what is lagging and emphasize what’s clicking. That comes from a solitary virtue: experience. Experience gives you the insight and the chops to reach into your bag of tricks to know where to lean in or back off. Examples include avoiding certain tunes, play tunes where you can play phrases without running out of air, using more or fewer ornaments depending on what’s hanging you up. You get the idea.

Some of the cures may at this point be beyond your ability. The key is to to be open to recognizing what works when you discover how you overcame a sticking point at one time or another. Analyze what you did and file it away for next time. With experience, you will develop a repertoire of techniques that you can draw on as needed.

Of course, some days you’ll just sound like crap. It happens, but trust that you’ll notice it far more than others will.

Re: Flute “bad days”

You always have good days and bad days, no matter your instrument or level of practice. The point of your practice regime is to elevate the level of your good days, but also to elevate the level of your bad days (which are, after all, now at a level you could only dream of at one point).

In terms of what’s going on, that wet spongy organ between your ears is incredibly power-intensive and it will always prefer not to do work to doing work (which is why so many other urgent activities such as dishwashing or Amazon purchasing or posting on a certain mustard-coloured website often present themselves when you consider practicing). Active, effective practicing is your brain’s worst nightmare, because it’s genuinely hard work and on some days it is better fed, relaxed, watered and in the zone. It’s hardly a surprise your ability to play depends mostly on your brain’s state.

So, the practical advice? One option is to try and make things more consistent: play at the same time of day, following a routine, that kind of thing. A good thing to do anyway. But I think the most important thing is: bad days are days for improving your bad-day-level. If you fire up the instrument and the tone isn’t behaving, the intonation isn’t behaving, then do the exercises that work on those things. And on the good days, work on the new, challenging stuff.

Re: Flute “bad days”

The same thing happened to me mid-set last night, and I don’t even need much of an embouchure. I feel fine, but it happens because there’s a bit of fatigue setting in and a loss of coordinated focus as a result.

So if your flute’s all good, the actionable things are:

1. Mop it and put it away
2. Remind yourself this is normal
3. Have a nice cup / glass of something
4. Ask yourself if you’re practising for too long, or doing long sessions too often

Playing fitness does improve with time, but it’s a slow, gradual process. There’s nothing you can do to hurry it up, except understand that if you’ve lost focus it’s because you need a rest.

You may as well take one. When cognitive and physical coordination slip out of alignment, the capacity to learn tends to go with them. You’re practising for long periods for a beginner, so it’s inevitable you’re going to get tired.

The hardest thing of all to learn is that less sometimes really is more. 😉

Re: Flute “bad days”

I’ll try another tack. To convince yourself that you and the flute are making a nice flute-like sound, stand next to (or close to) a reverberating surface like a wooden door. (Preferably a solid wooden door.) Keep you ear open towards that same surface. You should be able to hear many rich overtones, sonority, and resonance. I’m not a sound engineer, but I know that I can hear these effects when I park myself near the garage door (inside) for example. The fact that you don’t normally hear these, or they are weaker as you say on occasion doesn’t mean that they are not there or not producible. There may be distracting sounds, or the flute is dried out, or any one of the sundry causes mentioned in the other great replies. It also boosts the confidence to play & practice in a somewhat resonant space (slight echo). Of course when you play in a session there are lots of surfaces absorbing and appearing to quieten your sound.

You mentioned oiling the flute very regularly -- be sure that you are not getting a gummy build up of gunk / oil on parts that need to be kept clear. Finally, clean out the sides of the blow hole with a gentle cloth type action -- if there is build up on the attack side (opposite your mouth) there will be disruption of the necessary air flow. A well made flute should have very crisp edges on the embouchure. Do not damage them. The flute makes its sound at the blow hole and the tube and keys need to be air tight for you to be able to control the resultant notes.

Thanks for the interesting question.

Cheers

Re: Flute “bad days”

Yes, I can be inconsistent, and even after years, there are moments where my embouchure goes to hell, inevitably in a session when I’m trying to lead a tune on my own and I get nervous.

I have one, sure-fire trick, well usually sure-fire:

Play a tune or scales in the third register (above b). These flutes can play easily to c‘, c#’, d‘, eb’, e‘, and with a bit more effort (and probably keys) to f’, f#‘ and g’. These notes need a thinner, faster air-stream which in turn requires better lip muscle control and direction. By playing “up there”, you are forcing your lips to be stronger more precise. When you return to the lower registers, you will discover that your lowest notes have much more strength and power. By “much”, I mean much, MUCH more power.

Jump Coachman’s Whip up a full octave, and that takes you to g’ for extra spice.

Re: Flute “bad days”

Hammy Hamilton once told me that an adult learner really takes 3-5 years to develop a solid and consistent embrochure. My personal experience would align with that and I was putting in 15+ hours per week in the early years between classes, practice and sessions. There really isn’t any magic shortcuts to building up the face muscles and the instinct to keep the focused airflow optimally aimed.

Brendan Mulholland teaches a simple D F# G G F# D exercise to practice just blowing and finding that sweet spot for tone; holding each note for a long time. Concentrating solely on the purity of the sound and strength of support for the note. It’s a boring exercise. But definitely rewarding for 10 mins invested each day for a few months.

Posture can shift during play which can have quite a bearing on the tone produced. So too can fingers - you think you’re covering the holes well (especially lower notes) but aren’t quite.

I’ve found playing in groups/sessions beneficial because I’m not thinking about all the technique stuff and just get carried along on the flow of the tunes. Use every opportunity to play along with others.

And there’s always the YouTube monster to play along with. For flute tunes I love the Shannon & Matt Heaton online hour long virtual sessions: archive/indexed here - https://www.vgsdb.com. And also Hatao’s tunes… https://www.youtube.com/@hatao/videos. What’s good about these resources is that it’s easy and clear to hear and play along with another flute.

PS: We all have ’em. Take a break. Best wishes

Re: Flute “bad days”

All the comments so far are valid but, assuming no problems with the flute, your embouchure should not be collapsing so completely. It could imply that, while your sound is is good, your lips are not set up properly/optimally. There are many ways to get a decent sound but many of the lip/facial-muscle combinations can be “on the edge” and prone to collapse with some fatigue. An stream can be too tightly focussed. In general, when you get tired, the sound should suffer a bit but not collapse.
In such a situation, there is little point in practising the same thing over and over; you may develop some extra stamina but not really fix the problem.
Your comment about not being able to place the flute on your lip may be telling. It suggests that your embouchure set-up is not under full conscious control, which it should be. There’s nothing magic about blowing a flute; you (should) be in control and making the flute do what you want. Relentless experimentation is the key.
Since the embouchure may be not fully developed you could start experimenting by opening it out across towards the sounding edge of the hole, remembering to use the top lip to bring the air down “ into” the flute. You may have to back off a bit at the lower lip to avoid over-covering the hole and/or to keep a good position where the air stream strikes the sounding edge. Also, opening out towards the to the sides of the hole may help. Don’t look for immediate comfort, just check out how the sound behaves. And be ready to hit a few cul-de-sac’s on the way.
Lip placement may need some changes. In case you’re not doing this, try placing the edge of the lower lip against the edge of the hole but then let the support for the flute come from lower on your jaw, not by pressing the flute against your teeth. You’d have to roll the flute out as the flute goes lower on your jaw. This leaves great flexibility in your lips.
Might be of some help. Good luck!

Re: Flute “bad days”

OP asked “WHAT is happening?”. Since we cannot see them play, they are up to some self exploration. Try this.

By the book, stand in front of a mirror, finger the xxx’000 low G on your flute and blow it for 30 minutes with proper breathing. Repeat. (with or without the metronome). Most people will fail (or refuse outright).

The self-exploration question is - what prevents you from going for 30 minutes? Physical fatigue or mental fatigue? Do you become bored? Does your attention wander? Do extraneous thoughts intrude (“must wash dishes”, etc?). Do you feet/legs give out first and you want to sit down? Do you lips quit? Does your tongue quit? Does your breathing quit? Do your fingers quit (even though you are not moving them?).

Try this on your good day and on your bad day. Maybe you will identify at least if it’s a mental or a physical thing. After you identify a problem, you can work it.

Good luck.

Personal frustration is a choice/judgement.

If you know you’ll have good and bad days, then on the bad days, quickly find something else enjoyable to do when playing the flute isn’t working for you. Being frustrated doesn’t help.

Now, I’m not saying there isn’t value in all the suggestions for exercises, checking for leaks, moisture issues, etc., but if you’re playing for many days having nothing but success and then have a bad day and it’s not some issue with the flute, just acknowledge that it’s not happening today, walk away for a while and come back to it later.

If you had constant inconsistency day to day, then I’d have other suggestions much along the line of what others have offered, but you sound a lot like me, 95% great days, and 5% not. I’m happy with that ratio.

Other reasons not mentioned:

Any changes to your tongue, gums, teeth, cheek, throat?

I bit my tongue rather badly and had quite a bump on it for a while, which changed the shape of my mouth and altered my flute playing.

Similarly, after having a couple of wisdom teeth extracted, the shape of the inside of my mouth changed as well as it taking a long time for the effects of a elephant sized dose of lidocaine to wear off. Took me three months to get my flute playing back to normal.

Reading through this thread, it strikes me that one way you might get to the problem you describe is by starting with a configuration of flute and body and lip that is okay and gets you a good tone under good circumstances, but which either fails as circumstances become more marginal (fatigue, dehydration, dry flute, whatever) or which sometimes slips into a failing configuration when something slips out of its right place. This might be exacerbated if you have inconsistencies in the way you set yourself and your flute up for play.

So for example, since you say your typical playing day is two hours of good play and good tone, and sometimes this is interrupted by a failure of your tone, it might be that what’s happening is some minor shift in the way you’re holding the flute has taken your embouchure hole out of line with your air stream. And if on one particular day you happen to assemble the instrument with the head joint rotated a little more or less than usual with respect to the body of the instrument, that would make this more likely to happen, and it would be difficult to pinpoint the problem. Or, you might have a consistent setup and posture that usually works, but on one day you happen to be a bit dehydrated and that affects your lips, that could take your tone away entirely and again be hard to nail down as the problem. The latter could align well with your description of the problem, since playing the flute does tend to dry out the mouth a bit, which means you could start out feeling fine, play well for half an hour or an hour, dehydrating yourself all the while, and at some point you dry up and it just goes away.

If this is the case, then isolating the problem will be the key to solving it. That would involve first taking steps to make sure your address to the embouchure hole is consistent - not necessarily correct to begin with, but consistent - and then tweaking one variable at a time and seeing what helps. So for example, you might start by making sure you’re consistent in how you assemble the flute, getting everything aligned the same way each time, and then you might focus on consistent posture - if you play slouched over in your chair, slouch the same way each time, for example - and so forth. Identify variables, make them consistent, and then adjust them.

Honestly, extrapolating from my own experience in my own life, dehydration is probably the single biggest problem I have, particularly in winter. My lip just doesn’t cooperate when I’m dehydrated, and I often am at this time of year. If you make sure you drink enough water, and particularly that you you keep water nearby when you’re playing and refresh yourself between tunes, that might just be the thing you need!

I’m definitely not going to suggest that “it’s just that way” and that this is something you should just accept. There is a bit of a nondeterministic aspect to flute playing, but there’s plenty of things you can do to keep the variables bounded. I believe this is absolutely a solvable problem!

Remember that embooooooshuuuure (it’s a Halloween-sounding word, just like “paraploooooooweee”) is based on muscles conditioned a certain way. Muscles of all sizes are subject to performance variation depending on their condition, and some people don’t take care of that. Athletes study it and figured it out.

1) If you drink coffee or black tea regularly, or alcohol of any kind, you’re drinking a diuretic and losing electrolyte minerals and vitamins like calcium, potassium, magnesium, B vitamins, that are VERY important for muscle tissue performance. How much booze you drink guides how much you lose.
2) If you add a resulting dehydration, it gets worse and takes longer to remedy. If you were already dehydrated when you started drinking booze, even worse.
3) If you add exhaustion from performance, then the muscles need a couple days at least, WITH proper nutrition and rehydration, to process carbs and fats to have adequate energy of the right kind available the way nature designed it.
4) If you add in someone with poor dietary habits for whatever reason, expect more of a delay in recovery.

Embouchure muscles are relatively tiny. Don’t expect them to bounce back rapidly to peak condition after a big workout, and it takes consistent proper workouts to build the embouchure strength and stamina to allow fastest recovery rates. Practice and performance sessions should stop before you get to exhaustion levels, and if not, then plan to take a day or two off after. Some types of embouchure and bodybuilding exercises require peak output of some type, and again, if exercising at peak output (power or aerobics), including to exhaustion or not, expect to take a couple days off after, eat your veggies and a good diet and be sure you’re hydrated.

Re: Flute “bad days”

Actually, I would say that you want to find a way to make this something you _don’t_ pay conscious attention to - in other words, you want to make habits that put the flute where it needs to be, so you don’t have to think about it. If you can identify the things that matter - the things you’d be paying attention to - then you can probably come up with ways to automate that attention, which is what we mean when we say “form a habit”. So if consistent flute assembly is an issue, mark the flute so that you just have to align the marks when you’re putting the thing together - bam, there’s a habit. If posture is an issue, you can form a habit that leaves you in a good playing posture. And so forth - anything you can pay attention to consciously, you can probably make a habit of it, and if you want that thing to happen each time, a habit is much better than “paying attention”.

This is very much the mindset of a software guy, automating processes in order to free up the mind for more important stuff, but you can also see this in athletes. In baseball, a batter coming up to bat will usually have a little ritual they do before the come to the plate; the purpose of this ritual is to make sure their stance and orientation with respect to the plate and the pitcher and the first base line are all consistent, which eliminates variables and allows them to focus on the things that are not consistent, like the precise pitch that they’re going to try to hit. I don’t care much for golf, but I understand that golfers pay a lot of attention to their stance for very similar reasons. I would imagine that if you watch track events like the high jump, long jump, shot put, and so forth, you’ll also see similar behavior.

How you hold the flute controls how your lips present to the embouchure hole and therefore a lot of what makes your sound work. I had problems for years with the low d. Partly by thinking it needed to be pushed harder than all the other notes and therefore overdoing it, but also because I used the Eb key to vent on all the other notes, so when I raised my finger on it for the low D I was shifting the relationship between my lips and the flute with a resulting break up of the tone. Being aware and paying attention fixed this.
I have also experienced the sideways creep of the flute. Especially when sitting cramped in a session. To combat this I frequently roll the flute in all the way to feel the embouchure hole with my upper lip, which can somehow judge the position much better than my lower lip.
