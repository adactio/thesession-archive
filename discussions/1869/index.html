Let’s see, in no particular order…

Mmm. Well. That’s probably one of those questions with a million different answers. There’s normally, when a band gets put together, a really rather harrowing stage of putting together the repertoire, the patter, the look, and the mechanics of the show.

You don’t have to worry too much about the rep, at least, although you’ll probably want to make sure you’ve about four hours worth of rep for things like playing at weddings. Your tune sets have to change a bit for performances, since you’re actively entertaining people rather than playing for your own pleasure.

Some bands put together a whole look for the group, maybe not matching outfits or anything so uncool as that, but a certain kind of “look” to the thing, like “contemporary edged cool young things”, or “aging hippy” or “retro cool” or some such. You’ll want to have three or four “outfits” that you can just put on without thinking much of - this outfit for hot outdoor concerts, that one for playing a senior citizens home, and THAT one for weddings, and THIS one for when you really want to impress the cute young things… :)

Then you need ot make sure that you’ve your business processes in place: who books gigs? (easier to have one person in the band take care of contracts, for instance, as well as all the other minutae of getting a show organized), who owns the sound system?, who makes sure we have the check before we start playing?, etc. And for god’s sake, don’t go out without a contract, signed, and get a bank account with all of you as signers on it just for the band, that’s a help at tax time.

Also, be sure that everyone in the band knows where the band is on the level of priorities for each band member. If the entire band knows that Sunday morning church is more important than anything to one band member, and that another MUST take a child to soccer on a specific night, etc., then it makes it a lot easier, because you’ll always know that that time is set aside as not to be booked.

Then there’s the show itself -- work out a general order to the thing so you don’t have too many jigs or hornpipes or some such in a row; getting your patter down so it’s fun for the audience, who’s going to intro that set, who tells this story best about this song? who should never be allowed to tell jokes to the audience?

What kind of gigs do you want to do? What kind of gigs do you NEVER want to do? (AMF, for instance, doesn’t do stage shows, because none of us want the hassle, and we only do shows where we’re background music or at least not particularly expected to actively entertain. And we will never tour.)

Cut a demo. Make sure it’s a demo, not an actual CD for sale. Give it to everyone you know. Start beating the streets.

Decide as a group if you want to record -- it’s an expensive hassle, and you’d better know if it’s something you really want to do ahead of time so you can manage the finances (for instance, if there’s four of you in the band, split the take five ways, and the fifth portion lives in the band bank account for demos and recordings and gas or whatever).

Decide how you decide to take bookings -- can any member of the band book a gig, or must all of the members of the band agree to the date first? Do you have any alternates who know all your sets?

What else? If you end up specializing in a sort of performance, capitalize on it and sell it to your possible gigs. When I had a Christmas Caroling group, we specialized in retail. We had it down really well. We knew how to position ourselves within the stores to attract people into the stores. We knew how to stay out of the way of the displays and sales people. We even knew how to find a parking space quickly and efficiently at shopping malls at Christmas (mainly, follow someone walking out of the mall and ask if you can park in their spot). AMF specializes in pub gigs like wedding receptions and such. We know how to stay out of the waitstaff’s way and how to stay on their good side (for instance, making sure we tip ANYone how waited on us in any way at the end of the night out of top of the tips jar). We know the way most receptions run and keep an eye out for when they’ll want us to be quiet during toasts and such without having to be told. We realize that part of our duties as a pub band is helping them to pull people in to pay for food and bar, and ask our friends to show up and spend money as well as publicize that we’re there.

We provide posters, PR materials, and, if they’re providing a sound system, a stage and equipment schematic. We have business cards to hand out. I sometimes keep extra pr materials in my fiddle case for people who walk up to us during shows.

We have a regular rehearsal night set aside, even if we don’t always use it. These days we use it mainly to work on new sets.

Um. If I think of anything else, I’ll post it, but it’s 2:30 am now… :)

Advice for starters might be totally different to advice to experienced musicians getting together to form a band.

Research the band name - anyone else using it? Is it short, memorable, does it tie in with what you do? The name is what people remember you by - get a board with the name on to go in front of you (I got the local 6th form art students to design and produce our board). Stick with it - you waste all your hard work every time you change the name.

After 30 years, we still haven’t got down to a demo cd, as the lineup is flexible, we thought it wouldn’t give a true impression of who is in the band. We rely solely on personal recomendation or people coming to hear us (generally at other peoples weddings) - not much help until you get gigs, I know.
We get 70% of gigs from out website, but you need to work at it to get into the first page listings.
Business cards, yes, but we have never bothered with posters or blurb.

I suppose the main routes are - do you want to sing?
Do you want to play background music?
Do you want to play for dancing?
Do you want to accompany singers?
How far to you want to travel? Abroad?
Whichever route you take dictates whether you need a caller, band pad, singers/song pad, decent readers/buskers.
Your route should also be chosen for who else is doing similar in the area and is there a need for bands doing that. Similarly, if you want one, get a decent agent who specialises in your niche (unless you want to take all gigs thrown at you, even the unsuitable).

You have to be mega-organised with the paperwork - chase people up, keep a note of everyone who calls - even if you cannot do the gig as gigs sometimes dissapear, put everything in writing and get them to confirm, get a receipt book, keep records for the taxman - you can’t advertise if you are on the fiddle (ha ha). Keep a spreadsheet of gigs with start/end/location/players to keep everyone up to date with new gigs. Get an accountant and claim back everything.

The Musicians Union has a folk & roots section - join it - you will get your money back after one gig and free advice is at hand on all aspects.

Get some spare musicians into the band and rotate them so everyone learns the repertoire - can save on travelling to have occasional musicians dotted around.

Get out and hear/meet/chat to as many similar bands (if there are any) and pick up what you can. Swap work/musicians with them and they will reciprocate. The MU is still a good place to do this.

I will start another thread for pitfalls - gigs that mis-fire - something else new bands have to look for (and cope with).

Any further questions - fire away.

Re: Think twice

Danny, I tried to stay off this thread because I don’t have much good to say about bands…give me a plain old session any day. And I didn’t want to rain on your party. But Zina and Geoff sort of touched on what I think is the main issue when starting a band, especially one that evolves from a more casual structure (like a session). Expectations.

It’s crucial to make sure everyone’s on the same page about how serious you’re going to be--how much rehearsal time to put in, what the expected level of musicianship will be, who will be responsible for “fronting” the band, in short--how much *ego* is involved in the whole endeavor and whose personality will most shape the band’s character. Do this up front and as openly as possible. Otherwise it’ll come back to bite you in unpleasant places--“why is the drummer being paid as much as the lead players?” “Why did so-and-so get equal billing when she only sings one song?” That sort of thing. It’s easy to assume that the friendly, informal, unselfconscious attitudes that prevail at a good session will carry over to your band--but you’d be wrong. Putting a mic in front of a session player almost always brings out the ego--either as paralyzing shyness or They-Love-Me bravado that monopolizes the spotlight. Talk amongst your band members to find out who wants the spotlight and who would rather stay in the shadows. And just because someone wants to be the star, don’t necessarily let them run the whole thing. Likely someone else will be better at putting sets together, running the sound system, talking to clients, doing pr, etc.

I’ve seen ugly situations devleop over misunderstandings over such roles and responsibilities--when a band member runs the sound board and favors one instrument over another (to the point where one player could not hear himself on the monitors), or someone skipping rehearsals still expecting to receive equal pay for gigs, or letting anyone in the band book gigs (and then forgetting to tell the other band members). Playing music for money (and fame and glory ;-) magnifies the personalities of everyone involved. Be prepared for that.

Just be excruciatingly clear about this stuff before you start taking gigs. And, based on my own warped proclivities, think twice before you ruin a good session by becoming a band…..

Also on the subject of band names, our little group began life as “Sa Cheo,” as in, In the Fog. We liked the implied self deprecation, and all the jokes about how to pronounce it (Shuh-KAY-Oh, SACK-ee-Oh, and my favorite, SUCK--ee-Oh) gave us some stage patter. Problem was, no one else could figure out how to spell it, so the pubs would put up posters advertising a gig and just say “Irish music,” or worse, “Friday night, The Fog.” The final blow came when we agreed to open for another band at a sold out concert. Our guitarist, who’s an excellent Irish trad player with the rather un-Irish name of Jim Schulz, called the hall and told them our name, even spelling it out for them. So we were a little upset when we saw the posters, the articles in the newspaper, and the concert programs, all of which billed us as “Jim Schulz and friends.”

Of course, poor Jim got no end of ribbing about this, and still does. Whenever anyone asks, upon hearing the band name, what Sa Cheo mean, we’re sure to chirp up with, “It’s Gaelic for ‘The Jim Schulz Band.’”

Which reminds me, Danny, that a good way to name a band is to invent a fictitious leader and leave your groupies wondering who it is. So you could become, for instance, Mo Lester and the Gropers, or Big Head Ed and the Boys of Offaly…..

ELSE
