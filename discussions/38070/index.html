Re: Are Eb sessions real?

“E Flat sessions are proscriptive and anattempt to prohibit”

That is a huge generalisation, Richard. In the case of the session I mention in my first reply (3rd from the top), I believe that exclusivity was the primary motive (I apologise if that has set the tone of the whole discussion). But playing tunes in unconventional keys (whether by fingering them differently or by using differently tuned instruments) brings out different qualities in the music and should be explored. You could argue that insisting all tunes must be in played D and G (and a few other keys) is proscriptive, since it proscribes playing in all the other keys. Besides, exclusivity and inclusivity are not absolutes - a session has to have a degree of exclusivity to work (e.g. no people who want to play blues licks on sax all night, no session hogs, no Britney Spears songs…).

“Most fiddle players do not want to increase the tension on their instrument by tuning up.”

Some fiddlers, who regularly play Eb sessions, have a fiddle permanently tuned up a semitone. This is less harmful to the fiddle, I think, than repeatedly tuning up and down - an saves the bother of waiting for the strings to settle down every time you retune.

10-15 years ago, there was a small collective of musicians playing regular sessions in Limerick. As I remember it, they had two or three sessions a week, one of which was a designated ‘Eb session’. Certainly, the Eb session was ‘exclusive’ inasmuch as it excluded those that did not have a suitably tuned instrument, a capo, were not prepared to retune or did not have the technical skill to transpose up a semitone on the fly (the vast majority of trad players, I suspect). But it was inclusive of anyone willing and equipped to play a semitone above standard pitch, subject to the usual caveats that apply to any Irish traditional music session (many of which are flexible, to a greater or lesser degree). The only motive for playing in Eb was its musical value. I hope I make a clear distinction between this an the session I mentioned in my first reply.

As I said in my second reply, I prefer flat sessions (C, Bb etc.) to Eb ones. But that is just my taste. I think the trad world would be a duller place without differently-pitched sessions.

Re: Are Eb sessions real?

“Since when have fiddlers had to modify their playing to accommodate accompanists?”
That wasn’t what I was saying. When you start using drones, ringing strings or double stops you are adding harmony on the fiddle, in exactly the same way that an accompanist adds harmony, and you won’t want to use the III and VI for exactly the same reasons that a guitarist wouldn’t use III and VI. The rules of harmony are the same whether the notes are all played on one instrument or by a soloist and accompanist.

There is harmony implicit in even a single line melody, that is what keys are all about. Try playing a tune and humming a single note throughout. Almost always you will find the note you are humming is the tonic of the key the tune is being played in (occasionally you might find yourself humming the V instead, but 9 times out of 10 it will be the tonic.) So that tonic note, even when not actually being played, is implicit throughout the tune, and if you play a VI, just a semitone off, it will clash.

Perhaps the easiest way to see what is happening is to play about with a keyboard - play a simple tune with one hand and try holding individually notes with the other hand. You’ll find that you can comfortably hold the tonic probably for the whole tune, certainly for most of it. But try holding the VI note and the result is enharmony - the only time it harmonizes is when the tune itself hits the VI, which is usually only as a passing note. You certainly wouldn’t be able to hold the VI for a whole bar of any tune I can think of.

Paganini composed his first concerto in D, but it was advertised as being in Eb. Talking of old instruments in odd pitches makes me realise that is probably how Eb (and othe non-standard pitched) sessions originally came about - someone had a flute in Eb or a C/C# box or whatever and the fiddlers retuned their fiddles to play along. Then they realised it sounded excitingly different and began to make a habit of it. Of course, their is an element of exclusivity is such a session, and some people have learned to exploit that for the sole puprose of exclusion.

My feeling is (at the risk of sounding aerie-faerie), that mean-spiritedness comes through in the music, so it doesn’t ultimately make for a better session. In fact, the whole idea of changing the music just to make it harder or impossible for others to join in is completely self-defeating. There are far more respectful (to the music and to the excluded musicians) ways to define your boundaries - such as just saying, “Sorry - this is not an open session.” The ethics of holding such as session in a public space are open to debate but, at the very least, you must be prepared to shoulder the burden of turning away strangers that wish to join in. In my experience, sessions that are nominally ‘closed’ (i.e. paid gigs in ‘session’ format) are often happy to welcome another musician - maybe only because they hadn’t the heart to say “no”.

So, in conclusion, Eb sessions are, by definition, exclusive (of those without suitable instruments, unwilling to retune or lacking the skills to transpose). But whether the motive is purely to exclude or just to enjoy the experience of playing in Eb makes a big difference.
