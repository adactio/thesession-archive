The pickup is only half the story - you also need a suitable buffer amplifier - generically often incorporated into and referred to as a pre amp. The Fishman is very popular but it is very much at the lower end of current pick up technology, the reason so many players are sold one is that it is the only one stocked in many guitar and music shops. Remember that anything temporary/removable will always have serious reliability and consistency issues. Anything that fixes to the violin WILL affect the sound especially if fitted to the bridge - the “slot” that the V200 wedges into is there for very good reasons.

Violet - if you want real freedom of movement you should consider using a system that enables you to use a wireless transmitter …………. get rid of the lead and leap all over the place :)

Re: Pure Acoustic Pick ups

I’d go along with the comments above about pickups v microphones. I haven’t actually fitted a Skyinbow Pure pickup, but I have fitted a number of bridge pickups by other manufacturers and they are all pretty much the same - the sound quality is governed by the physics of how the sound is picked up rather than the particular pickup.

The pickups that fit into a hole in the bridge are much better than the ones that slip into the slot in the side of the bridge, but they are still not great. Because they are picking up the raw vibration of the strings rather than the whole sound of the fiddle, it’s like the difference between an electric and acoustic guitar, and it takes some heave equalization to get rid of the bow noise and make them sound anything like a violin.

If you want a decent sound plus the ability to move about the stage, by far the best option is a clip on microphone. I use a DPA4099, but there are cheaper alternatives from other manufacturers. The only disadvantage of the cheaper microphones is that they are less directional, so if they are positioned wrong they can pick up your breathing, and they are a bit more prone to feedback. The key thing is that you want a little microphone mounted on the fiddle, picking up the sound, not a pickup, picking up the vibrations.

Re: Pure Acoustic Pick ups

Violet, My DPA4099 was about £300 a few years ago, so they are probably a bit more than that now.
As titch mentioned, the big advantage of the 4099 is that (as far as I’m aware) it is the only clip on mike with a supercardioid response pattern - that means it picks up sound at the front (pointing at the soundboard/ff hole) and a little bit from the back (pointing at the ceiling) but virtually nothing from the sides, making it much less prone to feedback and other problems than other mikes.

If you are looking at other mikes the two things you really need to pay attention to are the response pattern and mounting position.

Most of the cheapest are omni-directional. Although they can give exceptional sound quality, they pick up sound equally from any direction, so breathing, bow noise and feedback can all become problems.

The better ones have a cardioid response, which means they don’t pick up sound coming from behind them.
That can be very useful, but you have to think about how you position the microphone - If it is on a gooseneck pointing down at the soundboard (like the DPA) the dead zone is pointing at the ceiling, which doesn’t usually have any major sound sources, so the cardioid mike used that way doesn’t give you any advantage over an omnidirectional one. The best place to mount a cardioid mike set into a little block of foam rubber just below the strings in front of the tailpiece, pointing forwards and slightly down, at the feet of the bridge. That way your face is in the dead zone, and it won’t pick up breathing noise (which is often the biggest problem with clip on mikes). If you watch traditional music on telly, you’ll see that this is probably the most common way to mike a fiddle these days, and if you are on a budget you can do it yourself - you just need a small microphone (maybe a cheap tie clip lavalier with the tie clip removed) and a lump of foam rubber (and the power supply/battery holder that came with the mike if it is an electret). The key point in choosing the mike is that it must have a cardioid response.

That arrangement will still give feedback problems if don’t think about what you are doing - the front of the mike is now pointing forwards and down - straight at the floor monitor if you put it in the usual position at the front of the stage. To minimize feedback you need to move the monitor to your left hand side, so that the mike is shielded by the body of the fiddle. (or better still use in-ear monitors, which solve nearly all feedback problems, I really don’t understand why anyone still uses floor monitors in this day and age. :-) )

I need to declare an interest before I reply to AlBrown’s comment as I’m a director of Pure Acoustic Ltd.

Al, to Kenny Johnson and I, the term “pure acoustic pickup” is anything but an oxymoron. We chose the name very carefully because it represents exactly what we believe that we’ve developed - a transducer/preamplifier system for acoustic instruments that delivers a pure and unprocessed sound. Designing our own battery-free circuitry has allowed us, for example, to build an audiophile-grade preamplifier into a custom violin chinrest. The system’s transducer is built by hand in Shetland (by Kenny) and its proprietary construction is the key to the sound quality of the Pure Acoustic Active Transducer System (to give it its full name).

On the more general points raised in this thread, there is no doubt in my mind that, in an ideal world, there is no better way to amplify acoustic instruments than to use high-quality condenser microphones running through high-quality mixing consoles (operated by high quality engineers) that are being fed into high-quality PA systems. During my time with Silly Wizard, that’s exactly what we had - and I loved it!

However, in situations where any one of those elements (or parts thereof) are missing, I’ve found over the years that pickups are definitely the best tools for making sure that the audience can hear acoustic instruments.

For me, it’s the consistency of sound across different venues and PA systems that makes pickups so useful. Have a listen to the video of Maggie Adamson (fiddle) and Brian Nicholson (guitar) on the pureacoustic.com website (http://www.pureacoustic.com/pure-acoustic-players/) and then compare the sound from that video to the other YouTube videos where they’re playing in different locations through different PA systems - you’ll hear what I mean. It’s also worth comparing those videos to the one where Maggie and Brian are on stage playing just through microphones……

As Titch said, “Not all pickup systems are the same….”

Wendy - and anyone else who’s interested - if you’re passing anywhere near Leighton Buzzard or Lerwick you’re more than welcome to drop me a line and arrange to try out a Pure Acoustic system.

Re: Pure Acoustic Pick ups

Hi Wendy, I’ve just picked up on this discussion. Hope my experience of finding a pickup is of some help to you.

I play fiddle in a band and two years ago when we got together bought a Skyinbow electric fiddle, made by Kenny Johnson in Shetland. Having tried other electric fiddles I was delighted with the sound quality (as well as the construction) of the Skyinbow. To my ear it was as close to an acoustic as an electric could be. However, it’s not an acoustic instrument and I hadn’t played in an amplified band before and had only ever played my acoustic fiddle into a mic. I found difficulty in the band in adapting to a situation where I wasn’t hearing myself ‘immediately’, as with my acoustic fiddle. I’m sure people will point out any delay in hearing between playing an acoustic fiddle and an electric is minuscule but, nevertheless, I was simply uncomfortable with what for me was a problem. I also found myself constantly tripping over the lead from the fiddle to the mixer desk. We considered different options to overcome my difficulties (two of the guys in the band are very knowledgeable, unlike myself, about amplified sound, electrics etc.) but to no avail. Eventually, I decided to use my acoustic fiddle instead of electric and bought a Pure Acoustic Pickup.

This decision was in no way a criticism of the Skyinbow electric fiddle. I still believe the Skyinbow to be an exceptional instrument. My thinking was if I could use my acoustic amplified then this would solve my problem. I found the Pure Acoustic Pickup to be excellent in this respect and I endorse Bob Thomas’s comments in this thread. Myself and the guys in the band all had nothing but good things to say about the sound quality. I should say at this stage that I have a very good acoustic fiddle. It’s a French copy of a Strad, c1860 and been in the family a long time. I guess what I’m saying is that no matter how good the pickup, it can only reflect the instrument (and the player!).

Thinking I had cracked the problem I then had to overcome the issue of tripping over the lead. My solution to this was to go wireless. Again, having tried various setups (some much more expensive), I opted for the Intellitouch FREEDOM ONE digital wireless system with compact transmitter and wireless pedal tuner. The ‘bug’ went straight into the chin rest jack of the Pure Acoustic Pickup. Then another problem developed…..

The Pure Acoustic worked great with a lead but a ‘knocking’ sound arose after a while playing with the ‘bug’ connected to the jack instead of a lead. I got in touch with Kenny in Shetland and sent him the pickup. He’s a really helpful guy and got back to me very quickly. Apparently the proximity of the ‘bug’ transmitter to the chin rest caused the noise. Before Kenny sent the pickup back to me one of the guys in the band made a very short lead that went into the chin rest jack and the bug into the other end. This eliminated the ‘knocking’ and I let Kenny know. So, everything sorted? Well,….

The short lead that I now had sat over my shoulder…….most of the time! What I really wanted was no lead at all. After talking to Mike Ward at Vintage Strings in Dundee I bought a MiSi Acoustic pickup. Mike was fitting a new bridge for me but of course the Pure Acoustic Pickup was fitted into the bridge already installed. I decided to remove the Pure Acoustic Pickup and bridge and have Mike fit a new bridge with the MiSi pickup, which just fits into the wing slots of the bridge. It’s an active battery-free preamp encased in a Carpenter style jack designed to work with the Kremona piezo pickup. Power up with the adaptor for 60 seconds gives 8 hours playing time.

So, Wendy, the band and myself are now happy (maybe the guys would have been better just getting a new fiddler!). Sorry if the above is too lengthy, I was just hoping yourself or anyone else in similar circumstances to mine might find some of my experiences helpful. Whatever you decide, sincere best wishes. My complete setup is: my acoustic fiddle fitted with a MiSi Acoustic pickup; the Intellitouch FREEDOM ONE wireless transmitter and receiver; connected to a Mackie 16-channel mixing desk; connected to a Bose-style column.

Re: Pure Acoustic Pick ups

It ain’t so easy for the violin.
Accepting loosing totally the beautiful acoustic sound when amplifying is step 1.
Then creating a psycho acoustic experience of an acoustic violin in a room or hall is step 2.

For this piezo doesn’t do it for me, it is tiring to my ears after less then 10 minutes.
Most piezo changes the sound too much since most systems dampen the bridge.
Barcus Berry came far with their violins I must say, and definitely so did Zeta. Jean-Luc Ponty sounds great on it, so we must know it is also the player who is very important for the end-result.

For my purposes I use a Schertler electro-static microphone, Stat-V, produced by Schertler from Swiss.
I does not dampen the bridge. No feedback problems until really extremely loud. Great source to use effects.
For best situation use both Schertler Stat-V and DPA 4099. DPA gives air tot the sound and the Stat-V gives core. Use it with a high class pre-amp like Headway or Arial and a class A reverb and the ear accepts it and the mind believes it.

Good luck.
