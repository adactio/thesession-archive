Free drink for musicians

Hi folks!
For half a year We have been running a monthly session in the most famous irish bar of Bremen, Germany. Its going strong and sessions may last 3-4 hours and music can be heard outside on the street as well. Right from the start we had an arrangement with the landlady that we get tips from the people listening to us. We divide the money and get reduction on our drink.
We are 8 to 18 musicians! However, we feel now as we draw a fair amount of people in the bar, the landlady should offer a first guaranteed drink for free. What is common practice at your session? Any advice? Thx for your help! And you are always welcome to join in!

Re: Free drink for musicians

The usual answer from me ..“It depends”.
My experience is that the arrangement varies from pub to pub, locality to locality, session to session.

Free drink is nice gesture especially for the more casual/non leading participants in a session who may be contributing quite a lot but are not necessarily the leaders or mainstays. The latter may also being getting paid for their “tunes” as well as free drink.

Personally, if I enjoy visiting a particular session, I don’t expect anything either in terms of free drink or remuneration. Nor if we’re just “borrowing” the space or room for the evening or afternoon whether on a “one off” or even fairly regular basis.

Of course, if a particular session is bringing in the punters or has been arranged with this purpose, then payment for the core musicians and/or some free drink is the least which should be done. Even then though, I’d probably just accept the one drink. Although, I’m much more temperant these days, this wasn’t always the case. So, it wouldn’t be fair to take advange. :-)

However, in many cases, sessions don’t necessarily bring in extra customers and are maybe even seen are a nuisance to some of the regulars. It doesn’t help that many players are non drinkers these days or just drink less(like me)than they used to do. So, in such places, the owner/landlord is actually doing us a favour. It can still work well though if there’s an adjacent room or snug/lounge or even a corner of the room which would normally be empty where musicians can play and other customers can listen..but only if they are really interested.

Re tips.

Again, that depends. It’s nice to be nice and, especially if we are getting free drink or cash, we should reward the staff for their work and for having us. It need not be done each time we visit the bar or even every night. It all depends on the circumstances again. Maybe a larger donation or gift every so often, especially at Christmas might be more appropriate in a situation where musicians aren’t getting paid of receiving free drink on a regular basis?

Re customers tipping the musicians….

This is actually still a rare thing “Here in Scotland” and has never really been the custom although it has become more prevalent in recent years too. Same as “tip jars” for bar staff and so on. In the old days, the practice was more along the lines of “Have one for yourself” or a worker was given a personal tip for special service. These days, it’s more communal and tips are divided.

Again, I wouldn’t expect tips just for being part of a session but I know other players who would disagree. It can be a nice gesture by the punters but we shouldn’t regard them as a right nor a substitute for proper remuneration if that is considered necessary.

Re: Free drink for musicians

Every situation is different, so it’s really down to being between you and the venue to come up with your own arrangement. If the session has a “leader” or “anchor” that did the original arrangement with the publican, then it should probably be left to them to negotiate if you think more compensation would be in order. That’s best done at a time other than during a session, where it can be a relaxed, private conversation. And it’s best to keep in mind that the session and the venue should be working together, not working against each other.

I have seen session compensation go many different ways.

One of my sessions was originally offered our entire food tab, but paid for drinks. But for the first year, there wasn’t much business, so they just cut it down to a $10 credit on each person’s tab (for either food or drink). But over the 12 years that session has gone on, the session night is one of their busiest, and out of the blue, the venue started paying us a few years ago, we didn’t ask for it.

I have seen scenarios from no compensation whatsoever, to a $100 tab for drinks and they would just warn us when it was getting low, to free drinks all night. And we never used to put out tip jars, but at one of my sessions, people tip us regularly, and always complain that we don’t have a tip jar. So at that session, we just keep a pint glass on the table so that people have a place to put tips.

But I will echo what others have said. Especially in the US, where wait staff make the majority of their money off of tips, it’s important to make sure that your servers are compensated. But it still makes sense even where you don’t have the same tipping culture that we have in the US. The more the staff likes having you there, the more successful the session will be. Same goes for the publican. If they feel like you have the pub’s best interests in mind as well as yours, it’s more likely to be easy to have conversations about things like compensation.

Re: Free drink for musicians

If the bar is smart, they’re going to look at pints for the musician not as a gift or as payment (though they might present it that way) but as advertising. The musicians might not always be the center of attention, but everyone in the place is going to at least look their way from time to time, and if they see a bunch of people without beverage, they’re going to get the wrong idea (from the perspective of the till, at least). So rather than looking at pints for musicians as lost revenue, you want to look at them as encouragement to the rest of the customers. The way that plays out is going to depend on the specific situation, but a reasonable system for all concerned would be to offer the first pint free for players. That’s going to make the players feel welcome, and it’s easier for all concerned than trying to keep track of a tab for the players.

The economics of it should work out pretty well for the bar as well. You’re talking about, for a typical session in these parts, less than $100 in beer for live musicians that’ll play for three or four hours at a stretch if you let them, and the music they’re playing is almost perfectly suited to moving pints across the bar. Assuming there are some reasonably experienced players in the room, that’s going to add up to a good night for everyone.

All of that being said, I go to the session to play music. If I have to buy my own pints, I’m not too bothered by that. If the cost of a pint makes a difference to me, I’m pretty sure that getting that pint is very much the least of my worries.

I’ve seen a few pubs in the UK which actively promote folk sessions, but the majority do not and both musicians and punters have to rely on word of mouth. In my experience most pubs don’t see a session as a hook to bring in customers. Some allow it because they enjoy the music, and others because they see their pub as part of the community and make space for community activities.

Sessions are primarily for the enjoyment of musicians, not to entertain the customers. In many cases the session has been in a separate room, so if anyone wants to listen they have to come and seek it out.

Payment, whether in cash or kind, is not usually expected. Sometimes the landlord will bring in jugs of beer and perhaps sandwiches, but usually this is a token of appreciation rather than expected payment.

I’m sure there are sessions which operate on a more transactional basis, but in my experience they are a minority.

So from this thread I’m seeing a clear indicator of what might produce free drinks; that the session host understands clearly how the musicians will generate extra income, whereby enticing the musicians with a payout of some free beers produces an exponentially higher income from the extra visitors arriving as drawn by the music and socializing. In harmony with that, the musicians consider how they’ll contribute to increased business and they must deliver that effect or the deal falls apart. The scale of this agreement would obviously adjust according to the size/count of the session musicians/audience.

Hence, a new equation that separates that grain from the husks. For, if you go at this with the attitude of wondering how your group of musicians can reliably earn your free beers, in the eyes of the session/pub host, you might have a chance at attaining some free beers if you produce the accommodating situation, but if you go at this without considering or accommodating the issues the host has, and just want free beers from strangers you can scam, you’re not a responsible session participant, you’re a town drunk or first year economics student with little knowledge of context.

Re: Free drink for musicians

We musicians enjoy playing, sure. I frequently hear bartenders and waitresses tell me they enjoy their work too; work for which they are paid. All businesses have wages to pay, electricity, suppliers and other expenses to pay. So, by the way, with the exception of wages, do the rest of us.

Those amongst us who depend on our playing (not only in pubs but anywhere) to pay the bills do not always expect publicans to pay a full hourly wage for our work in pulling in customers with session playing. But the expectation of some gesture of welcome and even payment in some form is not unreasonable. The publican is not doing us a favour by tolerating our hobby. He or she is gaining financially from our presence and talents and some recognition beyond mere tolerance is certainly justified. Some may be content with no recognition or compensation, but that’s not true for all of us. Why should our contribution to the publican’s bottom line be free? The suggestion that we players are taking up seats that would otherwise be filled by drinks-buying patrons is false. We players can rest assured that a significant number of patrons are there because there is music. Ipso facto the presence of sessions players is a net gain for the publican. I’ve never seen a publican turn anyone away. Not at all, they just stuff ’em in.

I have no experience of sessions in the UK, but quite a lot in Canada and Ireland. In Canada it is unusual for a publican not to provide at least one free drink for every player, or in some cases a stipend, say $100, to the session organiser who then turns around and buys the players drinks. By the way, with pints costing upwards of $8 (or Euros!), that $100 represents a tiny fraction of a bar’s daily intake.

Canadian punters commonly buy rounds of drinks for players. My experience in Irish pub sessions has unfortunately been quite different. At best, either a jug of water or nothing at all is provided to the players; and this in pubs where it is obvious that the punters are there at least as much for the music as for the company. I have yet to be offered a drink by an Irish publican or patron. I’m not sure if this is just another example of the “deep pockets and short arms” phenomenon of which we Celts are commonly accused, but as an Irishman I always find this embarrassing in front of visitors from away. As a Canadian in Ireland I don’t say anything, not wishing to be branded as—perish the thought—an “ugly Canadian”.

Re: Free drink for musicians

Jack,

We will inevitably get conflicting opinions re this topic as every session arrangement is different and there could be any number of reasons for their existence.

Yes, I agree that if a session is specifically set up to entertain and/or attract the punters then musicians should be paid and some free drink is a welcome bonus.

However, many sessions are spontaneous or exist in spite of the other customers. No harm in the landlord making a gesture, of course, but it shouldn’t be expected.

In many cases, at least “Here in Scotland”, sessions don’t necessarily bring in more people. Otherwise, they wouldn’t be cancelled when something better comes along…e.g. during The Fringe and so on.
It’s also the case that many musicians prefer premises to be a little quieter and may even enjoy playing in a side room, snug, lounge etc away from the main hub bub. So the only extra custom is that of the players themselves.
