How many tunes are enough, or a conversation on repertoire

In reading the thread about Tunes types, ranking best and worst, I reflected on what makes some tunes great and fun to play, and others banal and mediocre/boring to play. The larger point being that people’s choices are subjective about what tunes are better/greater than others. In terms of repertoire, in my own case, when I was just started learning tunes, I wanted to learn the newest shiny tune that everyone was playing in the session. The problem was there were so many to learn at the beginning. I felt I had to learn them all, even if I didn’t particularly care for them. My car was the one with the bumper sticker that read: He who dies with the most tunes wins. Once a tune was learned, it was off the the next shiny new (to me) tune to learn, and so on. An epiphany arrived when I was at Camp Harmony one Christmas and played with clawhammer banjoist Heath Curdts. He said words to the effect that life’s too short to learn/play tunes you don’t like. He said he only plays tunes he likes. Wow. What a revelation to me. That meant it was OK not to have to learn or play on every tune if I didn’t particularly care for it. I also now see that back then I had an attitude that some tunes were ‘beginner’ tunes, and I wanted so much to be seen as someone who could play the advanced tunes/repertoire with the ‘big boys’, that I convinced myself that the ‘beginner’ tunes weren’t as good as the more advanced tunes. I know now that’s obviously not the case; I enjoy the tunes because they’re great tunes, and fun to play. Maybe this is a common arc in the evolution of players and their learning repertoire.

Re: How many tunes are enough, or a conversation on repertoire

I think the thing about the social element of tunes is spot on - often times just associating a tune with a person, group, or session, means that it can gain added layers of meaning that help guide you as you keep playing it. This can also help with the muscle-memory elements of tune learning, by trying to think of the contexts in which I first learned a tune or first played it at a session, etc.

That being said, sometimes your gut tells you a tune isn’t worth learning, and very occasionally I’ve found that I was right all along about certain tunes - no recording, setting, arrangement is able to convince me that I’m dealing with something I want to learn. Some of those things can change over time, too - my attitude towards fiddle-heavy reels with lots of string crossing-intervals has slowly softened as I’ve gotten better at the music. But there are maybe a dozen tunes across various traditions that I’ve encountered, put a few hours of work into, and just gone cold on, with very little interest to return to them. Sometimes I welcome the challenge of working with a stubborn tune like that, but I have to really be in a good mood.

Speaking for myself, I’ve made a point of trying to pursue the tunes that either a) really catch my ear at a session or on a shuffling playlist or b), a more recent phenomena, that I manage to get a pass under my fingers if I’m trying to ear-learn at a session. According to my tune-book here, I’ve got something like 120 tunes in grasping distance - probably half of which I could start with maybe 10 minutes to sit and rack my brains, and probably 10% of which I could start right this second when asked by a well respected session host. (A good thirty are aspirational and I just saved them to not forget).

Is that enough? Doesn’t feel like it… but it has given me enough of a vocabulary to be able to quietly play along with a fair amount of the tunes called at the local sessions.

↳ Posted by AdamM on August 29th, 2023.

Re: How many tunes are enough, or a conversation on repertoire

People play this music with various purposes and goals in mind. But if we approach it as lifelong learners, we never reach “enough.” There’s always more to learn. More tunes, more technique, more musicality.

I’ve played this music for more than 50 years, can start and lead 1,500+ tunes at high-octane sessions. And I’m happy to learn new tunes every week, to work on improving my mechanics and chops, to strive for more fluent musicality.

In my experience, the notion of learning 1 tune really well vs. basic competency on 100 tunes is a false dichotomy. If you want to be welcome at most sessions, you have to do both. It’s not an either/or choice—you’ll improve more quickly and more comprehensively if you put daily time into polishing one tune AND adding new tunes to your quiver. Really drilling into one tune can teach you loads about fundamental through advanced technique, intonation, tone, ornamentation, differences in settings, variations, etc. And learning new tunes creates a conveyor belt of fresh material to keep challenging you as you hone old tunes. Often, each new tune has something new to teach you as well. Those lessons then influence how you play your old tunes that you thought you’d mastered. Over time, the more tunes you learn, the more musical ideas you incorporate into your playing, the more well-rounded you become at playing the tunes you know. It’s cyclical.

Re: How many tunes are enough, or a conversation on repertoire

A few quick thoughts about repertoire, in no particular order:
* I’ve been at this trad music thing for over four decades now, and still feel that I should be a better player than I am for all that time put in already. Still learning a lot, and enjoying it. Sure as heck wasn’t at 80% after three years---more like 20%.
* I see some players go for getting a huge repertoire---they seem to know everything that gets started at session--but their technique on the instrument suffers as a result. Speaking as a fiddle player, that would be bowing patterns, ornaments, intonation, and on and on. I’ll take fewer tunes played well and with good understanding and phrasing, over a big pile of them that sound all alike because of this lack. And often played a breakneck speed that kills the phrasing.
* As for how many to learn? Yes for sure, there is a “basic” repertoire that will get you going at most any session. Definitely go for that. There are lots of lists around, along with the Comhaltas books, etc. But beyond that, I’ve learned a huge number of tunes just from playing at our local sessions. Always trying to keep up with the tune heads, although have learned to relax if I don’t know everything. (See above about technique suffering at the expense of tune learning.) Although if a tune worms its way in to my brain, I’m cooked and will need to get it under the fingers. They have a way of endearing themselves!
* And yes, some tunes will just never get to that point of either making sense or just never be enjoyable to hear, so leave them be and don’t angst about it. I’m a lot more confident now about what I choose to learn than even just a few years back.
* One tune can take as long as ten others to get in to. (Looking at your, Finbarr Dwyer). And I heartedly agree with the previous comment about the ‘cyclical’ nature of the learning process. I’m continually coming back to tunes played for years, adding new or different notes, phrasing, ornament, etc. Have been unsatisfied for years about how I played one section of Limestone Rock, but recently saw a clip of Joe Burke playing it. Was an ‘aha’ moment.
* ’Nuf said for now. Sorry to carry on. Great, and fun, subject!
