Re: chords

You’ll find that there are lots and lots of people who have published editions of trad tunes with chords included. That folkguitar link is one of them. You’ll find that they seldom agree on what the chords are supposed to be, and if you try their chords, you’ll find that they’re often not what you would play if you were using your own ears.
That should tell you something important.

Accompaniment in this music is difficult to do well, and it really requires that you know the music well. You’re not going to do it well by reading chords off a web page, and if you don’t do it well, the people you’re playing with will probably be too polite to smash up your guitar for you, but they’ll be thinking about it.

So I’d say your best bet would be to learn the tunes, maybe on a whistle or something, and when you’ve spent five or ten years learning to play them, you’ll probably start to get a pretty good idea of what you might play on guitar that won’t get people thinking about that John Belushi scene from Animal House.
At that point, you should think about playing accompaniment.

Re: chords

See what you started, Bran Rogers? From the responses here, no matter how bickering and disrespectful they seem, you can learn a LOT from this.

First thing to learn is that it’s a can of worms. Even extremely good accompanists are often bashed a bit on these forums. Why, you might ask? Well, because this music is complete without any accompaniment at all, and some players actually prefer to play without accompaniment.

Second lesson to learn is that even if you are accompanying the music, it doesn’t necessarily fit within the confines of what you might be used to. Some of the more revered accompanists will actually play without ever using chords. They’ll play a combination of the melody, along with harmonic counter-melody.

Third lesson to learn is that the rhythm is provided by the melody players, not the other way around. And you need to feel that rhythm in your very core. If you disrupt that rhythm by either not understanding it, or trying to impose your own take on it, then you will be most unwelcome in most situations.

The last lesson to learn is that you must be careful, because this can be a slippery slope. Take it from the voice of experience. I started out trying to accompany this music on guitar, by playing chords. My first teacher wanted me to get familiar with a few tunes to that I could start to learn and feel the rhythm and harmonic changes that are inherent in the tunes, so he had me learn to flat pick a couple of tunes on guitar. From that experience, I became immediately aware that I would rather play the tunes than accompany them. And that started the slippery slope slide directly down into playing banjo! Consider yourself warned! ;-)

Brian, find some tune players and get playing. It’s only through playing this music with others that you’ll be able to start to hear (and hopefully play) accompaniment. Sure you can sit in your bed room and practice specific things but you need a direction in which to move, a direction that you will not find in a book, a direction which is dictated by the music and the individual spin put on the music by those playing it.

One good point raised in this thread is that the tunes have their own rhythm or pulse which varies in quality depending who’s doing the playing. The idea is to play an accompanying rhythm that compliments this. Chords aren’t as important as rhythm, you need to be playing “the right chords” but often you’ll find that you just need a few played in the right place to do the job. There are no hard and fast rules and you’ll find that what tune players want to hear from the accompanist, varies, some times dramatically, from one to the other.

So on your quest for tune players to play with seek out the ones who present their music as “what can you do with this” rather than the ones who’ll say “ this is what I want to hear”. Onwards and up wards…….

@Bran

Consider, if you like, Victorian hymn tunes. Such tunes commonly progress in whole-tone jumps, eg.

c e g

With the above phrase, you could back with a CMaj chord, and no note within the chord would clash with the tune.

Now consider Irish traditional music. Such tunes commonly progress in semitone jumps, eg

efg

Whatever chord you select, there is likely to be a clash somewhere.

Which is why backers commonly disagree on which chord is the most suitable to set with any particular phrase within a melody.

And that’s also the reason why many folks maintain that there should be no backing at all.

To tastefully back “on the fly” an ITM melody that you have never heard before *is* possible - but it’s a very rare skill - rarer than you might imagine.

You’ll usually find that those who can do it properly can also play the tunes on a melody instrument, and also have a good understanding of the tonal centres, modes, gapped scales etc. all of which have a bearing of the selection of suitable chords.

My website may be of some assistance to you in this respect:

http://www.intermix.freeuk.com/
