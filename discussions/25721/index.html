The Small Mountain Hut Band?

Confession: this is a bit of an overspill from the epic ITM thread, but here goes anyway.

I did like Nicholas’ allusion to “In the Italian Style” i.e. what many of us play is music “In the Irish style” rather than the purer definition of ITM. I think I can live with that.

But imagine you got good as a session musician, got together with a few mates, started playing together in a more planned way, named yourselves the SMHB (see above), did a few festivals, and got a recording contract. You released an album called “Out of the fog, into the rain”. You play good quality Irish trad-derived music… BUT…. your band is made up entirely of Englishmen. Or whoever - but not Irish.

Would you, the consumer, really want to buy that CD? Or pay top whack to see them at Celtic Connections? I really am not sure I would. Why? Because they somehow ain’t the real thing. And indeed, would you (the band) ever have got the recording contract in the first place?

O.K., so the odd Englishman made it into the line-up of the Battlefield Band once upon a time (though as a Geordie, he may hardly count ;-) ) and the same outfit has a Yank for a piper at present - but beyond that, how many musicians in the top groups come from anywhere other than The Homeland? I don’t meant to be racist or anything, but even despite Mike Katz’s excellent playing, you kind of know he’s still an interloper.

And those non-native bands who did use some of the music (I am thinking of the likes of Fairport and Whippersnapper) always seemed to mix it and ‘sell’ it as generic folk music, while downplaying the origins of their Irish/Scottish pieces.

I do entirely include myself in the above dilemma - but I suspect the situation is not a coincidence, no matter how we try to reassure ourselves that nationality matters not a jot in this…

Doesn’t mean I’m about to stop playing, though…

As for early Fairport, I doubt if they were *that* clued-up on the different traditions of these islands and on whatever courtesies or protocols their devotees might expect players of their music to observe. English folk and trad at the time were more or less up for grabs by anyone who wanted to breeze in and re-invent it - Fairport probably saw it as intriguing stuff from the attic that they could play with, rather than as something with maybe quite a formidable ongoing life. Scottish and Irish trad *were* that, back in the late Sixties, but English trad was - or should I say, seemed - little more than an antiquarian hobby.

As London kids, they quite likely assumed Scotland, Wales, indeed Ireland, were simply extensions of England to all intents and purposes, ROI statehood apart. It was and is surprisingly easy for people to do this, who grow up in some English environments. Scotland seemed to have a particular corner in ghost stories and gory ballads - that was about it.

Dave Swarbrick’s fiddling hardly indicates he gave a monkey’s for any UK or Irish tradition. It could work OK in the Fairport combo and band sets, doing their stuff in their particular way, but solo, it’s the equivalent of having your eardrums operated on by witch doctors.

These are subjective ramblings that someone out there may well correct.

Now, do we see trad music as a form of art or not? And I mean what we do personally, as well as the form generically. If what we are doing is ‘just a bit of fun’ (to quote a criticism made of my art-based arguments in a totally different field) then we are perhaps less concerned about adulterating what we do so long as it is enjoyable.

I feel highly ambivalent about what I personally do - I gain huge enjoyment from it; no, more than that, I gain a form of self-expression (isn’t that what art is about?). My friends and family seem to like what I do enough to have requested multiple copies of the CD I burned from my recordings.

Those who hear my music will not get a true sense of the power and beauty of traditional music. I may be contributing more to its dilution that its sustainment.

Conversely, I am entirely aware of those facts, and accept that being what I am, it can’t really be otherwise. I will continue to work to improve the situation. But here’s the rub: I make no cultural claims for my music at all. I don’t consider it to be Irish/Scottish music, because it isn’t, even though the tunes originate from there. There will always be a bit of me that regrets that fact, but it cannot be otherwise.

But when I go to hear a performance or buy a CD, I do want to have a ‘cultural experience’ - to know that I am hearing something that is at least closely related to the ‘real thing’ - which I suppose is a form of compensation for my own shortcomings. That ultimately involves hearing people who are properly embedded in their tradition, not those who are (even good) copyists.

I can understand that that may sound trite to those who either have very regular access to the music, of those for whom it is their own culture in any case, but don’t forget that there are many of us for whom an experience of ‘proper’ trad. music (i.e. as played by native exponents) is quite a highlight in the calendar.

You may also have (correctly) gained the impression that I am not over-enamoured with what is left of English culture, and I am not above admitting that this is probably to do with a personal search for a culture that has a stronger and more appealing identity than the one I grew up in.

I grew up in a suburban American home. My dad’s family came from Roscommon, but by the time they made it to the States, they did everything they could to erase their Irishness (or no one would hire them, or educate them, or give them the time of day).

I stumbled on the music on my own, and was lucky to orbit near folks like John Vesey and later Kevin Burke. I didn’t care where they came from, I just wanted to play fiddle like they did, to make music that sounded like their music. Vesey and Mick Moloney steered me toward recordings by Bobby Casey, Paddy Canny, and John Kelly, so I listened to a lot of Irish-born players. But the real connection came from sitting in front of Burke and being given a tune he got directly from Bobby Casey, and being told, “When you’re given a tune, it links you to all the players who gave it forward before.” It was a direct link to Casey. In Burke’s words, “You become part of the community.”

I believe that to this day. It’s how the music was passed along for generations, and it didn’t matter if you lived next door, the next village over, or 3 counties away. Why should it matter where you live?

I don’t play fiddle “in the Irish style.” I play Irish traditional fiddle. Yes, there’s no small irony that I do this in the boonies of North America, but that irony wouldn’t exist if my playing didn’t sound Irish, as authentic as any decent session fiddler in Feakle or Ballydehob or Gortnalone.

It’s not where you come from, or where you parents came from, or whether you’ve ever even been to Ireland. It’s how deeply immersed in *the music* you are that matters. It takes time and a life of listening and passion and a little luck. But it doesn’t require an Irish birth certificate.

As a history teacher, you yourself are surely aware of the dangers of making broad generalizations like “Celtic cultures of Britain have always defined themselves in *opposition* to English culture.” Often times they do NOW in an over simplistic (at times) way but didn’t always. Ireland was unquestionably treated as a colony by the English government and from about the seventeenth century to 1922 (longer if you include the Troubles) was engaged in on and off war with them. The 1801 Act of Union was Parliament’s response to the 1798 Rising, an effort to control an unruly colony.

Scotland was pretty divided itself and while certain parties therein were engaged in on and off fighting with English power, others were allied with them. After the Union of the Crowns in 1603, they had the same king. Not through violence or any kind of invasion, but through James VI, King of Scots ascending the English throne after Elizabeth I died. As she had been childless, he was the closest viable relative through intermarriage of other family members. The Union of Parliaments in 1707 was also something a majority in the Scottish Parliament agreed to, rather than something that was forced on them as the result of unrest. They thought they could get out of a financial hole and also, groups that were against it were so disorganized and busy fighting amongst themselves, couldn’t get it together to oppose it. It was pretty unpopular, but needless to say, not the last time someone’s legislative body passed an unpopular act. In any case, the last uprising in Scotland (if you discount some of the protests that went on during the Clearances) was the 1745 Jacobite Rising. While the Jacobites were ostensibly fighting against the English crown, it was more of a matter of which Highland clans allied with the Stuarts and which did not.
