Re: Are We Ambitious Enough for Irish Traditional Music? Article

I couldn’t be more peripheral to what’s going on at professional levels of the music industry, to be fair, but from the perspective of my sofa, I don’t see the issues that Toner Quinn sees.

“As long as this culture continues to produce highly skilled musicians they will want to make a career out of their playing, and while we are very good at education and transmission from one generation to the next, we are not so great on what happens once these musicians grow up. ”

I would argue that more traditional musicians -- not just in Ireland -- are producing more quality albums than ever before. Indeed, advances in recording technologies have freed people from the traditional model of contracts with record labels and enabled them to produce their own albums and sell them online. I’ve downloaded or bought many albums from the artist directly. It’s a competitive field now to make a living from playing trad, but between house concerts, YouTube channels, etc., there are more ways to get your playing out there. As the article says, certain elements of trad have entered the mainstream, which means people who aren’t really trad listeners are buying trad-ish albums (i.e. Peatbog Faeries, Manran, Shooglenifty, Talisk, etc.) and going to those gigs.

Still, at the end of the day, the numbers of people who can actually make a living playing will always be tiny. Just as a fraction of the kids kicking a football on a field will end up as professional players.

That said, I absolutely think traditional arts (and all arts) should be funded, but in a world where the pot is limited, I’d rather see funding go to increasing access to music and dance. In a perfect world, both the accomplished professional and the poor kid from the North side of Dublin could be supported, and maybe Ireland does do both. After all, I’m writing from a US/UK perspective, where music and arts education is generally the first thing on the chopping block when schools and government are looking to save money.

Re: Are We Ambitious Enough for Irish Traditional Music? Article

No. Nay. Never.

Anyone who knows history knows that Irish music and stereotypes were (and still are) exploited to make money. I think that is an separate subject from the music itself.

Those, like me, who play the music primarily in pub sessions for the lifetime of work it offers and the pure pleasure of playing music in a social setting with like-minded enthusiasts aren’t making choices or altering our behavior to maximize the profitability of our craft.

My local pub session draws in the customers, we get to work our craft for a free pint or two, the patrons enjoy the music, and the owner gets a nice steady stream of customers who come back every week to listen.

However, that ecosystem exists outside of what we are actually doing in the session itself, which is all about the music. We’re not there as entertainers looking to please the crowd, we don’t get a cut of the profits. That we are provided with free drinks is very generous of the owner, but we’d also be happy to pay for them.

I think it’s important to separate the business of the music and the music itself in these discussions.

I’m one of those “late in life” players having come to this music after decades of classical ensemble playing. I’m lucky to have the resources to purchase the instruments I play. I think there is a risk of many of these instruments becoming boutique items with fewer and fewer builders making affordable instruments for newer players, both youth and adult. I see this as a existential threat to the continued existence of the amateur player scene, particularly with concertinas, accordions, and Uilleann pipes.

Re: Are We Ambitious Enough for Irish Traditional Music? Article

The issue of entry level instruments is interesting. There are similar conversations going on in the world of hurdy gurdies. Like boxes and pipes, the hurdy gurdy is a complex instrument, and inherently expensive. There have been various attempts to produce simplified ‘entry level’ instruments, but the consensus is that these aren’t very good and tend to put people off rather than encouraging them. One solution that does seem to work is for the maker to build proper instruments, but then make them available to beginners on a rental or ‘rent to buy’ basis. Like pipes, the hurdy gurdy is a high maintenance instrument, and working on this basis also keeps the maker in touch with the instrument so that he can ensure it stays properly adjusted until the player has enough experience to do it themself.

For any form of high ticket price instrument boutique makers do tend to have full order books and long waits. But they still only just scrape by, because they try to keep prices as low as possible. That in itself causes a problem with supply, because players expect (quite rightly) to pay more for an instrument by a big name than from an up and coming maker. When the big names are only just making a living it makes it very difficult for new starts to charge less without starving.

Perhaps some of the arts funding available to trad should be put into training/supporting more makers of trad instruments rather than spending it all on training elite musicians.

“Perhaps some of the arts funding available to trad should be put into training/supporting more makers of trad instruments rather than spending it all on training elite musicians.”

Now that ain’t such a bad point. Spent a bit of time working with a couple of major instrument makers in the UK and they struggle. The biggest names have it pretty tough trying to make ends meet and Chinese and Pakistani imported instrument shaped objects really don’t cut it. The major players in certain markets all make their gear out there but have the turn over they can afford to keep full time quality assurance staff onsite. Mid range, small time makers (often very well known) just haven’t the turn over to do this. Hand makers of folk instruments don’t have a chance.
A good mate of mine was a world famous trumpet maker. He did a few repairs too but at best could only ever make 26 trumpets a year. It was a living but not mass market by any shout. Mid range makers do fill the gap there and the amount of school bands ( the few that still exist anyway ) ensure a market but even names like Boosey and Hawkes struggle and have had many ups and downs.
Its one of the reasons I rail against instrument snobbery and continually call out the uninformed who make sweeping statements about the materials that a good instrument requires. Their assumptions are almost always proved wrong by science and genuinely blind experience. That aside I genuinely believe that any attempt to make music more available has got to do something to address the question of affordability. That doesn’t necessarily equate to make more money for ITM musicians though as it appears the original article would wish.
