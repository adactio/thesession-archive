Re: Fiddle made in 1878

No, you can’t go by labels at all.

But generally if someone is going to stick a spurious label in, they’ll use the name of a known maker. (A fiddle labelled with an unknown name is no more valuable than an anonymous one).

So when you find an instrument labelled with an unknown name, the chances are it’s genuine. When you find one labelled ‘Stradivarius’ it’s guaranteed to be a fake.

An appraiser will be able to tell instantly if it is hand-made, or a workshop or factory product. And he should be able to give an idea of where and when it was made, although that is not always possible with self-taught provincial makers.

Re: Fiddle made in 1878

The trade lumps fiddles (and other instruments for that matter) into three catagories:

Hand-made means an individual craftsman has made the whole instrument.

Workshop-made means it has been made under the supervision of a craftsman - different people will have made different bits, and the design is standardised.

Factory-made means exactly that.

So yes, people working in a workshop do have hands, but they don’t make hand-made instruments.

Re: Fiddle made in 1878

You might be lucky and own a good factory fiddle. But the chances of it being as good as a workshop or hand-made fiddle are remote. And it certainly won’t ever be anywhere near as valuable, no matter how good it sounds.

When you say ‘the people in factories are professionals’ you are confusing factories with workshops. In a workshop yes, you have got skilled professionals - and they may well produce an instrument as good or better than an individual craftsman. (Although workshop companies tend to be making instruments to a budget, so things like timber quality tend to be lower than with an individual craftsman).

But once you get into the factories, you won’t see a ‘skilled professional’ anywhere. And the wood is barely touched by human hand. Everything is cut to shape by machines following patterns. No graduation of plate thicknesses, no tap-toning, no account taken of the different hardness and density of different bits of wood. etc. etc. So the chances of getting an instrument that sounds reasonable, never mind good, are very remote.

But when you talk about old fiddles you do need to make the distinction. If you look at the instruments being made today, there are probably 100 unplayable ‘student violins’ for every decent instrument produced. That ratio was the same 150 years ago, and the bad fiddles from back then are every bit as bad as the bad fiddles made today. It is very tempting to think that just because a fiddle is old it must be good, and valuable.

Re: Fiddle made in 1878

Workshop instruments are essentially hand made, but not by an individual craftsman. What typically happened 200 years ago was that a master craftsman would employ journeymen and apprentices. The master would make the ’top of the range instruments, and put his name in them. The journeymen and apprentices would make instruments for the cheaper end of the market, with the less skilled people doing the simple jobs, the more skilled doing the difficult bits.

Factory instruments were mass produced by unskilled or semi-skilled labour - even at the beginning of the 19th century they had copying routers that would carve a scroll or plate from a pattern. Even the design of the instruments is geared to fast easy production rather than regard to its roll making music - they frequent lack corner blocks and sometime linings, and have features like bass bars carved as part of the belly to save the cost of fitting a separate bar.

It’s hard to illustrate the role of workshops with violins because there are very few operating today (other than at the top end of chinese manufacture), the economics don’t really work. But you can see how it works with guitars - at the top you have the individual makers, tlike Stefan Sobell making instruments to order and charging accordingly. Below that you have people like Jimmy Moon, employing a dozen or so skilled workers, making instruments almost on a production line, but still working mainly by hand, so what you get is still essentially a hand made instrument, but not one that any individual can put his name to. Built to a standard design, and a much smaller price tag. This equates to the old violin workshops. And below that you have the factories, churning out plywood things.

I have no desire to defend the violin trade - as others have pointed out it is fuelled almost entirely by BS. And if you are looking for an instrument to play, yes, you can occasionally find good factory fiddles, and masses of very adequate ones. You can also certainly find a lot of hand made instruments that are total duds. But in terms of monetary value, the handmade instruments will always be worth far more than factory fiddles, simply because they are bought not only by musicians, but also by collectors, who don’t give a damn what they sound like.

Re: Fiddle made in 1878

Isn’t a Strad label usually inserted to indicate that the violin is a copy of a Stradivari design (as opposed to Amati, Guarneri, Stainer, etc)?

It may be worth pointing out to someone who has recently acquired a “Strad” that Stradivari made only about 1200 violins in his long lifetime as a craftsman, and of these only about half are still around. Almost all are accounted for - some owned by music colleges and loaned to the best graduates, others owned by orchestras or individual working musicians and played regularly, others owned by collectors (usually non-players or poor players at best) and rarely see the light of day, others in museums (but still played on special occasions), and yet others owned by consortia or foundations and loaned or hired to soloists who need them for their playing careers. A small number have been stolen over the years.

The great Hungarian concert violinist György Pauk, recently retired from the concert platform, told the story on BBC radio a few days ago of how he came by his Stradivari. He was giving a concert in Chicago in the ’70s and a member of the audience came up to him the green room afterwards (a regular occurrence for any soloist), said he was a collector of old violins and would Mr Pauk like to see some of them. Accordingly, the next day the collector turned up at Pauk’s hotel with 2 Stradivari and 2 Guarneri violins. György spent the next two or three hours happily playing them and then the collector asked him which of the four Mr Pauk liked the most. Gyorgy indicated one of the Stradivari. The collector then said “it’s yours” and gave it to him. György’s wife, who was there, said he turned white and nearly fainted. Anyway, György played this instrument, known as the “Massart Stradivarius of 1714”, in his concerts for the next few years but then started to have misgivings about having been given such an incredible instrument for nothing.. He contacted the collector and offered to buy it. The collector initially refused payment but eventually was persuaded to accept what György described in the radio interview as a ridiculously small amount. (This transaction would of course have formalised the change of ownership). György said, in a reply to a question by the interviewer, that the violin is insured for £2M and is kept in a safe place. We’re not likely to see it in a pub session any time soon.

Re: Fiddle made in 1878

“You can set up a good modern violin to behave and sound like Nicolas Strad”

There are specialist luthiers who will make a replica of an old master violin for you, not only as a look-alike but as a sound-alike and play-alike. This would typically, but not necessarily, be done for a soloist who owns an incredibly valuable instrument (like György Pauk’s). Some players reckon it’s generally too much of a risk to be trotting a valuable old instrument all round the world at all sorts of venues for a number of years (insurance companies don’t like it either), and if the audience can’t tell the difference in performance between the original and a replica, who cares? The original would of course be kept safely somewhere and only played in recording studios or on special occasions, and the cost of the replica would doubtless pay for the reduction in insurance premiums over a fairly short time scale.

A few years ago I met a cellist who owned such a replica instrument (but not the original). It had been made by a specialist luthier in Italy who spent several weeks examining the original Strad in great detail, and another few months in making the replica. The luthier used the self-same techniques, tools and materials that Stradivari would have used (such as sharkskin to rub down the wood), so what came out at the end of the day was an instrument that Stradivari would have recognised as coming straight from his workshop, except that the setup would be modern and not early 18th century.

The cost? I heard £25K mentioned (it would be rather more now, I suppose), and if the player was concerned that the replica was also aged to look exactly like the original - an important consideration if he is known to own and play the original - then, “that will be an extra £10K, sir”.

The cellist was giving a recital to elderly patients at a local hospital - my wife worked there so that was how I got to be at that recital - and I have rarely heard a more magnificent sound outside a recording studio.

Re: Fiddle made in 1878

Michael, there are good reasons why some musicians require exact replicas of an outstanding instrument - insurance reasons and security not being the least.

Luthiers who make these replicas would of course be well versed in making their own instruments which are not replicas; but they would still make them from templates derived from specific makers such as Stradivari, Guarneri, Amati, Stainer (the more important ones). Ask a luthier to make you a fiddle and he would likely ask you questions like do you want a Stradivari model, Guarneri model, etc - they all differ subtly in contours and quality of sound - and what sort of music do you intend to play on it.

An exact replica of a big-name Strad is a different proposition and more expensive because the luthier would spend a lot of time examining and measuring every detail of the original (getting permission to do this may not be an insignificant problem in itself!), getting hold of the right woods and other materials, and probably making it using the techniques and tools of the early 18th century. Ageing the varnish to look exactly like the original - scratches, blemishes and all - is a further expensive step which, so I’m told, requires yet another specialist.

Subject to what experts such as Skreetch may have to say on the matter I’d reckon that the design of the violin family has long reached its zenith. The only significant improvements being made now seem to be in string technology (they still can’t quite match the real gut sound), and bow technology - carbon fibre and the like (but be on the look-out for the occasional marketing spiel from one or two manufacturers which to me seems to be verging on pseudo-science - this doesn’t detract from the real usefulness and quality of the products, of course).

We mustn’t forget the virtually indestructible carbon fibre violins and cellos that are coming on the market; powerful instruments by all accounts, but do they have the tonal detail and richness of real wood? I am of the opinion that even the best carbon fibre bows, good and as attractive as they may be, do not produce quite the same tonal quality and resonances as a really good wood bow, and the same reasoning could be applied to carbon fibre instruments.

There are other improvements (or design changes - not quite the same thing) constantly being made to peripherals such as chin rests, shoulder rests, and tuning mechanisms for the strings (both at the tail-piece end and at the peg end). Some of these are useful; others are design variations.

There are also makers of acoustic violins who are going down the route of unusually shaped and designed instruments. I am not at all convinced that there is a significant acoustic improvement as a result of these outré designs (rather the reverse, judging by the samples I’ve heard!), but it does seem to me that if there were any real acoustic merit in these designs they would have been adopted by at least some mainstream luthiers.

I’ve had another thought about getting rid of the G string. Even if it isn’t used to play on it provides a lot of resonance not only for the G and B on the E string but the G on the D string and the B on the A.
