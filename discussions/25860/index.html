Re: Do the punters come to hear the singers or musics?

We had an interesting experience two weeks ago relating to what the OP asserts. The session at our local has an audience of tune listeners that come specifically to hear the music. Sure there’s always some that couldn’t care less, but we definitely have a population of tune-appreciating punters who gather around the edges and are obviously there for the tunes and they show their support in a variety of ways. If there is a singer we know of, we will call for quite and you can hear the sushes reverberating around the pub till all is still… and then a song will be enjoyed… but we can only get that for one song or two at a time at best, and only a couple of times during the night or the punters loose some of their enthusiasm to sush one another for songs.

But two weeks ago a gaggle of lads from Derry blew in on holiday and decided to impose a ballad session on our regular session. They kept calling for songs and jumping in with singing every time we ended a set of tunes. We tolerated it two or three times, and then started to ignore them and play tunes regardless of their attempts at intervention. Then one of them came into the center of the circle and stood next to the table to intimidate us and call for songs while we were playing. We stopped and sat there doing nothing, and I moved my instruments out of harm’s way and began going over scenarios about what to do. I could have easily removed him physically, but there were delicate instruments everywhere. I asked him if he’d like to return to the bar where his friends were because he was disrupting the session by standing where he was, and he told me his friends would tell ME what to do and not the other way around. At this point our beautiful yet formidable bartender came over to him, took his hand and suggested he might want to come over and finish his drink. This seemed to throw him off his game and he left the circle with her. The fellow hosting the session went over to the one that appeared most sober and tried to reason with him. That fellow lectured the session host on proper session etiquette, which was completely misconstrued to justify their attempted coup d’etat. Then they all became frustrated and left the pub with righteous indignation as the punters applauded their departure. As soon as they walked out the door a fight broke out amongst them with someone who was walking by, and I heard an Irish girl by the door who was playing pool comment to her friend, “It’s just like home isn’t it?”

The bottom line is that my anecdote contradicts the OP’s point, and there are indeed punters who enjoy tunes and would prefer them to songs… or at least someone’s attempt to impose songs on a session.

‘My way of thinking is that it is not a proper session at all if the whole crowd isnt part of it in some way’

That’s a bit of a utopian view don’t you think? In reality music is more often than not just a bit of wallpaper. Paddy Canny didn’t like playing in pubs because of the noise and disinterest, music wasted I think he said. Even in Friel’s more often than not (in recent times anyway) punters are watching matches on the big screen in the backroom and recently they had a boom box going in the backroom that drowned out the music.

Ideally though, people should listen and be interested, in reality, they’re often not so much.

For ten years or so I used to play in a country pub that had music going in some shape or form for 128 years or so when it closed five or six years ago.

We’d play for a few hours, some sets would be danced. Around midnight singers would be called and songs would be sung for a while (depending on the number of singers but some nights it could go on for hours). If the night was good, we’d go back playing for another while. The finest singers would come to sing there, if they were in the area.

It’s probably interesting to note singers expect (and demand) silence. You may have a room full of singers and they’d make a racket all night while you’re playing. As soon as a singer even thinks of a song dirty looks will be thrown to anyone making a sound. I remember one night whe na singer was giving out vehemently about a fiddle player who plucked his strings while a singer was getting ready to sing, to see if the fiddle was in tune . It was of no importance he probably hadn’t heard his fiddle all night because of the racket in the room made by singer discussing the ills of the world. Funny old world isn’t it?

Nice Kenny - I love stories about word origins. A quick google search came up with this:

The most convincing origin we came across derives from a version of the popular European game of baccarat known as punto banco, in which the players, i.e., those who stake against the bank (“banco”), are known as punters, from the Italian (and Spanish) term punto, meaning “a point,” although why exactly, we’re not sure. While the origins of baccarat remain obscure, it’s certainly an old game, since its introduction to France from Italy has been traced to the 15th century. Other relatives of baccarat include chemin de fer, baccarat banque (or et deux tableaux), and the now virtually obsolete basset or bassette.

So far so good. But how the term came to mean gamblers in general or, as it tends to today in the U.K. and Australia, horse racing bettors in particular, we’re not sure. In the financial world, someone who invests money in a chancy game or business (particularly the stock market), in the expectation of making a profit, is also referred to as a punter.

While in both countries a “punt” can mean a bet, in Australia “punting” is also used to mean gambling, while in England punting generally means “boating in a punt.” These days in England, the term “punter” is used to refer to any customer of a business, as in, “Let’s see if the punters like this new Swedish beer before we cancel our order for Bud.”

To all those who slag me for disliking ANY song at a session (you know who you are):

I don’t mind the odd song when it’s a good song. I don’t, really. I mind shight songs and I get tetchy when a tune session morphs into a song circle. Re: Do the punters come to hear the singers or musics?

I seriously dislike the term “punter.” It could be because the first time I ever heard the term it was from one of the blokes in our session (who happened to be a sh*te, soulless bugger - we got shut of him years ago). That gave me very negative vibes about the word. The people who come into the pub are just the people who come into the bloody pub. They’ve come for a night out and they are going to pay dear for their ale. They could have stayed at home drinking cheap Stella in front of the telly. “Punter” sort of implies that they’re not as good as you, somehow. They are at least as good and possibly better as it happens. They’ve come to the bloody pub when times are hard both for the pub and, quite possibly, for them as well. You talk about “the punters” and you’re kind of talking down. If they’ve come to listen to you, great. If they’ve come for the atmosphere and don’t really focus on the fact that you’re contributing to it, great. If they ignore you, great (as long as they don’t camp on the table next to you and bawl at the top of their voices all night: mutual respect is the name of the game). The pub in which I play is too far away for me to pop into for a few pints, but I get free beer for playing when I do go. I respect that, I feel a tad guilty about it (though I always manage to get over it ;-) ), and I’m not going to talk down about the people who come there and pay good money to do so, unlike me. It’s grand that you have a pub to play in at all. So leave out the “punters” bit. It’s condescending, and a touch arrogant to boot.
