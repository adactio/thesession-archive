Which notes to play?

It seems like I should be there by now. After almost 15 years of playing tenor banjo and mandolin, I still can’t play many reels at standard 120 dance speed. I’ve learned several hundred tunes, and play regularly in sessions, so it’s not like I haven’t been working at it – I just can’t play fast.

My conclusion, or rationalization, is that I’m trying to play too many notes. And, I believe there is some anecdotal evidence from other players indicating that if you want to play fast on fretted instruments, it could be crucial to be very selective about which notes you play, or don’t play.

Any advice would be much appreciated.

Re: Which notes to play?

IMO the solution is to turn away from tunes and concentrate on technical exercises. Thing with the tunes is that they are ‘form’ that you wish to achieve. You’ve come to a wall and need to sit back and figure out how to cross it.
Speed is a function of relaxation , but stress and wishing to achieve, limit your options and cause tension.
When we push, we use different muscles to when we pull, so tension is needed in one set of muscles for each movement and relaxation in the other. So you need to isolate the actual muscles needed to activate the string with your pick , in your mind, and relax the others. It’s far simpler to do this without attempting to play tunes at the same time.
Also Get your self a ‘speed pick’ it’s a really weird pick designed for exercise that helps achieve a lightning fast right hand. It’s real strange but persevere .
I’ve been picking banjo guitar mandolin etc over 40 yrs , sometimes many hours a day so I know very well the issues we face.
I play with heavy strings 12 on the E and a 1 mm Dunlop ….. So the right hand has to be rhythmical and strong while others use a light pick and light strings.
Anyhow exercises are scales and arpeggios with and with out a metronome. String crossing exercises , triplet exercises and the like. Concentrate on relaxing your face and shoulders; the tension triangle. Try to hold the pick and speed pick when ,not if, you get it. As light as possible so you may drop it. Occasionally, that’s ok. There is no pressure to achieve a form . Pick generally up and down apart from sweep picking and string changing and 1/4 notes.
IE the basic jig triplet rhythm is :down--- down, up down up. D-d udu D-d udu
play on one string; the D, then when you have it constant, use the A String For the up. The first D is a 1/4 note the rest are 1/8 .
Do a few weeks of these type of isolation exercises and when you go back to the tunes try to keep the same body feel, don’t tense up.
It’s like driving with the brakes on.
Forget trying to play fast with the brakes on and you use more fuel as well trying to.
The frustration causes tension and it’s a vicious circle.
Remove yourself from that circle .
Do it and ignore the naysayers and you will feel the benefits in just a short time.
Good luck

Re: Which notes to play?

I agree with the others here -- you can reduce or eliminate treble ornaments and hammers/pulls if you’re not up to speed with those, but don’t drop any of the actual notes of the tune. One does it of neccesity when playing flute or whistle, but I think it’s less noticeable with a sustaining instrument.

Building speed isn’t easy. It takes a long time for some of us. I can play most reels on my mandolin at full session speeds if I know them well enough. But there are still a few that I have trouble with, just due to the way the notes are laid out on the fretboard. Strathspeys are another problem for me. The main tune notes are easy and the tempos are never blazing, but those quick little 16th note runs embedded in some strathspeys are murder to pick cleanly on a mandolin.

One thing you might try is working with a metronome. I have trouble with metronomes myself. I think the mechanical beat is deadly to learning how to play with a good rhythm pulse and lift in this music (but that’s an argument for another day). However, for the purpose of building up your speed it might be useful. Set it to a slow tempo and practice your reels. Then gradually increase the metronome’s tempo as you get comfortable at each increment. Once you’ve developed your speed, turn the thing off and concentrate on getting the right pulse and feel in the tune.

P.S. did you really mean reels at 120 bpm? (counting as 2/2). Most sessions and dances in my area would play them around 112 bpm, often a bit slower. I’ve heard performance bands play that fast, but it seems a bit high for a typical session. Maybe we’re all slowpokes over here, but I sometimes have trouble hanging on at 112.

Re: Which notes to play?

dfost, you were specific about playing reels up to speed in session.

Yes, practicing scales, arpeggios, and isolation exercises will help you perform as a well-rounded musician.
There is a laundry-list of reasons why this is true. But it is also important to develop a deliberate approach to your practice, specific to where you are currently in using exercises, tune practice, tune learning and your level of playing in sessions (learners vs. full-tilt).

What about jigs & hornpipes? How are you w/playing them up to speed in sessions?

What about the old workhorse reels; Rolling in the Ryegrass, Drowsy Maggie, Merry Blacksmith, Drunken Landlady, Cooley’s Reel, Silver Spear… If you play any of these in session are you comfortable playing them up to speed? How about The Wise Maid or Over the Moor to Maggie; does your session push some reels more than others?

I’m not a fretted instrument player. But from watching them I think what may be easy for me (flute & whistle) may not be so easy on a fingerboard; particularly finger-crossing from high to low positions. I would love to see examples of cross-string exercises as it would help me (as a wind player) to visualise where string players need to move their finger position more than I do.

Also, about practicing at a steady tempo (because it doesn’t matter if it’s slow or fast when you’re just raggedly beating it out) … start slow. Excruciatingly slow. And use a loud metronome to drive home the point about just how difficult it is to play slowly. If it makes you want to kill the metronome it means you’re human but you’re making progress. If you love your metronome, marry it. If you can live w/trad imperfections forget monogamy; go to the relentless, metronome for it’s disimpassioned click (as needed) but be willing to abandon anything you’ve learnt there, in session, when said perfection falls short of the mark.

In any case, dfost, practice with deliberation & session for appreciation of people & music.

Remember the actual physical thing you are doing, be it tunes or scales or rifs is the secondary focus. What is important is using your minds eye and finding unnecessary tension. Face and shoulders mainly.
That’s why it’s not about tunes, or making music so much as body work. Once you ca. Isolate where the tension is , which slows you down, you will just naturally be able to move faster. Be it walking, dancing playing everything you do basically.
Concentrate on your breath and relaxation , let the mechanical stuff be an aid to this , which in turn will create a positive cycle.
By avoiding tunes you avoid the psychological desire to play well , or fast , or musically. It’s simply getting the body working optimally which will return dividends.

Re: Which notes to play?

I am not big on metronomes, but like Conical Bore said, this is one place where it would be handy. Find a comfortable pace for a favorite tune. Then turn the metronome up a notch or two at a time, not speeding it up again until you can play the tune well at that pace. Strive to keep playing the tune the same way, without shaving off notes or ornaments. Keep repeating this process.
Sometimes, you will find that there is a technique thing that is holding you back, an aspect of your playing that is throwing you off at higher speeds, and this process will help flush those issues out.
The thing that holds me back at faster speeds (besides lack of practice at this point in my life, which is a whole ’nother story) is the fact that I want to think about every note as I play it, which is difficult for reels at dance speed and above. One needs to let go and trust their fingers to do what needs to be done.
Oh, and PS, and also consider picking a tune that is not your favorite for your drills--it is easy to become quite sick of it after playing it so many times the exact same way.
Good luck!

Re: Which notes to play?

dfrost - forgive me if this is a silly question, but how are you learning the tunes? The reason I ask is that I know some folks who sight-read, others who learn off midi files or ABCs, and others still who learn exclusively by listening to recordings or other players. Take the tune Wise Maid for example. If you listen to a common ABC play-backs of it, it might be confusing to hear some of the grace notes and jumbled slurs of notes. I know people who by listening to it as an ABC file thought this was “the tune” and attempted to reproduce each one of sounds on their instrument. It really was a case of “too many notes” muddying up their understanding of the tune and they were simply unable to play it at speed in a session. Then, they heard a recording of it by a banjo player and suddenly it all clicked. This may not be your situation, but I thought it might be worth mentioning. Also - the best advise anybody ever gave me about increasing speed it to practice it slowly. Extremely slowly - maybe 10 times through at an exaggerated crawl. Then slowly increase speed until you’re flowing freely. One last point is arm and hand tension. As referenced in Enda Scahill’s books, you can’t play fast if you have a death grip on the pick and neck. That was the hardest thing for me to get over, and I still struggle with it the first half-hour of a session until I get loose.

Re: Which notes to play?

Transcription, with ornaments are really more for people who know the tune and the ornaments to show ways an accomplished player might use them and where.
I use a selection of books from a website that often show many different settings, some simpler some very complex and also show different ways the melody can be changed and still be the same tune.
Other than this I think transcription do a disservice to players approaching without the background fundamental already accomplished. Especially when we are also talking about different instruments! Banjo players in particular need just the basic bare bones. Learn a tune like that and in time you will gather the required technical proficiency to add your own variations.
Every instrument has its strengths and weaknesses, certain phrases fall under the fingers on pipes that are really tricky on fiddle and vice versa and this applies to all the different instruments.
IMO play all the notes of the tune, but play a setting that suits your instrument…. The tune will have certain notes that can be replaced with other notes from the same chord and still sound good and still be the same tune certain notes can indeed be missed out to create interesting phraseing and variation but the basic reality is that to play at dance speed requires good technique and fundamentals.
That’s partly why I’ve always made a point here about playing without ornaments at first , get the tune, get the speed, get the rhythm and drive and that’s all you need to play your part in a social occasion. The ornaments come after these basics.
It’s all very well bring able to play a heavily ornamented setting at half speed but that’s useless to the dancers! Being able to play for dancers, to put the notes under their feet, to make them WANT to dance, is IMO absolutely essential.
