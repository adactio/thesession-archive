Re: What do you think of this fiddle player?

My undersanding of what she intended by “emphasis on the beat notes” was to inject pulse into the tune on the 1st quaver in each group of 4 in the bar. In other words add the pulse in the same place you tap your feet.

Llig is correct in the sense that exaggerating this too much will sound contrived and it’s not a simple cure-all solution to take you to an advanced level. However, in this case, I think it might actually be a helpful thing to do (*ducking). Slowing down the tune and exaggerating “the beat notes” might actually get the message across that the tune must contain a pulse.

Lots of listening and immersion will hopefully make it clear to the player that although she now has a pulse, it’s still not quite right. With practice and lots more listening, the exaggerated beat will lessen, become more subtle, and the over-the-top contrived sound will disappear. But the pulse will remain.

While the phrasing point is on the ball, it’s still theory. Immersion is the key to getting a “feel” for how the music works. I’d be very interested to hear this young lady again in 6 months or a year, if she spends the time working hard at it. Ideally playing the same tune. I think it’d be an interesting contrast.

The thing about emphasizing the beats is that to make this music sound “right,” you move the emphasis around, from 1s to 3s, sometimes on the down beats (1s), sometimes on the backs (3s), sometimes both. You can’t just do all one or the other without it becoming trite and predictable. And how you articulate all the other notes gives shape to your phrasing, too.

Re: What do you think of this fiddle player?

Like all great tools the technique of using the bow needs to be taught and learnt in order to get the best out of it. In particular, if the player doesn’t have the technique to have full control of the bow s/he’s not going to get good phrasing with it - a similar situation perhaps to a flute player who hasn’t learnt breathing techniques properly.
Basic bowing technique is fundamental to good tone production, and underlies things like the patterns, down-up-down sequences, and string crossings - the prospect of which, from my own observations, seems to alarm more players than it should.

↳ Posted by fidicen on March 16th, 2010.

Re: What do you think of this fiddle player?

There’s no mystery to string crossing. You learn to go from the D to the A with an upstroke and a down stroke. You learn to go from the A to the D with an upstroke and a down stroke. You learn to do it changing direction from up to down and down to up, etc. Did I miss anything?

You learn to bow any combination of notes with any change of direction or slur. And you do this so that your choices of phrasing is not not limited by bowing patterns you’ve learned.

↳ Posted by Michael C. Gill on March 17th, 2010.

Re: What do you think of this fiddle player?

@llig, Perhaps to you and me there’s no mystery to string crossing, but that’s not the same for everyone. For instance, I’ve seen players fumble the string crossing in Sally Gardens when it gets to a critical speed (I used to do it myself). The real test in string crossing to my mind is jumping a string without changing bow direction (slurring) _and_ without sounding the string that’s being jumped, so that you get a smooth transition from one note to the other.

You’ve hit the nail on the head with reference to “learn to bow”. This is the most difficult part of playing the fiddle and needs to be taught face-to-face, the earlier the better, because no two pupils are the same and a good teacher will take into account the individual’s anatomy, physiology, and psychology. Books can do little more than to say “do as I do”, or, historically, that fiddlers have always done such-and-such (times change, of course).
