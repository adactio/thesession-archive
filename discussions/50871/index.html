Malik, to test that hypothesis, you’d need to use a recording that’s already widely regarded as “authentic,” and provide different mixes of that.

Your current track features several elements that scream “we like this music but clearly haven’t been immersed in it long enough to integrate the subtleties and sensibilities that would make it sound traditional.” There’s not much authentic about it.

You’d be better off with a recording of a solo instrument—one long and well rooted in the tradition, say, whistle, flute, fiddle, or pipes—with no bodhran or accompaniment. And played by a musician also long and well rooted in the tradition. Best if it were not a recognizable track, better if it was a relatively unknown but skilled amateur who’s lived in the tradition for decades.

Re: Participation Opportunity for Research on Irish Trad for my Bachelor’s thesis

Yes the tune itself takes us out of the “authenticity” quotient.

And about the question “does this sound like an Irish session?” that’s impossible to answer because there’s a huge spectrum of what an “Irish session” can sound like.

If the goal is to find out the differences in sound-mixing between people accustomed to mixing Irish trad groups and those who have only mixed, say, rock bands, I think your recording is going to have to incorporate some of the challenges involved. (I’m talking playing through mics, not with people having pickups.)

I think one of the biggest challenges with mixing an Irish trad ensemble is the disparate nature of the instruments. So to me your “test” recording needs to be a group with at minimum the following instruments playing melody in unsion:
-fiddle
-Irish flute
-uilleann pipes
-box
-tenor banjo
(only one of each)

and a minimum of these instruments playing accompaniment
-guitar
-bouzouki/octave mandolin/etc
(only one of each)

Those who have done a lot of playing onstage with clueless “sound guys” are familiar with the mistakes usually made. These instruments have a wide range of volume levels and are tricky to balance. If playing live through mics feedback can be an issue.

Even more challenging and informative would be to add Low D Whistle and/or High D Whistle. The Low Whistle’s low range will be hard to bring out and the High Whistle, being an octave above everybody else, must be tamed.

In playing onstage with sound guys unfamiliar with Irish trad ensembles, one thing I’ve had them consistently do is bring up the uilleann pipes too loud.

Seems that the thinking is “bagpipes are loud! I’ll make them loud!”

Or maybe they’re thinking “flutes and violins are soft! I’ll make them soft!”

I know people will argue with this but in my mind there’s a trio of instruments which have been the melodic core of many Irish trad groups

-uilleann pipes
-fiddle
-Irish wooden flute
(one of each, generally)

and a major component of mixing Irish trad groups is finding a way of making these three instruments blend in a satisfying way. If your sample doesn’t have these three playing together in unison, in my opinion it’s missing a core feature.

I added box and tenor banjo because even if the sound guy has the pipes/fiddle/flute blend right adding box & banjo creates additional challenges, especially the punchy, percussive banjo.

A studio recording imitating a pub session is not exactly what an average traditional musician from Ireland (or anywhere else if they’re steeped into tradition) would associate with authenticity. Irish traditional music is most at home in someone’s kitchen, in a quiet space of someone listening to a solo performance or “in the moment” of a bunch of friends sharing tunes together. To me, authenticity feels like a very tricky concept in relation to music. First of all there are so many possible meanings:
- A new recording of Michael Coleman has emerged, believed to be authentic [it actually is Coleman playing]
- this is a recording of an authentic Irish jig [the tune is an actual old Irish tune]
- this is a recording of authentic Irish fiddle playing [it’s an Irish fiddler playing in a very idiomatic style that they’ve learned by growing up in the tradition]
- this is a recording of an authentic Irish session [it’s some musicians in a busy pub in County Clare]
- you can only get the authentic experience of Irish traditional by hearing it live [a fair point]
- the bass guitar / cello / trombone isn’t really an authentic part of Irish music
And so on.
Personally I think that authenticity only becomes a concern in situations that are not really ‘authentic’. No Romanian shepherd ever said, ‘and now I will play an authentic regional doina’, they just pull out a pipe and play a tune they heard somewhere in a way that feels right to them. But authenticity is very much a concern for people trying to mimic a tradition that they weren’t born into.
That’s not to judge - that’s me, for one. But I think we can all be authentic to ourselves - play the way we want, based on all the music we’ve heard and loved, without worrying too much about whether it’s ‘authentic’.

ITM is authentic in its own right. Sessions are a recent phenomenon. I would not use a session as a barometer of authenticity. Sessions are kind of “trad against all odds” and we certainly do keep trying.

I think “authentic” is in the compositions themselves and in the unadulterated, correct performance and/or recordings.

In presentation, it’s also about instrumentation. Two kazoos can’t play authentic trad. Two bass violins can’t play authentic trad. A violin played through a flanger or wah wah pedal isn’t trad. They can be trad-spired. Please don’t bring a tuba.

In the studio, if you start grabbing for VSTs for anything besides minor compensation for a sonically dead studio space, compression for vinyl pressings, or re-leveling because one player recorded elsewhere, it’s not an authentic trad performance.

Hey Malik!

It’s a whole new area of literature on our beloved sessions- brilliant!

It’s so painful to see the misguided replies that come across as so arrogant etc.

I’m trying to figure out where the misunderstandings come from here.

Here’s my list of suspects of mistaken assumptions by those who are apparently wanting to teach you things that you clearly know extremely well.

-people who assume you think authenticity is a singularly defined idea

Those who assume you are not well versed in many more definitions (and the problems of definitions) of authenticity than them. Those people who (painfully) assume you are naive to the complexity of the definition.

-those who think the aim of the study was to share a number of ‘super authentic’ recordings and get them to pick their fave.

These people don’t understand that the task is a *comparison* task not an *identification* task. They seem to assume that you were sharing what *you* define as authentic. Of course, you are sharing a number of mixes that you’re asking people to compare to *their own* intuitions tell them is authentic. The mixes have a number of variables changed - and you’re looking for what suggests authenticity to people.

The research is set out in the obvious and standard way for such research - and the suggestions that you “should” be researching different things etc are so painfully misguided.

Authenticity means different things to different people - and the research is clearly designed for people to share their different ideas on that- it’s well set out.

-those who do not consider the differences in recordings as significant, misunderstand the nature of the research.

These people do not understand that the research is laid out in a way to measure significance of the differences - including the possibility that the changes are not significant- seem to assume it was a task to “identify a correct authentic recording” - as if it were a test.

It may be because they had an inability to hear the differences - or because they felt that the arrangement was inauthentic (including myself) - but many seem to assume that there ‘had to be’ a definitively authentic recording.

I think that if they knew that things can appear slightly more or slightly less authentic - even in the “gut” - this could help them understand their is no problem in asking if one inauthentic arrangement seems slightly more authentic than another.

In essence, you were doing a very simple thing: asking if these excerpts felt more or less authentic. If the answer was ‘no’ - then so be it - we’ve identified that the factors that were not important - and that will show. It’s ok to say no.

But the responses that your research was significantly flawed - or that you should study something different - are sometimes painful to read. Perhaps due to a lack of understanding that your discussion of the findings will include an appreciation of the complexities involved.

Understanding academic research processes is not an obligation - it has its well known limitations -

But apparent assumptions that others know better about your research etc - come across badly.

Thanks again for sharing the great articles above - it’s nice to take our understanding behind these online social spaces, and read from people who have immersed themselves into deeper learning on the complex phenomenon of sessions etc.
