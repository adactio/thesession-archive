Trevor’s comment does not surprise me! OK, some of us like a bit of a rough edge on the music, not a bit of music on the rough edge.

Fidkid’s clip of Néilidh Boyle playing The Moving Cloud was quite fun to listen to. There’s certainly lots of drive in there. The funny thing is (and I’ve never really thought about about this before), when almost every note is out-of-tune, it adds another dimension to the msic. Not sure what - I can’t figure it out. It keeps making you re-listen and re-listen. Each re-listen still makes the crappy intonation jump out at you - but there’s something endearing about it. Seriously weird :)

Comments here, and on other threads say a lot about the way people react to some music. Trevor’s comment was, I think, fairly normal - other people would have laughed at the sound.

People laugh when they hear musicians who parody other musicians - you only have to look at the series of “Itzak Perlman shreds xxxxxxxx” on YouTube. Some may have laughed at the sound of the clips on this thread - I didn’t, but only because I’ve heard so many like them. It wasn’t meant to be a joke anyway.

This general theme (scratchiness, bad tone, poor intonation) has been heatedly discussed in other recent threads (with relevant YouTube clips to reinforce peoples’ point of view). What came out of all of that was quite interesting. The main point was that in the “tradition”, allowances should be made for poor sound, if the drive, “nyahh”, and sincerity are there.

Fair comment - although, owing to the stubbornness of some posters, it got to the point of almost inverted snobbery, where it was not clear just how much you could push the limits of poor tone, intonation, etc and still be acceptable (and not laughed at).

All these clips posted here, are part of the tradition, and will always be. Yes, for sure, I “get” it (how could I not?) - I just dislike the sound.

But then again, I dislike the pure sound that Martin Hayes makes, plus some of his musical mannerisms, although I really can’t fault his playing.

Brendan Breathnach wrote a few paragraphs in one of his books, on the subject of the “status” of one’s musical ability, sound and general respectability (within the confines of Irish Traditional Music). I can’t locate it right now, but the essence of it was that the collective opinion of those respected musicians, peers and informed listeners was what really counted. In other words, the majority vote.

Well, at least on here, I’m clearly out-voted.

So be it :)

Re: Audio of fiddle player Ellen (Nell) Galvin

Probably not close miking Trevor, but almost certainly mechanical recording (hence the almost total lack of any bass). At the time the recordings were made, portable tape recorders had only been available for a couple of years and were still very rare.

Mechanical recording would mean she had to play ff the whole time just to cut the wax, which would have a big impact on her playing.

Added to that, she was touching seventy when the recordings were made - I know several of that age who were very good fiddlers in their day, but none of them are that great now.

All of that means that, whilst Nell Galvin undoubtedly was a very good player, these recordings are not a true representation of that playing.

Yes, they hold some clues as to how the music was played, which are of academic interest, but they really can’t be considered representative.

Even at the time they were recorded and broadcast, it was done because they gave an interesting glimpse into the past, not for their musical merits. So anyone who listens to them today and says “this is great music” really is seeing the Emperor’s new clothes.

As for Mrs Galvin,

She is respected by countless people who might be conisidered ‘experts’ in traditional music. Listen to Peter Browne’s ‘Rolling Wave’ programme about her and other similar old style players http://dynamic.rte.ie/quickaxs/209-r1-therollingwave-2010-02-21.smil

Her style is wild and way outside the realms of our modern concept of equal tempered tuning, however to me she sounds generally consistently ‘in tune’ with herself and clearly has a great sense of expression and ornamentation and her rhythm is fantastic.

A lot of older players had their own sense of tuning and consistently stuck to that. They’re are obviously going to be some mistakes but you can hear lots of intonation mistakes with ‘highly trained’ classical player too.

If you find this hard to take you might need to take a gradual trip from Martin Hayes to Tommy Potts to Paddy Canny (without piano backing) to Bobby Casey to Martin Rochford to the Rainey Brothers and finally back to Mrs Galvin. If you don’t ‘get it’ after that then you probably never will and it’s your loss!
