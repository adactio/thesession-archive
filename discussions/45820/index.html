Re: Scottish vs Irish Music Session (Traditional)

Excellent question. My answer is yes, but not because it’s “Scottish” per se, but because each region has its own stylish differences. Players use different types of ornamentation, for example.

It’s not fair to say that any of the music we typically call “traditional Irish” is Irish at all as we have no evidence that these dance forms (jigs, reels, hornpipes, etc) were utilized in any organized way in Ireland prior to the 1950s or 60s, whereas we do have this evidence of them utilized in Scotland and other parts of Europe.

Re: Scottish vs Irish Music Session (Traditional)

> I know that that form of piping died out but not sure if the pipes did.

The real story of what exactly went on both in musicological terms and in terms of instruments is still not that well understood, though plenty of people are happy to present their views as the last word in factual correctness. Was there ever truly a “Border piping” culture or was there simply a “music” culture sometimes played on these instruments that could be found all over urban Scotland, and quite possibly large areas of England as well? Keith Sanger has also made a prosecutable case that modern GHB technique is in fact a fusion of Gaelic piping technique and the pre-existing Lowland technique, and that much of the early repertoire was as Lowland as it was Highland.

The modern Border pipe is both redesigned and to a large extent re-purposed, and it’s the kind of thing that would probably have been invented sometime between 1970 and 2000 had it not already existed in historical form. That said, the modern Border pipe is closer to the historical Border pipe than the modern smallpipe is to its historic equivalent. But for the most part, most makers make re-designed instruments inspired by historic instruments rather than copies of them.

“What’s the difference between an Irish tune and an ”Irish“ tune?”

It was a shorthand way of referring to the tunes generally heard at Irish trad music sessions, which this site serves, not all of which are Irish in origin.

“And what’s the difference between Border pipes and so-called ”Borderpipes“?”

Because “Borderpipes” is a misnomer, usually applied to a modern type of bagpipe developed in recent decades, having little connection to the various 18th and 19th century instruments which Baines called the “Scottish Lowland pipe” and “Northumbrian Half-Long Pipe” and which early price lists from Glen and other makers offer whicb they call “second-size Reel pipes, or Lovat Reel pipes, blown by mouth or bellows”.

I owned three chanters from three modern makers, all more or less identical, none of which were called “Border” chanters by their makers (Nigel Richard, Hamish Moore, and Jon Swayne). McCallum and Fred Morrison collaborated on their mass-produced version which likewise isn’t called a “Border” pipe.

However all of these things would probably be called “Border pipes” nowadays, a convenient umbrella label, however inappropriate historically and however little-used by the current makers.

The terms Border pipes and Lowland pipes tend to be used interchangeably for pipes that are roughly the same size as the GHB but that are bellows blown and have their drones set in a common stock. They are often grouped together with what are more accurately called Scottish smallpipes: also bellows blown and with a common stock, but smaller, and usually playing in A or D - these could also be regarded as a form of Border pipes since they are very close to the older form of Northumbrian smallpipes that was played up to the 18th century, when pipemakers in Northumberland started to produce sets with closed chanters, which had keys yo extend the basic scale and produce sharps.

The best place to find information is the Lowland and Border Pipers’ Society:
https://lbps.net/j3site/

There is certainly a distinctive Lowland/Border repertoire but it is far less extensive than that of the GHB.

Re: Scottish vs Irish Music Session (Traditional)

Makers may have changed their nomenclature over the years, but in 2006 when I ordered a chanter directly from Hamish Moore it was called the “Reel” chanter on his price list and receipt.

The chanter I ordered directly from Nigel Richard the same year was called the “Session” chanter on his price list and receipt.

The chanter I ordered directly from Jon Swayne the same year was called the “Lowland” chanter on his price list and receipt. (He also offered a “Border” chanter but it didn’t use Scottish fingering, it was basically a Central French Cornmuse chanter, using Cornmuse fingering.)

All three of these chanters were the pretty much the same beast: a volume more or less halfway between the Highland pipes and Smallpipes, they all played in A=440, they all used standard Highland pipe fingering, they were mostly or entirely chromatic.

Yes they would be called “Border” chanters today, as I said a convenient umbrella term. But at the time I ordered them those three makers didn’t call the chanters I ordered “Border” chanters.

For sure other makers might have used the term “Border” for their pipes, I don’t know about the other makers, I only know about the ones I communicated with and ordered chanters from.

BTW I played these chanters in a 100-year-old Glen 3/4 size set, which worked perfectly.
