Slow airs in traditional Irish music sessions

Dear All,

I am a musicologist based in North Carolina. I am currently researching Irish slow airs in trad sessions. So far, my research has been limited to interviews conducted in the American Southeast, but I’m interested in branching out and seeing what a broader audience/readership has to say. This discussion board seemed like a neat place to post some of my questions, and so without further ado:

In a session, how many slow airs are typically performed? Do performers make a concerted effort to include slow airs, or limit the number of slow airs performed? Do performers make an effort to situate slow airs, say near the end of a session?

When performing in a communal space (a pub, a restaurant, etc.), do performers notice a change in audience or patron interaction during the performance of slow airs? Do people tend to become more or less attentive? Do people tend to interact more or less with the performers?

What do you feel is the main function of slow airs in trad? Do they simply provide a break for the musicians not performing? Are they used to “wind down,” or to indicate that the session is coming to a close? Are they as important to a typical trad session as slides, jigs, reels, and other dance tune types?

Thanks in advance for your insights! I appreciate any/all comments you might have on these issues. -Megan

Re: Slow airs in traditional Irish music sessions

Yikes, back to the “performer/audience” debate! :-O

Megan, some of us sessioners (maybe even many of us) don’t think of what we do as “performing for an audience” at all. I often play with my eyes closed, so I couldn’t tell you anything about how the other folks in the pub react while I’m playing a slow air. I don’t mind at all if they enjoy it, but that’s not why I’m playing it.

The oft-cited “problem” with slow airs in sessions is that they’re best played freely, rather than adhering strictly to a meter and beat, so they’re usually played solo. Sometimes, people who’ve played together a lot, will play an air together. But it’s not the sort of thing that invites mass participation (and when it does, the results are too often disappointing).

For many players, sessions are mostly about playing lots of tunes with other people, all together. A slow air now and then is okay, but too many, or at the wrong moment, can spoil the energy and flow of a good session.

That may be why many experienced players wait till late in the evening, as people are packing up, and the mood is quiet and calm, to play an air. That doesn’t mean that an air’s “main function” is to wind things down, or to give other players a break. No, the point in playing an air is to play an air, to express the joy, sorrow, anguish, utter content, etc., that only that piece of music can convey. No other function or purpose is needed.

Each session is different. Some encourage airs, some allow them, and some shy away from them. And this can change from night to night, or over the course of a single night. Depends on the abilities and moods of the individual players.

Re: Slow airs in traditional Irish music sessions

You may also want to consider the underlying issue of people actually learning slow airs to begin with, and the cyclical relationship with their appearance in sessions: if people tend to hear airs in sessions, they’ll learn more airs, and conversely, if people don’t really know any airs, they won’t crop up in sessions, which seems obvious but has been conspicuous in my experience. Fiddlers influenced by Sliabh Luachra music, for example, or pipers who listen to a lot of the canon like Ennis and Clancy, will simply bump into a lot more airs in their listening experiences, and those airs will end up in sessions. On the other hand, I’ve met a few students of Brian Conway who seem to have learned “Where you at the Rock?” as the somewhat obligatory slow air, and then never learned another.

Re: Slow airs in traditional Irish music sessions

Please note that I am an American in the Midwest, with only about four years of pub session experience. So the sessions I participate in are not necessarily the most advanced or ‘authentic’ (whatever that might mean).

In my experience, slow airs are a rarity at pub sessions. As Jim notes, they might take the place of a song. Typically, there are no airs at sessions at all. Occasionally there will be one or two. There is no concerted effort to include or exclude airs, it’s just that most people are there to play tunes together, and airs, like songs, are usually solo pieces. We’re there to play, not attend a concert. As a session winds down, though, slow airs are more likely to occur, rather than at the beginning.

I notice no difference in the patrons’ behavior at all during a slow air, except maybe to get louder.

Slow song airs are absolutely critical to the Irish musical tradition, though. Every bit as important as dance forms like jigs and reels. They’re often the most personal, impassioned vehicles for expression within the idiom. Keep in mind that Ireland’s musical tradition does not begin and end with pub sessions. Slow airs are performance pieces, to be shared with an audience, with friends and family in the privacy of a home, or in solitude. And sometimes at sessions.

Re: Slow airs in traditional Irish music sessions

“In response to the wounded hussar’s question, I do mean slow airs as solo instrumental (or vocal) pieces performed in a relatively arhythmic manner and stylistically derived from séan nos singing. I am especially interested in how solo (or lightly accompanied) music fits into what is, I think, generally conceived as a community-centered art form.”

There are two notable presuppositions here, one regarding slow airs and the other regarding solo playing.

1) There are at least two broad schools of thought as regards the playing of slow airs: the “song” style, and the “piece” style. Some musicians very specifically equate slow airs with sean-nos songs, and attempt to play them (rhythmically, tonally, ornamentally) in a style which reflects the way that a singer would sing them; indeed, musicians in this style often suggest that you sing the words in your head as you play the air on your instrument, to get the correct phrasing and all. The “piece” school seems to have been something of a piping tradition in particular, somewhere between 1850 and 1950 roughly, with outliers of course, but this style generally treats as airs as simple melodic material rather than song. As such, the “piece” style is generally characterized by different types of variation and ornamentation, generally suited to the instrument at hand, in spite of what singers might do; the repertoire also be slightly different, and there are some interfacing examples (the Fox Chase is a piece, and also includes an air; the Old Man Rocking the Cradle is a song, but the melodic shapes were adapted as a piece popular in Sliabh Luachra). Sean o’Riada notably criticized the playing of airs in this way, as ruining the “pristine simplicity” of the basic melodies.

2) There have already been some discussions on this site as to whether “real” Irish music is solo or ensemble, or whether one is “better” than the other, so you might expect a few heated responses sometime soon. Historically, it seems that the pipes and fiddle were commonly heard in solo contexts, either for listening music or for dances, but there’s no doubt that musicians would have gotten together for casual tune-swapping in their homes. Ensemble playing like ceilidh bands, as well as the modern public “session” as we know it, are likely artifacts of the move to dance halls around 1935.

Thanks to those who have directed me to other discussion threads, and to Danjo for pointing out some presuppositions. I hadn’t thought that the piping and singing schools would have such a different impact on players’ approach to and feelings about airs.

Two trends seem to be emerging, which I hadn’t gotten from my interviews here in the American southeast.

1. Many musicians seem to view airs as an indulgence - albeit one people are willing to make for friends and colleagues. Is it an indulgence specifically because only one person is playing or singing? Do certain musicians expect to play or sing an air during a session, or is the indulgence “granted” by others in the session?

2. Many musicians seem to prefer the singing of slow airs rather than the playing of them on solo instruments. I’m curious as to whether people view the singing of a séan nos song as an indulgence in the same way that people view playing a solo song or piece on an instrument. A few people have made this point (directly or indirectly), but would anyone say that the séan nos song is of greater import to the session, or of greater value to the community because of its text? In other words, does it have more “status” in a session than a song played without words? Most of my Irish friends feel pretty strongly about the importance of “promoting the mother tongue.”
