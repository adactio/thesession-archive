Drinking where you play

How many of us actually drink at a session? I mean properly. In recent years, I’ve noticed that musicians, their friends, groupies etc drink far less during an evening, if at all. It’s not uncommon for a player to buy a soft drink and make it last all night. So, can we blame landlords for being less than encouraging towards the music when we’re “not bringing in the money”? Compared to other types of clientele who frequent bars, we tend to be a fairly miserable lot. I (of course)and a few others are notable exceptions.
Yes, we should be given free beer but there’s no harm in putting our hands in our pockets too. Also, those who “tag along” to listen should support the session by getting “a few rounds in” at the bar.

Of course, I’m not suggesting that we should all get totally p-ssed or spend money we can’t afford. There have been too many casualties over the years already and we don’t want to go back there. However, can we really blame pub owners if they look for more profitable attractions than a music session?

Now, you might suggest that virtues such as extreme moderation, temperence, even abstinence are all good. Probably, you’re right. However, if the majority of us are coming around to that way of thinking, we should maybe consider whether or not “the pub” is the best environment for sessions in the future.

Re: Drinking where you play

Um…the question wasn’t about how alcohol affects your playing. It was about whether you feel an obligation to support the host establishment by purchasing refreshment.

At our session, drinks (draft beer, wine, softdrinks) and two or three appetizer plates for the musicians to share are on the house, but we are expected to tip the bartender generously. A few of us will sometimes come early and order dinner (during which we pay for our drinks). Once in a great while someone will buy a round of whiskey late in the evening when the session has gotten small. Probably a third to a half of the regulars don’t drink alcohol and the rest of us drink moderately (I’ll have two to four beers in a four to five hour session).

Six people come week in and week out to hear us, all of whom order drinks and most of whom order dinner. Quite a few others come to hear us every couple of weeks and also order lots of food and drinks.

The new owner (for the last 15 months) is happy with the session. The previous owner is a singer and never evaluated the session objectively on business grounds. (Which is not to say that he didn’t use it very effectively to hook customers - soliciting and singing requests, inviting punters to sing, dancing with the women, etc. Just that if nobody came, we’d probably have the session anyway so he’d have a chance to sing.)

Re: Drinking where you play

‘why do we still mainly have sessions in pubs?’
There’s a different atmosphere in a pub to virtually any other place you might play apart from possibly a church. Cafés and coffee shops can be fine, and I’ve played some very nice sessions in such places, but there isn’t the settling in and the feeling of rapport and of ‘spending an evening with’, or ‘spending an afternoon with’ the other musicians and the listeners. Cafe’s are places where people drop into, eat and leave. They don’t settle down and make themselves comfortable for the duration. They have big windows and pssers by can easily see in, so the session doesn’t have that cosy feel.
The reason I mentioned a church is that pubs, like churches, are places cut off from the rest of the world, with a time of their own, where people congregate and do consciousness changing type things, so there is a receptiveness to music and time to absorb it that you wouldn’t get elsewhere. There’s an almost church-like feeling you get in a quiet old pub on a summer afternoon, with the quietness, or maybe just the distant sounding burble of the racing on the telly in the corner, and the dustmotes dancing in the sunrays filtering through the windows, and that smell of wood and old cigarette smoke and yesterdays beer. And the feeling that this is the right place to play the music. You don’t get atmosphere like that in a coffee shop (actually you get it in precious few pubs these days)
