What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

Re: What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

I’m 77 and started playing Trad at about 15 - 60 years ago most Trad musicians were playing for dancers at Ceili Dances, and on refection, with the odd exception, many were just average players with a very limited knowledge of keys and tunes In the forties and early fifties the only trad musician I remember who had a solo spot on Radio was Leo Rowsome - Various ceili bands were featured on certain afternoon sponsored programmes on Radio Eireann (15minute slots) and on some BBC Northern Ireland programmes The only records I came across in the forties/early fifties was a 78rpm of Frank Lee’s Tara Ceili Band and another of Paddy O’Brien Accordion. There were however, many recordings available by Scottish artists such as Jimmy Shand etc. Nowadays there are many more people of all ages playing Irish Trad music and many are much more skilled than the players of my generation. The speed at which the music is played has increased to such an extent that I sometimes lose track of the tune. Lastly, over the past years there has been a noticeable increase in the use of the bodhran, an instrument that I first remember seeing one being played at a session in the sixties.

Re: What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

One thing that will soon be very positive is the advent of lossless 5.1 and 7.1 recording. We’re not really taking advantage of it yet, because the digital era has thus far emphasized crappy compressed mp3 files for ease of streaming and storage. But a few record companies, like AIX, are putting out lossless 5.1 and 7.1 acoustic records on DVD… actually capturing live performances, and they sound glorious.

Money has always driven the music business. That’s nothing new.

The technical level of players in most genres is also getting better and better. That was also ever thus. 100 years ago only a few violinists in the world could pull off Paganini’s 24 Caprices. Now they are common in violin repertoire, for example. It’s like the 4 minute mile. First no one would ever do it. Then it became routine in world class races.

It’s become tougher to find places to perform with a band, because of one-man bands playing to backing tracks… that’s getting a bit tougher… and venues are competing with streaming movies on demand and cable. There’s also DJs in competition with live bands, but that’s been true for decades. Karaoke has also displaced some of the one-guy with a guitar gigs on off nights.

Still great stuff getting recorded every year, though. And it’s easier to hear both new and classic recordings, because we have streaming services like Spotify now, and YouTube. I can hear almost anything I want now, when I want it, for free. I used to have to pay 10 bucks for the LP, 2.50 for the single, or request a song on a radio and keep listening until they played it. Now I can find almost anything for free, on demand.

That’s not always great for the artist, but not terrible either if they know how to monetize the Internet and are creative about using YouTube to promote themselves.

Also, larger rock acts …the Van Halens of the world, used to break even or lose money on tours, but tours sold records. Lots of them! So they made it up and then some.

Now I believe bands have to at least break even on tours. So ticket prices, adjusted for inflation, have crept up.

Another thing: Symphony music is dying. Audiences just don’t go to symphony concerts, outside of a few big cities. So they’re going bankrupt or going part-time and playing easier music, more pop, and cutting back on size. This means fewer professional opportunities for conservatory graduates. Though it’s also easier to promote a good chamber act.

Finally: “smooth” jazz ruins everything.

Re: What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

The down side is the loss of individuality -- as soon as some super recording artists appear on the market, everyone wants to emulate them; whereas when I was a youngster in the North of England, everywhere you went people sounded different. The few folky programmes on the radio featured only the recording artists of the time, such as the aformentioned Jimmy Shand (he’s got a lot to answer for!), The Clancys, then The Chieftains, The Dubliners, The Bothy Band, et al. But there were loads of clubs with up to a hundred joining in, and unknowns like the Fureys, Stefan Grossman, Martin Carthy, Dave Swarbrick doing the circuit. These days people expect to be entertained, whereas at one time you did your own entertaining.
The first time I heard the bodhran was about the mid- or late-sixties, and I immediately started to learn. I never heard another for at least a decade; but now everybody plays one. Same with the bouzouki. People played the bones, and a bit of spoons, and the odd jews harp and mouth organ. Uilleann pipes, mandolin, fiddle, a bit of guitar, the melodeon, accordion, whistle and flute. Oh, and the banjo. All the instruments were hand-me-downs, mis-matched, with bits missing. I never saw anyone play the harp, but maybe that was because of the size. There were no cars then, of course, or hardly any. And only posh people got taxis.
The up side is that the standard has improved no end. People are usually in tune, and at concert pitch. Everything is faster, as Free Reed has mentioned. But the youngsters are amazing, given the distractions that must confront them continuously. And access to top-class music has never been easier. The instruments are superb and comparatively easy to come by. I don’t like to see the gradual erosion of the boundary between trad and pop; but I suppose that’s inevitable. My hopes lie with the youngsters who recognise the joy to be found in the music. And I watch with a mixture of pleasure and satisfaction as the recording industry tries to hold back the tide.
The Cnuts.

Re: What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

The advent of recording was the biggest leap in folk music, possibly ever. You were no longer tied down to your local players but could hear bands you never knew existed playing tunes you’d never heard before. As a ten year old I heard the first Planxty album and THAT set trad music for me. I was sort of learning fiddle at the time and I more or less stopped and went straight to guitar and whistle… couldn’t afford or find anything else! By 1981 I had a guitar, bodhran, bouzouki and various whistles. I was into all kinds of other stuff, from the Bothy Band to Horslips and all kinds of English / Scots / foreign stuff ( Stivell / Boys of the Lough / 5 Hand Reel / The Albion Band) but any other Irish folk band, if it had fiddles and played any kind of ceili tune, was just so… yesterday.
As Gam says music has evolved as money becomes easier to find (or did!). People played whistles as they were cheap, flutes were ancient hand-me-downs as were violins. Nowadays they have all kinds of new stuff, people seem to be able to design their own instruments and the technology finds its’ way into the music - follow the evolution of Clannad from basic folk to electronica, Moving Hearts with keyboards and drums, Planxty with Donal on keyboard, electric violins, 4/5/6/9/11/16 string instruments abound. You no longer have to learn a tune from a player - just record him and practice at home.
It’s hardly surprising that among all the modern stuff the old bands reemerge every so often…. Planxty or Clannad for example. It’s a continuous process - music will always evolve with the technology (I used a battery keyboard a few years back for an accompanying drone) but there are enough of us Luddites who want to stick in one style to offset the young’uns who want to reflect their own times. I went through the whole electric guitar / keyboard / amp thing in the 1980s, sold the lot and went back to acoustic. Popularities come and go, but in the main not much is ever totally lost, if anything.
The biggest problem these days is the sheer VOLUME of music coming out. Back in the 1970s there may have been twenty good bands, now there are hundreds to choose from. It’s hard to keep track of them all.

Your question seems to ask how we feel about the evolution, rather than to describe what took place.

If my assumption is correct, then here’s my response

I started messing about with traditional music in 1969 and have done so ever since. I don’t think the music itself has changed much if at all. How much can a traditional tune evolve? A melody is a melody. There are a hundred ways to play a given tune, and you can bet that they’ve all been tried and seen as orthodox at some time, as fashions have come and gone. It’s still the same tune. Drums and guitars etc, whether they’re used for ITM accompaniment or not, have nothing much to do with that - literally and metaphorically.

I love Irish music even more now than I did in 1969. I play it every day and will do so as long as I am able. So how do I feel?

Well, in 1969 folk music in general seemed to me to be an excuse for a lot of pontification and bull, chiefly from people who talked better than they played. My feeling is that that is what’s actually evolved and grown over 40 years, and I have to confess that I’m not entirely innocent myself in that respect.

So is suppose I should feel a bit guilty

Then, a few years later, a person came to the same festival where I first saw contra dancing, to teach set dancing (:)). He came two years in a row and I went to the festival each time. And that’s one of the big developments for me over the last 40 years; my awareness of all the set dancing that is going on. Whenever I get to Ireland, I always look for set dances either to join or just watch, or sometimes play for a few of them since, because I’m one of the older folks, my knees are not too keen on dancing much anymore.

So that’s it for me. Really my own evolution. I love the sense of community that comes out of set dances. And it is always fun. And the music works perfectly

Re: What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

I can only speak for my experiences in England and (to a lesser extent) in Wales.

Back in the 1960s/1970s the “folk scene” was dominated by folk clubs. In those places, it was mostly songs that you heard - instrumental sets being a relative rarity.

Since those days, the number of folk clubs has drastically declined, and the “folk scene” has now become dominated by sessions. At most sessions, tune-sets are the norm and songs are a a relative rarity.

For this reason, I think that the standard of trad tune-playing has improved but that the standard of folk singing has probably fallen somewhat.

Back in the 1960s singing by the general public was much more common that it is today: people sang in churches, during school assemblies, on coach trips, in pubs and at “community” singing events.

If you don’t use it, you lose it.

Hi Jerone,
I’m still very much a beginner at the fiddle, but I remember about twenty years ago, speaking with a man in his seventies who played the fiddle. He told me a story about his childhood in the midlands of Ireland.
Frank and his father used to walk two miles once a week, to a friend’s house to listen to a music programme on the radio. Well, this one evening as they listened, Frank’s father ‘shushed’ the room to silence in order to hear a new reel being played. He knew he would only hear it played twice through, or maybe three times, so he had to absorb it quickly. When it was over, Frank’s father walked out the door without a word and headed for home, all the time running this tune through his head so as not to forget it. About half way home, Frank cheerfully started to whistle another tune, thus causing his father to lose track of the new one!
At this point in the story Frank began to laugh as he recalled running the rest of the way home with his father in hot pursuit!
Changed times towards now with the internet, where we can just type in part of a tune, or a tune name and have it delivered in both written and audible forms within seconds. I sometimes wonder if there is a danger of taking it all for granted, or do we really value and appreciate the music as much as the older generations did?

*I* had access to a crummy family gramophone which could be switched down to 16 rpm…and back in 1977, I subjected the first Planxty LP to its iron yoke so as to find out exactly how Liam O’Flynn was doing the rolls on the whistle as he played Junior Crehan’s Favourite (aka The Knotted Cord). I can honestly say, that had been a quest that had haunted and driven me for years:-).

As the needle ground its way through this singularly beautiful track, I felt like some watcher of the skies when a new planet swims into his ken (Keats…) - or, more precisely, as if I’d turned a telescope on some fuzzy speck in the night sky and seen it much expanded, into something like a swarm of clearly distinguishable gnats or whatever. I could hear the individual notes of the rolls and other ornaments, and tell what they were!

Some learn this stuff from another human being. I had to do so from an appliance (the 16 rpm speed, I mean) which I find hard to believe was ever used by any other human being in the history of the Earth. It is probably more usual to be brought up by monkeys.

I long since flogged off my LPs. I wonder who (has) got my first Planxty one. He or she may be listening to it right now, to the ghostly tones of O’Flynn’s whistle intermittently audible through tremendous gravel-crushing sound effects, and thinking:“It must have been great to hear him for real, back whenever, along with Morrison / Coleman / other…”

Re: What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

I’m relatively young myself (35) and can’t vouch for Ireland, being an Italian; so this cannot be in any way a first person account…
The things that strikes me most when comparing old records to new ones is the sense of rhythm and tempo.
Contemporary records are quite polished and sound like they’re played with a metronome. This doesn’t mean the metronome is there, but I believe there is a metronome ticking in the musician’s head.
With older musicians, and most accomplished contemporary ones, the metronome is not there, it’s like these people have a better grasp at time and are able to kind of bend it, slow it down, make it faster, etc.
Ciaran Carson talks about “negotiating with time”, and I think it’s a good metaphor.
This sounds like “they’re off time” to the untrained hear, but they’re actually more in time, I believe. Quite interestingly, the same “offtimeness” can be heard in older jazz records (ie: Django Reinhart).
Many contemporary albums have also that kind of feeling, although to a lower degree; I am thinking about Martin Hayes’s foot tap, which is exactly timed, but not that exactly…
I hope I am making at least some sense :)

Re: What has it been like to watch and listen to the evolution of music over the last 40+ years?

“With older musicians, and most accomplished contemporary ones, the metronome is not there, it’s like these people have a better grasp at time and are able to kind of bend it, slow it down, make it faster, etc.”

This is expected of all good players in the Scottish bagpipe scene, especially in piobaireachd and in the genre called “competition 2/4 marches”. Anyone playing as if to a metronome would not be reckoned a good player, not reckoned to be under competent instruction.

Specifically, in 2/4 marches the tunes are divided into phrases consisting of four main pulses, the first and fourth pulses lengthened at the expense of the second and third. The effect is often extremely subtle but effective.

A well known friend and acquaintance once described how his latest album had been processed in a way that he would never be able to reproduce ‘live’, bits being moved about and tweaked to make something perfect, or near as. This was accomplished by a company that at the time called themselves “The Music Factory”. They even changed the ‘attack’ on some parts, borrowing from something possible one way only on that particular instrument, live, and now possible anywhere you’d like, on any note. After doing several takes for them to pick and choose from, more time was spent diddlin’ the music in production than it took for him to give them the four or more takes he’d made for them to choose from. That’s one album that isn’t on our shelves, bless him, preferring his take on things live, or his less perfect and more honest earlier efforts at recording.

The little house gatherings and church hall ceilis were kinder and more considerate, in general, than the current modern electrified ceili band push and shove and compress as much into a couple of hours as possible. Generally, like watching a train wreck in slow motion. Firstly, I don’t believe machines have the right to usurp the human ability to play music. They are meant to be our servants after all, not vice-versa. BAD machines, and BAD producers who use them! Also, people who use pitch correction on vocals, whether for extreme effect or otherwise, should have their microphones taken away until they have the ability to sing a song from beginning to end with minimal pitch problems. Also, the music industry has mostly been and still is focused on generating music-like sound for children during the period in question. I think that’s a shame, because the (appropriately called by those who distribute it) product they generate is pretty much mostly crap, and that insults the intelligence of those children who would be far better off hearing the good stuff. That said, there IS good stuff getting recorded and distributed too, and always has been, otherwise I would never have taken up music myself.

In trad music, some very interesting stuff going on, though I loathe most “fusion” in any form - just a marketing scheme to siphon brass out of hipsters’ pockets. Some of the newer groups (Teada, Flook) are jaw-droppingly killer by any standard and deserve their renown. “Evolution” is IMO a much-abused and overused word, on the order of “genius” in the vast swamp of steaming poop that is the music industry and its attendant remora-like critics. As I said, the general trend has been devolutionary, or status quo at best. Evolution either happens organically or not at all - trying to catch the ear of the hipsters and get a grip on their brass is a sucker’s game at any rate and I wish anyone who wants to get involved in it well, but puh-leeze don’t try to sell an old set of big ears any crap about it being “evolutionary” when you play trad on the Swinette or throw in some didgeridoo and djembe.

In any case, something to consider might be an old adage passed on by an older and far better musician than me at a very good age to hear it: records aren’t music; that’s why they’re called RECORDS. Music happens when people play it. All the rest is just the accoutrements, artifacts, and dross that collect around music.
