What is your approach when you spend time learning a tune and when you go to a jam it is played differently?

I have a list of tunes I took time to learn, I thought I did anyway, but it is inevitable that when I go to a jam the way I learned the tune is different enough that I am unable to follow along so I never volunteer to start a tune. I am one of those people who finds it very difficult to play a tune and ignore what others are playing, my muscle memory can’t switch gears that fast to adapt, I hear what they are playing but find it very confusing in my mind and I usually have to stop playing, any tricks to overcome this problem? Following along is always the safer bet for me, one day I would like to be able to offer a tune and get through the whole thing no matter what.

Does the session you’re attending allow the use of an audio recorder? Either a standalone pocket recorder like a Zoom H2n, or a recording app on your phone? That’s the best shortcut I know to learn the local session repertoire when it’s different from other tune versions you’ve found.

There is a skill that can be learned, where you adapt “on the fly” to a tune version that’s slightly different from the one you know. But that takes years of practice playing with other people.

Because, in normal times (if there such a thing), my attendance at sessions is dominated by my one local one then the versions of tunes I learn will be those I’ve heard at that session. But if I take a new tune along then I would expect the other sessioners to play my version, even if they have previously played the tune somewhere else.

It is a skill (that develops with practice) being able to pick up and adapt to setting variations that you hear at new (to you) sessions. But, as conical bore says, developing that skill can take years. Your best option is to find a regular session with a fairly stable core of participants. Listen and learn their settings of tunes. That may take several months, but then when you lead your own sets with some of those tunes you’ll be more confident that the other players will be playing the same settings as you.

Listen and adapt.

If you aren’t able to adapt yet, just listen.

Focus on the specific places (or even single notes) where the setting of a tune deviates from the one you know. Perhaps silently finger the new version if you can on your instrument.

Keep listening. Keep adapting.

It gets easier with time if you just allow yourself to keep listening deeply and not stress about it.

Keep listening. Keep adapting.

When I practice from reading, then my muscle memory is building on letters.

If I play to a midi audio track of the same tune then it’s similar, the fingers are thinking in ‘bits’, and I’m starting to put words together, but my rhythm/reel and slip jig bounce gets messed up. (I play it straight).

When I play to a SLOW youtube vid that has different variations I can occasionally miss a measure or play just a couple of notes per measure in parts and have time to imagine a sort of thesaurus of each word (four note pattern).

I’m similar to some of the others who’ve posted though, if I hear even a slight variation, it puts me off. In that sense I follow individual notes rather than the phrases or harmonic undercurrents.

I’ve just started to learn tunes backwards, it really helps. The last measure then the second to last, then together. It makes you think of the words outside their context.
-but I have to map out returning anchor points otherwise I jump into another tune.

“I am one of those people who finds it very difficult to play a tune and ignore what others are playing, my muscle memory can’t switch gears that fast to adapt, I hear what they are playing but find it very confusing in my mind and I usually have to stop playing, any tricks to overcome this problem?”

Stop playing. Listen.

Tom’s comment above on using features on YouTube and other online video platforms that allow you to slow down the audio on videos without changing the pitch is an excellent suggestion as a way to begin learning the skill required, but you’ll need to use other software tools if your intent is to learn the versions of tunes at your local session.

If you can, and they don’t mind, record the versions being played. Work them out at home, use software tools like Transcribe or Amazing Slow Downer to slow them down to where you can start to pick out the differences and adapt. This is a skill that one absolutely can learn, but just like learning tunes, there isn’t a “magic bullet” that will allow you to start adapting your playing “on-the-fly” at full speed. Know that if you take the time to do the work, you will eventually be able to pick up the differences in real time. It will take as long as it takes, be patient with yourself.

Re: What is your approach when you spend time learning a tune and when you go to a jam it is played differently?

As others have mentioned, it can take years of playing with other people to develop the skill to be flexible enough to react to what others are playing without getting lost.

When first starting out learning these tunes, people inevitably rely on kinesthetic memory (muscle memory) to facilitate playing the tune. You basically have to do this to be able to play a tune at first. But it can also be a trap, because you end up getting lost as soon as the finger pattern is disrupted.

What ultimately needs to happen is for you to really internalize the melody of a tune, get it to the point where you “own” that tune. I like to think of it like tunes as being stories to tell. When you know the story really well, you can tell the story and put your own spin on it… Some players ultimately become really eloquent storytellers!

So the question is really how you get yourself to that point… The first thing I recommend for my students to do is to start playing with simple variation. Try moving your ornaments around, leave them out, add them in different places, etc. So you’re not really playing any different notes, you’re just changing how you play them a bit. This can help you break reliance on the kinesthetic to remember how a tune goes. Ultimately the muscle memory still helps you play your instrument, but if you control the flow of the melody more in your conscious brain, then you can keep the tune going when something unexpected happens…

The suggestion of getting recordings of how people play the tunes you’ve learned is also a good one. Then you can work on being able to adapt to what they’re playing when you’re at home. If you’re able to keep the tune going in your head when what they play diverges from what you play, then you can learn to anticipate it the next time it comes around, and concentrate on where and how it diverges. You can work on that at home with recordings, and ultimately you can develop the skill to be able to catch the differences and pick them up on the fly. (Of course, then what inevitably happens is that you adapt to what they played, and they adapt to what you played, and the next time through, you end up switching places… These are good problems to have, and show that you’ve developed flexibility in your tunes!)

Re: What is your approach when you spend time learning a tune and when you go to a jam it is played differently?

I’ve always had this issue: whatever version I learned, at whatever session I went to they were playing it very differently.

As people are saying, you have to set aside the version you know, listen, and learn however they play it at this new session you’ve decided to attend.

Sometimes the process is, in truth, learning a new tune, because the session version and your version might have very little in common (though the ear recognises that they’re related).

The two versions could be in different keys, have a different number of parts, could have the parts in a different sequence, and so on.

The point is: it’s neither here nor there what you played in the past. This new session you’re attending is the reality you must deal with now. It has its own tunes and own settings. If you’re going to participate you need to learn them.

Re: What is your approach when you spend time learning a tune and when you go to a jam it is played differently?

Tom, I’ll add just a bit on what our Rev was saying, something that I’ve said before that I think is very useful, even imperative for players who learn by ear. I think of it as an intimacy with the instrument. I’ll use an analogy. Many, if not all, skilled sight readers see the note and instantly know how to manipulate their instrument to make that note happen. What that note sounds like they may. or may not, know until it’s played. My wife, an excellent pianist, is one of the ones who is surprised by the sound of the note. It’s s skill many singers have too. I believe it’s one reason why they have some difficulty learning by ear. As aural learners we need to be able to instinctively know where that sound we heard is on our instrument and do it with thinking about where we put out finger placement, breath control, embouchure … we just know in the way we know how to speak. That kind of intimacy lets us learn and adapt more easily because we can “hear” the interval, and not have to think about what the note is. We can reproduce what our “inner ears” hear. I’m not going to start an argument about the value of scales and exercises versus knocking’ out tunes, but I will say that without that kind of “oneness” learning, and in this case adapting, is a LOT harder, especially “on the fly”. To me it goes deeper that what the Rev rightly calls kinesthetic memory. Think of players who can transpose and can switch keys in an instant. That’s the kind of thing I’m talking about. I don’t know how I can explain this idea any better so I hope this makes some sense.

Anyway, Tom, I hope we meet up again sometime. I look forward to the day.

Another problem with playing Farewell to the Creeks in sessions is that while pipers know the full 4-part version, many other people know the tune via the song “Farewell to Sicily” by Hamish Henderson, which was written to the melody of the pipe march, but only uses parts 1, 3 and 4. Many years ago I heard Farewell to the Creeks played on the Northumbrian Smallpipes, the piper took the tune rather slowly and flowing, more like the song than the Highland pipe tune. Though a Highland piper I prefer playing it that way. I’ve played many a Bride down the aisle to that tune, at a relaxed flowing pace perfect to walk to.

Highland pipe 6/8 marches are played quickly- they’re Quick Marches after all- and with strong “pointing”, giving them a bouncy feel.

As a piper at a Highland Games what I dread most of all is being the Duty Band and having to play for Drum Major Competition, where they always want 6/8 marches and they always want them fast.

BTW most Highland pipers do an odd rhythmic thing in the 2nd bar of the 4th part of Farewell to the Creeks which doesn’t appear in any of the written versions AFAIK. I haven’t tried writing it out, not sure how to write that rhythm, basically it goes into 4/4 time for a beat, I think.

This will be the second attempt at comforting I’ve done today. Larry playing your “part” against what someone else is playing is the, without a doubt, hardest part of playing with others. It is a skill that both can be, and must be, learned. For someone who learns by ear and in an environment where the melody is the main, sometimes only thing going on, it’s doubly so. Every part of playing that way requires that you listen to what the next guy is doing while you play your part(unless of course, you mean to adapt), and not get sucked into matching them. Even if it’s not an arranged harmony or counter-melody and just a slightly different take on the tune or an error, it’s easy to fall out of sorts. The job of every musician in the orchestra, of every union musician who picks up a gig and handed the score, is to play what they’re given and not to play along with the other players. They practice it. It’s not automatic. I’ve been a bassist for 6 decades and even though my part is far from the melody I still struggle with it from time to time. I’ll want to follow the fiddle, or sax, or piano, whatever, and I sometimes drift from my “part”.

So … point is, everybody has to deal with this same issue. Others have given all the advice here to help overcome the problem. Be assured that if you practice some of that advice you WILL get over it. Just like every other skill you learn, at first you really suck, but if you stay at it you get pretty good. But I think you already know that.

Re: What is your approach when you spend time learning a tune and when you go to a jam it is played differently?

Just catching up on this thread: pleased to see that “Farewell to the Creeks” has stirred up some interest in those not familiar with it! Definitely a march of the “dotted 6/8” variety, therefore not a jig.
Written by Pipe Major James Robertson during WW1, but only became a song “The Highland Division’s Farewell to the Banks of Sicily” when Hamish Henderson put words to it during WW2: more info and a bagpipe score here: https://leedspipeband.org.uk/content/tune-origins-farewell-to-the-creeks/
I prefer to believe the story that “the creeks” are those of Portknockie, where PM J Robertson came from.
As Borderer says, if you try to play the tune just as the tune in a session, someone will inevitably launch into the song, which uses the various parts thus in the various verses: 1, 3, 3, 4, 1, 3, 3, 4, so it doesn’t fit the sequence in the 4-part tune. (11, 22, 33, 44).
As for that 2nd bar in the 4th part that Richard mentions, I came to the conclusion when transcribing on YouTube from a pipe band playing it, that instead of the more usual crotchet/quaver combination for the first half of the bar, they were playing crotchet tied to semi-quaver, followed by semiquaver: difficult if you are playing at a fair speed: so in fact the first note is 5/6 the length of the second!
Now enjoy the video! https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KoMxwr9WRi8
