How did you learn ?

A recent discussion I stuck my nose into about teachers resulted in a bit of acrimony - I know I can do this to some people, it’s the Tourettes Syndrome sets them off, I can’t help it, but there you go.
Anyway, my question is; how did you learn ( or are still learning ) your skills in ITM ? What percentage of the knowledge did you gain from teaching, as opposed to informal tips and hints, and also just watching and practiciing, and working it out for yourself ?
Personally, I had 6 months of teaching ( and that was in finger-style folk guitar ) back in the ( omigawd ) 1960’s, before my guitar-teacher told me to p**s off ( in the nicest possible way ) because he’d taught me everything. So everything else, to whatever standard I have attained, has been by watching, listening, working it out myself. Is that unusual for ITM ?
PS Not a jocular remark about TS in my first sentence; I do have a mild degree of it. The upside is that it makes you enthusiastic to an obsessive degree, useful in a musician.

Re: How did you learn ?

Funny you should start this thread, Pete, because after the kerfuffle with Fred on the other thread, I thought: Wouldn´t it be interesting to start a thread about we all learned to play.
But while I was still mulling over the idea, man of action that you are, you went ahead and started it!

I am one of the dwindling band of pre-Planxty Irish musicians. I took up the concertina in 1969, my inspiration being the late Tom McCarthy in London.
I´m entirely self taught (and, as you say - it shows!) partly because there weren´t any teachers around that I knew of in those days.
Brendan Mulkere had yet to establish himself in London and it just didn´t occur to me - nor would I have dared - to ask Tom or raymond Roland for lessons.
The natural and logical way seemed to be to listen to as much music as i could and just work out how to play the tunes (I don´t read music).
The only players in London I could watch and listen to were Tom and Raymond, and in Ireland i spent a lot of time watching and listening to Packie Russell.
There was a gap in my playing from the mid-70s when i moved to Spain until about 1990 when I met up with some kindred spirits here and started playing again.
I´ve been to a couple of concertina workshops in Ireland hosted by Micheal O´Rahilly (excuse spelling) and Dympna O´Sullivan and, while they are both fabulous players and good teachers, I felt that learning tunes i could do at home with a tape recorder.
What I needed was either a master class that concentrated on technique, or private classes, but by this time it was the mid-90s and at my stage of the learning curve, i felt that it had become a lot less urgent.
I´m reasonably happy with the way I play but I´m always learning new tunes and trying to improve my technique and polish my style.
If I had my time over again though, I´d definately take classes and would recommend any new musician to do so.
However, class learning is like the visible tip of the iceberg. It´s the learning and practising that you do at home and at sessions that makes you, eventually, a good player.

Re: How did you learn ?

Never had much in the way of music lessons at all -- I don’t say that with pride, just as a statement of fact -- so my learning has largely been inspiration followed by trial and error. When I realized I had to learn chords to play guitar, I got myself a big poster board, and sketched out diagrams for just about every chord I could find in the music books. Then I got songbooks which had songs for which I knew the melody well enough to sing and learned the chords.
I would hear something I liked -- whether it was Creedence Clearwater Revival, Martin Carthy, Fairport Convention or the Clancy Brothers -- and try, if not to emulate it per se, to find at least some elements I could take onto myself: the “spirit” of the song or tune, for instance, or the mood being expressed by the performer’s style.
The more adept I got at the mechanics of playing guitar, the more I could explore and experiment. Little things I picked up at the occasional workshop, or by reading Sing Out! magazine helped add to this hodge-podge of musical development -- such as, for instance, the use of alternate tunings.
More and more, I sought to perform, like at college coffeehouses or folk clubs (such as Dingle’s -- remember, Pete?). People by and large seemed to like what I did, and that encouraged me to continue.
But however much I might’ve gained by playing for myself, I learned far more playing with other musicians, such as in the context of a group. It helped me to have some sort of structure, some kind of context, and what I did either worked or I’d have to go off and try to put something together that did.

That’s still mainly how I operate today, even though my grasp of music theory is considerably better than it was when I started out. I still look for someone or something to inspire me, and then I try to “make it my own.”
I’ve also found that the inspiration can come from unlikely situations or sources. For example, a few years ago I took a week-long guitar workshop from Chris Newman. Chris plays a much different style of guitar than I ever envisioned, and in fact tends not to favor DADGAD -- which I’ve been playing for more than 20 years. But what he said that week about the way one approaches the guitar -- for instance, being more comfortable playing further down the neck in the 7th-12th fret area -- made a big impression on me, and really prompted me to focus on these “little things.”
Somewhat more recently, I had been playing regularly with a fiddler who was in my daughter’s high school-age rapper sword team. She’s about 30 years younger than me, but she turned me onto the whole concept of using relative minors, and substituting thirds in the I-IV-V progressions.

Just gotta keep your antennae up and your ears open, I reckon.

Re: How did you learn ?

I got a $15 guitar for Christmas (when I was ten years old and Elvis was twenty-two) and started noodling around on it, but I didn’t learn very much until a year or so later, when I spent most of my fifty cent allowance on a little paperback book of folksongs that had some chord diagrams. After that, I learned by listening and imitating. In my mid-twenties, I got a Segovia album and was drawn into that world. I didn’t know how to find real classical guitar music (this was small-town Alabama, long before Internet), but my wife played a bit of piano and had some books of “easy” classical pieces, so I learned to read the stuff and ported some pieces over to the guitar.

Eventually, I had some classical guitar lessons, but I had a horrendous work schedule (in a sleep research lab) and couldn’t keep up the practice, so I quit after a few months. The classical experience made me keenly aware of some important ideas about learning and practice, and several years of teaching guitar reinforced and expanded all of that.

I applied that learning when I took up other fretted instruments, and went about it very methodically. Oddly, though, I approached the fiddle very differently. It was way down the priority list and I guess I treated it more as an occasional amusement than as an instrument I was trying to learn. But, after several years of this, I realized I had developed some basic competence, so I started learning some “real” tunes. At the same time, I started playing with some Cajunistas and found a niche playing second fiddle parts – “rhythm fiddle”, so to speak. This gave me a good workout in the rudiments - bowing, pitch, tone, etc. – which fed back into the Irish tunes.

So, I can describe in some detail how I learned the guitar, (clawhammer) banjo and mandolin, but the fiddle just sort of gradually happened without much conscious effort. And I suppose it shows. I still think of myself as an advanced beginner.

I don’t know when I learned my first Irish tune – probably around 1968 or 69. By the mid 80’s, I had a very modest Irish repertoire, learned entirely from recordings and played mainly on mandolin and guitar. Then, after a bad strain injury, I played very little for the next twelve years, and forgot most of the tunes.

I’ve regained some basic competence, so now I’m in tune learning mode. I learn tunes by applying three main principles – listening, listening and listening. Once a tune is in my head and wants to be played, I stop listening to recordings of it and just play it. This encourages me to inhabit the tune in my own way, instead of being constantly tempted to mimic somebody else’s strokes. It’s a slow way to learn a tune, but it works for me and it suits my current situation (lots of commuting = listening time).

After 30-odd years of mostly self-taught jazz guitar, I acted on my historic (but largely unrequited) love for the “tunes” o‘ the Celts, and started covertly lifting some ITM tunes from this very site to learn ’em. I could make jigs sound jiggy enough, but the reels didn’t reel at all. After going out one night almost three years ago to hear Slainte Mhath (Cape Breton trad-rockers), I came home vowing to take up fiddle, and borrowed one from a friend the very next day. After five minutes I said “OH MY GAWD I NEED LESSONS!”, looked up local teachers, and stumbled on the mightly Denis Lanctot, who can play any style brilliantly. We sit and natter for a while, then go through some tunes, then he patiently sorts out some bugs in my playing, and then shows me something new. I’ve immersed myself in the tunes, players, and styles, and lift tunes from records, the dots, and other players. I someday hope to play decently for human ears, but becauseI have high standards, I won’t let that happen until it’s worth heariing. Right now I usually just torture the cats (they deserve it). But… it isn’t getting any worse. I can tell because they used to shoot me a filthy look whenever I took out the fiddle case, and bolt from the room. Now they just arch an eyebrow and go back to sleep (shiftless beggars). Still learnin, but should have it down cold in another 150 years.
