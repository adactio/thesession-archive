I still meet people who’ve never heard of Irish sessions at all. The setup’s obvious (why wouldn’t people who know the same tunes play them together?) and radical (anybody who can play it can turn up and join in) at the same time.

Re: We play tunes in sessions. Deconstructing the ‘elements’ of Irish Trad Sessions?

I enjoy sessions and will continue attending them as long as I’m able.

Unfortunately, I’m not that well enough disciplined to go to sessions on a regular basis… i.e the same nights and locations on a weekly basis. So, while I will still attend several over the year, they won’t all be in the same place. This is partly due to competing interests, travelling(though not as much as before), concerts etc and just life getting in the way sometimes.

Like some others, I also play a lot at home or for occasional events with larger groups of players.
I’m also keen in seeking out new tunes and trying them out on my various instruments. The best ones are usually the most transferable, I’ve found. :-)

Sessions vary, of course. You can just dip in and out of some and the repertoire never seems to change. So, I don’t feel I miss too much but not going every week. In some others, I might not know many of the tunes or some of the musicians are “too flash”. Then I feel I might not want to impose myself on a too regular basis either!

The best scenarios are something in between. A nice mix of the familiar and new with good competent players who are welcoming to “most” visitors. :-) …Also where professional musicians will sit down with regular players etc.
Sadly, such sessions are a “rarer beast” now as there seems to be much more of a “demarcation” both in terms of playing levels and choice of repertoires.

I’ve also noticed that good pubs/venues are fewer and “the session” has to fit in with all the other activities which take place. e.g. meals, sport, quiz nights and so on. It is more often the last card in the pack as far as the pub management is concerned.

We are lucky for places to go in Edinburgh, Glasgow etc but it is still mostly the “set night” scenario whereas, years ago, you could just go along to certain pubs where you would always be welcome to strike up a tune. Sometimes, it might be quiet but “buzzing” on other nights. That was all part of the fun. It was real “word of mouth” back then too as opposed to social media sites and texting your pals on the phone.

Re: We play tunes in sessions. Deconstructing the ‘elements’ of Irish Trad Sessions?

Two continents, 3 countries.

Earlier the participants tended to be young folks, under 30’s. As time progressed the age did too. Today my local sessions here, San Antonio Texas, are mostly old folks, but there’s a few exceptions. Next I have noticed a slow creeping trend of sessions being dominated not by strong players but by accompanists, Guitar, Bass etc. IOW the tune players, Fiddle / Flute / TW / Accordion / Concertina etc rotate but not the rest. Then like all other types of music jams folks will often get involved for ulterior motives like promoting their thing, what ever that is, or, being they are experts already on an instrument, just to socialize, and the genre is not why they are there. In such cases I have met quite a few professional musicians moonlighting. Again these are not your local trades folk having a tune, they are way more skilled and tend, when they perform, to be the center of things, often soloing for a few tunes, on shut up and listen breaks.

Yet since the ‘session’ evolved from an Irish ‘in Ireland’ drinking party, rarely with any music at all, the socializing thing is gradually becoming more popular as the tunes recede a bit. This is especially the case where there is a big ‘match’ on TV. Reminding that all my sessions over the years had a TV in the venue, and it is usually on at low volume, but if there is a match it will be turned up so everybody can hear it.

One great example of this sort if thing is Molly Blooms in Tel Aviv Israel, https://www.facebook.com/bloomsfamily/ where they sometimes have a crowd just for a big match, eg Soccer, Rugby. Others, it is hell for leather sessions will wicked good fiddling. It is Israel, so you cannot expect but the best Violinists in the world!

My friends and I had a nice session in a New England “Irish” sports bar. But every few months they’d get a new manager, and the new managers would increasingly walk past us, looking as if to say “What exactly are you guys doing?”

I knew our time there was limited.

They were good enough to turn the TVs down, but one night I noticed something new. We’d finish a set of tunes, then do a bit of chatting. At that point the house stereo would come up. This happened a few times, so I went to the bartender (who was turning the sound up) and asked what he was doing.

“Dead air,” he said. “We don’t want any dead air.”

He finally agreed to stop and let us chat, but from that time on I felt a subtle pressure to keep the music going, if you know what I mean.

Re: We play tunes in sessions. Deconstructing the ‘elements’ of Irish Trad Sessions?

Thank you, Ben, for initiating this discussion. Greetings to all from Fort Myers, Florida. We have been fortunate to have been able to enjoy an almost continuous Irish Traditional session here for over twenty years. We are blessed with a large contingent of retired Irish Americans from the midwest, especially Chicago, who have consistently turned out to support our efforts, which has encouraged a succession of pub owners to provide us with space and sometimes stages to play. Over the years, our core group of about 10 has been host to visiting musicians from Ireland, Europe, all over the US, once even one of the Keane family! There are other groups in Tampa, Naples and Miami. and we occasionally make road trips back and forth, so it’s like a clan of musicians stretching all over South Florida and it is truly grand. I hope anyone visiting this area lets me know and joins us for a session!

In regard to the topic: We had a session cancelled recently due to NFL Thursday night football! Fortunately for us, and new Irish pub opened at one of the local mall, and we are back at it twice a month. That sort of thing has been going on forever, and you really just have to live with it. I certainly sympathize with those who live in more socially isolated areas. It shocked me to hear that someone in Edinburgh could not find a session!

On the subject of proficiency: the core group have played together for 20 years, and have seen many folks arrive with a lot less experience. Tact does not always work when someone decides that playing the bodhran must be easy. I find that a direct request to stop and listen is often the best approach. One of my mates likes to ask if they might step outside and tune it up a little. Most of the time, we try to be encouraging if a newer player has clearly been doing some practicing and shows up more than once demonstrating some improvement. One of our best whistlers is a nightingale who was once a crow.

Re: We play tunes in sessions. Deconstructing the ‘elements’ of Irish Trad Sessions?

For me, sessions are where I became a musician. Having been involved in music in one capacity or another for almost my entire life, I had always dreamed of being a musician. I bought a guitar at 19, and tried to teach myself how to play. I played mostly things I made up, although, I did try to learn some popular music to impress girls (which never really worked). I worked for the largest record label in the world, met hugely famous artists, promoted live music, worked as a DJ, and saw hundreds of concerts. I ultimately found most of that to be unsatisfying as I was growing older…

Then I happened to fall in love with an Irish step dance teacher, who was also learning to play the music. At first blush, it was definitely *not* my kind of thing. But then, we went to Ireland along with a few (now fairly well-known) Irish musicians, and I watched how sessions work, and a lightbulb clicked. This was something different!

So I tried to channel my limited guitar skills toward accompanying Irish music. That slippery slope took me through bouzouki, mandolin, and right into the gutter with a banjo. So I went to a tune learning “session” regularly, and learned tunes. I went to camps, and took classes. I could play a bunch of tunes, but I still didn’t feel like a musician. I felt like a guy that could play some tunes on a banjo…

And then I started going to sessions… slowly at first, but soon found some sense of community, and then it started to become an ob-session. Now, sessions are where I feel the most at home. They are the source of the vast majority of my friendships. And most importantly, they are where I learned to become a musician! They are where I learned to be playful with the music, instead of being a parrot. They are where I expanded my repertoire to over 1000 tunes. They are the most consistent source of blissful endorphin flows for me. And finally, they are where I finally feel like I am who I always wanted to be…

I was a classical silver flute and baroque winds player from early childhood all through my 20s.

I didn’t start playing trad until I was well in my 30s in the 1990s.

What appealed to me was that it was another virtuoso music genre with a lifetime of work, but with a social dynamic that I never found in the classical music world.

Now I’m about 30 years into it, and there still is no end to the work. Like Reverend I probably know 1000+ tunes, but those include all the tunes I can play if someone else starts them. I can probably start 300 or so.

I co-host a couple of sessions in Southern California. The Tuesday night session I host at The Ould Sod here in San Diego is now in its 25th year.

The number of players waxes and wanes over the years. That’s the nature of a college/military city like San Diego. We managed to survive COVID just fine, having moved the session to a gazebo in a local park, then first back in the airy back beer garden room of The Ould Sod, and finally back in the main pub just in the last few months.

I have a fairly recent new tenor banjo/fiddle playing co-host who is constantly finding and learning new tunes. I pretty much take on learning whatever he sends out as this week’s favorite tune.

We also have our old sets we all learned in the late 90’s and early 00’s that get played fairly often. It’s all good.

I enjoy small sessions, huge festival sessions, kitchen sessions, or even just a couple of us playing in the park.

Pretty much music is my entire life now after retirement, either playing it or working on tools to help others learn. I am focused on figuring out ways I can teach and recruit new players for our local session scene so I can have others to play with in whatever time I have left on this spinning orb.

Re: We play tunes in sessions. Deconstructing the ‘elements’ of Irish Trad Sessions?

I’m fortunate to have three sessions either in town or within an hours’ drive from where I live here in the USA. For various reasons, only one is my favorite so that’s where I go as often as I can make it.

I value the session experience not just for the camaraderie and that dopamine hit of playing music with others, but because it keeps my playing calibrated. It reminds me how fast some of these tunes should go at a higher level session. Otherwise it can be easy to get lazy and complacent when practicing at home, when you don’t have better players pulling you forward to where you need to be. I don’t like sessions where an Alpha Fiddler is taking over and pushing things too fast, but these tunes do need a certain tempo to come alive.

A local session is also a source of new tunes aside from whatever I’m picking up from artist recordings. I’m selective about that. I don’t try to learn every tune I don’t know at a local session because some grab my ear more than others. But over the years I’ve picked up some great tunes from sessions that I probably wouldn’t have learned otherwise.

The bad news is that within a year I will be moving to a different part of the country where I’m not sure if there are any ITM sessions within driving distance. I’m lucky to have my wife who plays fiddle with me at home and we’re both deep into ITM, so there will still be music in our house. But we won’t be exercising the “session muscle” that keeps us calibrated without a local session. We may have to settle for yearly trips to out of state or out of country festivals and workshops, to remind ourselves how this music goes when it’s played live with others.

Interesting discussion – thanks Ben. Couldn’t see where you were going with it at first… 😉

Schadenfreude makes me just a bit glad that there are people out there struggling to find sessions. No offence folks, but it makes me count my blessings that though we are few, there are enough players locally for us to run a monthly session here in South-East England – and that I live close enough to Ireland to get over there to play from time to time.

I think it would be regrettable if ITM lost all its geographical/cultural meaning and just became another generic form of world music, with all sorts of weird and wonderful(?) things being done in its name by people who may have had too little access to the original to know/care better (see YouTube…) So if there are parts of the world where it is still not found, maybe we are not at that point yet. I’m glad that much of the music is still determined by what happens in its homeland – even if it isn’t all good.

But I suppose all those world acts are a tribute of sorts to the appeal of this music – and I’m no native player either, so who am I to criticise?

It also makes me give thanks for this website – it’s no exaggeration to say that it is, at root, 100% responsible for shifting me from a solo player with only the walls listening, to being a regular session and band player. A number of good friendships and many hours’ great session playing all trace back to here. Thanks Jeremy!

Loss of spontaneity? Well yes, the buzz word of the moment seems to be “curated” – which seems to mean edited or controlled by some kind of production person or entity. It’s a euphemism for the increasing commercialisation of just about everything, and if there is one thing a production line doesn’t like it is unpredictability. Luckily, we don’t seem to be experiencing it too much; maybe being well off the tourist ITM trail is actually a blessing in that respect?

I suppose our own session is a little curated, in as much as there is inevitably some crossover with our band material – but I suppose any session is curated by its owners to the extent that they evolve a common repertoire.

Our host publican seems happy that we populate her premises on a Sunday evening, and on occasions it has been packed and dancing. She will even turn the piped music/TV off for us. It’s not all doom and gloom.

Interesting comments all. The only suggestion I can offer to those seeking sessions not too far away is: try organizing one! Perhaps in a private location at first (library meeting room, a local cafe, town meeting space, etc.), with publicity well in advance to music teachers and any/every musician you can contact, to determine how much interest there is. If you can recruit 6-12 musicians, not all of whom will be able to show up every week, and determine which days/times work for them, you have enough information to approach publicans with a proposal. For us, there are only a few nights publicans don’t have other things planned (sing-alongs, trivia, sports, etc.) - but weekends around mid-day or during the afternoon are another possibility if they are not a “sports bar”. Or, if most musicians have retired from full-time jobs, weekdays during the afternoon could be another possibility.
