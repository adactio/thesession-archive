So Let’s Argue about Posting Tunes

I’d like to take everyone’s pulse on how we all post tunes to thesession.org.

Given that the *first* posting of a tune becomes the de facto “primary” setting on this site (it’s turned into sheet music and a sound file, and is given a link for access from the Search function and Recordings section);

and

Given that the resulting archive of tunes has sort of a permanent, encyclopedic feel to it (as much as one of these things can, on the web), and serves as a valuable source of tunes for people looking to play Irish dance music,

and

Given that Jeremy started this site to foster an exhange of tunes within the tradition of Irish dance music,

I pose the question:

Should we, collectively, be more careful and selective in how we post tunes?

By that I mean, shouldn’t we try to post *playable, session-ready* settings as the primary post, and use the comments section for variations, settings in weird keys, and settings whose value is mostly as a historical, regional, or discographical curiosity.

I’ll admit that Trevor’s penchant for posting tunes from his New England printed sources is what drove me to raise this question, but I honestly don’t mean to pick on him (in my book, Trevor’s a great and welcome asset to this site). To me, the question is larger than that. To date, I think we’re mostly on the right track, but I’d like to hear what everyone else thinks about ornamented settings versus plain melody lines (does it muddy the tune to include ornaments, or do you like seeing where experienced players put them?), and how to account for likely differences in settings for fiddle or flute or pipes or banjo, say.

I know we’ve dealt with some of these things before, and some of it also seems to take care of itself for the most part. But--in the days since Jeremy opened the site to contributions from the masses (and I include myself in there; once upon a time Jeremy alone could post tunes here)--I’ve also thought it’d be worthwhile to be conscious of the choices we make while we’re building a quite remarkable tune collection here.

Re: So Let’s Argue about Posting Tunes

Troy’s point gets to the heart of my concern. Bearing in mind that there is no such thing as a “definitive” version of an Irish trad tune, nonetheless an experienced player will recognize when a setting wanders too far afield to be considered “standard,” even if there are several “standard” versions in circulation (e.g., it would be fine to post the Foxhunter’s Reel in A *and* G, but not likely in B major--at least not as the first-time, “definitive” post. Similarly, you could post Blackhaired Lass with all C sharps, or all C naturals--both modes are commonly played in many sessions. But *not* also with F naturals.

What’s most problematic to me are the tunes that get posted from written sources without being cross-referenced against how they’re actually played at sessions. You can usually spot them because the melodic phrases are incomplete or jumbled, or there aren’t enough notes to carry the trad pulse, or they’re in some weird key. But inexperienced players, or those too isolated to have a broad exposure to the tunes, will have a tough time distinguishing these from more viable settings.

Re: So Let’s Argue about Posting Tunes

Either I just had an epiphany, or the soup I had for lunch was a little too heavy on the beans….

I think what I’m getting at in this thread is to ask for the same sort of discretion we’d show at a real-world session. If you wanted to introduce a tune to your circle of friends down at the pub, I doubt many of you would open a book on the table or pop a cd in the boombox for them all to listen to. You’d learn the tune yourself first, and when you were comfortable enough that you liked the setting and could get through it two or three times without crashing, you’d play it for your session mates.

I’m hoping we can do the same here, the difference of course being that we post the tune in abc notation instead of playing it on our instruments. But even though the mode of transmission is different, each of us should still play the role of filter--making sure we’ve got a recognizable, playable, sessionable tune before we foist it on our friends. Part of the problem I’m hoping to address is that we’re relying on written transmission to post tunes here, and that opens the door to the superabundance of sliphsod or skewed written transcriptions and collections out there. If an experienced trad player used those for a source, s/he would digest the tunes first to find their true trad nature.

Does that make sense? Of course it presumes that anyone posting should have some familiarity with the music, and I don’t want to discourage *anyone* from posting tunes, but neither am I interested in seeing tunes posted in a way that demonstrate little understanding of the repertoire or tradition.

Gawd, if I’m being too harsh or anal about this, please somebody tell me to shut up.

Re: So Let’s Argue about Posting Tunes

Cuch, it’s not just a tune or two that Trevor has posted. I’ve posted settings that were questionable because of odd key choices or too many fiddle ornaments (which tends to make them unfriendly to flutes, whistles, and banjos).

But more to your point, what’s remarkable about this music is that there are a couple thousand tunes circulating in sessions around the world, and despite there being no “definitive” versions of those tunes, there *are* recognizable settings. If you know those, you can play in most sessions anywhere your travels take you.

Trevor has given us a wealth of tunes from his New England tune books, but most of them aren’t common session tunes, or perhaps even likely session tunes, and the others tend to be in awkward settings for playing at session speed. Case in point was his post of Staten Island Hornpipe. It missed the mark for what many session players would say is the essence of that tune. Is Trevors setting worth noting? Yes, sure. But I’m suggesting that it would be better off in the comments section, not as the primary setting of the tune in our archives here.

Put another way, there are settings of tunes that make experienced players sit up and say, “Hey, that’s a good version. I want to learn that.” And there are settings that don’t get that response. There are even settings that lead experienced players to think, “Well, I guess that’s the xyz reel, but it’s missing the tune in a few places, and it doesn’t really fall into place on my fiddle/whistle/box.”

Maybe it’s that the structure of this music is idiomatic--experienced players can tell at a glance if a tune fits in the tradition or not--but without an idiomatic understanding, it’s hit or miss whether your transcription will help other session players.

Cait, I’ll try to clean up my act when I post tunes and focus on the body of the tune first, saving the variations and ornaments for the comments section. In the past, I’ve tended to include some of the nuances because to me they *are* the body of the tune as much as anything else. But I like the idea of transcribing the basic melody, and then showing how all the other stuff fits in.

Well, this thread must be quite high up in the stakes for the fastest growing! I’ve only just logged on late this afternoon after a busy day and a half backing up and refurbishing my PC, and attending an orchestral rehearsal and then a session, so there’s a lot to assimilate and think about.

One of the fundamental problems, as I see it, with handling trad tunes, from whatever source, on a database such as this is that the raw data (the tunes and their names) is “dirty”, by which I mean that there is no definitive version of a tune, or indeed the way in which it is notated in ABC (consider the different ways of notating a dotted 1/4 note followed by an 1/8th note, for example), and, as Jeremy once pointed out, a tune may have 20 different names (with variable spellings) and one name may be applied to 20 different tunes - a “slight” exaggeration but you get the picture. This makes searching in databases, whether a title search or an advanced search for an ABC character string, problematical, and in programming terms there’s probably little that can easily be done about it.

I fell foul of this problem only a couple of days ago with The Piper’s Lass, a tune we know well in Bristol UK, but I didn’t recognize it as a close variation of Rolling in the Ryegrass (there must be a connection between those two titles!), and my usual searches failed to find it. I therefore submitted it. A kind soul pointed out my mistake, so I’ve emailed Jeremy asking him to delete The Piper’s Lass and then I’ll post this particular variation as a comment to Rolling in the Ryegrass. Given the nature of the problem I don’t suppose I’m the first or last to trip into this hole.

It occurs to me that one way of dealing with mistaken submissions is to provide a longer delay, say 48 hours or more, between the initial submission and the conversion of the tune into sheet music and midi. This will give more opportunity for others to spot the mistake and enable something to be done about it quickly before things are finalized on the database. I also like the suggestion (Zina’s?) that in borderline cases the potential submitter could email one of the experts with the ABC for an opinion. I might very well do that if the occasion arises. Volunteer expert please?

Regarding my submissions of tunes from old sources, my choice usually reflects, at least in part, how I like the tune. I can assure everybody that there are quite a few tunes in those sources which I do not consider worth submitting, so I don’t! There are other tunes (probably about 25-30%) that are already on the Session’s database, so are not submitted; although if I see an interesting variation I may well post it as a comment.

The Welsh hornpipes from the National Library of Wales’ Tro Llaw are an interesting case. There are about 200 of these, varying in length from 4-bar 2-parters to a 7-part monster. They are all in either G or D major, with little or no modal characteristics. Some use arpeggios a lot and may require the fiddle player to go up to the 4th position. This, together with the restricted key choice, strongly suggests the harp origin for some of the tunes, but they are all eminently playable on the fiddle if the player is prepared to take the trouble to learn up to the 4th position - by no means an inherently difficult task. If a tune written turns out to be technically awkward or impossible on another instrument I see no reason why a judicious re-write shouldn’t be done; after all, musicians have been doing this for hundreds of years.

Some of the Welsh tunes have Irish and Scottish equivalents already on the Session database, so I might post a few as comments. One tune turns out to be an interesting Welsh hornpipe version (in the A part at least) of the Irish Washerwoman jig. There are others which have English sources, and once or twice, some Welsh musician has taken the opportunity to take a dig at the English tune title, so the “Swindon Hornpipe” becomes “Pibddawns Mochdre” - “pibddawns” = “pipe dance”, “moch” = “swine” or “pig”, and “dre” = “town” (for those outside the UK, Swindon is a small town about 70 miles west of London). Other Welsh tunes seem to have close historical connections. One, “Wellington’s Lads”, a jaunty fiddle tune if ever I saw one, would appear to date back to the Napoleonic Wars.

I am certainly not going to submit all the Welsh tunes, only the more interesting, better ones, and at a rate of about one every 10 days or fortnight; and I’ll clearly indicate their Welsh origin, even when that is obvious from the title. As might be expected in a 200-tune collection, the quality is variable, but there are some tunes I think would enhance many a session if they were better known.

This forum, in my opinion, gives an excellent opportunity to resurrect tunes that are no readily available. Maybe a lot of these tunes will wither and die, but there are others that will surely flourish given the chance. It’s called survival of the fittest in another context.

Sorry to be weighing in so late into this discussion.

As you’ve probably noticed from my own tune submissions, I much prefer to submit bare-bones, mostly unornamented versions of tunes but with some suggestions in the comments.

I don’t want to make this way of doing it a “rule” as such, but I would recommend it very strongly.

To me, it’s like the difference between the tunes in the original O’Neill’s 1001 and the same tunes in Miles Krassan’s version of the book. I find the tunes in the latter to be practically unplayable because they’re so heavily ornamented and skewed towards one particular regional way of playing.

I’ve said it before and I’ll say it again, but The Session is not intended to be just a database of tunes. It’s all about the communication and the personal connections to tunes. Quality rather than quantity.

In short, I’d much rather have someone post a bare-bones version of a tune that they know well, together with some comments about ornamentation, history, etc. rather than ten heavily-ornamented tunes straight from a recording with no comments at all.

Does that sound reasonable enough? :-)

Re: So Let’s Argue about Posting Tunes

Mark the Fiddler’s comments about the O‘Neill’s book being “notorious” for being overly ornamented is a good example of why it is important for us to be thoughtful about what and how we post, and how this site can have an effect on the tradition. My guess is that he is referring to the Krassen book, properly described, IMHO, by Jeremy as mostly unplayable, and not the original books, the 1850 and the 1001. The original books are good examples of how to transcribe tunes in a simple and staight forward manner, a few grace notes here, a few triplets there. (This is not to say they are perfect.) Mark the Fiddlers comments may mislead beginning players into thinking that the original O’Neill’s books are not worth investigating. What a mistake that would be. ITM without “The Book”?

This site certainly will not radically change ITM culture, but assuming that it has, or will have, ‘no’ effect is a mistake. Thinking about what and how we post is important.
