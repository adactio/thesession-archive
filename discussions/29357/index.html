There are plenty of good recordings to listen to, but let me point out that often it’s not the tempo that makes the tune “slow” but where the player puts the accent and how they approach the tune. Often the things that players do which make a tune sound especially “fast” are things that in the hands of a less experienced player sound sloppy or out of time. You don’t necessarily need to play slower if you gain control of the tune. On the flip side of that, I find I gain a lot of control over a tune if I spend a lot of time playing it as slow as I can get while still keeping the dance rhythm in it. I’d even say that I would much rather be able to play a tune slow, and keep it danceable, than to play it fast. Playing it fast is just reflexes, but to play it slow you have to know the tune or it doesn’t work.

Re: Could ya’ slow it down a little?

Jerone, blessed are those who can play a tune at an aisy (easy) lope and infuse it with lift and groove and pulse and life. Velocity has its joys, but not without the aforementioned qualities.

I recall a turning point in my own playing when I realized that the trait that most endeared me to certain players wasn’t speed or flashy twiddly bits or even nyah, but how ***effortless*** it all sounded, at any pace.

So something I then paid attention to was how to breathe space into a tune, how to sound relaxed and effortless regardless of the pace. Of course, you can’t rely on a pretense of effortlessness--you have to *be* that relaxed and loose and easy with all of it. And then that comes out in the sound. But it helped me by reframing the goal--not speed, not flash, not even nyah, but simple, clean effortlessness.

If you sound “fast,” especially “too fast,” then chances are your playing is hurried, breakneck, frantic even. No space between the notes, no lingering on big notes, no time to ponder what comes next. If you’re in a hurry to get to the next note, rather than giving each note its full due, then you’ll sound “too fast” even at 60 bpm.

I’ll second Jon’s advice. Slow down. Even slower. In the old days here, Zina would say, go even s-l-o-w-e-r. No, s l o w e r.

All right. You think that’s slow? Now go even s

l

o

w

e

r.

Pay attention to how you start, carry, and end every single note. Don’t just throw them out there. The details, nuances, are important, critical, crucial, essential. Focus on them.

And then the speed will build on its own, and not for its own sake, and not without lift and pulse and nyah and life.

Re: Could ya’ slow it down a little?

the funny, yet also sad, thing about this is that the recordings being suggested here are by no means slow, far from it. i have the julie langan/verena commins record going in my car player this week, and that is not slow music….what has happened is that contemporary taste has degenerated, oops, um, changed, to favor amped-up hyper speed. fascinatingly, it has happened across genres. there is a like phenom in the paris musette genre, the bluegrass genre, etc….it’s really an awful thing.

anyway…..often if you are hearing “too fast,” it indicates that you don’t have a beat, or if you do, it is not staying in the same place like a steady pulse. it may also mean, you do not have swing or syncopation, or, if you are attempting to, it is not staying in the same place along with your a steady beat..

everybody knows that if you can’t hit the notes accurately at a given speed, you have no business playing that speed. and everybody knows that if you can’t keep to the time signature at a given speed, you have no business playing that speed. but what it takes a lot of learners a long time to get----because the vast majority of teachers are not conscious enough of it to be able to enunciate and teach it--is that even if you can hit the notes and keep to the time signature at a given speed, if you a) don’t have a beat, or you have one but it is not consistent and steady; and/or, b) don’t have swing or syncopation, or you do but it is not consistent and steady------you ALSO have no business attempting to play that speed. the steady beat and steady lilt/swing/syncopation are actually number one in importance, even ahead of note accuracy.

the best teacher i have ever seen in this department is mary macnamara, who will say, “there is a difference between playing in time and playing with a steady beat.” that is really the essence of it, regardless of whether you are playing relaxed speed or galluping. actually, galloping, it is even more imporant. there is nothing more vile than that super-fast session playing that is in time but has no discernible steady pulse or swing. unfortunately, it is a widespread phenom, isn’t it?

Nothing wrong with a crutch as long as it’s used appropriately and discarded when it’s no longer necessary. Suggesting that a beginner practice with a metronome “some of the time” isn’t like giving crutches to a marathon runner; it’s more like having a toddler take their first steps in the vicinity of a piece of furniture to hold onto.

I’d shunned the metronome advice until it was suggested at a workshop by none other than Randal Bays, who certainly doesn’t seem to have been crippled by it. Some time ago I noticed listening to recordings of myself that there were certain parts of tunes that I’d consistently speed up but that sounded just fine to me in real time. Practicing them periodically with a metronome forced me to notice the problem while playing, and consequently reconcile how I thought I sounded with how I was playing. Judging by recordings I later made of myself, sans metronome, it seems to have worked.

I want to know more about the fiddler named Wayne…. :-)

Even when playing fast, you want control of the timing, the phrasing, the swing. Otherwise you get the sessions or tunes ceemonster described, less of a controlled gallop and more of an out of control bolt.

The timing within the phrase is one of the most important things, and for me, was one of the hardest things to get a handle on. Struggling with this seems a common failing, especially amongst pipers, who seem to let notes and entire tunes run away with them. I hear loads of supposedly experienced pipers and the occasional player of something else who may have a steady overall beat, but waver all over the place within the phrase. You have to be in control of every note.

Achieving and swing is also really bloody hard to explain. When I was doing loads of workshops, I was struggling with it and would ask the teacher if they knew how to find the lift and swing. Most could not tell you what specifically to do. You just had to find it for yourself. Which wasn’t the most helpful advice at the time even though it eventually happened. Eventually you just hear it and internalise it. But in the meantime, the control you get playing slowly helps. Trot over little jumps before you go out and run a cross country course.

Re: Could ya’ slow it down a little?

Fiddlelearner - Thank you for your search and willingness to learn. You will be blessed with musical playing and the incidental result of appreciative listeners.

Much great advice is written above. I would like to add the following, wch are implied, but need to be clearly stated:

Without correct understanding of concepts, we go off in nonproductive detours. Centered in the concepts, we are productive even in the smallest increment.

Here are important concepts for all musicians, regardless of style or ability:

Before plyg, relax your thoughts and neck, shldrs, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles.

Time is the most important aspect of musicmaking. Any nursery rhyme in Time is acceptable with one or more wrong notes. But none is acceptable with accurate notes and the Time destroyed.

Time is not a function of the brain. The brain is fickle; it can count at any pace and convince itself of whatever it wants.

Time, the pulse, is a function of the body. The body has cycles long and short, so give Time to the body, not the brain. In other words, when plyg always strongly/distinctly feel the Time in your body.

Feel Time in your center, the navel area. Musicmaking is not flailing at the extremeties; it is cooking at your center. Waggling a foot or beating with arm or hand do not promote strong Time, and usually detract from it.

Feel the pulse as a cycle, a circle in the body. This may feel like a circle moving forward (a wheel rolling), or a forward-back or up/down cycle. These motions = *Yes*. Never sideways, wch = *No*. As others have mentioned here, walking (even in place, see pipers) is one such cycle, and is eminently useful to find and keep the Time.

This means that when plyg, your knees must be bent, even slightly, so the body is flexible and moves naturally with the pulse.

This goes also for plyg dances, where the dancers *count* on you to play exactly in Time every time, so they can place their feet with confidence [confidance].

Plyg for dancers, and with others, Time is first. No one is impressed with anything else. Time first, Time last. Or leave off public plyg til you have learned this.

To paraphrase Hamlet: *Get thee to a dance hall.* Do you dance? No use talking about or attempting Time if you don’t/can’t move your body in Time every time. Dance to the music you want to play: dance at home alone, also with others. Dance in all your daily movements.

All physical pursuits have the basics in common. No dancer or athlete plays without bending their knees and moving in Time. Walking, as suggested by posters here, is also moving in Time with knees bending.

If I don’t dance when I play, the dancers cannot. Or they must generate the entire Time themselves, which is the case when the musicians don’t generate Time energy. Then the dancers are working, not playing. Therefore, I play standing for dances, and standing in all situations except when it’s absolutely nec to sit.

Sitting, one must also generate Time, cooking at the center. I have always admired how Itzhak Perlman plays so well seated, without the help of full-body Time (knees bent and generating Time). While seated, you must also have the circular Time feel. With each cycle, the knees feel the cycle and flex slightly, as when standing, but more subtly. You can also feel as if you are *sitting again* with each pulse.

You will notice that some ITM plyrs do not move their knees with the Time, yet their timing is strong and accurate. These have a very strong (and all-too-rare) pulse at their center that is not outwardly visible. But you sure do hear and feel it.

Always learn music by singing it (or hum: this is not a concert) so you learn the Time, notes, phrasing with your breath and your whole body. Sing before plyg. Listening to a new tune in a session, sing (inaudibly) to learn it. Don’t just get the finger patterns, which aren’t the *music*.

*Fast* and *slow* are not musical concepts: Kato Havas (Hungarian violin prodigy, wonderful pedagogue, for many decades still living in Oxford) says: *Don’t play slowly; play the same as you will when up to tempo, just at a slower tempo.* This includes all motions, length of strokes, etc. This is not semantics: it is valuable info.

When you are generating the Time by cooking at your center, you realize that it is your center that plays the music, not your extremities. Too much attention can be (is) focused on bow, hand, arm, foot, toe, which are the branches and leaves. Play from your trunk. Then appropriate energy will emanate to the respective parts which will create the end result, in appropriate Time and expression.

In fact, the arm and hand do very little *action* when they are the result, not the cause. Heed the martial arts: shldrs and arms released, the forearms soft as grass. The motions come from your structure, standing or sitting.

The elbows should be between shldr and hand, in other words: as close to the trunk as possible at all times. Not sticking out to the side, which dissipates energy and causes imbalanced mistakes, and therefore much needless practicing to try to overcome this inappropriate behavior. You do not walk, push something, or play computer doing such - so don’t play music that way.

Study physical motion in other pursuits, especially potentially life-threatening ones such as martial arts and gymnastics, where any small mistake could result in great physical harm, which means that they must move right the first and every time or else. (Plyg out of Time for dancers could be life-threatening for you if your Time is bad enough :-)

Are there any stiff or hurried masters of any art?

Your comedian analogy is perfect. Play relaxed, and from your center, and you will be encouraged by others. Force anything, and it grates, as their comments mirror.

Don’t play by rote. *Fast* plyg also requires attention to all details, as mentioned above. Thinking, hearing, feeling ahead is imperative. But you must know well what is *ahead* so you can prepare for it during plyg. This way, you are always poised, never rushing, because you know just what you will be doing for the next few seconds, and the next…

The little notes are the most important musically for timing and expression. Don’t leap from pulse to pulse; love everything in between.

All these concepts apply to everything, so don’t compartmentalize (*mental* is the key here) actions or activities. Learn from everything else you already do.

For some unfortunate unknown reason, whenever people are in proximity to a musical instrument, their natural tendencies and motions flee them, and they *think* they must do unnatural things to play music. Then they wonder why plyg doesn’t sound natural. And they practice and practice getting further away from the natural and the musical.

Musicmaking, as any study, is primarily not a study of the result; it is a study of YOU. Then all results in all pursuits are positive, musical.

Best wishes,
vlnplyr

Let’s look at touhey’s quote in full:

“Three months is not an unreasonable length of time for a beginner to practice reading and playing the scale in the key of D, that being the scale in which the Irish chanter is pitched. After he has practiced the scale in two octaves, until he can sound it up and down lively and without any after sounds between the notes, he may attempt some simple exercises, but above all things he must avoid playing for dancing or playing dance music even, except very slowly, for at least six months. Once the amateur starts to playing for dancing, and playing too fast, he can seldom or never learn to play slow or proper time.”
