Re: Practice Habits

There is some odd terminology being thrown about (practicing v. noodling v. playing). I am unsure what is the point of the OP question.

There are the usual debates about ‘deliberate’ practice v. mindless practice. Personally, I distinguish practice from playing. If it is not about problem solving or purposefully progressing some aspect of my playing, I do not consider it practice. And, as such, there is time spent listening (to recordings of myself and others) and analyzing settings (written and recorded) that I consider an important part of practice. To me, ‘noodling’ refers to the noises one makes when trying to learn a tune in real-time while others are playing, and should usually be avoided. Playing can be done for many reasons -- for my own pleasure or as a performance for others. As I said, if I am not working specifically to improve my playing, I do not consider it practice.

To ‘fill out the form’:
1. fiddle, guitar
2. daily
3. I don’t keep ‘logs’. I guess it averages about one and a half hours per day of real practice.
4. mostly technical aspects of playing (bowing technique, intonation, tone production, phrasing, speed, etc.), and repertoire review and expansion. Also performance prep work -- rehearsals, etc.
5. I don’t noodle. And I would not refer to playing as dismissively as ‘just playing’. The ratio of practice time to playing time varies a lot. But I probably spend three as much time playing as doing real practice.
6. I do not know what scale you would use for this. I am never completely satisfied with my playing, and do not find it very useful to compare with others. I can more or less make the sound I want, and there are enough other people who want to listen to or join with me that I find it a rewarding pastime.
7. Almost 50 years

“I don’t see how you are distinguishing ‘noodling’ from playing from practicing.”
I recently got a set of Scottish smallpipes, on which I have been practising. I can’t play them well enough to noodle, which I don’t have any problem with on the fiddle, on which I haven’t practised for years.
The distinction, I think, is that in practising, you have an end goal or target in mind. With noodling, you are just wandering about (hence the name) without any particular direction in mind. It could be compared, perhaps, to making for a pre-determined location via various pre-selected routes (practising), or setting off for a walk with nowhere particular in mind --just going where the fancy takes you. (noodling).
There is also the distinction that in order to practise something, you have to be able to do it already -- as opposed to learning something new, which I do all the time.

I think a common perception of term “practicing” involves playing scales, arpeggios, and other various technical exercises. People who don’t do those things, are probably not going to call what they do “practice”. I generally play tunes I know, learn new tunes, and I will work on rough patches to smooth them out. Sometimes I will take a phrase and play around with different ways and nuances of phrasing, which sometimes includes listening to as many different players as I can, and comparing the differences.

I try very hard to change up the ornamentation each time through a tune (although I’m not always successful). I don’t consider that “noodling”. To me, “noodling” is what someone does when they try to play along with a tune they don’t know, when they should be listening. Learning a tune, for me, consists of listening (over and over), memorizing (I can hum it in my head), and working it out on my instrument. I may play along with a recording (or at a session) once I have learned the tune, but not while I’m learning it.

I don’t noodle. I learn tunes mainly by ear but I check the dots, which I can read, if in doubt and I try to play everything accurately and in a style suited to the tune and the occasion.
