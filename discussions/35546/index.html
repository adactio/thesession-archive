Re: Getting bored of playing again what is your motivation to keep it up?

Just a thought, Arthur:

Sometimes the more important something becomes, the scarier the prospect of failure -- or in the case of the fiddle, not being able to play as well as you want to. So the better you get at it, the more there is to lose if you can’t keep it up. People hit plateaus when they suddenly feel that what they’re doing isn’t good enough and will never amount to anything. This could be what’s happening to you. So, like others said, let it go for a week or a month. You might find you start to miss it, and you might find a new appreciation for the fact that you can play, period, whereas most of the people in the world can’t.

And don’t be surprised that if and when you do come back to it, you’ll find some entirely new insight into your playing. Sometimes a week away does more good than practicing for a week -- or month.

If it’s just a case of being stuck in a musical rut, and things have gone a bit stale, I find that the following helps:

Have a few weeks of not playing much
Listen to some music from different genres to what you usually play
Play a different instrument to your usual one
Play your usual instrument, but in a different tuning
Play purely for your own pleasure, without putting any pressure on yourself to perform, or improve your playing
Go to a gig and be entertained and inspired by someone more talented than you.

Re: Getting bored of playing again what is your motivation to keep it up?

The solution is simple.
If you find it boring - quit (you can always start again in a year or two, or a decade, or not at all).
If you find it boring, but still want to improve - don’t quit.

You won’t level up overnight, but you can learn a new tune (or improve an old tune) tonight. Have fun with it tomorrow. Learn another tune. Improve that the day after tomorrow. Make up your own sets of your favourite tunes. Mix them. Play sets in reverse. Play them in different keys. Try other bowing patterns (I know Jim suggested some for you in the Dowd’s thread). Try triplets the “other way around”. There are lots of ways you can work on your current repertoire and technique.

I wish you the best of luck!

Re: Getting bored of playing again what is your motivation to keep it up?

I think the solution to your problem may simply lie in your attitude toward playing in sessions. When you’re not used to playing with other people, you can certainly feel uncomfortable and exposed. But that’s not a reason not to do it. Playing in a session is a lot more about “melding in” than it is about “performing”.

What motivates me to keep playing is the session playing. There are moments in sessions that are the “perfect moment”, where the music is flowing effortlessly, and everybody is in the groove. To me, that moment gives a real sense of euphoria, and I wish it could last forever. I have sometimes compared the pursuit of that moment to be a bit like chasing the dragon. Where people that experience certain drugs (usually heroin) spend the rest of their lives trying to find that feeling again. The difference is that with the music, you can experience that euphoria again and again. The cruel downside is that the better you get at playing the music, the harder it is to find that experience out in the wild. But at that point, you can start to look for the euphoria simply from within your own playing.

So you can quit playing if you feel that’s the thing to do, but if you take into account what I just said about sessions, I think that you might find that getting out and playing in sessions might just turn out to be your motivation too. Then it, too, would turn into something that you look forward to, and never have to feel like you’re forcing yourself into something you dislike…

Re: Getting bored of playing again what is your motivation to keep it up?

You can make it hard work, slog at it, work at it and you will have just destroyed it. It’s like hitting a butterfly with a hammer.

Instead, buy a tinwhistle and fool around with it for a while - days, weeks, months - until you’re frustrated by it’s limitations, then you’ll want to explore the fiddle again. Let the butterfly of the music go free, wander about, be playful.

Chill. Hang loose. Ban the word practice. Just play. Sometimes play tunes, sometimes play technique, sometimes play whatever comes into your head, no tune, just exploring the notes, the relationship of one note with another, the ornamentation effects you can create, listen to your intonation, experiment with rhythm. It’s another country for you to explore.

Or go play badminton.Whatever you do, start enjoying your life. It’s for living, not slogging through.

Ashamed? Quite the opposite- you should feel proud. Learning by ear can be very daunting at first, and like all of us you’re bound to run into obstacles or make mistakes. One advantage to learning by ear is that you’ll mostly know what it is that you don’t know, and if you find it helpful to look at the dots to clarify a few notes, there’s nothing wrong with that. As time goes by you will find there are fewer and fewer notes that you need to check, and that the whole process takes less and less time, and becomes more and more enjoyable.
