Should melody players be able to back tunes too?

Hey all! I’ve often seen it said here that all backers should be able to at least play a tune or three on the whistle, but what about the opposite scenario? Does it benefit one’s melody playing to be able to back tunes?

What say ye?

Re: Should melody players be able to back tunes too?

I’d suggest that everyone should aim to be able to play at least two instruments, one of which can be used for backing e.g. a guitar, and interchange as necessary. The best sessions I’ve been to have multi instrumentalists who can carry a tune or support another. Some of the better fiddlers can use their fiddle to play chords. OK whistles and flutes are slightly different but can still play the root note of a chord to keep things going along. So yes, the ablity to back another player (which incidentally is not as easy as some might suggest), I would say, is a good thing for a melody player to be able to do. Players are only limited by their own imagination.

Re: Should melody players be able to back tunes too?

All melody players inadvertently back. The fiddles double stopping and clonking a low string at the beginning of a phrase, drones and regulators, box basses, the pan modal cloud made up of all the various ornamentations and melodic variation of any instrument, banjo-ness, even the implied bass of the bodhran. All this is backing. A good backer just mimics holistcally everything you hear. There isn’t necessarily a harmonic sequence per se, this is mainly modal music. There are loads and loads of fabulous exceptions as well!

Re: Should melody players be able to back tunes too?

A couple of points ….

It is not true that a note played on a piano (or any instrument actually) is at a fixed pitch. Every note has width in frequency, overtones, undertones, etc. And so, two notes, a semitone apart, overlap in plenty of ways to create acoustic effects at many different frequencies. The relative intensity of these effects can vary significantly, but the human ear is pretty good at detecting certain effects that are down a few dB.

The effects of the human perception system are quite significant. There is plenty going on acoustically that is not heard, and there is plenty that seems to be heard even though it is actually not going on acoustically. A reasonable point of view is that what matters in music is what is perceived.

I think some of the terminology gets quite misleading at times. ‘Bending’ a note is quite straightforward to me, and I always think of a ‘bent’ note as coming from instrument like guitar (bending the string), fiddle, and most wind instruments. I know pianists use the terms ‘tinkling’ and ‘rolling’, but I wasn’t sure if I’d heard ‘bending’ before, in terms of piano, so I looked it up. For sure I knew it didn’t mean adjusting the pitch gradually (eg like a trombone slide, and it was interesting to find out what it really meant in terms of an attempted sound effect.

I know Will Evans questioned it right away, but to be fair, he’s been around here for a long time and believe me, there have been some really outrageously ridiculous ideas propagated from some folk on this forum over the years, so I’m not surprised he thought that ‘bending’ notes on a piano was being meant literally.

As for Wikipedia, I’ve never really found much fault with it, although the use of the word ‘sliding’ in the ‘Discrete glissando’ section, in piano terms, to me, is ridiculous. Look back as Rosemarie’s diagram :

https://iamnotmakingup.files.wordpress.com/2014/07/staircase-discrete-pitch.png

…‘stepping’ would be a far more appropriate word, I think.

In fiddling (or violining) terms too, many people don’t really know what a position ‘shift’ is. Many think it’s simply a ‘slide’ - of course, physically it is, because in moving from one position to another, there is nothing in between - no frets to bump over and produce notes, no keys to hit in order to make a note sound. All the same, a shift is a shift, and not a ‘slide’.

Often, students are told they need to get from 1st position to 3rd position, immediately, with nothing to sound in between. It’s a mindset that’s developed, and if they slide instead of shift, they will simply whine. A lot. So once they hear the whining that they are making, they learn und understand what ‘shift’ really means, and aim to develop a good technique.

Re: Should melody players be able to back tunes too?

My answer to the original question is “no”. This being said, there’s no harm at all being familiar with keys, chords, modes and what not - if nothing else, that makes you a better allround musician, and you’ll have a better feel for which tunes go well together, and on which instruments. I’m not talking about going from a reel in Em to one in F to one in Bm to one in C, but simply that a straight-forward tune in G shouldn’t not be followed by a Paddy Fahy tune in Dm and then Road to Errogie in B if everyone but you are playing simple-system flutes/tin whistles without keys.

“Does it benefit one’s melody playing to be able to back tunes?”

Yes, if (and that’s a big if) the people encourage backing à la double-stops, the occasional micro-variation/harmony/deviation. If not - no.
