↳ Posted by aliceflynn on January 15th, 2003.

This is what Hammy Hamilton had to say about it:
"Michael Robinson’s comments about the session and the development of non-solo playing are very relevant. I’ve been working in
this area for some time and I believe that there is a strong connection between the improvement in social and economic conditions in
Ireland at the end of the 19th century and the rise of amateur playing of traditional music. It seems that previously the vast majority of
players were professional. Non-solo playing doesn’t really appear until the early recordings of the 78 RPM period in the States. The
session as we know it today is a much later development, in the majority of cases not being common until the 1950’s! The earliest
date that I can establish for a pub session is in the late 1930’s and I think this would have been very unusual at the time."

I think we all have heard ‘sessions are a recent invention’ phrase before. Just on instinct, I’d have to say there is a bit more falsehood in the statement than truth. Zina’s example of Chief O’Neill would support that groups of Irish musicians sought out and enjoyed the company of other musicians, and have been doing it for some time.

Margaret Steiner of Indiana University notes that singers were given special attention where as the musicians were regarded as background music. She therefore concludes that the soloist has more respected traditional roots. This conclusion, on the face of it, is absolutely prejudiced to support preconceived notions.

To give full attention to the soloist singer is a world-wide mark of respect and common courtesy, not an indication of the non-traditional nature ‘of ensemble playing that people associate with the session’.

Re: How long have

Flutemaker/researcher Colin Hamilton, writing in Fintan Vallely’s “The Companion to Traditional Irish Music” puts the timeline more or less like this:

First solo playing until the late 19th century.
Then solo with accompaniment (he says that there is evidence of the bodhran being used as accompaniment as early as the 1830s).
Then ensembles, ceilli bands, starting in the 1920s.
Informal pub sessions start happening around the time the pub became an important part of social life, in the 1930s or 1940s, depending on the region.
In the 1950s, the fleadh gave this a boost, because it reinforced the importance of musicians getting together to swap tunes.
In the 1960s there are newspaper accounts of “impromptu concerts”.
In the 1970s the publicans “get it” and start paying one or two musicians to show up regularly to ensure that a session would happen. The term “session” (probably from “jam session”, ugh) seems to be from the 1970s.

Hammy writes exclusively of the Irish side of the pond. I’m not sure about the USA diaspora, but i think musicians sometimes would play together after work, for fun, but it’s hard for me to think of large sessions as we have today, before the explosion of interest in Irish stuff here in the US, in the 1990s.

↳ Posted by glauber on January 17th, 2003.

It’s hard to think things were once different, but first of all, there was no money. People working menial jobs can’t afford to go shopping for fiddles and flutes. Then, the whole thing of being Irish wasn’t really high in the social scale; people tended to associate Irish with rowdyness and fights, not with dance music.

Even one generation ago, thinking of the giants, like Junior Creham, Josie McDermott, these were solitary people keeping a tradition they got from their elders; they weren’t exactly doing Riverdance in Vegas.

The other current of the tradition is people who did recordings, commercially. That was usually with (bad) piano accompaniment. And the ceilli bands. The session repertory comes from these non-session modes of playing.

Re: How long have

Going back to the meaning of the word “session”, its root is the Latin sedere - sit. (Funny that it is dance music, but session means sitting, no dancers required.) The modern meaning refers to the regular gathering of a court (congress is in session, parliament is in session, etc.) and the connection to sessions of sitting in London pubs drinking and singing has got to be considered in how the Irish session began.

As mentioned in the earlier discussion on IRTRAD, it was traditionally considered IMPOLITE to play while another musician was playing, to the point that …"In Cape Breton, which has probably the
most conservative tradition in Gaelic music, it
was unheard of until quite recently to have more
than one
fiddler playing at a time. To play while another
person was playing would have been considered just
as rude as talking while another person was
talking."

I’ll be interested in seeing what Barry Finn can add about the Boston Dudley Street sessions circa 1930-50.
