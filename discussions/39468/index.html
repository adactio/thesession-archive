Re: ascap

“Do you really put that much planning into what you’re going to play at a session?”

Probably not, but neither does ASCAP. In spite of the gazillions of dollars going through ASCAP and other such collection agencies, they really don’t have either the money or the manpower to cover all the little pubs, restaurants, and meeting houses across the world. For the most part, ASCAP et al is concerned about people using copyright material while making money doing so: in a recording, live performance, a venue where a cover charge is regularly collected, or radio stations playing over the airwaves or Netflix streaming on the internet etc. Fortunately, the vast majority play by the rules so that artists can be paid for their intellectual property. For example, here in the States, if you want to record a CD with twelve songs, all covers, and you want to make 2500 copies to sell, it will cost you about $3000 (more or less) for all twelve covers. That’s the kind of money ASCAP wants to go after, making sure that their artists get compensated for the use of their material in these types of situations.

Re: ascap

Sadly, I have some experience with this. In the U.S. at least, the agencies (ascap, bmi, sesac) employ representatives who search their local areas for venues that offer live music. That person will visit each venue, hoping to hear something under copyright. When they inevitably do hear something somewhat contemporary, the agency sends form letters to the owner threatening a lawsuit unless the venue buys a license.

Some venue owners just ignore the letters, because only a small fraction of the cases are ever brought to trial. But the agency invariably wins, so many venue owners play it safe and cancel the live music, whatever it might be. Saying that you play only public-domain music will not help, because the letters will keep coming, regardless. Our session lasted about seven years at one place that ignored the threatening letters. But at another place, my little “karaoke act” solo gig got canceled after a few months, for that reason. (Yes, I admit that I played licensed material. I would have offered them some of my fifty bucks per gig--twice monthly!--but they never asked.)

On the other hand, ASCAP and the others get their money from thousands--millions?--of licenses sold, to venues of all sizes, even very small ones. That money is split between the copyright holders (most of that portion going to the international pop stars, of course) and the head office of ASCAP or whatever. They don’t really care about the public-domain music excuse, and will just ignore it. It’s all about going through the motions, to effectively produce revenue. From what I have seen, you (the owner of the venue) either pay up, or take your chances. There’s not a lot that session players can do about it, it’s up to the owner.

Re: ascap

1) True story: In our area, a tiny ethnic (not Irish) restaurant opened, and the owner’s brother dressed in his ethnic costume and played very skillfully on the front sidewalk to advertise the restaurant. A nice article with photo appeared in the local paper.

A man claiming to be from ASCAP soon showed up and demanded payment. The owner said “Those are all old traditional tunes, except a few tunes written by a friend in the ‘old country’!” The ASCAP man said “Something in there must be copyrighted. If you don’t pay up, we will take you to court and we never lose.” So the owner told his brother to never play there again. The restaurant closed not long after.

2) Some friends and I play at a local farmer’s market. The national farmer’s market community was agonizing over this a few years ago. One article giving advice to market owners claimed that as long as the music being played was Irish or Appalachian trad (as we do), there would be no problem. However, I did contact the copyright owner of one well known session tune, and he graciously gave me permission to play it at a farmer’s market.

3) Regarding such public places, what would ASCAP do if, without permission from the owners, the patrons of a farmer’s market decided to walk around singing (say) “Rocky Mountain High”? To me, that’s an indication that acoustic music played not-for-profit should be below the legal radar.

4) Personally, I think this harassment of pubs, etc. is legal extortion. The only ASCAP members that actually benefit are pop stars.
