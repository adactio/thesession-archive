Re: Keyed Flute vs. Keyless

If you can’t listen to good players in the flesh, then buy good albums, and if you want to learn tunes from recordings then stick to individual artists, rather than large ensembles: you’ll be better able to hear the subtleties of their playing, and the exact notes of their versions of tunes. Remember that when you hear an accomplished musician play a tune, it will be their version, and may well differ from versions commonly played at sessions. The way around this, if you’re only able to learn from recordings, is to listen to several versions of the same tune, and pick up the fundamentals of the tune from those versions.

This site is very helpful that way, as you can often find the name of tune, and using the ‘search’ facility, find out who has recorded it. Beware that certain tunes, particularly those known by an individual’s name, will not necessarily be the tune you’re looking for: you can check the sheet music to see if it is the tune you want.

A helpful tool for learning tunes from CDs is the ‘Amazing Slow Downer’. You can download a limited free edition from http://www.ronimusic.com/ to see if you like it. I think there’s also a slow-down facility on Windows Media Player, but I’m not sure if it has a pitch-bending tool.

I don’t know much about traditional flute players, but you can’t go wrong with Matt Molloy. He has a large number of great albums, some are more ‘purely traditional’ than others, and he’s likely to freak you out with his virtuosity, but once you get a tune in your head you’ll probably be able to pick out the additional ornamentation and melodic interpretation from the tune itself. I would recommend ‘Contentment is Wealth’, an album he recorded with Sean Keane and Arty McGlynn as fairly ‘pure-drop’, if that’s what you’re looking for. Another album you might care to check out is ‘A Clare Conscience’ by Aidan MacMahon and Anthony Quigney. Quigney has beautiful laid-back Clare style with no hurry and no unnecessary embellishment, so it might be a little easier to learn from than some of Molloy’s more fiery Roscommon playing. The tunes are fairly well-known, without being over-played, so it has many advantages as a learning tool, as well as being a cracking album.

Other Sessioneers might suggest some flute recordings you could buy to get your collection started?

Re: Keyed Flute vs. Keyless

As people have said above, the normal flute in D is capable of playing the vast majority of traditional Irish music, that is if it is played in the normal keys on normally-pitched instruments. The reason for this is simple: the flute, along with the uilleann pipes and fiddle, are the three primary instruments which Irish music as we know it evolved upon in the 18th century (and earlier). Obviously an instrument is fully capable of playing music which evolved upon it. Indeed, the music is perhaps best heard upon such.
There does exist a relatively small repertoire which probably originated on fiddle which either due to its key or range isn’t suitable for the normal keyless flute. These tunes often go down as low as G below the flute’s bottom D and/or are in keys like G minor, D minor, or C major. So, many flute players and uilleann pipers bring a C whistle to sessions to handle these.
Paddy Carty (the famous Galway flute player) played a mechanised flute (Radcliff system) on which he was able to handle tunes in D minor and G minor fluently. Many Irish fluteplayers who play flutes with keys can also do this.
Another factor in all of this is that many Irish instruments are sometimes made in non-standard keys. Many Irish fluteplayers (like Molloy cited above) own a flute in E flat and everything they play comes out a half-step sharp. And, alto flutes in B flat are heard (Molloy has one of these too).
Likewise button accordions are sometimes heard in D sharp or C sharp. Uilleann pipes are often encountered in B, C, and C sharp. Sometimes even D sharp and B flat. Obviously a piper using such will unwittingly cast everything they play into odd keys. Mary Bergin plays whistles in several different keys on her influential “Feadoga Stain” album and I know more than one “classical” flute player who diligently learned every tune off that album in the keys they sound in, only to be informed later that no Irish player would actually play them at a session in those keys.
Bottom line, the normal keyless Irish flute is perfect for 99% of Irish tunes, with the caveats listed above. Yes to play along with albums where the player is using an instrument in an odd key you’ll need a whistle in that same key.

Re: Keyed Flute vs. Keyless

One important point about all slow-down software that is often overlooked is to never slow the tune down so much that you can no longer recognise its shape, or indeed as the tune. It’s no good playing notes so slowly that they don’t have any apparent reference to other notes nearby, or that you’ve got to alter your technique in order to do it - this applies to all instruments.

Ornaments are a special case. Unlike those in classical playing, in Irish music they are pretty well independent of the speed of the music, and should be played with that in mind, but always of course keeping to the beat. It’s no good practicing ornaments slowly, because you’ll be catching detail that you can’t hear in real life and which will, in any event, vary from one playing to another, even in the same tune.

It’s also relevant to ask whether you would wish to be inadvertently practicing the technical errors (which you don’t notice at full speed), which all musicians make at some time or other.

If a tune on a CD is going at a cracking pace of 240 (i.e. 240 1/4 notes per minute) or more, then I’d consider slowing it down to no slower than about 200, or possibly 180 (that’s 75%), at the slowest. That way, you should still be able to hear the music as a tune and also with all the note detail intact in its context.

If possible, try to do without slow-down software. Keep on listening to that track and eventually you’ll find yourself hearing all the notes and the ancillary details. (It’s a bit like catching all the details in a spoken foreign language you’re learning). What you’ll be doing is basic ear training. It will take longer than the short cut of a slow-downer, but it will pay immense dividends in the future.

Half holing G# and F-Nat

Hi Sokol - What flute are you trying to half hole on? I find that it is a lot easier to half hole on a flute where the holes are relatively large - luckily my smaler holed flute has keys :-)

Took me a while since I got the keyless after the keyed and I had to fight against the “oh my god! this will never work!?” first. But it was worth persevering. B Flat and C-natural I play cross fingered or with a thumb hole for C-Nat. Eflat I don’t even try.

I find that holf holing the G# I separate my LH third finger a bit more from my middle finger without changing the length of the finger so that I cover the lower far side of the G# hole only. I find this gives me the most control of how much hole is covered. I have also heard of people doing it by stretching the finger so that it is too straight to cover the whole hole (much as
one would do while sliding from G to A). In this second case the side of the hole nearer me is partially open.

In both cases it is critical how much of the hole is open, so experiment. Even more critical is to sound the note properly since the response of the flute will be weaker than with the whole hole. Breath support and embouchure have to work _even_ harder to get the half-holed note out right. But it is possible!

But for a really sustained accidental that matters, then I would probably say keys are the only way to go.

Re: Keyed Flute vs. Keyless

Sokol- if you listen to good Irish players, rather than trying to half-hole a G sharp on an Irish flute or whistle, they instead will create a version of the tune which avoids the G sharps.
Seems to me it’s sort of a non-Irish approach, “if it’s written on the page I’ve got to play it!”.
Instructive in this regard is Paddy Keenan’s performance of the jig “Out On The Ocean” (I think that’s the name). Both on an album but also at a session he plays the tune first in the usual G major but next in A major. Now, if he played the same melody one step up the tune would have quite a few G sharps in it. No problem, you’d think, because his chanter has a G sharp key. Does he use it? Not at all! He restructures the melody so that it has no Gs at all.
Likewise are all the many uilleann pipe/flute versions of tunes which on the fiddle/accordion/banjo have F naturals. The pipes/flute/whistle versions either bypass the Fs, use F sharps but jump around so that they are of short duration, or do a glide through the F so that its pitch is not clear. Or in some cases, just use F sharps big as life with no attempt at the original tonality.

Cross fingerings can be found in many places on the web. They can vary a bit in their response from flute to flute, but here are a couple of the common ones:

C-Nat - 0x0 xxx
C-Nat - 0xx 000 (works less often on flute than on whistle
G# - xx0 xxx, xx0 xox.
Bflat - xox ooo - works for me…

FNat - half hole…
Eflat - use key…

Generally I find that half holing is the way to go on larger holed flutes while cross fingering works better on the smaller holed ones.

Rewriting the tunes to avoid notes you don’t want to play is a skill that you need anyway, if only to play the stuff off the bottom end of your flute. But sometimes the tune just isn’t the same without the accidentals, e.g the F-Nat in the second part of Ashokan Farewell. And try playing Scottish fiddle tunes without a G#! Those tend to be the two I need most (after C-Nat of course).

Re: Keyed Flute vs. Keyless

Well what I’m really saying is that different genres of music and different musical instruments don’t float around independently, perhaps especially in “trad” music. There exists a tradition for each instrument which has three components, the instrument, the technique, and the repertoire. Highland pipes are simply most effective playing with the traditional technique (and its progressive twists) developed on and for the instrument, and playing the repertoire developed on and for it. If you parse out the tune and leave the other two-thirds of the equation behind you’re left with something which is very diminished. It’s like translating a joke or a great piece of poetry into another language- it’s never going to be the same. Ditto with the so-called “irish flute” and the Scottish fiddle.
Believe me, I’ve played Highland pipes, uilleann pipes, and Irish flute for 30+ years, spent endless hours listening to and playing with Scottish fiddlers, spent much time adapting Irish and Scottish fiddle tunes to the Irish flute, etc etc. and in the end any idiom sounds best on the instrument+technique+repertoire that evolved together over time.
Cathal McConnell perhaps has spent more time playing Scottish fiddle tunes on the Irish flute, and doing it better, than perhaps any other person alive. Still, his playing of the Scottish tunes never has the same appeal as the same tunes do when played on fiddle by a good fiddler, and the Irish flute never sounds as idiomatic or effective when playing these tunes as it does playing good old-time honking Irish flute tunes.
It’s a huge fad in the Highland pipe world nowadays to play Irish tunes (from the uilleann repertoire in particular) and these things, though effective in their own way, just never are as effective as the tunes sound on the uilleann pipes.

Re: Keyed Flute vs. Keyless

I have that album on which the Tannahill Weavers play the Jig of Slurs, and they way they play it is NOT the way the tune was written, which is the way it’s played by Highland pipers. Yes I know that band has a Highland piper in it, but he’s not playing the original tune but a re-imagining of it as I said. Another example of this is the way that band plays the Atholl Highlanders, which is not the way the tune is ever played amongst Highland pipers.
Perhaps you and other Irish musicians do not even realise what the title “Jig of Slurs” means, but understanding the title is critical to the issue of how the tune has been mangled in its translation into the Irish idiom. A “slur”, in Highland piping lingo, refers to a lower gracenote, what Irish players call a “pat”. The Jig Of Slurs is entirely based upon pairs of notes of the same pitch seperated by slurs/pats. Highland bagpipe jigs often progress by pairs of notes, and quarter-notes, and dotted quarter-notes. Often there are only two notes or four notes in a bar. Irish jigs usually progress by trios of notes (often in the form of rolls) and bars usually contain six notes. So, Highland bagpipe jigs if played as is on any Irish instrument would sound halting, stuttering, and unidiomatic. The Tannahil Weavers completely altered the Jig Of Slurs, turning it into an Irish-style jig with six notes per bar.
