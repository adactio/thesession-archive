To Back Or Not To Back

So there I was at the end of a great session in which I played the melody on about half of the tunes and backed several others (mostly with mandolin). Another mandolin player interjected strongly into a conversation I was enjoying with an Octave player. The unsolicited statement went something like, ‘I haven’t played a chord in two years and I don’t think they belong with Irish Traditional tunes… the music wasn’t designed for that’.

I’ve heard this conviction before, though delivered with less insolence. In an attempt to give other people’s opinions equal consideration with my own, (and I really haven’t put a lot of though into the whole ‘what is purely traditional and what is not’ quandary) I realize that my playing accompaniment where there may not have been any a century past, may deviate from some people’s view of traditional. I would also submit that music lives and has always evolved with time, region and culture, no doubt driving some purists to drink strong spirits.

My question is: To what degree should players of traditional music seek to preserve a cultural tradition? Do we lock on to what was done or maybe only what was possible instrumentally in a certain region and time? If so, which region, what time? Or do we strive to preserve the melody, the presentation and the spirit of the music taking advantage of modern accoutrements such as instruments that were not known to the region and time or orchestrations that could not have been played (i.e. you couldn’t play chords on a penny whistle). I’m not promoting either and I certainly don’t want to dilute a beautiful cultural musical tradition if providing backup commits that sin. I’m honestly not certain how I, as a perpetual student of Irish Traditional Music, should regulate the scope of my reproduction and interpretation of that wonderful tradition.

Input??

Re: To Back Or Not To Back

>> This issue - purists against the progressives- is as old as the hills.

Ain’t it the truth, morning star!

A tradition is a living, breathing thing. We’re all part of it. If we were trying to “preserve” it, we would put it in a jar on the shelf and forget about it.

The fact that there are “trad nazis” and “progress faeries” is part of the tradition. The fact that both ends of the spectrum exist is a good thing. The fact that most of us fall in the middle of that spectrum is also important.

What we generally do nowadays isn’t all that “traditional”. Playing organized sessions in pubs is not as old as people might think. Many of the instruments that we play haven’t been used as long as people might think. The point is, that it doesn’t matter. A lot of what makes this music fun is that we’re all part of it. Things that didn’t used to be accepted are now more widely accepted. Backing is part of that.

If you find yourself in a situation as a backer with players that don’t like backing, don’t play with them. Go somewhere else and play with players (like the majority of us) that do like (good) backing.

Having said that… Another thing that I find intriguing about this music is the history. It’s important for me, personally, to try to be true to the music itself. The fact that I’m playing a banjo, which didn’t even exist in its current form 130 years ago, doesn’t mean that I should ignore where the music has come from. The music has a certain feel to it that is different than just about every other form of music. The swing, lift, drive, and lilt that make it Irish. I try to stay faithful to those aspects of the music.

So in some respects, many people might consider me to be toward the “trad police” end of the spectrum. And I’m OK with that.

And it’s also OK in my book that there are people out there that muddy the waters by mixing scottish, bluegrass, and old time bits into their playing. (But that doesn’t mean that I want to play with them…)

Re: To Back Or Not To Back

I think the music just is what it is. The musicologists can do what they like, and the musicians can do what they like. I’m a believer in the democracy of doing whatever you want with it. Make it into horrendous house music if you want, make it into heavy metal. Make it into new-age cack, I don’t care.

However, my proviso is to make it into good music.

Individuals often have the arrogance that their own personal contribution can be better than the stacks of generations of contributors of the past. This is bad enough, but there is worse: Individuals often have the arrogance that they don’t even have to learn the contributions of the past in order to make good music. Oh what common folly.

Strumming is often an inevitability of this. I know, I’ve been there, when I was a kid I used to strum in sessions. But somehow I decided I’d like to learn the tunes. There was no hi-fluted ideal to it, and it wasn’t an overnight revelation, just a gradual shift into what I thought was more fun.

This is not to say that strumming doesn’t belong in this music, that’s a really stupid thing to say. One day I might get back into strumming, who knows. But at the moment, the tunes have me. And if I could offer any advice to anyone making a move into playing the tunes, learn an instrument that can play rolls etc. It’s much more fun.

Re: To Back Or Not To Back

Speaking of *implying* chords…

That also leads more back into the topic here too… with the music being “designed” with chords in mind… well, yes and no… you can find chords that work with the melodies. But very rarely does the music say “This HAS to do a D MAJOR chord, followed by a C MAJOR chord…”

This music is more modal. There are a lot of tunes that you can’t even definitively say what key they’re in! Let’s say there’s a tune has the notes: D, E, F-sharp, G, A, and B. You would probably say “that’s D major”. Well, what if I told you a tune has the notes: E, F-sharp, G, A, B, and D. You would say “that looks like E minor”.

Yep, but they’re the same NOTES! The only way you could really definitively be able to tell is if it had the C or the C-sharp in it. But a lot of tunes fall into this category. A lot of the “music theory” that we use to describe this music is incomplete, or inaccurate. It’s an overlay that we place on top of it, when a particular tune was probably originally written by a guy that didn’t know much if any music theory.

IMHO, this is one of the really appealing things about this music. It is open to personal interpretation. I might come up with a beautiful variation that tells the same melodic story in a completely different way. Or a backer might choose to back the above tune in D one time through, and Em the next time! Brilliant!

(And as long as he’s not doing bluegrass boom-CHUCK chops, I’m good with it!) ;-)

Re: To Back Or Not To Back

After reading all of the really interesting responses, I think I understand the GENERAL consensus to be as follows:

ITM can come in different flavors, some given gracefully to artist backing up and some not so.

Contributing backup is not a violation of the laws of nature, but must be applied in such a way that it does not subvert the essential Irish-ness of the specific tune.

An individual providing backup should be sensitive to the interpretive influences of their chord selection and not attempt to pigeon-hole a given tune into a rigid key structure.

The degree of attack with which the accompaniment is delivered and the inherent pitch of the instrument (mandolin vs. octave or guitar) may determine the appropriateness of said backup.

Some tunes should be left alone, i.e., when in doubt… leave it out.
