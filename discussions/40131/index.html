The thing that makes this difficult is that ornaments are created based on the capabilities of the instrument. Re: Before borrowing.

Keeping a steady tempo (not rushing bits or otherwise throwing off your timing) is very important to becoming comfortable with playing tunes. If you get comfortable with the pulse there are basic articulations which, when you discover them on whistle, actually can help with making your playing steady and rhythmic. John Skelton, a flute and whistle player, instructed a workshop where he demonstrated how to use certain articulations as ‘roadblocks’. What he explained was by sticking to the basics a player can maintain a steady tempo with their use of familiar articulations. In other words, work with what feels most comfortable and what you’re already familiar with to establish a foundation & build on that.

By the by, the reason not to switch instruments as a beginner is that the things you need to work on now will largely remain the same regardless of what instrument you play. Re: List of any ornament for any instrument

“ I’ve been thinking whistlers borrow a lot of things from the piping tradition, and what would happen if a whistler (like myself) tried to borrow ornamentation from other various instruments? Would it be possible?”

While on the one hand my first mentor advocated that I listed to a variety of music when I was first learning Irish flute, not only various Irish trad instruments, but also other musics (he played Andean and Bulgarian flute albums for me) still I think you, at this point, should be listening most closely to old-school trad Irish whistleplayers and fluteplayers. By old-school I mean people steeped in the tradition who play within the tradition, rather than people blending Irish trad with jazz or pop music or what have you.

The best players who are pushing Irish trad to places it hasn’t been, are usually deeply rooted in where Irish trad has long been.

As far as what’s possible, an Irish whistle has six open holes, and any sort of traditional music based around open holes is equally possible, say Bulgarian kaval music, or Indian bansuri music, or Bolivian quena music. Just because something is possible doesn’t mean that it’s idiomatic or desirable.

Well, you seem to have glossed over button boxes: I do what ornamentation I can on a B/C box, but it probably won’t be the same as perhaps a fiddler or whistler would do, down to the fact that some notes are ONLY available on the push and others on the pull. You can do a nice mordant if all notes are all on either pull or push, but not if it involves a very rapid pull-push-pull or push-pull-push, for example:
G-A-G is not slick as it involves push-pull-push, whereas the inverted mordant G-F#-G is all on the push.
And you can do a nice full “turn” on D as it is all on the pull: D-E-D-C#-D (using your outer row E).

I don’t think I agree with that. Most ornamentation is articulation not melody - the extra notes are added in to separate the notes of the tune, not to add to the tune. If you think of a continuous sounding instrument like a hurdy gurdy or pipes, they sound continuously so if you just finger the tune it comes out legato and rather boring. You can’t stop the sound between notes, so you put in extra notes to separate the notes of the tune and give them definition, but the notes you add aren’t extra notes in the tune, they are the gaps between notes. You can change the grace notes, change the ornaments, but it doesn’t change the tune, because they aren’t part of it.

Re: List of any ornament for any instrument

On Irish flute and whistle, as played by most of the older players when I was starting out, there’s really no “ornamentation” per se, that is, devices which exist for decorative effect alone and which are superfluous to the melody, at least with their reel and jig playing.

Rolls are melodic and rhythmic devices and in many situations are intrinsic to the melody. Strip them all away and in many cases the tune just doesn’t sound like the tune any more, at least the way the tune is traditionally/ordinarily played on flute and whistle. Certainly it’s possible to find outlier players, fife style players etc, who don’t use rolls at all. And certainly it’s possible to find tunes for which rolls are not intrinsic. But when you hear a tune played by hundreds of different players and they’re all putting rolls in certain spots it tells me that those rolls are an ordinary part of that tune, and not superfluous ornamentation.

Well you said “for any instrument” so here goes…

On the Highland pipes they have an ancient elaborate set of ornaments used in the traditional classical music of the pipes. Here’s a bit of one of those pieces of music, played on practice chanter, where you can hear the ornament called “crunluath”.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=swfHg3n_kdk

Highland pipers and many others regard this music as not working on any other instrument, in part due to the way these ornaments (which are regarded as essential parts of the tune) fall readily under the fingers on the Highland chanter but aren’t performable on other instruments. (Yes I’ve heard this music played on fiddle where the fiddler is doing ornaments that give a reasonably similar effect.) Perhaps accordion could render these just as the pipes play them, I’ve heard Phil Cunningham do Highland pipe ornaments on his piano accordion.
