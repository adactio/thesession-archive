5stringfool has it, above.

“Our” C in this music is a slightly sharp C, not the 12TET C natural. I’ve heard it called C Supernatural or the Piper’s C. Just a little sharp, so it fits the music and all the odd instruments used to play it.

I had a rough understanding of it back when I was only playing mandolin melody, and after learning some flute, I can see how it works. I have a couple of different “C’s” depending on how I cross-finger or blow the embouchure, and the one that’s a little sharp is the one that always fits.

When I play a tune like… ahem, “O’Farrel’s Welcome to Limerick” on mandolin, that note *never* sounds quite right when I’m playing with fiddlers, because they’re instinctively shifting it a bit sharp and the mandolin can’t get out of 12TET. Especially if there are any flute players or pipers around. Same thing with Banish Misfortune… needs that C Supernatural.

Hmmm, a curiously unfocused discussion, or maybe I’m getting old. Kook doesn’t seem to say what he replaces “natural c” with - is it another note, or does he opt for a gapped scale?

Breandán Breathnach raised the c issue in the preface to his first volume of Ceol Rince na hÉireann:

“The two notes C and F are also exceptional in another way: they are somewhat sharper than the corresponding notes on the piano. It’s said that directly halfway between B and D on that instrument lies the C natural of traditional music, i.e., pipers and fiddlers would play C a quarter note higher than on the piano. This may be the reason why C# is so often played for C-natural by the box-player. In a slide up from E to F# the traditional fiddler makes F-natural, so that this is not a fixed note. For this reason I didn’t use the ordinary sign to indicate it but used instead an asterisk. Generally, it’s better to play F# on the piano or box.”

I don’t know if that helps or muddies the water further.

@ dunnp:
“As a rhythmic device it pitches shouldn’t matter much but how does a fiddler think about it?”

No, I don’t think the main issue here is about “rhythmic devices” that fly by too fast to hear the pitch at all, like cuts.

Some of these tunes we’re talking about, like “O’Farrel’s Welcome” and “Banish Misfortune” land on that Piper’s C, or C Supernatural -- whatever you want to call it -- for a longer period of time than the notes leading up to it. It’s an emphasized note in the tune, and then it picks up again with notes coming faster afterwards.

If that expression of the note wasn’t important as one of the melodic anchors of the tune, the tune wouldn’t hang there for so long. Whoever wrote those tunes was going for the intentional effect of the slightly sharp C, as a landing zone within the tune. They’re working the dissonance or consonance, however your ears hear it.

“Anyone want to analyse what Mike Rafferty is doing here:”

I didn’t see any C naturals. Just using C# (the fingering at least… the pitch is a bit flat of an ET C# as one would expect). That’s exactly what I was talking about. Interesting how normal it sounds to us, so much so that some people think he’s playing C naturals. To get technical, he’s fingering C# but the pitch is rather flat (though still closer to C# than C) due to the way most ‘Irish flutes’ have that hole designed. (Boehm spends pages talking about the difficulties posed to flutemakers about that particular hole, being responsible for a number of different notes, as it is.)

The ‘music theory’ thing would be that those tunes, rather than being in G Major, would be considered to be in G Lydian.

Anyhow it’s very common for whistleplayers, fluteplayers, and uilleann pipers to use C# in places one would expect C. Breandan Breathnach calls the concept ‘inflection’.

About Cabar Feidh and the Highland pipes, it’s good to be aware than up until fairly recent times the equivalent note to C on the Highland chanter, G, was tuned to a ‘neutral 7th’, being around halfway between G and G#. So, Cabar Feidh would neither have had G natural, nor G sharp, but a note in the middle.

Thus to hear an Irish musician do the same thing with Rakish Paddy (finger C# and producing a note somewhere between C natural and C sharp) isn’t surprising.
