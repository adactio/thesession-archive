Rudall & Carte…….. Tenor Recorder!

Saw something a bit unusual today, especially considering I didn’t have to go anywhere, it came to me in my little coastal village on the South Pacific Rim! (Is nowhere safe?) A tenor recorder, made by Rudall & Carte, sometime around the 1920’s. And not something they had cooked up - it was their copy of an earlier instrument made by 18th century English maker, Stanesby Jr.

Whereas we are used to seeing recorders with twin holes on the lowest and penultimate holes, these had just a single hole on each, relying on half-holing for the semitones.
And whereas the recorder normally has three parts - head, long body and foot - Stanesby Jr had imitated the newly-fangled transverse flute of the period and given his tenor four parts - head, LH, RH and foot. And the foot had lost the bold terminal flare we associate with recorders, just ending with not even a ring. Modern brutalism had struck, way back in 1732!

Stanesby published a pamphlet promoting his new instrument as the “True Concert Flute”, kindly reproduced here by maker Philippe Bolton: https://www.flute-a-bec.com/textestanesbygb.html . It’s worth a look at just to enjoy words like “tranfpofing”, “muficke”, “fuppos’d” and “ufefulnefs”. Ah, they don’t write like that any more…..

The Bate Collection in Oxford includes examples of the Stanesby original and the Rudall Carte copy. But it was a bit surprising to come across one in my own home….

In England flute meant transverse flute until last quarter of 17th century when the recorder was popularised as “flute douce” then just “flute”, overtaking transverse flutes. After the first quarter of 18th centuty the transverse flute again became more popular and so the name “flute” took on a qualifier, for example transverse or german for sideblown and common or english for the recorder. Hence “common english flute” or “german transverse flute”.

What is funny is that the popularity (french/german) sort of followed the royalty of the day.

Re: Rudall & Carte…….. Tenor Recorder!

Yes, Seperatio, it isn’t far from a low D whiftle. Differences remaining include:
- Low C foot, rather than Low D being the lowest note
- Thumb hole for c, although a whistle could have that.

The holes are rather small though, like the baroque flute’s holes, in order to give some crossfingerings. So it would be a quiet low whistle.

I forgot to mention the timber involved, which we think is spalted sycamore. Spalting means infected by a fungus, often during the seasoning process. It’s not regarded as a problem, more a feature, unless it gets too far, whereupon it’s called rotting! But Sycamore is very light (circa 0.4 to 0.6) compared to say Boxwood at around 1. The instrument feels light.

The bore was in excellent condition, probably a combination of not having seen all that much use and abuse over its lifetime, but also well sealed with oil, which would have been absolutely necessary given it’s otherwise light, coarse and porous timber.

My guess is that Rudall Carte didn’t make these recorders in house, but purchased them in. They also list brass winds in their catalogues which again I’d be surprised if they made in house. Robert Bigio would be the one to ask.

Band flutes (such as played in the Northern Ireland marching bands) are also transposing instruments. There are about 6 flutes in the range, stretching from piccolo down to bass, and they come in three pitches - Bb, F and Eb. But the music for them is scored as if they were in D. That way, if on a particular day the bass player couldn’t make it, one of the other players could sub for them. I imagine that those flutes can also play with the clarinets, saxes and brass you mention.

Now keep in mind that this is a recorder, and so has a thumbhole as well as the fingerholes. And an extra fingerhole on the foot.

So what we expect to see as low E on the flute (xxx xxo) presents as xxxx xxo o, where the first x is the closed thumb hole and the last o is the fingerhole on the foot.

And the second octave E (also xxx xxo on the flute) presents as *xxx xxo o, where I’ve used * to denote the half-opened thumb hole.

Does that help or have I skillfully converted confusing to diabolical?

And London Plane is a little heavier on average than Sycamore, but still a long way down on the woods we like to use in flute making. Re: Rudall & Carte…….. Tenor Recorder!

Yes, part-opening the thumb key is the way you get to the second octave in the recorder, and you soon get very good at it. That’s how the recorder gets around the problem in the whistle that the second octave is significantly louder than the low octave. We can get away with that sort of thing in Irish music, but not in classical and early music. You’d cop a very dark look from the harpsichordist.

And clarinets and saxes have speaker keys that do the same thing.

A very long time ago, when I was just starting to make flutes, I stupidly cut my left thumb very deeply on a roughly set up table saw. Wrapped it up and headed for hospital, where I was dealt with by a surgeon called Dr Stubbs. Not an encouraging example of Nominative Determinism! I spent quite a long time in a plaster cast, and found the thumb hardly moveable after the cast was taken off. Depressing! Stubbs explained that that was because he’d had to sew the tendon back together, and it was jamming in its passage way. He said he hoped that I could regain some movement with exercises.

I found the exercises - squeezing a ball, etc - dismal and unproductive, so wondered what additional exercise I could add. What uses the left thumb? Aha, the recorder! So I dug out my old treble and some wonderful virtuoso baroque pieces for it and set to. It was slow painful work, but I perservered. A month later came time for Dr Stubbs to review my progress. He was astounded, and quizzed me deeply on how I had achieved such remarkable recovery.

Just picked up the first recorder I could find in the Office cupboard - a tiny Soprinino - and tested myself. Still able to switch octaves easily and intuitively, even with the Soprinino’s 3.8mm thumb hole!

And yes, the fruit woods are a bit heavier and finer. I’ve made a flute for a chap from his late mother’s plum tree. Certainly still a bit light on compared to the woods we prefer, but not too bad. I think I oiled it three times to try to get some weight on board!

This is a useful website for timber density. https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/wood-density-d_40.html

You can click on the top of the right column to reset the list into density order. It doesn’t include woods like African Blackwood or Australian Gidgee, but they come in around the same as Ebony.

And interestingly, the heaviest wood, Lignum Vitae, is not generally regarded as a great flute wood. So treat density as one criterion, not the only criterion!

Re: Rudall & Carte…….. Tenor Recorder!

The trick here I think is how to get enough oil into the softish timber. I did an experiment recently with some rough pine, turned into a hollow cylinder to look like a head. It weighed 27.51 gms dry, and I soaked it for a day in Linseed oil with a bit of Gum Turpentine added to thin it a bit. After then draining and drying, it weighed 36.05 gms, an increase in mass of just over 30%. After a second dipping and drying it went up to 37.57 gms. Both times it tried to float in the oil and had to be held down to submerge it.

So we can see there that I didn’t achieve anything like saturation. Possibly heating the oil (careful, this stuff is flammable!), or evacuating the space above the oil (or will that just cause it to evaporate?) might have achieved better penetration. Or vacuum to pull out the air and then pressure to squeeze in the oil? Or even leaving it in there for a few weeks? Or all of the above?

I tried a long soak on pear with natural (food grade) linseed oil , after an initial 24h soak, because I wanted to stabilise it more. When played it would take in water and warp, go straight when left to dry. The reason for this was obvious, knot wood at right angle to normal grain in one part of the flute. So starting with it as dry as possible and wondering if the new soak would get past the old drier layer of oil, it was put in a cylinder and forgotten for a few weeks. Previously I would make sure a flute was pushed under the oil with a spacer, but I thought “maybe an open end in air will have lower pressure and allow the oil to push through or something” , I don’t think it mattered in the end. I was still expecting it to be floating when I opened the container after a few weeks, but it had sunk. Density of solid wood is 1.5 , pear around 0.7 , oil 0.9 . It was more dense than the oil . So it was allowed to cure for a few weeks in air afterwards. I won’t know how it will finally sound until maybe a year or more, because that might be the time it takes for the oil to cure right through (it cures with O2 saturation) . Now it sounds “whole” and clean but seems to lack subtle detail compared to previously also…I like the sound or feel of slightly less heavy wood … or in more original state wood … not sure which.

So for example vacuum soaking does not seem nescessary. To test the density of an old flute, it could be submerged in inert liquids of varying density and judged by whether it floats or sinks. Slightly medieval, but you would find out if it was a duck or not.

I spent ages looking for the fingering chart of an early Haman plastic recorder for an idea of what they were up to, and then remembered I actually have a plain recorder with that tonehole layout (e.q. German , all single holes also) . That is thumb plus seven toneholes.

So I am going to possibly correct something I said. The lowest pinky tonehole is not necessarily there to allow access to a C note (say imagining a D whistle) .

Actually the scale is the same as a whistle but starting with a little finger instead of the usual. That allows start of second octave to be played in first octave mode using the “moved up” index finger and thumb (eq. D and E second octave) . That sounds like first octave tone. Then the rest of the notes second octave play straight overblown, the highest prefer some lower fingers placed to increase harmonics.

So there you have a basic whistle scale and setup. If I plug the top tonehole and thumb hole, to leave a whistle layout, it plays like a whistle. Lifting new top tonehole jumps octave for D, or it jumps if overblown also, as the rest do (top notes still prefering some lower toneholes covered for strength via harmonic effect) .

This doesn’t bring in chromatic playing etc. , but I think it is the pattern this recorder is based on, because new top tonehole is just right for lifting to jump to second octave (the old top and the thumb not , with simple fingering) .

Clearly, the choice of scale best suited to this recorder might be different for the actual layout it has, at least you would not be learning whistle finger placement with it, but you would be learning whistle scale from the first six toneholes.

Since I’m the person who showed Terry the Rudall Carte tenor recorder a few weeks ago, here are my own notes on the instrument, which should help resolve the various queries that have arisen in the posted discussion. (And thanks to Terry for signposting the significance of this instrument.)

-x-x-x-x-

RUDALL CARTE & Co. Ltd. (London, 1927) Tenor recorder, A=415, near-copy of Stanesby Jr’s “New English Flute”, “finished” by Joanne Saunders (Melbourne, 2023)

Owner: Geoffrey BENJAMIN, Singapore, who wrote this note.

[I failed to insert a photograph of the instrument.]

According to Robert Bigio (by email to me in 2000), this Rudall Carte tenor recorder (serial no. 50) is listed as follows in the company’s own records:

“25 October, 1927. Bb Tenor. High pitch. Sold for £6-0-0 on 21 November 1927 to Miss K. Bouverie of Highworth.” [No individual maker’s name; no indication of the material.]

Wikipedia shows that the purchaser would have been “Katherine (sometimes known as Katharine) Harriot Duncombe Pleydell-Bouverie (7 June 1895 – 1985) … a pioneer in modern English studio pottery, known for her wood-ash glazes”. I have found no mention of Ms Bouverie as a musician, however.

Rudall, Carte & Co’s “Catalogue of Musical Instruments, Military and Orchestral” (1931) lists five sizes of “Copies of Old Original Recorders”, from sopranino (£3) to bass (£12). The tenor cost £6, which is equivalent to around £550 in current (2023) purchasing power. That sum can buy a good-quality factory-made tenor recorder today.

Although it is listed in Rudall Carte’s records as a “high-pitch recorder in B flat”, the instrument was obviously based on, or closely related to, the (four-joint) Stanesby Jr in the Paris Conservatoire collection or on the (three-joint) one in the Metropolitan Museum. Presumably, the “high pitch” referred to here must have been the A=452 Hz (?) standard, still largely employed in the UK in the 1920s. This would bring the ostensible “B flat” closer to a modern B, and therefore to a “Baroque”-pitch C at A=415.

This is an antique (almost 100 years old) pre-“revival” instrument made by the leading wooden transverse-flute maker of the time, with fingering closer to the original Baroque pattern (i.e., neither Dolmetsch “English” nor Harlan “German” fingering). The lightweight spalted wood is probably sycamore or maple. The windway is slightly curved; the toneholes are undercut throughout; there is no key; the bore is lacquered; and metal rings support the tenon ends. With the headjoint pulled out by 5mm, the instrument plays comfortably at A=415 Hz. Fred Morgan made two matching external tuning rings to stabilise the instrument when the headjoint is pulled out.

The tenons were recorked by von Huene, who also supplied a brass fleur-de-lys thumb rest. Unfortunately, both von Huene and The Early Music Shop (UK), said that nothing could be done to improve the voicing – although the instrument played reasonably well as it was, albeit rather quietly and with some special fingerings that covered more than two octaves.

However, the voicing has since (in July 2023) been “finished” in a masterly fashion by Joanne Saunders (Melbourne), 96 years after the original manufacture (https://www.joannesaunders.com.au). Joanne’s finishing included changing the block’s longitudinal surface from convex to concave and adding chamfers at the windway exit. The instrument now serves as a flexible concert-worthy Baroque-pitch tenor recorder, that can take strong blowing and is also unusually (for a keyless tenor) comfortable in the hand.
