Re: Another question about composing advice

Kellie, Don’t worry about rules, if you’ve got the creative urge just write tunes. Write music that sounds good to you, without worrying about whether it is ‘Irish’ or not. If you are steeped in Irish music they will probably come out sounding Irish anyway, but if not they will still be good tunes. And later on when you are familiar enough with the music that you don’t need to ask about rules, then you’ll be able to adapt them to sound Irish if you want.

As for the difference between dancing music and listening music, as far as the tunes are concerned there is none: the difference is all in the way they are played. The exact same tune could be played different ways in Ireland as a solo performance, by a ceili band or for step dancing, in Scotland by a pipe band for marching, a ceilidh band for dancing and a fiddle orchestra for listening, And in America in any one of several traditions. It might even land up as part of a classical composition in England! It isn’t the tunes that make the tradition, only the way they are played.

Re: Another question about composing advice

When it comes to would-be composers, I do have certain pet peeves, that is, things that will make me hit the stop button real fast.

1) Getting drunk on too much tonic. Hey, there are other chords out there, and there’s something called a leading tone to help you get to them.

2) Too much repeating repetitive repetition. Composers come up with a great idea, but then beat it to death like a dead horse. This is usually a dead giveaway for a lack of creativity. At least give me a little bit of variety.

3) Lack of harmonic progression. This is a little bit different from #1 above. You can have three or more chords but you can still get yourself stuck doing doughnuts in the parking lot. Before you even put the first note to paper or screen you should map out (generally) the harmonic progression you want your composition to take. Admittedly, with a typical ITM tune that’s a 16 measure two part format, you are not going to get vary far, but you still have to have a plan that goes a bit beyond I-V-I.

4) Lack of a hook. This is a bit harder to define, but the “hook” is that concept or idea, a catchy phrase, theme, harmony, or lyrics, that grabs the listeners attention and makes him sit up and say “hey that was interesting, I want to hear that again!” If you don’t have a hook, you won’t catch any fish. Certainly not this one.

There are probably a few more but that’s all I can come up with now.
Hope that helps.

Re: Another question about composing advice

I actually encourage people just starting out with the music to try composing tunes - not because they should play them in public or share them, but because it’s a good exercise in figuring out what makes this music sound Irish. If you compose a tune, record it, and listen to it, see what sounds right and wrong about it to you. Try to figure out what makes it seem not-so-Irish in parts. Then maybe give it a rest for a month or two, and go back and listen to your tune again to see if your impressions have changed.

From that exercise, you can see if you learned anything about tunes in the tradition that you didn’t notice before. Like tunes that sometimes chase their own tails, and don’t resolve until the beginning of the tune comes around again, or tunes that cleverly share phrases between parts, but you didn’t realize it until you learned it, etc.

The main thing that composing will do is give you a much greater appreciation for the good composers and good tunes, which never seem forced, and just feel like they were meant to be… I’ve composed a bunch of tunes over the years, most of which are pretty worthless, but a few of them have what it takes. And I would say that composing was one of many important pieces that contributed to my understanding of the music, such as it is right now. Another thing that I found interesting is that I composed some tunes 10+ years ago that I thought were crap, but now that I’m a better player, they sound better to me because I can play them better.

One other thing to note is that forcing composition has never been very productive to me - I can force myself to compose, but the resulting tunes always sound a bit contrived. The best compositions are ones that flowed out of me when I had been playing a lot and listening a lot.

Re: Another question about composing advice

I compose quite a lot of music, including some Irish (sounding) tunes.

I used to think it was easy to compose Irish tunes - and in some ways, it is. Very easy. Simple form, repeating patterns, moderate tonality, and all the notes mostly within 2 octaves. What’s the problem?

Well, those same simple and limiting parameters can also make it very difficult. For it to be “good”, your comp needs to have the approval of those who know the genre well, and is a bit (almost) striking, or at least can get their attention. There are thousands of Irish tunes, and I know this is subjective and is just my opinion, but I don’t think there are too many of those thousands that stand out in that manner. Those ones are played for different reasons…

So, I’m only happy with a small percentage of my Irish comps, and this is verified by my “peers”.

My other comps appear to be fine :)

I guess my Irish tunes need that certain “Jenny sick quacks” (sorry, Diane Lovejoy!)

Having said all that, you just need to follow your musical instincts when you compose - and above all, believe in what you do :)

Re: Another question about composing advice

Many trad tunes do have repeated phrases in them: a common pattern for a 32-bar tune might be:

A tune:
Phrase 1 - 4 bars, Phrase 2 - 4 bars, ending on a note that fits with the V chord,
Phrase 1 again - 4 bars, Phrase 3 - 4 bars, often ending on tonic (key that tune is in)

B tune: (make it different from A - perhaps go higher, or tune A tune upside down?)
Phrase 4 - 4 bars, Phrase 5 - 4 bars,
Phrase 4 again - 4 bars, Phrase 3 again (gives a sensation of returning home!)

This may be an over-simplification, but a lot of tunes do work this way. And you can, of course play both A and B tunes with repeats to give you 64 bars.

Play tunes only once, keep your mind empty and your gullet well full of liquids, ramble through tunes you know and then throw in some self spontaneous composed tunes on the spot.
