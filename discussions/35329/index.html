Yes, if you want to play traditional instrumental music (jigs & reels etc.) then you will probably want to get it re-strung for GDAE, but if you plan to use it for vocal accompaniment then you may well want to keep the CGDA tuning. Either way, it doesn’t really matter at the moment, you can start learning with it tuned as it is, and make the change later on if necessary, everything you learn now will still apply if you change pitch.

You won’t find much information about the tenor guitar, but everything written for the mandolin and mandola is equally relevant to the tenor guitar. One of the best sources is "Music Theory For Modern Mandolin

Re: Tenor guitar scales?

Just as a bit of background, both the tenor banjo and tenor guitar were generally tuned CGDA in the early 20th century, but for playing Irish, it makes much more sense to tune them GDAE, which is the same tuning as the fiddle and mandolin (albeit an octave lower), because the tunes fall more naturally on the instrument that way (since they sit well on fiddle in most cases).

Setting a tenor guitar up to be strung GDAE usually isn’t a problem. There are a few things that might have to be done, however. GDAE strings are fatter than CGDA, so sometimes, you need to have the grooves in the nut and the bridge widened. And if the string gauges provide too much tension compared to the originals, it will raise the action (you actually want it raised a hair anyway, because the fatter strings need more room to vibrate, and you don’t want them buzzing on the frets). Any local music shop or luthier should have the nut files for making the grooves bigger, and getting someone like that to set it up with different strings the first time is probably a good idea if you’re uncomfortable with the thought. :-)

My tenor guitar is my favorite of all my instruments to play, so much so that I’m thinking of buying another one from the same luthier, so I can have both a flat top and an arch top. Good luck with your journey!

Re: Tenor guitar scales?

Clare--
As far as scales go, there are a few simple patterns that you can learn and apply to any note to get a scale.
Lots of irish stuff is played in D or G major. There are patterns for major scales and minor scales, but mostly you’ll be dealing with major.
When I say a whole step, I mean two frets, when I say a half step, that means just a single fret. So, a whole step going from the open D string would be on the second fret, which is E.
So, the pattern can go like this:
open, whole, whole, half, whole, whole half
so, D, E, F sharp, G, A, B, C sharp, D
or in G:
G A B C D E F sharp G
On the guitar you have since it is tuned in fifths, a cool trick that you can do is instead of climbing all the way up the neck, you can just switch strings when you get to the fifth fret.
let’s say you’re playing a D scale on the D string.
We start off with an open string, then hit the E, then the F sharp, then the G on the fifth fret. Instead of shifting position and sliding up the neck, we can just hit the open A, the B, the C sharp and then the D. This keeps us in a nice small little area to work with.
I hope this helps some. If you’ve any questions feel free to shoot me a message, always happy to help.

Re: Tenor guitar scales?

As a historical note the tenor banjo came first, and it was tuned CGDA, like a mandola. It was very popular in the early 20thc for the rhythm chordal instrument in jazz bands, both marching and static, as its bright tone could be heard through the attack of the brass and wind instruments.
When tastes and music began to change and guitars became more popular in the less raucous dance bands some banjo players were unable to cope with the 6 strings of the guitar, and asked for a 4-string guitar that they could play like the tenor banjo. So, although there has been a revival of interest, most old tenor guitars date back to the ‘20s’ and ‘30s’, and would then have been tuned CGDA.
One might ask why the tenor banjo was so named, because this nomenclature in recent times transferred to the “tenor” mandola, a misnomer because the mandola was already in existence, and tuned CGDA like the banjo.
A word of warning; tenor guitars and baritone ukuleles look very similar in size and shape, but be careful not to confuse them; like the difference between classical and steel-strung guitars, the baritone ukulele is designed to resonate well under the lower tension of gut/nylon strings, and will not long resist warping under the higher tensions of steel strings as would be fitted to a tenor guitar, whatever its tuning.

Re: Tenor guitar scales?

A scale is a scale whether it’s being played on a tenor guitar, a piano, a ukulele etc. However, practicing scales isn’t as essential in Irish music as it is in jazz, rock or bluegrass. In Irish music it’s all about the melody so once you grasp the concept of scales you can just think of the tunes themselves as a variations of the scale(s) from which they are built.

One way to start would be to understand the D-major scale (D, E, F#, G, A, B, C#) as compared to the A-major scale (A, B, C#, D, E, F#, G#). From an intervalic perspective they are exactly the same. So then learn a simple/common tune in D like John Ryan’s Polka or Silver Spear. Once you have the melody learned in the key of D, try transposing it to A. For example, every “D” note now becomes “A” and so on down the line. This will be really complex and confusing at first and it’s definitely not something you need to do every time for this style of music, but learning this concept will be invaluable as you start to recognize the patterns that are essential to ear training.

The reason that mandolin and indeed tenor banjo Tabs are the same (in general) as for tenor guitar is that they are (in general) tuned the same. Normally that’s GDAE, but sometimes with the banjo and guitar they may be CGAE. You could play it just the same way in either tuning but it would come out in a different key (That’s because each of those notes sit equally five steps apart). The big advantage if you are tuned GDAE is that you could play the same as your mandolin teacher (just on a different instrument). If you are tuned CGAE then to be the same you would put a capo behind your fifth fret. That would take you up to be playing in GDAE. The only reason that this would be easier is that being further up the neck, the frets are closer together. I think a mandolin teacher is a good idea and he should clarify everything for you.

Re: Tenor guitar scales?

it is called “chord melody” or simply “solo guitar”. What you do is make the top note of your chord the melody note. You can put notes above the melody as well, making your melody an inner voice or even the bass line, but you have to bring out the melody by articulating that string louder than the rest when you don’t have it sitting on top.

Its pretty much high end stuff. you have to know chords like nobody’s business and have sure hands. You’d be surprised how little margin for error there is when you are playing walking 10ths with an inner line motion.

you can also use the rules of counterpoint. That’s actually easier for just 2 or 3 voices (if you happen to know the rules of counterpoint).
