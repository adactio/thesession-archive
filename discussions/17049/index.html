The Future of ITM, what are your predictions

If you told a traditional musician 100 years ago that Irish trad music would be hugely popular all over the world mainly due to a Broadway dance show and that guitar and bouzouki’s would become standard instruments to see at sessions and that professional groups would form touring the world mixing trad with jazz, classical and world music influences, they probably would look at you like you were mad.

So what do you reckon will happen to Irish trad over the next 100 years, what new instruments will become standard, what new trends will influence the way the music is heard and performed?

Disclaimer:

This post is not an excuse for people to complain about how guitars and bands and Riverdance have been the ruination of the music, however right any of those sentiments may be!

I’m just interested in what crazy ideas some of you might have as to where the music is going, for better or worse.

Re: The Future of ITM, what are your predictions

As for the bouzouki, in 1908 it was probably still in Turkey. It became known in Greece after 1922, when a Greek invasion of Turkey was thrown back and ethnic Greeks living in Turkey - those who survived - had to flee to Greece. Some of these, it seems, brought the bouzouki with them to Athens and other towns, where it became the key instrument of the Rebetika subculture.

The people who lived in it were seriously bottom-drawer, often dispossessed refugees from Turkey, sinned against as well as sinning. After WW2 the bouzouki became respectable (though the military junta banned Rebetika) among ordinary Greeks. Thus it was discovered by tourists, Moynihan and Irvine.

Re: The Future of ITM, what are your predictions

To return to backer’s predictions, I remember jotting down my feeling that the central matter of ITM - its playing by the generally recognised instruments of the tunes recognised as ITM or ITM - compatible, by the best players on the scene - has achieved any perfection it’s going to, or pretty near. (This is in terms of what can technically be done with it, rather than in terms of particular highs experienced by individuals at any given time.) To this end, the records of the past have been much investigated and used, both tunes and songs.

The ITM boom from the late 60s was driven by young people whom the postwar decades allowed unparallelled opportunities to pursue their interest in a still underexplored field with masses to find and develop, music of unquestionable beauty that was still little-known outside Irish communities. Again because of peace (outside NI) they were able to take it abroad. They could feel they were combatting a residual narrowness in Irish society and be aroused one way or another by this - though not being Irish I can’t know what 60s, 70s Ireland was really like. In short, everything conspired to make their careers, and postwar ITM as we know it, take off on benzine. And that particular generation only has so much fuel left.

And the future? Here’s my guess:

Economic troubles, shut-downs. More controls, on movement and much else. Green legislation and emergency measures will be invoked, and almost certainly applied at times partially or politically.

This may mean a dearth of factory instruments, strings, etc; maybe albums. And of course much else that matters. More overt control of the Web, possibly its closure in a crisis. More vetting of travellers, perhaps unaccountable turning back or holding of these. Costlier travel.

In an economic storm, the foreign exchange value of the UK pop industry may vanish, and the more subversive or subversive-seeming kinds of music and their players and audiences may be seen by the authorities as a net liability, to be reduced one way or another accordingly. Classical music and the easier-listening kinds of pop, meanwhile, would be upheld as kosher. Perhaps the rhythms of ITM are too infectious for it to land in the second category.
