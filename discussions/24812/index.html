Re: smooth banjo picking

You could try Economy Picking which is a hybrid of alternate and sweep picking.

You would use alternate picking (DUDUDU) except when changing strings. In that case you would use sweep picking in which you move the pick in the direction of the string you are going to. This minimizes right hand movement and jumping strings to get to the next note.

0.6mm seems like a very thin plectrum to use. I’m not a serious banjo player so please excuse my intrusion on this thread. It’s quite possible I have no idea what I’m talking about. However, I’m pretty serious about my guitar playing as my living is derived from it. I can tell you that almost all guitar players will use a much thicker plectrum than 0.6mm. Most guitar players playing at any kind of velocity (jazz, gypsy jazz, bluegrass, rock, etc) will use a heavy plectrum - at least 1mm. A light plectrum has too much flex in it. The wrist must fight this flex between notes and get the plec‘ in the right position for the next note. A heavy plec’ has no flex and it will be right where you need it to be for the next note. The wrist can thus be more relaxed and you won’t get the build up of tension you get when using a plec‘ that’s just too light and is flopping around the place. In addition a weighty plec’ will produce a more solid and definite tone. It takes a bit of getting used to if you’ve using a light plec’ but it’s well worth making the effort. You’ll have more control and produce a better tone. I favour the Fender Classic Celluloid heavy plec’s myself. There’s something about celluloid that seems to grip the string and produce a nice snappy tone. These plec’s are very popular with some of the world’s most discriminating guitar players:

http://www.wegenpicks.com/

I have no idea what the top banjo players use so I could be talking total tosh when it comes to the banjo. I would be interested to know.

Hi Michael,

I would use DUD DUD. The consecutive down hit marks the tempo and dynamic of the 6/8 rhythm of jigs. Of course there will be times when a smooth passage might not need to be played in such a regimented fashion but a lot depends on how the musician interprets the tune. Rhythm is important in banjo playing especially in a group where it can have a lovely hypnotic effect. In fact if you were to be really precise, in 6/8 jigs the first DUD is strong ,the next DUD is weaker, then the Next DUD is strong followed by the next DUD weaker (and so forth) so one is getting a sort of wave like quality to the jigs. Well, I should have stepped in to clear all this up before, but I guess it’s not too late to get this in.

Picking patterns are the work of the devil. They make banjo players sound like mechanical mice. Stay away from them, learn the tunes instead.
(Shantys got the right idea…)

As for picks, the only thing to do is to pick up a bunch of them in different styles and thicknesses and try them all. The pick that’s right for you will depend on a number of factors, many of which are physiological and therefore beyond discussion here. Your height, posture when playing, the angle you prefer to hold the banjo at all fall roughly into the “physiological” factors.

Setup factors include the material, size, and tension of the head, the thickness of the strings and the banjo’s scale length (and thus, by extension, the tension of the strings), possibly the material of the bridge, height of the strings…

Then the temperature of the room will affect the tension of the skin to some extent, which affects all of the setup factors.

So it gets a bit complex. “Always use a heavy/light/medium/Dulop/Pearse/Clayton/tortoiseshell/whatever pick” is guaranteed to be the correct advice - for the person who gives it. For you, not so much.

Okay, back you your discussion.

Re: smooth banjo picking

I always ask the DUD DUD vs DUDUDU question when I go to
workshops and mostly I get the answer that a beginner or intermediate player should learn DUD DUD. This jibes with the advice that most experts in most fields give: learn the classic way of doing things.

The reason for this is that once you have a tried and true method down, you can then learn to deal with the exceptions. The same argument can be made with the guitar vs mandolin fingering with the left hand.

Baxic technique is learned to the state of occuring without thinking. This way you can bypass the technical issues and play music from your head to your hands in an automatic manner. The reasons that GO’C give for DUD DUD being the basic technique are pretty clear. Virtually every other master level player I have talked to state the same thing. Learn DUD DUD first and then become more sophisticated.

There are exceptions to the rule, of course, but in every case they are immensely talented individuals who are deeply into the music. As a general rule they are efficient, concise, and totally relaxed. Enda Scahill is pretty clear that he recommends DUD DUD in his book but when you pin him down, he will play DUDUDU for the purpose of changing the tone of the banjo and for other technical reasons. This does not mean that he advocates anarchy but that he has clear reasons to go to an alternate picking style (in both senses of the word) at a certain time in a jig. Switching back and forth is an advanced technique which might explain why there are various ideas about what to do; those who are exploring their own methods are at various levels of ability and technique and are reporting a snapshot of what they do.

Re: smooth banjo picking

hey guys, sorry to be joining the discussion a bit late in the day.

all of this discussion about triplet patterns all hinges around one major assumption, that your down stroke is stronger than your upstroke. Now under this restriction, This leads to this sort of byzantine discussion about how you make sure that you’ve dragged the plectrum to the down stroke position in order to put emphasis in a tune.

Now this seems to be a fairly reasonable, if you are playing holding the plec between the pad of your thumb and the side of your first finger. You use the downwards motion of the thumb, with the sideways motion of your first finger, backed up by movement from your whole wrist. In this instance your down stroke will indeed be stronger.

However if you hold the plec between the pads of your thumb and first finger, like you would a pen (keeping your second finger out of the way) you can use the downward movement of your thumb, to play the down stroke, and the downward movement of the first finger to play the up stroke. These two finger movements are much more evenly balanced, and let you play a much more symmetrical style, where you can be almost indifferent about whether you are playing an upstroke or a down stroke.

If you can relax the assumption that the down stroke being the stronger one, then you don’t have to worry about DUD patterns. All that matters is what was the direction of the previous note.

This gives you much greater efficiency, because if you think about it, to strike two consecutive down strokes on the same string, you have to bring the plec back past the string, and if you’re going to be going past the string, you may as well hit it on your way past.

In short if you play a jig DUD UDU, you are making six plectrum movements. but if you are playing DUD DUD you are actually having to bring the plec back across the string twice without hitting the note, so you play DUD (u) DUD (u) where the movement in brackets is silent, so you are making 8 plectrum movements for six notes.

Similarly if you only play Triplets starting on the down stroke (DUD triplets) they you can only really ornament in two of the four places you can put in a triplet in a jig. I.e. DUD D DUD D. i.e. between notes one and two, and notes four and five. If you can play and udu triplet, then you can play D udu D udu, ie. between notes two and three, and notes five and six. Giving you an entirely different sounding tune.

Another thing to consider is that you’re not necessarily looking to hit the first and fourth notes of a jig any harder, you’re looking to hold it a fraction longer than the other notes. listen for instance to Joe burke playing these two jigs. http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mGbgF7Uy3V4

Another key thing in its favour is when you are considering tone. The key factor in getting the best tone, is to strike the string with the flat of the plectrum, (not be leading with the front or the back. you can check this out by start by striking the string with the front edge of the plectrum and rotate it slowly in your hand until you find the best tone.

The most efficient way of delivering the power from your hand to the string, is to strike the string from directly above. you can try this by trying to strike the string from different angles, and then try doing triplets from the angles.

Keeping in mind that the further you move a plectrum beyond the string when striking it, the further you have to bring it back for the next note. So effectively all you are doing is looking to move the plec a short distance either side of the string, rotating it as little as possible, because you have to rotate it back to strike with the flat of the plec.

Now if you have the plec between your thumb and the side of your first finger, you are introducing a natural bit of rotation into the movement of the plec because your hand will move in a curve. but if you hold it between the pads of your fingers, you are pushing the plectrum over and back in a straight line across the string. If you keep the top knuckle of your first finger over where that string meets the bridge, you can get a very smooth controlled efficient movement.

This makes it much easier to play triplets, because all a triplet is is a burst of three notes played in the time of two, so this is easier if each movement is as small and controlled as possible. If you’re over the string when you do this, your triplets will become a lot easier to play, and you’re less likely to miss it.

Now most people can keep a nice steady rhythm when all three notes are on the same string. but the bit that catches most people is when they have to shift string in the middle of a group of notes (see mayor Harrison’s Fedora for a good example) GBEG BGAF GFGA BGAG FADF ADFA dCdB AGFA.

This problem comes from keeping your hand static and turning it to make the jump across the strings. This plectrum movement relative to your hand is much longer than playing it on the same string (i.e. you have to stretch a lot further, this takes more time, and unless you are skilled enough to compensate for this, your rhythm will suffer).

If you do it by shifting your knuckle from string to string however, you can maintain the same DUDU plectrum motion with your fingers, and so the rhythm will sound the same, Ie. you play down on the D string, up on the A string, and Down on the d string, but because you have moved your knuckle over and back across the bridge, as though on an invisible rail. it’s as though you were just playing the one string.

In effect instead of keeping your hand stationary and doing all the work by stretching your fingers, you’re doing the work by moving your hand slightly and stretching your fingers less, because it’s stretching your fingers different distances, that makes keeping rhythm difficult

In effect, there is no change in how far the plectrum moves relative to your hand, so it will sound the same. It takes a small bit of getting used to, but if you try it, you will find your rhythm improving. The tricky bit it so make sure that you are consistently moving your knuckle. You actually have to practice that until it becomes automatic.
