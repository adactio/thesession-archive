Re: Do you like baroque/Bach/early music?

Both early music and Irish music feature dance forms (gigue being an overlap) and emphasize melody. Smaller ensembles are typical, with acoustic instruments (historical versions thereof often having a softer sound than their modern equivalents). So it makes some sense that if you like one, you might also like the other; I do.

Re: Do you like baroque/Bach/early music?

It’s pretty difficult to ignore the link between baroque music and trad. In the C18 it was all just ‘music’. But when art music evolved into the Classical and then Romantic periods some musicians didn’t follow the fashion and ‘traditional music’ was born. What people call ‘Baroque music’ today is just trad, with a veneer of pretentiousness.

The link is much easier to see in Scottish trad where there are surviving contemporary written sources - nicely scored out for chamber ensembles or with figured basses, but much of what they contain are tunes we still play today as ‘trad’. If I pick up one of those books and play from it it’s trad, if a baroque group picks up the same book and plays the same tune, it’s baroque.

Re: Do you like baroque/Bach/early music?

Since my comment seems to have sparked a debate, perhaps I should clarify: By “traditional music was born” what I meant was that this was the point where ‘traditional music’ and ‘music’ diverged.
It helps to understand what music was really about in the baroque era - it wasn’t large orchestras playing Handel’s Fireworks - orchestras were a new thing and very much the preserve of royalty and the ultra rich elite wanting to show off their wealth. For the vast majority music meant family and friends sitting down after dinner and playing together - basically a session. Every gentle-person was taught to play an instrument (and to dance) and if you visited another household there was an expectation that you would play for them. But in the baroque mainstream musical taste changed quite rapidly, driven in no small part by the new availability of printed scores which introduced interesting new styles from the continent. So in centres of population, where there was access to the new music, fashion changed. But in some remote, rural areas like Ireland, parts of Scotland, Wales, Cornwall etc. people just kept playing what their forbears had taught them - trad.

Re: Do you like baroque/Bach/early music?

“Irish trad, I guess, existed from the time people settled in Ireland (Celts?). And according to Wikipedia, there is evidence of fiddle being played in the 8th century.”

That is a wild jump over a conceptual cliff, if I may say so. Let’s assume (not unreasonably) that in several hundred BC the inhabitants of Ireland made some kind of music. Any suggestion that such music bears a close relationship - not shared with the prehistoric music of peoples living off the Irish island - to the Irish music we know now is, frankly, material for fairytales. Even the idea that we would recognize the music being played in Ireland 1200 years ago is far fetched, and would require a mass of evidence to be taken seriously. Evidence that, moreover, is rather unlikely to exist.

Your position also seems to ignore the fact that Irish music is part of the same cloth as music across Europe. It has great similarities with much Scottish music, a significant similarity to English music, is clearly related to Scandinavian music, not to ignore Italian music, and so on. As we move away from Ireland geographically, the differences in flavour grow larger, of course. But there is no great rift between Irish music and the rest of European music.

Re: Do you like baroque/Bach/early music?

I think anyone who has listened to western European early music or traditional music from different western European countries and regions would be able to tell you that there are commonalities. The world wasn’t nearly as connected as it is now of course, but there were sailors and pilgrims and ways for musical ideas to travel. I think it’s fair to say that any European traditional music form fits into and belongs to a wider European musical culture.

That doesn’t take away the distinctive local flavor of most of these music styles. Even classical (or ‘art’) music has distinctive national tendencies, and it’s far more international and cosmopolitan than most traditional music.

But to deny ‘foreign influences’ on Irish trad would be a bit ridiculous. Almost everything we have in terms of Irish tunes comes from a time when Ireland was tightly bound to England and Scotland. And Irish people have always had some degree of access to classical music, jazz, rock, country, whatever genres are in vogue.

As other people have pointed out, waltzes,polkas, mazurkas, Germans, barndances and probably a few other things definitely came into Ireland as they became fashionable throughout Europe. Did they then get played in a distinctively Irish way? Of course! And did Irish people then compose new ones or adapt existing Irish tunes into these new dance forms? Yeah, obviously. But they don’t originally come from Ireland.

I believe jigs are a bit more complicated, but there is at least a strong possibility they started out elsewhere. Hornpipes possibly came from England. Reels might have been Scottish, but the further back we go, the more Scottish and Irish culture merge together.

We have maybe a handful of marches that are old enough to come from a kind of ‘pure Gaelic culture’, but even there it would be ridiculous to claim that culture was completely isolated from the rest of Europe. I mean I’m pretty sure the likes of Hugh o’neill and Rory o’donnell Drank wine. Christianity made it to Ireland way back didn’t it?

Music transcends political boundaries. It doesn’t spring up ex nihilo . There are always influences. Often those influences are from other countries. Often those influences are from the community the musician belongs to. These aren’t contradictory ideas.

Telemann’s father died when he was very young and his family fiercely opposed any musical training. He had to make his own way and was supported by his teachers when he was a teenager.
