I disagree with Bill. I have had several outstanding fiddle teachers (in the Scottish idiom, however) who strongly encourage players to experiment with counter melody, harmony, and drawing bass at sessions. Re: Parrot or counter melody

I am “turned on” by counter-melodies but you can only use them if the others know the tune. Some sessions will stop if someone plays anything but the unison, others will forget the tune and try and follow you.
My favorite trick is not counter-melody, but playing the tune late in imitation. e.g. Try the 3rd part to Dingle Regatta, but play it a dotted crotchet later. It works very well (with some judicious tweaking).
Also try this with the 2nd part to Jimmy Allen.
It also works very effectively where hornpipes have descending triplets as in the 3rd part of Belfast Hornpipe (providing you play it in triplets and not as the tune on the session).

I had a music teacher who swore you could counter-melodorise the first part of an irish jig with the second part. Until somebody asked him to try it. Egg. Face.

A cellist and I were messing about with some tunes from the Lunasa book at the weekend. The book Donogh Hennessy’s chord progressions scribbled above the tunes, and Robert, who adapts easily and intuitively, extrapolated several different approaches.

The first and most obvious was a very baroque-ian broken chord kind of deal - didn’t work very well. That is, it sounded “nice” but neither tradly nor particularly interesting.

Then he tried a jazzy walking bass, plucked string vibe. A bit more interesting, but not as appealing to me, at least.

A few other permutations, finally ending up with a mixture of two approaches: 1) Bowed and played high in the cello’s range, with snatches of melody in unison giving way to soaring “high drones”, and 2) deep, low range percussive droning with the occasional cadence-friendly walkabouts.

Once we had that style down, we pretty much threw the book away and played all the tunes we could think of that way. It really felt great to play, and I’m pretty sure it didn’t sound too bad either. (Oh yeah, I was playing a low F whistle - a nice pairing). Needs a guitar though. And a bodhran.

Playing in canon’s a great idea, btw. Must try that.

This concept of playing counter harmonies is one of my own personal pet peeves. I’ve been hosting sessions at the Plough & Stars Pub in San Francisco for over a decade now, and I’ve had some experience with people that have this predilection. Besides being out of the tradition, concentrating on counter melodies during a session can be downright annoying. To blame the musicians who are playing the tune for becoming “confused” is a convoluted argument. Occasional harmonies or variations can be fun, but a constant counter melody would be confusing to the music -- not the musicians… no matter what level they happen to play at.

I have also found this practice to be a cover by some musicians for not knowing the tune being played. Jamming along with a session when you don’t know the tune isn’t really appropriate, no matter what reason you might have for doing it. Having said that, I’m certainly not suggesting that KS or anyone else here is doing this, I just wanted to add my own observations and experience with this practice.

Re: Parrot or counter melody

Welcome to The Session, Jack, and your opinions are always welcome.

Kieron -- that question is probably one of those best answered, at least in my opinion, with “it depends”. In the most strictly traditional of traditional Irish music, the answer “no, there’s no counter melodies, there’s no harmony, there’s nothing, in fact, except for melody playing, including bodhran and guitar and other backing instruments.” (Although if people have very different variations, often it can sound a lot like harmony and countermelody.)

On the very other end of the spectrum, you’ll have Irish players who will throw absolutely everything into the pot. Bluegrass, Old Time, Balkan music, Spanish music, even Chinese and other such influences, all lending a bit to the mix. Electronica! Electric guitars! Saxophones and trumpets and didgeridoos, oh my!

Most people tend to fall somewhere in the middle of these two extreme-ish viewpoints.

A little oddly, Americans tend to be the most purist -- it’s been drafted that this is because we sort of have to be, and in another sense, it may be partly because of the same reasons why Cape Breton used to be more Scottish sounding than the Scottish players, due to isolation of form. Insofar as I can tell, often Irish players are more forgiving than those from outside the country. (There are some notable -- and sometimes strident -- exceptions, of course.)

That probably doesn’t help you much, does it? *grin* My suggestion is to pretty much stick with the melody if you’re a melody player when you’re playing out. If you don’t know the tune, then don’t play it. That way you’ll be safe (along with your reputation, which can sometimes take a while to lose if you manage to pick up a dodgy one right away) until you know your sessionmates well enough both personally and musically to know if they’ll welcome such a thing or toss you out on your ear.

BTW, I’ll soon have a disc of mp3s for you for A’s workshops. :)

You might want to pick up a copy of “A Pocket History of Irish Traditional Music” by Gearoid Ohallmhurain if you’re interested.

Some references of note - there’s an 11th century poem in the Book of Leinster describing a fair that occurs on August 1 every three years, explicitly describing pipes, pipers, whistles, and “fidil” among other instruments. They’re quick to note that this fidil has no connection to the modern violin.

There’s also this nice passage from Welsh cleric Giraldus Cambrensis, who visited Ireland in 1183. He specifically notes that the quality of Irish music is more agreeable than that of England:

“I find among these people commendable diligence only on musical instruments, on which they are incomparably more skilled than any other nation I have seen. Their style is quick and lively. It is remarkable that, with such rapid fingerwork, the musical rhythm is maintained and that, by unfailingly disciplined art, the integrity of the tune is fully preserved throughout the ornate rhythms and the profusely intricate polyphony.”

To all those who believe harmonizing/counterpoint is a new addition to Irish trad - yeah, right.

Hmm… the ceiligh dances are said to be descended from round and square dances of the 1400s. I don’t think you can divide the old and the “new” dance music so completely; surely if the dances survived their accompanying music did as well.

The literature from ~1300 - ~1600 focuses on singers, harpers, and pipers. (All of whom were outlawed by Henry VIII in the 1530s, and Elizabeth I went so far as to execute any that were found…) You don’t see another mention of fiddle music in Ireland till about 1670, while there are profuse references to pipers throughout this period. You don’t see fiddles being used widely until the appearance of the dancing masters in the 1770s. The basic idea here is that the fiddle picked up the airs (that could no longer be legally sung) and dance tunes (that could no longer be legally played on pipes) simply to fill the void left by the others. But these are pochette / pocket-fiddles; I’m not sure they qualify as members of the violin family. I doubt that there were a lot of violin makers around the country as a whole, there’s only one recorded maker in Dublin (Thomas Perry) by the end of the 1780s.

Re: Parrot or counter melody

“My main point was that the fiddle wasn’t the same form of instrument that we know today, and it was nowhere near as popular as the harp and pipes.”

Agreed.

Welsh cwrth (traditional bowed-string) is an unusually close analog to the medieval crwth or crowd, known throughout NW Europe. It’s a bowed string in the shape of a lyre (eg, with a central fingerboard support on either side by “arms” that connect to a soundbox with soundboard and soundpost). Uses several “outrigger” strings that run from the neck to the upper sound-arm, but free of the fingerboard; available as drones.

Depicted widely in medieval MSS and also on carved stone crosses, the latter a very useful source of information on medieval Celtic performance practice.

Cwrth is relative to the medieval cruit, a simple 4-6 string plucked lyre (eg., same shape as the crwth, but without the central fingerboard). The two are quite similar, one bowed, one plucked, often depicted together.

The Ann Buckley cited above is in fact one of the world’s authorities on medieval Celtic musics, and a professional colleague with whom I’ve worked closely on the three CDs cited below (sorry for the wrap). She’s a quite rigorous scholar and has coined an apt term for the desire to find direct connections b/w medieval repertoire/performance practice and “modern” (eg post-1600) repertoire/performance practice: she calls succumbing to such a desire, to find connections that are not proven in fact, “wishful Celticisms.”
