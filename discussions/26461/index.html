Re: Learning to read and write before learning to speak?

Well, I just had to come in on this one! :-)

Tend to agree with methods that offer easy solutions to things like this, or learning spoken langauges. There aren’t any. On the other hand, I can agree with some of the sentiments, e,g.:

>>they should be able to read and write music as easily as they can read and write words

After all, ’tis but another form of language… I can understand the reaction of many here to formalised notation etc., but while it has its limitations, I don’t agree that it is undesirable. O.K., we can speak without being able to read, but overall, the ability to do the latter is both useful in its own right, and it is an efficient method of increasing vocabulary. Learning a language without some appreciation of grammar is highly constraining on how you can use it *deductively*. Likewise the ability to write is an efficient form of self-expression.

The trad world may manage without notation, but it still throws up some big problems in the process, not least in sharing music other than in person. I suspect that trad only evolved the way it did because of the lack of formal musical education/literacy of many of those involved, not as a matter of deliberate choice. Only more recently have we formalised the techniques in their own right. And before anyone shouts at me, that ISN’T a criticism!!!

>>but the ability to understand music should be accessible to all.

I reckon the dominance of the poplular ‘music’ sector would be seriously challenged if the majority of the population had greater critical skills in music - they would demand something more musically interesting. Like anything else, the more you know, the more discerning you become. Ref. members of Mustard Board!!!

Marklar:

>>Music education is best when focused on students interested in music.

That’s a bit like saying we should only teach those people to read who are already interested in reading. Besides, how do you know that you are going to be interested in it if you’ve never tried it? Which is not to say that forcing kids to learn instruments to a high level when they don’t want to will work. On the other hand again, modern music education seems to have gone too far down the ‘exploring with noise’ road for the sake of braodening its appeal, and it now underplays the more formal elements.

If that site has a big problem, it is that it appears to derive too many assumptions from the classical music mentality that there is only one ‘proper’ way to understand music.

But then, I ’ve never seen why learning notation is so mystified - it’s not especially difficult.

Re: Learning to read and write before learning to speak?

Hello, everyone-

Been lurking for a long time and enjoying the varied opinions and information shared. And before I weigh in on this thread, for the record, let me say that I have a deep affection for, and appreciation of, trad music. Trips to Ireland and Scotland awakened a love for this music I didn’t even know I had. Probably has something to do with my ancestors who hailed from those parts.

So…here goes. I have been an elementary school music teacher for more than 30 years. I see 600 kids a week, grades kindergarten through sixth. I’m not writing to endorse or criticize any particular teaching method; there are many, and they all have their good and bad points. However, I respectfully disagree with the statement that music is best taught to those who are interested.
It is my job to offer the experience and hope to awaken a love for the subject. At my school all students have the opportunity to play West African style drums. We start with getting a feel for the beat by playing call & response songs, echo songs, and simple patterns created by the kids. Not a written note in sight! No “music theory”! (like trad, West African drumming is an aural art.) Across the board, the kids love this and I see skill develop in kids who struggle with success in other subjects.

When kids are older (grades 7-12, ex.) and their preferences are developing, then it makes sense to focus on those who are truly interested. But I can immediately think of 3 high schoolers who “came to music” late in their high school careers. The opportunity to learn should always be available, no matter the age.

Re: Learning to read and write before learning to speak?

My guess is that we all saw a bit of the elephant in looking at the videos. (If you don’t understand the illusion go read the poem about the blind men and the elephant).

I saw rhythm and melody taught through sound and sight. I saw students learning to hold a tempo and be aware of a tempo. I saw sound concepts related to notational concepts. Seems to me those are all good things. I didn’t see “every good boy does fine” and “FACE” and memorize the look of these various kinds of notes. In short I saw the notational elements of music constantly related to the sounds those elements imply. To me that’s a good thing. To me that puts notation in its proper place as a visual representation of sound. Properly done the student’s ear is trained in the context of learning music reading.

All that said, the method would not be useful without constant singing and playing of patterns and tunes useful to developing the student’s ear. I’m not enamored with any method that doesn’t have a healthy hearing and repeating component. I’m not sure this approach has it. I’d prefer the Suzuki and Kodaly approaches. But,this approach actively engages students in music making using many different senses and that is all to the good.

I suspect a good teacher of Irish fiddle (or whatever instrument) would build student skills in very similar ways though they may choose to ignore the matter of relating the sounds to notation. That’s not bad or good in and of itself. It just lines up the various goals differently.

One of the problems for music educators is that they need to find a systematic way to introduce all the bits and pieces of “music” in a way that holds student interest and allows different learners a chance to learn. This system (and lots of others) allow that.

What is truly sad is how few music programs adopt and teach in ANY systematic way about music. So, I give this approach positive marks. It is not a particularly new though approach and from the videos we can’t see much of how the detail carries out.

I suspect it is another “fad.” Like Wm. Steiner’s approach or Mary Helen Richards, or Orff or Kodaly or Sara Ann Glover or ….. The approach gets hyped. Experts testify how wonderful it is. Publishers crank out materials. Teachers go to workshops. Everything goes well for a bit. Then bits and pieces of the program change because materials are no longer available or cost too much to replace (orff) or because teachers in the system change. Eventually someone gets back to the basics, devises a “new” approach, and the cycle starts again. We’d be best served with consistent application over many years of some basic principles that began with heavy emphasis on listening, singing, playing, and eventually notation.

About Turlough O’Carolan
By Glenn Weiser

This article first appeared in the November 1996 issue of Acoustic Musician magazine. The guitar piece, Elizabeth McDermott Roe is from my book, Celtic Harp Music of Caorlan and Others for Solo Guitar, available here. Click here for two of my Carolan arrangements. For check out my Celtic guitar CD and listen to MP3s from it, click here.

Turlogh O‘CarolanPerhaps no figure in the history of Irish music is more beloved than the harper-composer Turlough O’Carolan (1670-1738). A plaque in St. Patrick’s Cathedral in Dublin honors him as “the last of the Irish bards,” the man who brought to a close the centuries-old tradition of the wandering poet minstrel. The beautiful tunes he wrote can be found in the repertories of virtually every major Celtic band, and a section of “O’Niell’s Music of Ireland,” the Bible of Irish music, is devoted to his compositions. Carolan’s pieces sound wonderful on guitar, and in this issue it is my pleasure to offer you an arrangement of one from my new book, Celtic Harp Music of Carolan and Others for Solo Guitar.

Carolan was born near Nobber in County Meath. His father John, most likely a small farmer, moved the family to the Roscommon-Leitrim district when Turlough was fourteen and eventually came to work at an iron foundary at Aldersford owned by the McDermott Roe family. Turlough’s intelligence impressed Mrs. McDermott Roe, who generously had him educated alongside of her own children until he was eighteen.

Then disaster struck and Turlough was blinded by smallpox. In those days music was often the only possible livelihood for the blind, so it was decided to make a harper out of him. After three years of lessons, he was given a horse, a guide, and some money, and sent out on the road as an itinerant harper.

Which really wasn’t so bad - the harper, like the poet, had always been honored in Ireland and was welcome in the mansions of wealthy landowners. There he would be lavishly feted, and, in exchange, compose a song in praise of the host. After a few days, he would move on to the estate of another rich man. Carolan was thus able to live handsomely, mingle with society’s upper crust, and compose many lovely tunes.

The music that Carolan produced was different from that of his predecessors, though. In the great houses of the Irish gentry he heard Italian Baroque music, which was starting to become popular with the aristocracy as the country became Angloized. Carolan admired the new music and sought to emulate it in many of his tunes. But he could never become a classical composer for two reasons - he was blind and therefore unable to study harmony, counterpoint, and musical form, and, the Irish harp, being diatonic (imagine a piano with white keys only and you’ve got a harp) was incapable of playing classical music with its’ accidentals and key changes. Still, the tunes he composed are a delightful mix of folk and classical elements. Most traditional Irish tunes usually have two repeating sections of equal length. In Carolan’s music, though, the second sections are often longer than the first and are extended by unusual melodic twists and turns, which was one of the ways he attempted to give his compositions a Baroque sound.

By the time Carolan died at 68, he was a famous and respected man. But by the early nineteenth century, the harp itself was extinct in Ireland. His tunes were popular with harpers, and were later kept alive by fiddlers, pipers, and whistle players. Still, they may have been lost had it not been for the efforts that were begun at the end of the eighteenth century to collect Irish music.

Because the bardic tradition was an oral one, music had never been written down. Harpers prided themselves on their memory, but this had an unfortunate result - only 30 or so tunes known to have been composed by Irish harpers before Carolan have survived. Considering that 2,000 lute pieces and 600 virginal pieces from the Elizabethan period have been preserved, the loss of several centuries’ worth of harp music was clearly a cultural tragedy. Fortunately, Carolan’s case was different - 214 of his tunes have survived.

In 1792, it was realized that harping was dying out. In Belfast, a committee was formed to gather Irish harpers together and write down the tunes. Edward Bunting, a nineteen year church organist, was hired as the transcriptionist, but he only notated the melodies and not the basses. Because of this, our knowledge of how the harp was played is very limited. Still, 40 of Carolan’s tunes were recorded, along with scores of others. Following Bunting’s lead, other collectors gathered songs and dance tunes, and by the early years of this century, thousands of tunes had been notated.

In 1958 Donal O‘Sullivan’s biography Carolan - The Life, Times, and Music of an Irish Harper was published. In this work, the standard reference on Carolan, O’Sullivan collected and edited all of his tunes, bringing them together for the first time.

This led to a revival of Carolan’s music in the early 70s, when the Chieftains began recording his tunes. A few years later their harper, Derek Bell, went on to make the landmark solo record Carolan’s Reciept, and more records of Carolan’s tunes and earlies harpers followed. Soon fingerstyle guitarists began to discover this rich tradition.

I first heard Carolan’s music fingerpicked by Eric Schoenberg in the late 70s and was immediately taken by it. After collecting several Celtic harp CDs, I began to arrange Carolan for solo guitar in 1979. As I worked on this, I also studied harmony and counterpoint out of textbooks that I would buy in used bookstores. I wanted to write a book in which the arrangements would have bass lines and inner voices that were correct according to the rules of voice leading and still retain a folky flavor.

Re: Learning to read and write before learning to speak?

When I was studying linguistics there was a lot of evidence which seemed to indicate that there’s a shift in the way we learn languages - in a nutshell, the difference is one of acquisition in early life versus learning in later life.
This evidence may have been overtaken by more recent work, but the distinction is not based on the brain losing the ability to create new pathways - obviously, we continue to learn throughout life. It’s more a matter of the mechanism: as children, our brains are disposed to search the world around us for grammatical patterns, and to absorb those. This is a process of acquisition, not of active learning. The child, that is, does not learn by studying rules and verb paradigms, but by exposure.
After this mechanism shuts off, we can still learn languages, but now we have to do it actively and consciously, whether it’s by reciting “yo tengo, tú tienes, él tiene” or by listening to tapes or some other method. If we had all the time in the world to sit around and just listen, after the critical period is over, we are no longer capable of simply sitting around listening to language and becoming fluent.

This doesn’t mean that one can’t learn foreign languages as an adult - I’ve learned Spanish, Portuguese, and German, and smatterings of others - but it does mean that it takes a certain diligence and attention. Sort of like learning an instrument. And oddly enough, a lot of the same ideas apply: a little focused work every day rather than sporadic cramming, that’s the the main thing, along with engagement and interaction with the language in a conversational setting. (The session isn’t so far removed from the Stammtisch) A good teacher can be a great help, but the student is going to be doing all of the real heavy lifting. Very similar to learning an instrument, really.

Re: Learning to read and write before learning to speak?

Oh, okay. Yeah, I can go with that. But we can’t do this as adults in the way that children do. A child that gestates and lives its first five years or so in a language community will be a fluent native speaker of that language. If that child then goes to a completely different community, it will absorb the new language readily within a few years, and it will do a much better job of this than its parents will, and end up speaking more or less like a native, with perhaps some trace of an accent. A child who moves into a new lanugage community at the age of, say, fifteen, will have as hard a time learning the language as any adult making the same move, and what’s more, that child will learn the new language mostly as a set of translations from its native language, just as an adult similarly situated will do.

So I think that there is still a role for unconscious learning, but the mechanism for learning is basically different after the “critical period”. You’re probably right that in linking that unconscious learning to the “drinking beer and listening” phase of learning tunes.

That critical period, by the way, is often said to end around the onset puberty, but as far as I can tell that’s a rough guess and there’s no basis for correlating those two events, tempting as the hypothesis is.
