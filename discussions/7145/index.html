Stage Presence: How vital is it?

I find myself often in the minority in the ITM community when I say that I like a band such as Gaelic Storm or Great Big Sea. Why? They ‘aren’t traditional’, or ‘they suck’. Indeed, Gaelic Storm doesn’t have as much musical skill as many other bands, and Great Big Sea usually only has one melody player at a time (Bob). They also let other themes other than traditional Irish (or Newfoundland) music into their music. However, I enjoy them for two reasons: They sing well, and they have phenomenal stage presence.

I have yet to see an Irish band with strong-really strong-harmony vocals. I have yet to see one that comes halfway to GBS singing General Taylor, a capella. Also, they have stage presence. A lot of ITM bands have stage presence; but, despite the fact that I’ve been to see many bands, only GBS and Gaelic Storm have gotten people out of their seats and up dancing. Only they have made it more like a concert, a party, than a recital.

What are your thoughts on stage presence and harmony vocals?

Re: Stage Presence: How vital is it?

IMHO - Stage presence that you define is vital - if you’re playing on stage in front of people that have paid to see you perform. Strong vocals and harmonies likewise - if that is what they are paying to see that’s what you should give them.

On the other hand, traditional Irish folk tunes aren’t necessarily performance pieces. They are meant to accompany dancers primarily. The development of the “session” has primarily been a means for players of these traditional tunes to get together, enjoy the music in it’s purest melodic form and share some craic.

So it’s really about what you are trying to accomplish. If you want to sell a lot of tickets, sell a lot of beer, and get a crowd jumping and screaming, you don’t want to be playing traditional Irish folk music.

Re: Stage Presence: How vital is it?

It depends on what you are interested in…either as a musician/group or listener.

This all seems kind of simple but…

As a musician/group if you’re interested in making money then, of course, stage presence is of vital importance no matter what the musical ability (with exception to orchestas, although the rubric may stand). As a musician/group, if you’re interested less in money and more in keeping a tradition alive or simply trying to show off your musical abilities then stage performance mean very little. You’re simply going to do you what you love…no matter where.

As a listener, two questions come up…1.) are you a music lover? 2.) are you familiar with the music you are listening too (in this case ITM)? If you’re a music lover then stage presence means nothing, unless, you are unfamiliar with the music you happen to be listening too (I know many “music lovers” who don’t appreciate music outside of a particular set, mathematically speaking. I am, therefore, considering a “musical lover” to be self defined, rather than defined by my rubrics). If you are a music lover who is unfamiliar with the genre, then perhaps stage presence is important to convince you or suck you in.

I am generally a ‘listener’ and from that point of view, stage presence means very little to me. Perhaps stage presence can mean a more exciting recap of an event, but it does not entail the essence of the musical performance. As far as ITM…I don’t go to see a show so I can tell all my friends about how awesome all the tricks (stage performance, piro-techniques, etc.) were. I go to see quality musial techniques and/or ideas.

So, to conclude…as far as ITM, for me, stage performance means nothing.

Re: Stage Presence: How vital is it?

To go back to the original question………

Stage prescence is something intangible, that can lift a performance from the enjoyable to the ecstatic, but is not neccessarily part of many traditional music forms, although, simply through their virtuosity, many bands like, for instance the Boys of the Lough in their early US concerts, were found to be full of prescence. In the continuation of the tradition, through the small session and other similar opportunities, it is an irrelevance.
Harmony singing, or part singing, as opposed to singing in unison in small groups, has not ever been a large part of even the english folk tradition, let alone the irish one. I well remember my surprise in about 1967 when I first heard a duo in a London folk club perform a Copper family song in 2-part harmony.
Oh, General Taylor, the sea-shanty. Most sailors didn’t have time to worry about multi-part harmonies, they were too busy trying to haul on ropes in time.
PS Many years ago, a little after attending a concert by Maddy Prior and June Tabor, aka The Silly Sisters, I was asked my opinion of it. The evening had been filled with lots of fine music, a variety of good songs, and excellent harmony singing. After a little thought I replied “It was very good, but it didn’t seem to have a climax”. I suspect, Zazzaliss, that you are quite young, and you do like your music to have its climaxes. Without trying to sound like a greybeard, as you grow older you might come to see that there is more to music than that.

Re: Stage Presence: How vital is it?

“They are meant to accompany dancers primarily. . . If you want to sell a lot of tickets, sell a lot of beer, and get a crowd jumping and screaming, you don’t want to be playing traditional Irish folk music.”

Ummm, have you ever been to a proper social dance?

Are you aware that R&B, Rock & Roll and Country music are all meant to accompany dancers primarily and now fills the roll for the general populace that ITM once did?

Dancing doesn’t mean those highly trained silly little kids in their silly little outfits doing step dances. It means regular people drinking a lot of beer, getting on their feet and a wee bit of jumping and screaming.

A better way of thinking of traditional Irish dance music isn’t “dance music,” but *party* music.

“I suspect, Zazzaliss, that you are quite young, and you do like your music to have its climaxes. Without trying to sound like a greybeard, as you grow older you might come to see that there is more to music than that.”

Irish dance music is supposed to have a climax and the dancers expect and look forward to it. There is even a type of tune specifically intended for this climacitic role.

Before the climax the music is supposed to have a lot of internal stucture, with changes in beat and tempo, to keep things interesting and fun for the dancers. One of the reasons that dance music is made up of short tunes is so that a “sonata” can be assembled out of them, like musical Lego, to suit the mood and need of the dancers and skilled musicians work this to manipulate the mood of the dancers, warming them up, bringing them up, taking them down again, then bringing them to a rousing climax just before a break in the activities in which to have a rest, chat and beer.

But Irish dance music is almost all played out of context these days, even that played for dancers (step dancing was just a performance interlude in the social dance), and so the art of playing traditional Irish dance music properly seems to be largely lost and the dance halls (you call them “clubs,” but a dance hall is as a dance hall does) all play Rock and Techno these days. It gets people on their feet and sells a lot of beer, like dance music is supposed to.

Bereft of the context the musicians have turned largely antisocial, objecting to the the punters in the pub responding to the music in the manner it was intended to be responded to and play almost *nothing* but the type of tune that was intended only to provide a climax to signal a break in the dancing.

The fast reel.

Re: Stage Presence: How vital is it?

Let’s not forget that stage presence can also be a function of where the stage is located, and how big it is.
Here’s an example, albeit with somewhat limited ITM relevance: Some years ago (here we go, Guernsey Pete!), during a year’s sojourn in the British Isles, I had the opportunity to see Shirley and Dolly Collins -- who rank among the greats of the 1960s UK folk revival -- in a London folk club; Chestnuts in Walthamstow, if anyone’s interested. Shirley is a quiet, self-effacing person who nonetheless showed a great deal of charm and not a little gentle humor, in addition to singing gorgeously (with lovely keyboard accompaniment from sister Dolly). In that setting, a small venue with a fairly small crowd (maybe 100), she had all the stage presence she needed.
A few months later, I saw Shirley and Dolly at the Lisdoonvarna Festival. This was a gigantic outdoor event, with a fair number of audience members whose enthusiasm was undoubtedly abetted by ingestion of alcohol or other substances, and featuring quite a few acts -- such as De Danann, the Furey Brothers and the Richard & Linda Thompson band -- that got the adrenaline rushing and the crowd pumped.
Shirley and Dolly gave a terrific show, to my ears, and were by no means booed off the stage. But it was plain to see that a lot of the audience didn’t quite know what to make of them, because their music and stage presence -- in that context -- seemed such a stark contrast to most of the other performances.
So, was it a mistake for the Lisdoonvarna organizers to have hired the Collinses, since they didn’t appear to “fit in” with the atmosphere? Or was it a bit of shrewd programming, adding some variety in terms of personality, tone, etc.?

Re: Stage Presence: How vital is it?

Zazz…,
I like Great Big Sea also, and love seeing them live for precisely the reasons you do, they draw you in, and you quite happily join the mass celebration. And stage presence is a vital part of performance. But that stage presence varies considerably--there is good stage presence at a rock concert in a hockey rink, and good stage presence in an intimate coffee house--the one common thread is connecting with the audience.
The trouble with a discussion of stage presence on this site, is that a session does not necessarily involve audience participation, so that always puts something of a spin on the discussion..
Also, while there is a good amount of harmony singing in Irish music these days, the song tradition in Ireland is more of a melody thing from what I can see. Most of the group singing I have been involved with in pubs involves unison singing, like the Clancy Brothers engaged their audiences in. So it is understandable that there is not a lot of harmony, not because Irish musicians have missed something, but because they come from a different tradition.
A good recorded example of the type of stage presence you are discussing, in a traditional instrumental music format, can be found on the first track of the newest Celtic Fiddle Festival CD, “Play On.” That one has the “get on your feet” qualities I think you enjoy.
