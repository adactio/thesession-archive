Something old, something new

Before a few of you curmudgeons reach complete dementia, purgatory, &/or nirvanic ascension. & while the rest of you lot are still cocksure, jumping into things without the least bit inhibition could you share something about yourself? How did you come to meet up with your musical mates, tradwise?
For me it was a friend handing me a blue tip Generation. The next thing I knew we began having weekly sessions at his house, until his partner (& grad student) insisted she needed time to study. After that the session alternated between his place & Cannon’s.
She graduated!
He’s still playing tunes.

Re: Something old, something new

I was hitching around Ireland in 1978 and heard about a great festival in Lisdoonvarna so I decided to go. I got there a few days before the festival and met another fiddle player. We played a few tunes together ( maybe we had 15 tunes between us) and hung out in the pubs in Doolin. There were hundreds of people there at the time and it seemed like they all played music. And my friend and I played all day and into the night. Learned a zillion tunes. We met a fellow from France who was also a fiddle player, although he didn’t have his fiddle with him. So there we were, three fiddle players and two fiddles. So we passed the fiddles back and forth so we could all play. Our French friend would sing French tunes for us and we’d learn to play them on the fiddles. This went on for several days.

The festival was fantastic but honestly, I have to say that it was being with my new friends that really is memorable for me.

We traveled around Ireland for a few more weeks then we all decided to go to Brittany to pick up our friends fiddle and play more tunes, which we did. We had met a woman from Germany while we were traveling around Ireland and she had invited us to come and visit. So we all hitched to Germany to meet up with her again. At the time, busking was very good in both France and Germanyso we made plenty of money to support ourselves and buy beer. It was a wonderFul time.

I wish I could say these are my musical mates still, but not entirely so. The other Aerican plays Greek and Turkish music. We keep in touch loosely o Facebook. My French friend plays music of the Pyrennes and, of course, lives in France. I did reconnect with him a couple of years ago and went to visit him and his family when I was in France. It was amazing. We still knew the tunes we used to play together and just started playing again as though we had never stopped.

Sorry for the long story. That’s nostalgia for ya.

Re: Something old, something new

I started playing tunes with friends in a kitchen with a guitarist, a box player and a bottle of bourbon, myself on mandolin and fiddle. The box player taught us simple tunes like John Ryans polka, Boys of Bluehill, Tripping Up The Stairs, Oro Se Do Beatha Bhaille, the Rose of Aranmore etc. It was all mixed up with old time and bluegrass and we always closed the night with a drunken, howling a capella “Rank Strangers” thinking we sounded like the Stanley Brothers. It all kind of ended when the guitar player’s wife had a baby, I got married, and the box player moved back to Boston.

I took instruction from a fiddle player through Comhaltas for about 6 years and her other students became my first really tight musical family.

Since moving to a new city for work, I’ve met my current circle of musical friends by going and listening a few times to different sessions, and then taking my fiddle to one I wanted to be part of, walking up to one of the regulars and asking if it was ok if I sat in and played along on the tunes I knew.

My wife brought to me a newspaper add which stated; “Adult tin whistle lessons for beginners.” It was a night school course and I went for it. Our teacher was Aelred Gannon from Sligo town, County Sligo and man could he ever play the whistle! I was mad for the tunes as the music just touch me as no other music ever had. Aelred and I grew to be very good friends and I would meet with him at his house every Tuesday night for lessons, tea and biscuits and good conversations with him and his beautiful wife, Bernie. It was at their house that I learned more than just tunes. I learned about the Irish struggle, the culture, the people and so much more. That was back in 1992 when it all began for me. Sadly for me Aelred emigrated again. This time for Wissconsin to open a great Irish Pub of his own. The tunes have never left me and now I play not only the whistle but flute, mando, tenor banjo, octave mando and drop D guitar. I’m still mad for the tunes and very grateful for my good friend, Aelred Gannon.

Re: Something old, something new

It’s been a long journey, largely because I live in a rock-n-roll town. In 1971, I heard some Celtic tunes on a distant and odd radio station and was immediately intrigued, but I could never find out what they were. Then I moved to small-town Georgia (USA) where it was all country music & rednecks. :-(

But one music loving friend had bought a cutout Chieftain’s 5 album in Atlanta. He said “This is weird. You might like it” and gave it to me. And I loved it. Then my guitar teacher turned me on to Bothy Band and Planxty, and gave me a tin whistle. (I already played clarinet and recorder.)

We escaped Georgia, but moved to this rock-n-roll town. I played alone for years on whistle, then (badly) on fiddle with my young daughter, but found nobody with interest in these tunes.

Then one friend bought a whistle and asked me for a few lessons. Soon we were having small, slow, halting sessions in my basement with other novice friends. We were not very good, perhaps, but we persisted. Next, my friend heard that someone had planned a one-off public session. It turned out to be more like playing backup behind a guy singing Clancy Brothers songs, but we persisted. We organized more sessions, and we eventually settled on taking turns around the circle, picking tunes or songs. Gradually, the tunes won out over the songs.

We were kicked out of the parish hall due to a misunderstanding, but we persisted and found a restaurant to host us. That place closed, but we persisted and found a bookstore. ASCAP shut down music at the bookstore, but we persisted and found a community center. They began restricting our dates, but we persisted and found a lovely new Irish pub, where we’ve been ever since, and it’s been brilliant.

The important points have been, I think, that we’ve always welcomed newcomers and treated them well. And we’ve persisted.

Oh, and: Through the sessions I eventually met other musicians who asked me to play fiddle and whistle for monthly dances. Last night, once again, I played fiddle and whistle with friends for a dance, and my daughter (now mother of my grandson) played beside me. It’s been wonderful!

Re: Something old, something new

When I was young, I did not even know I liked music. There was none in our house, except maybe some nasty Russian pop of that time (like this - http://clck.ru/4T1I0 - yup, at that time I thought that the whole music thing was like that, no surprise I did not like it). I was 15 when someone gave me a CD of Rammstein - that was really, really cool! I found out that I was missing something big and important. A whole world has opened for me. Then was some classic rock of late 60s and 70s - Led Zeppelin, Queen, Deep Purple, Jethro Tull, Doors, Jefferson Airplane, some heavy metal, some Russian rock of 80s that you wouldn’t know…

Then, when I was 19, I met this somewhere on the internet: http://clck.ru/4T1E2

Silly Wizard it was, a great Scottish band. I did not find too much of its music at that time, just a few tracks, and this one was among them: http://clck.ru/4T1EA . “Hey, I want to play like that! I want to play THAT!” - decided I, and got a tinwhistle. I soon learned that jig, Scarce O’Tatties, and played it happily. But while learning, my tinwhistle brought me not to Scottish, but mostly to Irish music, which I soon became a big fan of. Two years later I realized that my love is for this music, but not for this particular instrument, the tinwhislte. I switched to B/C button accordion (being the first in Moscow to get one, and one of the first in the country), and play that thing since that time.

Right now I’m in Kilnadeema, Co. Galway, taking classes from Joe Burke. Will be in Dublin for Temple Bar TradFest soon, and will go home after that. Back home, in Moscow (and in Kiev, in St. Petersburg, in Odessa, Kharkov, Tula, Khabarovsk, Rostov-on-Don, Minsk, Chelyabinsk, Krasnoyarsk, etc) I have lots of friends I met through that music - and I’m pretty sure I will meet more friends in any other city in the world big enough to have some Irish music scene if I come there. No other thing I ever done brought me to so many people I would be happy to have as friends. That’s priceless. And then comes all the fun I got and will get from listening and playing that misic!

When I look back, it feels somewhat weird how one little jig I heard six years ago could change so much in my life.

Re: Something old, something new

I’m aiming for nirvanic ascension some day.

I grew up listening to my father’s assorted Chieftain’s albums, but playing the French horn and I was an alto in a choir throughout high school (where, among many things, we did in fact sing Loch Lomond and Ye Banks and Braes of Bonny Doon; I don’t think anyone in that choir knew where either place was, but we were used to singing lyrics we didn’t understand, as we did a lot of choral stuff in Latin) then, being very “under resourced” and clueless at the time, I tried to find my way into “Celtic” music via an Appalachian mountain dulcimer, which seemed like a good idea at the time, but wasn’t as easy as it looked. I had a CD of someone, who must be a genius, playing Irish and Scottish jigs and reels on one, and to this day, I am mystified by how you strum it in reasonable reel or jig rhythm. Last time I was visiting the parents, I gave the old dulcimer another wee go (now fully familiar with playing jigs and reels on pipes and whistles) and was still like, “No, still can’t play this.”

Between 3rd and 4th year of uni, I went to Ireland for a summer and found a lot of music, and yes, I’m ashamed to say it, I bought a bodhran. I started taking it to sessions (sorry!) but then wanted to move to a “real” instrument so dabbled a bit on clarsach, as my parents had one lying about unplayed (as you do). Then I finally manned up and got a practice set, still hoping I’d keep up on the harp as well, and then I didn’t. I ended up selling the clarsach to buy some drones.

Re: Something old, something new

For me it was a Beach Boys concert at Regis Fieldhouse in Denver about 1974 or so. As a kid, I played violin which I gave up for the guitar in the mid to late 60’s. (trying to be popular with the girls, you know). Growing up, I would hear bits and pieces of tunes that would catch my ear -- sometimes classical or jazz, sometimes rock-n- roll or country. I could never quite put my finger on the sound I liked, sort of an up and down modal thing. Anyway, warming up the Beach Boys that night was Steeleye Span. When Peter Knight launched into some Irish reels, I couldn’t believe my ears! Maddy Prior dancing on stage while the rest of the band churned it out. The key turned in the lock! That was it! As I remember, I left the show a few minutes into the Beach Boys first set, just totally blown away by what I had just heard. The next day, I had my hands on Parcel of Rogues. A few days later, another one. Then a Chieftains album. A week after that I had myself a cheap, pawn shop fiddle, being thankful for my earlier violin exposure. Yup, all those years not knowing what it was I wanted to hear, eventually discovering the name was ITM. After hearing Planxty and the Bothy Band for the first time, I was lost, a goner, hook line and sinker, never looking back. My friends at the time couldn’t figure it out, thinking I was caught up in some sort of religious cult. It just goes to show that Serendipity exists, even at a Beach Boys concert.

Re: Something old, something new

In ninety-five, a friend from high school called for us to have pints at a well-frequented pub in down-town Montréal. On-stage were a couple guys making an unbelievable racket with no more than a guitar, a fiddle and their voices. The man playing the fiddle must have been in his late fifties or early sixties then, and was literally jumping up and down. On hearing and seeing the scene before me, any question in my mind about what to do in life was permanently silenced. The other main option had been law.

Home had been a place of classical music if it came from Mom, and sixties/seventies classic rock when it came from Dad if he wasn’t hooked into bad FM. Did three years of violin from age seven along with singing in the parish choir, moved on to the trumpet, then cornet, then stopped playing music altogether for about five years.

But I think I’ve never *not* been a musician. Now, it just so happens that Irish music is my favourite sort.

Trad is never too far away in Quebec; we get a good dose of it at least once a year to usher in the new one. IMHO, the Québecois, Irish and Scottish musical types require the same kind of technique, and differ mainly in aesthetics. All three types were commonly played at the sessions I eventually attended. Turns out somehow it had always been in the background somewhere.

The gentlemen in the aforementioned anecdote became mentors and friends, along with many of those I came to meet at the local sessions I henceforth discovered. Being rather on the “musician for hire” circuit, my experiences with other players is mitigated, but always something to learn from. Am thankful to all that were patient with me.
