playing along with recordings

This was mentioned on another thread and I thought it worthwhile to have a specific discussion about it. I was accused of being elitist for my views and I’d like to show that I don’t think I am.

Firstly, there are two quite separate discussions here. The first concerns learning tunes from recordings, and the second concerns playing with recordings for recreation.

As we pretty much all agree, learning tunes from any source is fine, with certain provisos - for example, if it’s sheet music, to get the most out of it, you are best to be already versed in the style. I also think there is broad agreement that the sources listed by preference would go something like:
One to one (someone teaches it to you).
In company (could be a session).
From a recording (made specially for or by you).
From a commercial recording.
From notation.
(Though in practice, of course, these methods are often combined.) I don’t want to get hung up on this, suffice it to say that I probably learned my first 100 or so tunes from commercial recordings and to this day still consider it a very good start. And would still recommend it to anyone who for any reason doesn’t have access to more personal sources.

Which brings me to playing with recordings for recreation. If you are an isolated beginner, as I was, you will be doing this, as I was. And not just jigs and reels, but everything from Beethoven to Led Zeppelin. But an examination of what constitutes music is in order, and one of music’s most fundamental properties is communication.

I was accused on the other thread of thinking that learning tunes or just playing along with recordings was “uncouth”. My point is simply that you are not communicating.

Then, “It must then become pretty embarrassing for some people with this particular view, when someone who might have learned their tunes pretty much totally off recordings goes to a session and can play them into the ground!”

This quite simply can’t happen, and I speak from experience, for no matter what technical ability and speed and anything else you may have learned from these recordings, you will not have learned the fundamental basics of communication.

Hey Michael, I’m surprised you see fit to start another thread if you were concerned about the dialogue in the other one in which you were a part (Windows Media Player Masterclass).

I’m sure the other contributors to that previous thread would be more than interested in your views, so I hope they see this new thread. Personally I think people were getting quite a bit from that dialogue, so hopefully the continuity won’t be lost…

Anyway, I don’t think the description of ‘elitist’ is necessarily an ‘accusation’, although you have apparently assumed that it is. Although the word has unpopular connotations, being elitist is not anywhere near as unpopular as being ‘exclusive’.

I don’t think anyone thinking reasonably about ITM would deny that it is a pretty hierarchical ‘society’. There is definitely an ‘elite’ group, and levels of expertise, etc, down from there.

It isn’t a democracy I’d say, it is in fact hierarchical, evidenced for example, by the confusion that happens at places like the recent Ennis Trad Fest where ‘foreigners’ as they are termed, join in sessions without being asked. ‘Elitism’ probably commands greater acceptance in ‘old’ countries than it does in the ‘young’ ones.

The generous view, I believe, is that people learning from and/or playing along with commercial recordings, either because they have no access to other ways of learning, or whether they just prefer that, shouldn’t be made to feel excluded. Let’s not confuse ‘elitism’ with ungenerous ‘exclusivity’.

Communication in the learning process might be possible for some, and not accessible for others. Making ‘communication’ an apparent prequisite for a preferred learning experience in ITM, smacks a little bit I think of exclusivism.

Yourself, John B, and strungup, are lucky (or have made your own luck) in being in places where you might be able to get personal one to one tuition. Most others are not - and because they are not, they should not be made to feel inferior because of their choice of learning.

If it wasn’t for commercial recordings since the early part of the 20th century, ITM would not be where it is today - global.

Re: playing along with recordings

Many years ago, before WW2 in fact, you could buy gramophone recordings (78s) of classical music on a label called “Music Minus One”.
Typically, you’d have a recording of a Mozart string quartet, say, with one of the parts missing. You would play the missing part. Such a recording would come in 4 sets, with a different part missing from each set. You could also get recordings of concertos with the solo part missing, and you’d play it along with the orchestra on the recording - if you were up to it. Surely the ultimate in playing along with recordings.
I don’t know whether anything similar is available today, but it shouldn’t be too difficult with modern recording techniques and CDs. Imagine it - a Music-1 recording of De Danaan _without_ fiddle or flute.

Re: playing along with recordings

Playing along with recordings is a good tool for learning tunes whether you’re just learning or already accomplished. We once had the pleasure of including Laura Risk in our line-up, (who specialized in Scottish music more than Irish at the time.) She wanted to learn the tunes we played, and we sat down and played through tunes with her at sessions, but she wanted something to work with at home. Instead of having her record my flute and concertina to work with, I compiled the recorded sources of tunes that I play, (that often came from fiddlers,) all at concert pitch so she could just put it on and play along. This worked quite well since she was playing fiddle and my versions on flute that went below D sometimes needed to be folded a bit here and there. Also, I thought it would be better if she heard a fiddle to learn from instead of me playing a concertina version based on fiddle. Before long she was playing great on the Irish tunes.

Re: playing along with recordings

The big difference is whether you are doing it for learning or recreation. I know that in the ideal world the two have an enormous cross over. If I didn’t enjoy learning I wouldn’t do it. And, very importantly, you should never consider yourself in a position where you could say you are done with learning.

But as with all the best dichotomies, the most can be got out of them with the attempt to tease them apart. Looking at either end, the black and white, it’s pretty straight forward. At one end we have the luxury of being able to learn tunes and styles from a past master’s recordings. But at the other end you could be sitting alone in your house, month on month, year on year, just playing along with your favourite set from your favourite players’ records.
