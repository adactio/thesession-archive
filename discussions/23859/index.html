Re: What is a tune?

I liked the analogy of a non-verbal story from Hup.

I’ve always been able to visualise pathways in good tunes. Some are straight and lead you directly from point to point, creating small moments of tension which have to be resolved - and so you move on to a conclusion.

In a good tune, well played, the pathway is a forest track with diversions created by ornaments and pulling sometimes at the route so that sometimes you’re taken off the main path, but you get to the destination in the end and generally find yourself well happy.

At other times the same tunes just sparkle and beyond saying that, I can no more describe what’s going on in in visual terms than fly. That is pure musical emotion - something different from everything else and which can be found in Mahler, Mozart or Elgar etcetera - most of whom knew how to write a great tune, or in tunes created and played with love by lesser known names in pubs.

A sprinkling of both sorts of experience are necessary for my musical happiness at different times.

I really love this description from JNE
“A wordless expression of a moment in time, an emotion, an aural painting of a scene one conjures up inside an imagination”

Re: What is a tune?

I like this thread. I’ve been lurking for most of it, seeing what you all think.

Tunes are weird, ephemeral things that exist both outwith us and within us. Does the tune exist before someone plays it? The sofa I am sitting on as I type this exists regardless of whether I’m in the flat or not. It’s not as if it’s brought into existence by using it or talking about it. But tunes are. We can talk about the Maid Behind the Barrel and we both known what we mean, but until we get out our instruments, that tune doesn’t exist in that space and time. But at the same time it does because it’s in our heads and fingers and we can imagine what it sounds like, but then does that count as existence? Does something have to be out in the world in order to BE? In which case tunes are like May flies -- they appear, flit around briefly, then they’re gone. Only to be resurrected the next night or the next week.

I wouldn’t mind being a tune: existing in a state of non-existence until some musicians suddenly, with a few twiddles of their fingers, bring you into being.

Re: What is a tune?

“if I dislike shifting out of 1st position because playing high notes gives me the willies, do I have Acrophobia?”

It depends on your synaesthetic pitch-space associations. If your brain associates high pitch with high altitude, such that you experience an ascending run of notes in a similar way to, say, climbing a ladder, then yes, perhaps you have acrophobia. If, on the other hand, you experience the departure from 1st position as leaving a familiar and secure environment and entering an unfamiliar and insecure one, then perhaps you have *agoraphobia*. If your dislike of leaving 1st position stems from the fact that you have difficulty playing in tune in higher positions, then perhaps you have *ailurophobia*.

Re: What is a tune?

“Welsh harp and choral traditions might underpin local session players’ having more of a feeling for harmonies or harmonics than might be the case in other areas”

A good question. The sessions I frequent, don’t really involve harps (only because there are no harpers who come to them) and song of any kind is a very rare occurence. None of it can be classed as typical however and other sessions have a large number of harpists who attend. There are some extremely good singers around, but there seems to be more of the performance, cerdd dant style of singing which goes on and less which involves the crowd joining in with interesting harmonies. Possibly, Welsh (Cymru Cymraig) session goers are more perfectionist about harmony singing than their Irish or English counterparts, needing to work things out beforehand rather than experiment.

However, this is pure speculation and you need someone who was born into and grew up in the Welsh tradition to give you accurate information.

As far as improvised harmony and chorus song goes, there is a lot of that in the well established Friday night session in Bangor. This is now largely (though not exclusively) based around Irish traditional tunes. I prefer it when there is an interesting mixture. This session has evolved over the last 50 years and has absorbed many styles from ‘regulars’ who were university students for a few years and came from all over the country and from men who worked on the rebuilding of the Britannia Bridge when it burned down in the 60s.

Song plays a big part in the life of this session, but only a portion of it is what would be understood of as Welsh.

The simple answer is that I don’t really know where the tune colouring thing comes from. Everything is evolving, new sessions have sprung up and are finding their own styles - some of them are of a very high musical quality with excellent, good humoured players, who have no axe to grind other than playing the music well and these are an absolute joy to be a part of. One of them is within walking distance - bliss!
