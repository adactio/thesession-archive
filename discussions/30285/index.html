↳ Posted by Will Evans on July 23rd, 2012.

Simplification toward increased understanding of rhythm and a ‘steady’ tempo ~

“Really simplify the tune, take as much out as you can and play around with the phrasing , forget about all the ‘tricks’ rolls etc and try to make the tune bouncy and alive just by your emphasis and phrasing/timing.” ~ Will E

This is a trick some ceili‘dh’ bands use, which also allows them to keep track of each other more easily and remain ‘in the groove’. It is, as Will has said, a good way to get grounded in the rhythm, and to better realize the interest there too. Then you can work back in a twiddle or two, to your heart’s desire. But try not to sacrifice too much of the joy of rhythm by beating it into submission with excessive twiddlin‘ around, fuffin’ about. Rhythm is the heart, all those other bits and fun are just decorations, but they can, used wisely, lend definition and articulation to the rhythm. They can be pluses and negatives, however well you have mastered them individually.

Also what can happen is that we get into a ‘rut’ with tunes, always playing them the same, same speed, and I find by changing key this can facilitate getting out of that rut.

↳ Posted by ceolachan on July 23rd, 2012.

Re: Correct speed for a jig??

The dancer does NOT tell you how fast to play in competition, except for modern set dances in opens. Tempos for competition are set by An Coimisiun or NAFC, not the dancers.

Current tempos are:
Light jig - 118
Single jig - 124
Traditional heavy jig - 92
Advanced heavy jig - 73
Traditional jig set - 96 for St. Patrick’s and Three Sea Captains, 90 for Jockey to the Fair
Modern jig sets where the dancer calls the tempo have to be danced at a minimum of 66.

The slower jig tempos are not something you’ll find in sessions. It takes some practice to hold a jig down to 73 bpm, or a set to 66. For “general use” I usually tell students to aim for maybe 110 on the metronome. I’m not a fan of overly fast jigs.

I was objecting to dancers being trained only with very precisely paced recorded music, who learn the steps as a physical skill, without interacting or becoming part of the music. ElaineT describes the imact of that up above. In my opinion, this approach produces a dancer who is rigid, sometimes even robotic looking, and who has no feel for the tune.
Almost as unpleasant to watch as it is to listen to a tune that some eedjit has ‘stripped down to its fundamental parts.’
