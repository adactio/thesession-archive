Ornamentation within Traditional irish music.

So, we have spent a lot of time debating when and if we ornament. I have suggested that the tunes should at first be learnt straight.
So i now raise the subject of ‘how’ to ornament.
I will offer 2 suggestions ;
1]; The ornaments should, if possible’ be quieter than the tune, not louder. To fit in subtly.
2]; Triplets are to be played within the context of the individual tune, at the ‘right’ pace .
3] That when appropriate triplets have a leading note making in fact four notes in a triplet. eg; DC#B triplet can be played C# DC#B.

I am not so much asking for others to comment on these but to offer their constructive suggestions to aid us all in our understanding and performance [shock !]of ITM, and in particular, ornamentation.
However if you want to say what you think of them and why, do.

Re: Ornamentation within Traditional irish music.

I have suggested in the past that learning ornaments as part of the tune can be a useful tool when you’re first starting out - it allows you to learn some of the basics of ornamentation early on. What invariably happens then is that you can’t play the tune *without* the ornaments, or change the ornamentation, because you tend to learn kinesthetically when you’re first playing an instrument, but I think that’s a fair tradeoff for a beginner.

As I have gotten more experienced, I now learn tunes without ornamentation, for the most part, and they automatically appear once I know the tune, and I think of that as a good way to go about it, because I am more flexible about it.

I have had to go back and “re-work” a lot of the tunes that I learned early on, because I often played them exactly how I had learned them in the first place. But I would still submit that learning how to do some ornaments very early in your learning process is key to being able to do them well later on…

Re: Ornamentation within Traditional irish music.

As a flute player, I often need to ornament just to get through the tune. For example, I would never play two or three notes of the same pitch in succession. To do so would require tonguing, which stylistically is best avoided in most cases. Substituing a roll, cut, or strike improves the flow from the get-go, and so becomes a part of the tune even before it is completely learned. Beyond that, I will incorporate various forms of articulation just to avoid tonguing and because at this point, many of the ornaments I use are automatic to the way I play. The trouble with trying to learn a tune without ornaments is that you spend a great deal of time trying to perfect something you are going to change. I find ornaments to be organic, and by the time I’ve learned a tune, most of the ornaments (including those that allow me to breathe) become intrinsic to the learning process.

Re: Ornamentation within Traditional irish music.

Jig, when I first started, I simply imitated what I heard. I never separated ornament from tune, even if I was only approximating the ornament because I did not know how to do it properly. Tunes I learned from written sources were much more spare because at that time, how and when to ornament was still a mystery to me.

If I were teaching someone to play flute, I would suggest they use ornaments to help them breathe and to get past triplets and more than one note of the same pitch. In other words, functional ornaments that get you through the tune, as opposed to simply tarting up the melody.

Flute and whistle in ITM evolved from the piping tradition, where you couldn’t use your mouth and tongue for purposes of articulation. Frankly, it just sounds better to use the fingers for articulation (I also use glottal stops and even my lips). I even use Irish ornaments in popular and some classical music because it imparts a nicer phrasing (IMO) that the tongue. I keep the tongue for more staccato effects, which seldom come up in Irish music. Usually, if you want emphasis, you kind of cough up the note rather than punch it with your tongue.

I’m a beginner and I play the flute. I have been learning what ornaments I can from the very beginning rather than putting it off.

I started with cuts. Now when I learn a new tune there are places where I naturally want to put cuts even when they are not needed strictly for articulation. So I put them in.

After cuts I learned rolls. First I try to learn the tune without the rolls, unless I’ve never heard the tune without rolls. Then I look at where I can put rolls in and I try it out. Sometimes I can even do short rolls, which I think are harder than the long ones even though I learned them earlier.

Before I do rolls though, the first thing I have to figure out is where to breathe. Breathing changes how I will articulate or ornament any particular place. I enjoy finding new places to breathe because I can add a bounciness at that spot. But I have to admit I find it difficult to find breathing places sometimes.

If it’s a tune that I’ve never heard without a roll I try to experiment with what might go there if the roll wasn’t there. For example, I learned Give Us A Drink Of Water with a lot of rolls in it, but I think there are too many so I try to take them out and hope I don’t ruin the tune by doing so. Usually, since I don’t know what’s supposed to be there, I just take an opportunity to breathe.

Lately I’ve been trying to learn taps. I find them kind of hard because I’m still stuck in the kinesthetic playing, so my fingers get confused if I make them play a note lower than the note I want. But I’m getting better at it. I can put them in on Fs anyway.

Re: Ornamentation within Traditional irish music.

At last, a thread I can contribute to. I am a piper- Great Highland pipes, that is. The GHB is very different from the Irish pipes. The Irish pipes, as I understand it, can play all the notes a whistle or flute can play, along with all the attendant cuts, rolls, and slides. The GHB, however, can only play nine notes. Since the air never stops, you distinguish one note from another using “doublings”, or combinations of grace notes, depending on the note you are going to.and coming from.

Since many highland pipers compete, all use the same doublings in the same places, because adding your own or modifying the ones in standard use will count against you in competition.

The GHB uses standard music notation, and there are many tunes published in notation. It is not difficult to read, since there are only nine notes possible. Please PM me if you have any specific questions on the GHB.

Re: Ornamentation within Traditional irish music.

First I have to say I’m coming from the perspective of someone who plays uilleann pipes, flute, and whistle.
As I’ve posted before, I consider cuts and pats (and the short rolls and long rolls created from them) to be articulations, not ornamentations. These “digital” or finger articulations are part-and-parcel to the reels and jigs themselves and removing them from a tune is an artifice and renders the tune a shell of itself, and no longer a piece of ITM.
So much of the rythm, the lift, the drive of a reel on an Irish wind instrument comes from the rolls. Listen to the beginning of The Green Mountain played by any decent wind player:
A + F# roll
A + F# roll
bottom D + F# roll
Take out the rolls and not only do you not have any drive or lift, you don’t even have a melody. The melody IS rolls in many cases.
Ornamentation, that is by definition something superfluous to the melody which is added for decorative effect, is actually not all that common except on pipes, on which you’ll not only hear trills on high F# but also the whole catalogue of staccato triplets which do fit the definition of ornamentation, in that they are not fundamental to the tune and are added ad lib.
Also as I’ve posted elswhere, when I’m learning a new reel up to speed by ear (which is how I usually pick up tunes) the rolls are the first thing I get in place. Then I get other stock motifs like the rocking motif. Then arpeggios and scalar runs. Last come all the little linking notes which actually on the flute are often breathing spots anyhow.

I agree. Having played old-time and now learning ITM, there is a big difference. There are many tunes that are played in both styles, but sound very different. The old-time folks that I’ve encountered usually don’t like that “Irish-stuff” and vice versa. A reel in ITM is played as a hoe-down in old-time. The jigs sound different as well. It’s the timing, the accents, the bowing (for fiddles). I’m not sure how to describe it, but ITM is much more “flowing” and has a particular lilt. Ornaments are similar, but not quite the same.
