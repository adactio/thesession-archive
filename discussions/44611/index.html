Cuts on whistle/flute/pipes when going down: when exactly does your finger start to move?

Learning the Scottish style (with electronic pipes and following the “green book”) I’ve realized that, when you’re playing a note and you go to a note below, which you emphasize with a cut, if you play the Scottish pipes, your “cut finger” is supposed to start moving before you actually get to the second note.

And I have no idea what’s the usual way in Irish trad, but I’ve always started the cut just after I arrive at the second note. Almost simultaneously, but certainly not before. This is when you’re playing a B and go down to a A with a cut, for example. However if it’s going up (G --> A with a cut) then I do start the cut before I get to the A.

P.S. To add more confusion to this confusing thread: I’m using Irish trad terms. In the Scottish pipe world, when I say cut I mean grace note (I think), and when I say B, A and G I mean F#, E and D.

Re: Cuts on whistle/flute/pipes when going down: when exactly does your finger start to move?

PJ, if you drop me an message with your email, I’ll send you a PDF I use that goes through the mechanics in a bit of detail.

Basically, a gracenote/cut has a start note, an up, and a down. During the up phase the gracenote sounds *and* any fingers leaving the chanter come up at the same time. The movement finishes with the fingers coming down together to the final note.

For extra marks, the beat is placed when the fingers land for the final note.

I would say there’s a lot of stuff written in tutor books, and while it might be a useful mental model, it often doesn’t reflect what good players do.

Hello, PJ. On whistle & flute I tend to use the term tap or strike (rather than ‘cut’) when fingering a lower note. For any note above I call the ornament/articulation a ‘cut’. This is different from how most fiddlers refer to either ornament as a cut or pipers, etc. using the term ‘grace notes’ or ‘gracing’.

What I’m getting at is any ornamentation depends on the instrument & the actual sound because terminology & notation differs; especially if it is specific to an instrument. Re: Cuts on whistle/flute/pipes when going down: when exactly does your finger start to move?

> I don’t know, to me the cut sounds less powerful if you do it this way.

Part of that is that your technique, which is not a cut, requires the cut itself to be extremely quick. Now that you’re putting that extremely fast movement onto a pure note change, there’s less to hear than you are used to.

And in fact, in North American GHB playing that is the standard style, for what it’s worth. I make the point that the gracenote *ends* on the beat because that is what allows you to expand it and shape the quality of sound you want to produce.

Do not try and reinvent the wheel at your stage of learning: try and understand our system of embellishment before you decide to rewrite the rulebook. We do it a particular way for a reason. The book Will recommends is well worth it.

Re: Cuts on whistle/flute/pipes when going down: when exactly does your finger start to move?

A cut or grace note going down on the flute pretty much requires an interruption in the air flow to make it sound good. e.g. Glottal stop or tongue. So, B to A
xoo ooo
oxo ooo plus Glottal followed by
oxo ooo with glottal released followed fast by…
xxo ooo

The middle fingering only sounds after the glottal, that means that the air is stopped as or before you move the first finger off. I would feel the beat at the point where the glottal is released.

Aficionados of the instruments involved will note that this is _totally_ unrelated to how the similar effect would be achieved on the pipes.

Re: Cuts on whistle/flute/pipes when going down: when exactly does your finger start to move?

I play Highland pipes and also uilleann pipes and Irish whistle, and the mechanics of how fingers move when executing cuts/upper gracenotes is the same.

With all such technique issues there are various camps which PJ (the original poster) should be aware of:

1) players who aren’t aware of what they’re doing, it’s unconscious muscle-memory, and they have no vocabulary to describe or teach it.

2) players who aren’t aware of what they’re doing, but have created a construct or theory about their own technique which doesn’t correspond to what they actually do. When they teach, they will confuse their more perceptive students.

3) players who have a keen self-awareness of their own technique and who are able to correctly verbalise, break down, and teach their technique.

In any case, about going down the scale with an upper gracenote, a theory or pedagogy sometimes heard in Highland pipe teaching is “lift one, replace two”.

The simplest example is this, which uses the same finger action on Scottish and Irish woodwinds, though the lower-hand fingerings vary. For simplicity’s sake here’s the fingering on whistle going from B to A with a C cut:

xoo ooo
ooo ooo
xxo ooo

Now that’s the common theory (lift one replace two) but in practice I myself move the melody-note finger in the middle of the duration of the gracenote/cut, like this

xoo ooo
ooo ooo
oxo ooo
xxo ooo

The same is true going from any note to any lower note with any cut/upper gracenote, say

xxo ooo
xoo ooo
xxx xxx

where I’m actually moving all those melody-note fingers in the middle of the duration of the cut:

xxo ooo
xoo ooo
xox xxx
xxx xxx

Re: Cuts on whistle/flute/pipes when going down: when exactly does your finger start to move?

For sure the physics of how a Scottish Highland or Scottish Smallpipe chanter work are very different from flute or whistle, or even uilleann pipes. I know because I play all those.

It’s why I chose examples that used the same finger action on all of those instruments.

Where things get Scot-specific is the way that Scots Highland and Smallpipe chanters are solidly locked into the fundamental octave, which enables the distant gracenotes that are so common in Scots piping, that usually/often don’t work on Irish woodwinds.

For example the main “working finger” on GHB is the top finger, the “high G gracenote”. (The Irish equivalent would be a C cut.) In most Highland tunes there’s a high G gracenote on every beat in every bar, you can watch that finger and it’s like a little metronome keeping the beat.

Two lower notes cut with the top index finger on GHB

x xxx xxxo
x oxx xxxo
x xxx xxxo

x xxx xxoo
x oxx xxoo
x xxx xxoo

On whistle:

xxx xxx
oxx xxx
xxx xxx

xxx xxo
oxx xxo
xxx xxo

They work in the low octave on my whistle, but in the 2nd octave they don’t do all that much. It’s why on whistle they usually cut the lower notes with less-distant cuts.

The most-distant possible cut on the GHB is the thumb cut on the bellnote, here going from high G to low G with the high A cut

x ooo xxxo
o ooo xxxo
x xxx xxxx

It’s not uncommon at all, but try it on most uilleann chanters and you don’t get much (well, a squeal, quite possibly).

So you have to know how GHB, SSP, and uilleann chanters function to know which ornaments will work on both and which are instrument-specific.

And not just ornaments, but melody notes too. Very common for GHB tunes to leap from high A to low A and back, or high G to low G and back, etc.

Also high A on the GHB has a veiled sound, much lower volume than the lower notes. High A has a way of disappearing into the drone sound, which is why so many GHB tunes have high A alternate with low notes giving a staccato effect. On Irish woodwinds it’s the opposite, with high A leaping out louder than the low notes. It’s why so many GHB tunes don’t have the original effect when played on Irish woodwinds.
