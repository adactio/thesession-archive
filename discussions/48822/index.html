Naming tunes during sessions

At many of the trad sessions I’ve gone to, including slow sessions, those starting to play don’t share the name of the tune before playing. I find this challenging. If I know the tune being played, it can take a couple of verses before I’m sure I recognize it, so I don’t join in until it is half over. If I don’t know the tune, I don’t have the name to learn it later. I’ve tried using Tunepal and other apps, but they often can’t identify the tune. Is there a reason to not name tunes at a session? Thanks.

Re: Naming tunes during sessions

There’s no real reason not to name tunes before you play them but also, in my opinion, no real reason to name them either. The tunes are often so well known at a particular session that everyone is in after the first few notes.

There can be times when I will name a tune that someone else normally leads as a suggestion that they do just that, or if I know that there is only one other person who knows the tune I want to lead off with I might give that person a heads up.

It’s also the case that sometimes people can remember the tune but not the name - having to name the tune would stop them playing it.

Finally, naming what you are about to play is more like what you would do for a performance to an audience rather than for a wee get together with a few friends.

Michael, this is a frequent question about how to know which tunes are played in a session. If it’s a learner session they may actually have a list of common sets. Question is, “who has the list?” Any list will change over time but certain tunes will be common knowledge for the regulars. If the information is not available during the session just don’t go home until you break someone’s will & they confess to know the names of the tunes.

If it’s not exclusively a beginner’s session the sets tend to be spontaneous. Even if someone has a specific tune in mind they may change it at the last minute. Or if a set begins with a specific tune and the person starting the set calls it out, doesn’t mean the following tunes will be what is usually played.

The bottom line is, it depends, slow sessions should be relatively predictable but they are still sessions and all sessions crave some degree of spontaneity. The only sure way to learn which tunes and sets are played in a session is if it’s OK to record the music (easy to do these days) do that & take it home to suss out the tune names.

Re: Naming tunes during sessions

“Furthermore, if playing a set of tunes, there would simply not be time to announce the next tune. And yes, it would take away some of the spontaneity.”

Hmmm. When playing at sessions, I make up my sets on the fly. I may not know what the next tune is until I’m at the tail end of the previous one. But often as not, as we careen through the last measure or two, I’ll lean over to the player next to me and let them know where I’m going: “Abbeyleix,” or “Brosnan’s, B minor.” It’s still spontaneous, and if you can grunt out a few syllables while playing, there’s all the time in the world. Having at least one other player make the transition with you helps keep the overall momentum going.

In my experience, this practice is fairly common at high-end sessions, especially when at an “irregular” gathering—a fleadh, say, or Willie Clancy Week. In mid-flight, the leader of the set will bark out the name of the next tune to alert anyone close enough to hear. I remember at one such session, an American fiddler from Chicago didn’t just name the next tune. While rocketing through a twisty reel, he engaged the player next to him for help choosing the next tune: “I was thinking of going into Frank’s Reel, but we could do Trip to Miriam’s instead, or maybe Forget Me Not, or Paddy Fahey’s, you know, the one in D dorian….”

That said, at any session, it’s almost routine for someone, during the lull at the end of a set, to ask, “What was the second tune?” Nothing inappropriate about providing the verbal liner notes then.

Re: Naming tunes during sessions

“What use is there in the middle of a set to call out the tune names….”

Well, as I said, it helps maintain flow and momentum at the change. This may not be a concern when playing with your regular session mates because they’ll instantly recognize your next tune, maybe even anticipate it. But when playing with people you’ve just met, it’s a courtesy and prevents the session from abruptly throttling between full roar and a lone instrument then back to full roar every 1.5 minutes.

It’s also useful after a tune like, say, the Tarbolton, when you *don’t* want to go into the Longford Collector next.

Calling out a tune beforehand is also a nice way to give a boost to someone who’s sitting on their hands, not knowing many tunes. If you know their repertoire, you can add one of their favourites (or just one of the old standards) to a set and give them a heads up so they’re be ready when it comes round. It helps newcomers and beginners feel welcome.

Sure, if talking while playing is a challenge for you — as it is for me when I’m sober ;-) — a simple “hup!” at the change is fine.

yhaal house, agreed. I’ve known players who had a NATO code word for every key. One such code was Arse, Bollocks, Cacks, Dope, Eejit, Feck, and Gas.

Hmmm well, I’ve read through most of this thread, and as with many, some views are very polarised and entrenched, as in “My way is the only way”. I would rather go with what frkrygow said a couple of days ago, or gimpy’s last but one post, especially his paragraph about potentially leaving people out. IMHO a lot depends on the type of session it is, especially skill level: I can quite understand why, in a free-flowing session of advanced players with broad-ranging repertoires and fantastic ear-playing skills, you would not want to interrupt the stream by naming tunes or even keys.
Just the opposite for beginners, and learners, where giving out tune names or keys in advance of playing can be very helpful: it doesn’t necessarily mean that they’ll be reaching for books or electronic dots, as at least some of them will be ear learners. Don’t leave them sitting on the sidelines not playing if it’s a tune they perhaps do know. All sessions are different, so no hard and fast rules.

As for knowing tune names - just as I have made myself play more by ear over the years since I started in trad melody playing, I have also made myself learn tune names: some apparently disagree re the need for this, but I do find it helpful. I also take an interest in knowing the composers and provenance of tunes: maybe not entirely necessary for enjoying playing them, but I’d rather know that x tune is 200 years old and not just written last week. Same with songs. I’ll get my anorak.

As for keys and not hearing them properly in a noisy session , there is a sort of sign language, though it depends on knowing some music theory and key signatures. One digit up, G or Em: two D or Bm: 3 A or F#m, and so on. One digit down F or Dm, two Bb or Gm, three Eb or Cm and so on.

Re: Naming tunes during sessions

Ah, the “ear V dots” thing. :-)

Yes, I’m sure this is a relevant issue for many. Obviously, if we learn tunes by “osmosis” and/or have never known the title, we would have learned them by ear. There’s a distinction though between “learning by ear” this way and being “taught aurally” e.g. in a workshop or even making a conscious effort yourself to learn by ear from recordings.

For me, once I’ve really learned a tune, it doesn’t matter too much which method I used. Many sight readers, apparently, still imagine the dots in their head when playing without them even although they’ve memorised the tunes. Some others also think of “fingerings” etc even when they already “know” the tune too.
However, when I know a tune very well, I wouldn’t think about most of these things. Even if I originally learned the tunes from sheet music or some other artificial method, the tune will have eventually “seeped in” by osmosis… or “naturally” is maybe the better word…. from my own playing and that of others.

Of course, we all have tunes in our repertoire which are at different stages of development. It also depends on how often or long we have been playing them. If I’ve not heard it for a while, I may momentarily forget the name as I said earlier and I sometimes get a slight “mental block”. Not on all occasions though, by any means.

Notwithstanding the above, I still wouldn’t expect to have the tune titles pre announced in the average session situation. Even less so “mid set”. There’s nothing necessarily wrong with the idea either, of course. It all depends on the local practices and circumstances.
Also, it might even just be a very informal statement or request e.g. “Let’s play…….” Or “Do you know……”, for instance.
