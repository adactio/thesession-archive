Re: How slow …

I suggest a good way to use a metronome when practicing is to select a suitable speed, run the metronome for a few seconds or so to get that speed into your head, turn the metronome off and play the tune. Remember your speed when you finish the tune, turn the metronome on again and compare the two. Most people will be surprised to find that their end speed is not the same as the one they started with.

Try to think where you changed speed, and repeat the exercise.
The point of this is to encourage you to listen to yourself when playing, and try to spot changes in speed without reference to an external device. You’ll never achieve the ability to do this properly if you always play to the ticking of a metronome. At best, you could turn into a human MIDI :-)

There are certain areas where you’re likely to speed up without being aware of it - scale passages of 1/8 notes, and getting louder - so these are ones to think about.

When you are aware of your speed at any point in the tune then you’ll be able to control it and use slight changes of speed effectively here and there in the service of the music.

Warning: if you’re playing for dancers they don’t like unannounced changes in speed!

Finally, slow practice never did anyone any harm.

Re: How slow …

I sometimes use a metronome, but more often ABC to play along with. It helps with intonation as well as timing. One thing I have found is it helps if I know exactly which note the beat is on, which is not always what I think it is. If I slow the tune right down, say 60-70 bpm and play each 3rd note (for 4/4 tunes) loud and the rest very quiet -- as big a differential as I can manage -- it gets me to home in on the exact note. Sometimes the problem arises when the beat is on a little triplet or similar, and the actual note is one where I am lifting a finger off rather than putting it down, or the second half of a slur or a crotchet. Once I’ve got the exact note to coincide with the beat, I change to each 1st beat loud, and 2 3 4 quiet. It works for me, anyway. And I’ve always said that if you can’t keep time with a metronome, how do you expect to keep time with another player? True what llig says about having to adjust to get back on time; but once you’re in the groove, as it were, the sound of the metronome disappears, and you know your’e spot on.

Re: How slow …

I tend to speed up or fudge the rhythms when I am not comfortable with certain passages in the tune. In order to remedy that, I isolate that particular passage (usually eighth note runs in reels that seem to “run away” from me) and work on it slowly, altering the rhythms as I practice.

When practicing these bits, I vary the rhythm *on purpose* and repeat that section several times - for example, dotting the eighth notes so that it’s like a hornpipe rhythm. Then, once I can play the section comfortably with that pattern, I change the rhythm and do it all over again (eg switch the dotted rhythms so that it is like a run of scotch snaps). Then I go back to the original rhythm and voila! It seems as though it falls under my fingers as easily as Mary Had a Little Lamb and I am not floundering around, trying to find the right notes, and end up going at break-neck speed. Normally, I don’t incorporate a metronome, but you could use it to start slowly and gradually work up to speed. Then turn the thing off and repeat the process.

I learned this method of practicing when I was taking classical piano lessons, as well as when I took some classical violin lessons. Some folks here might balk at the idea of using “classical” methods, but it works fer me, and maybe it will work for you, too. To each their own, eh?

Re: How slow …

Can’t recall where I read this, but it’s appropriate:

“When you feel out of rhythm, take your own pulse.”

In the long run, you want the mechanical motions of playing your instrument to take on the “alive” qualities of your own body, including your inner sense of pulse. As others have said above, we humans tend to walk to a rhythm, breathe to a rhythm, and our hearts beat to a rhythm. Our aim as musicians is to let that inner sense of pulse into our bow hands, or fingers, our airstream as we pulse it into the flute, etc.

I suspect that the more mechanical your instrument, the trickier it can be to infuse it with such aliveness, but good musicians everywhere do in fact do just this. It takes a high awareness of your inner pulse, and fluency on your instrument. One without the other isn’t going to be musical. Work on both. Identify which needs the most attention. Be patient, but always alert to (listening and feeling) what your doing as you play.

Re: How slow …

Ben~
Using a metronome is like reading dots--you rely on an external source (dots, clicks, etc.) to give you the music and/or rhythm. What the others said about singing the parts causes you to rely on and develop your own inbuilt sense of internal music and rhythm. If you rely on the former too much, they become like unto a crutch. Take away the crutch of dots and clicks if you’ve become dependent on them and you fall flat on your face. Develop your internal sense of tune and rhythm and you’ll be a much stronger player in that you’ll never need to rely on external devices to maintain a steady rhythm.

***That having been said, I -do- use dots to remind me of a phrase or turnaround or ending of any number of tunes if my memory fails me when practicing. But I don’t depend on it as a rule; only as a memory jogger.

Likewise, in analyzing your rhythm variations, try using the metronome as little as possible, only just to verify you are at the desired speed. Try it this way: if it is a midi-style metronome, set the light to blink at the desired speed without the auditory click track; then play at speed, looking at the blinking light. Next close your eyes and continue playing for a bit. After a while, reopen your eyes and consult the machine. Were you spot on? Did you remain at tempo or speed up? This way you don’t have to stop playing to start and stop the metronome.

When you work on a tricky passage too much, your body wants to
play it on autopilot.. The metronome can help you crack that.
But so can leaving off the ornaments, or exaggerating
something or dropping a note or taking a breath
in the middle of it … etc etc. Classical players will take the notes
from the problem passage and lay all sorts of different rhythms
and articulations over them. Even try playing the notes in reverse
order. After you do that for a while, when you go back to the
proper tune you have a feeling of great control.

Re: How slow …

Slow repetition (looping) is a good technical way of tackling a tricky technical problem - usually no more than three or four notes, or a string crossing or bowing - but like all good things it can be overdone. So stop the looping when the problem’s solved. For me, I find it sufficient to do no more than a minute at half-speed, and thinking well about what I’m doing, followed later by a minute at three-quarters if and only if the half-speed was ok, and then later ditto at full speed. If you’re thinking about solving a particular technical problem when doing the looping you’re not going to get into the autopilot scenario (autopilot by definition implies that you’re not thinking about what you’re doing).

Talking about looping, the French composer Erik Satie about 100 years ago, composed a short piece called “Vexations” (for piano, string trio or voice, it doesn’t matter), and suggested that it be played (looped) 840 times without break. It has been performed in public, sometimes by a team, and more rarely by a solo performer - one wonders whether kindly people in white coats were in attendance :-). I don’t know whether the 9-CD album is still available … For more information about this interesting and extraordinary composition see http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vexations.

A PDF of the Vexations score can be downloaded from http://musicweb.hmt-hannover.de/satie/Vex-Score.pdf . Be aware in this piece there are no bar lines and an accidental (sharp/flat) applies only to the note it relates to. The harmonic structure is complex, Wagnerian to say the least, with many enharmonic key changes - an enharmonic key change is an esoteric, not to say eldrich, type of trucker’s gear-change, e.g. G## = A, or E# = Fnat. You can’t tell the difference on a piano, but a good string quartet would show it.

Re: How slow …

It is a good time to repeat some of the advice of my former guitar teacher, Matt Heaton. He always told me to play everything at a steady pace. If there was a phrase that needed work, I should slow the whole tune down, until I had the entire thing working as it should at that pace. Then, I was not to speed it up any faster than I could continue to play the entire tune without glitches and mistakes. He said too many people getting into the habit of slowing on the tricky bits, and rushing other parts to catch up. And he said that if you practice mistakes, you will build them into your playing. Speed, he said, will take care of itself.
A wise man.

Advice given to me that has served well is play the piece over and over until you finally get it right, and *then* be sure to play it at least as many times right as you did wrong in the first place.

Something I see with my music students. They practice a piece until they get it right, and then stop. Which means if it took them 9 times through with mistakes to play it a 10th time right, they’ve played it wrong 90% of the time and right only 10%. I suggest to them that they at least make the ratio even, equal. And better yet, err on the side of playing it right more often than wrong.

Re: How slow …

I think you’re missing the point of Ma’s quote if you take it as an excuse to make “self-indulgent noise.”

I think his point is that nearly anyone will make better music, at whatever their current ability level is, if they allow themselves to be expressive, rather than worrying about playing it “right” and as near to perfect as they can. There is a backlash afoot in the classical music world against the whole idea of practicing toward perfection. Read Madeleine Buser’s book on the art of practicing, or William Westney’s “The Perfect Wrong Note.” Based on recent studies in neuroscience and a more science-oriented approach to how we learn physical skills, many teachers are finally leaving behind the old school of thought that all mistakes are “bad,” and the old saw about “perfect practice makes perfect.” In fact, all evidence indicates that perfect practice makes for leaden, mechanical playing, that still features mistakes (because we’re human). Other ways of practicing allow for mistakes in ways that teach you to notice them without being judgmental, and then to consider them as useful or helpful, either as new ideas to incorporate, or as helpful cues to focus on a specific aspect of playing that needs improvement.

Westney in particular makes what I find to be a helpful distinction between careless, sloppy mistakes and what he calls honest mistakes. Honest mistakes are the sort of thing Miles Davis might have been getting at when he said there are no wrong notes. Case in point: in the fifth part of Kid on the Mountain, the “usual” setting goes like this:

|edB dBA G2….|

But in full flight one session, I inadvertently played it this way:

|edB cBA G2…|

I’ve played this tune thousands of times and never stuck a c natural in that phrase. But there it was. A good example of Westney’s “honest mistake.” And it works beautifully right there. So I’ve kept it, as an interesting variation.

Again, I’m not arguing against avid, ardent effort toward playing to the best of your potential. But I am suggesting that “perfection” in any form, as a sort of goal, can be counterproductive and even destructive. Much better (for me and my students so far; ymmv) is to work on improving skills and techniques toward the goal of enhanced musical expression.

Sorry to say this again, but this idea is so simple and so profound: “The only difference between stumbling blocks and stepping stones is in how we use them.” Aspiring to perfections tends to steer people toward seeing stumbling blocks, while aspiring to masterful expression tends to steer people toward seeing stepping stones that lead to greater improvement (and happiness).
