The subtle art of Variation and Style

Hello, I saw someone on this site say (I forget who) that it is better to know 20 different ways to play the same tune than it is to know 100 tunes. Now I may be paraphrasing a bit, but that’s what I remember. First of all, do you guys and gals think this is true? Secondly, this got me thinking that yes, I know a lot of tunes, but I have never been successful when writing variations. The thing that I’ve come to love about this music the most is the fact that you can change a tune to fit how you want to play it. Then someone with a different vision for the tune can change it around to fit their style, and so on and so forth. There are many musicians who seem to just connect themselves with the tune they are playing. Noel Hill, Caitlín Nic Gabhann, Seamus Tansey, Micho Russell, Mary Bergin the list goes on. They all seem to have some sort of skill for bringing a new way to view and really feel the emotion that a tune can portray. For example, Micho Russell is a great player there’s no denying that, but have you ever stopped and thought about what makes his playing of tunes so enjoyable? I’ve found that he just seems to really understand what makes the music and as a result has a way of playing with little complexity and yet such drive. Same thing with Caitlín Nic Gabhann she just seamlessly connects her variations to the original tune. To the point where you really have to stop and listen really closely (sometimes this includes a slow downer) to actually hear every little detail she throws in. On the surface her style may seem simple, but it’s actually very complicated with her decisions of where to add such nuances. She never plays the same thing the same way twice. (at least that’s what I’ve found) Noel Hill also seems to have this down, but in a different way. He seems to connect with the tunes he plays through complexity rather than simplicity. His style is much more flashy than that of the two others I’ve mentioned. You can really hear the energy that he puts into every note, every ornament and variation. He really puts a lot of himself into every movement of his concertina, and you don’t need to hear every little detail to know that this man loves what he’s doing. That they all love what they are doing. Anyway the point is I know I’ve only been playing 11 nearly 12 months, but should I be doing the same? Should I be looking for ways to change a tune every time through, so that I don’t end up repeating myself 3 times? Where should I even start? Should I instead focus on repertoire? Is there a trick to such nuances? This is what I’ve been thinking about for the past couple weeks… I really want to be able to do such things, but I’m just not as creative I guess. I know I’ll get there someday at the very least, but sometimes it seems that someday just can’t come fast enough.

As usual, I don’t know if this makes any sense, but thanks for listening to another of my rambles! :)

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

Listen to lots of music and keep playing.

There are easy tricks, like substituting an A roll with the notes ABA and then say an ACA triplet. Melodic variations come to you when you know the music inside and out and have a zillion hours of playing under your belt. I doubt anyone can play melodic variations after a year.

It’s a bit like learning a new language. My high school level Spanish means that I can put together rigid sentences with unyielding grammatical rules, but I can’t do it fluently, on the fly, like a native speaker. If I got fluent, I would. That would take time and effort but it’s possible.

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

“… it is better to know 20 different ways to play the same tune than it is to know 100 tunes … do you guys and gals think this is true? ”

It depends how literally you want to take it. A repertoire of *one( tune would be taking it to extremes - 100 tune is not many for someone that’s been playing a few years. But, in principle, I think it holds true - 100 or 1000 tunes, played exactly the same way every time, have little musical value, at least in the context of traditional music.

“I have never been successful when writing variations.”

I think you are looking at it the wrong way. Variations do not need to be *written*. They are part and parcel of playing style.

“Should I be looking for ways to change a tune every time through … ?”

Yes… but not too hard. From what you write about the playing of various musicians, it is clear that you understand this music well. The fact that you are able to so eloquently describe different personal playing styles suggests that you are already doing what you need to do - keep listening to the good stuff. Everytime you listen - especially with the intensity that you obviously do - you absorb some of what you hear and eventually, based upon that, you will find that you form your own ideas on what to do with a tune. Keep playing and gaining more familiarity with your instrument, and you will eventually be able to express these ideas fluently through your playing.

As an exercise, it might be useful to copy variations in detail from other players, to work out exactly what they are doing. Whilst it should not be your ultimate goal to play exactly like anyone else, it can be a good way to add tools to your kit, so to speak.

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

Just keep repeating the same tune over and over. Somewhere between the 10th and the 15th repetition your mind will automatically come up with variations to avoid melting out of boredom.

If that doesn’t work, here’s some ideas:
- For every tune you learn, listen to recordings of 5 different artists, and take 1 or 2 embellishments from each recording. Isolate them in your practice routines. Then practice the tune, sticking in the embellishments at random.
- Listen to one artist you very much admire, and try to work out the 5 most characteristic embellishments he or she uses. Then practice them like you with the ones above.
At first they’ll feel borrowed, but keep using them and they’ll start feeling like ‘yours’. Remember that anything you want to turn into an automatism must first be practiced deliberately.

I’ll add that after writing or learning a tune, I’ll often come back a month later and purposefully add variations, different basslines, harmonic flavournotes etc. (because when you’re in the process of writing or learning a tune your attention is often elsewhere: internalizing the melody, finger positions, structure, etc.). These’re part of my more personal repertoire of tricks. I’ll even jot ’em down if I feel the need.

With all this it’s quite possible to gather a neat bag of tricks pretty soon, and the ability to pick from it at will.
And maybe that’s the kernel of truth in the ‘20 vs 100 tunes’ quote; if you learn 20 tunes well, and with plenty of variations, you won’t need to spend as much time making the next 80 interesting, because they will be cool pretty much by default.

As I recall my teacher said “better to learn 20 ways to play one tune, than to learn 20 tunes.”

I don’t know how “prescriptive” it was. I felt it was the opposite, in that rather than teaching me a rote way to play a tune he was teaching me how to create my own musical material.

He did this by teaching me a large number of traditional phrases/motifs/building blocks which can be endlessly recombined to generate new variations and new tunes.

Not that he verbalised it like that. It took me years to figure out and fully appreciate what he had passed on to me. His teaching wasn’t “lessons” per se but rather the two of us hanging out and playing tunes.

His basic expectation, from the very start, was that I would be creative.

With tunes I learn, their early stage is me just figuring out the basic tune. Many tunes I play haven’t progressed beyond that point. But the more time I spend playing a tune the more different ways I end up playing it.

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

I agree with all the others that these skills come with time and playing. It’s difficult to explain them.

Don’t think, yeah, I can play that tune through three times without making a mistake, I’ve learnt it, on to the next one; because you haven’t.

The fundamental thing about these tunes is that they are dance tunes and meant for repetition. Good tunes are ones that can be repeated … a lot.

Try taking a tune that you know pretty well, ie. one of those that you can play three times through without making a mistake and start playing it, and don’t stop. Keep going at an easy pace, play it for twenty, thirty, forty minutes and more (make sure no poor soul is within earshot). Close your eyes and just go with it.

It can be a strange experience, but go with it, you’ll find yourself starting to play with the tune, make variations, play with the rhythms and then come out of the other end feeling a bit “odd” for a while.

Now, obviously you’re not going to do this in a session or other public event, or do things as extreme with the tune as you’ll probably end up doing. The point is that you need to play these tunes until the you’re not consciously thinking about the notes but the contours of the tune, the idea of the tune.

Yes, I know, I’m not meaning to go all weird on you, but it is a worthwile exercise.

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

I feel uneasy with using the word “variations” in ways that suggest an Irish trad player is “composing melodic variations” – like the way a baroque classical player would, or a jazz saxophonist. In jazz you state the melody, the “Head” as they call it, then improvise away. That’s not what an Irish traditional musician is doing. I might use all the things we employ in Irish music to introduce variety but I don’t think of myself as writing variations when I phrase or bow or ornament something differently the second time round.

Now of course we might know a “variant” of a tune. A version that differs from someone else’s. It could even be that the reason the variant we play came to exist was that someone once picked up on a particularly striking piece of playing by some other player, in a way that was untypical of the “usual” ways of playing a tune. But that’s a completely different thing altogether to the basic concept of introducing variety to a tune via rhythm, phrasing, emphasis, note-length, ornamentation, intermittent polyphony etc etc

“I feel uneasy with using the word ”variations“ in ways that suggest an Irish trad player is ”composing melodic variations“ – like the way a baroque classical player would, or a jazz saxophonist. In jazz you state the melody, the ”Head“ as they call it, then improvise away. That’s not what an Irish traditional musician is doing.”

I used to think that, until 1) I studied Baroque flute at University and 2) discussed this sort of thing with some jazz sax players.

Baroque players are expected to throw in ornamentation and subtle variations in a way very similar to what happens in ITM. That stuff isn’t written in the sheet music, it has to be generated by the player, who learns how to do it by studying the traditional style.

When a jazz sax player is doing his improvised solo he isn’t playing any random thing that pops into his head. He’s using traditional motifs which have been played thousands of times by thousands of sax players going back at least to the 1920s, but he’s choosing and recombining to create a new and unique thing.

Ever notice how each jazz instrument has its own vocabulary of motifs? How you can tell a trumpet solo from a sax solo without hearing which instrument is playing?

This was brought to my attention by a professional sax player who told me that most of his practice time is spent practicing this stuff, practicing creating solos. When he’s onstage playing a solo he’s not making stuff up on the fly and playing things he’s never played before- on the contrary he’s spent thousands of hours practicing it, daily practicing of it. What is new and unique is the way he puts these practiced elements together.

Listen to several recordings of Paddy Keenan playing Harvest Home- it’s the same sort of thing.

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

The first thing I would say is don’t get too stressed about variations, (or anything else for that matter). I’d much prefer a nice relaxed rendition of a tune than a tense one where someone is trying too hard to do too many different things with it. I think a lot of others would agree.

For me the simplest way into variation is by changing the ornamentation. There are often figures such as GF#Gor bdb that can be played any number of ways, so play a roll on them one time through, or a triplet, or just hold G or b, or GGGor bbb, with or without cuts, or change the middle note GAG or bGb, or GDG or add a harmony note… you’d be amazed how much you can do with such a small canvas.
Crotchets in reels can also benefit from similar treatment.
Another area thats ripe for small little tweaks is in Pairs of notes like bd for example you can just stick a c into the middle and make it a triplet run bcd. Or you could do the same thing in reverse and hold the first note of bcd making it b2d.
Just play around with those ideas for a bit at home and before you know it You’ll be playing around with them at the session, at least when he tunes are being played slowly.
Ideally you’ll eventually start applying the same ideas to other little passages or phrases. I’ll take the castle jig as an example that really clicked for me.
So it’s written out in lots of ways, but we can kind of identify the important notes and then fill in he gaps. I would say they are something like c-- B-- A-E D-G, A, so we could just do descending runs, since the phrase is descending anyway. cBA BAG AGE DB,G, A,.This is fairly standard. Or put some rolls in ~c3 ~B3 AEE DB,G, A,.
Or cAA BGG AEG D2G, A,.
Or cdc bcb AEE D(A,B,)D A,.
Or cAc 3(bcd b AGE DB,D A,.
Just mess around and find alternative routes that take you through the same important notes, generally but not exclusively the downbeats.
I wouldn’t suggest writing down and memorising all these different routes. Maybe occasionally you’ll find something really nice and want to remember it. But really this is just an excercise to get you used to changing a few notes here and there.

Another thing that sometimes makes variations pop up is transposing tunes. Different parts of the tune suddenly get easy or hard and lend themselves to new treatment. It also can help unmoor the fingers if you’ve really sunk one particular version of the tune deep into your muscle memory and can’t escape it.

Anyway, don’t put pressure on yourself. Run through a few tunes nice and slowly on your own and just change a few notes here and there. Eventually you’ll start doing it on easier slower tunes at sessions and then you’re off!

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

The topic of variation is a wide ranging one. You have to be a bit careful when talking about melodic variation with regards to the great players. Often times what you hear in a recording - especially a solo recording - are beyond what would be acceptable in a session, and many of those were probably “written”, meaning that the player(s) sat down beforehand and figured out what they were going to do with the tune to vary it.

The next point is that there are different levels of “knowing” a tune. There’s a difference between knowing every note of a tune and being able to play it reasonably consistently, and really understanding the contour of the tune, and what its message is. When you’ve really internalized a tune, you can start to think about different ways of interpreting that underlying message in the same way that you might tell a story more eloquently than just reading the words. You can start putting emphasis on different parts of the melody to give it a different meaning or feel - even without changing a single note. (In the same way that emphasis on different words in a sentence can change its meaning - “Can you read THIS sentence?” vs “Can YOU read this sentence?”, etc.)

And lastly, really good variation in this music comes both from familiarity with the tune AND familiarity with this music as a whole - usually from years and years of listening and playing. (I know you get this advice a lot! It’s OK for you to push and want to think about these things before you’re ready. Just as long as you keep it in the back of your mind that you’re still relatively inexperienced with this music, so what you learn now may or may not be relevant to you when you’ve garnered more experience and start to really understand).

Having said that, players of Irish traditional music are encouraged not to play a tune the same way every time. But that doesn’t necessarily mean coming up with wild melodic re-phrasing. Here are a few ways to start:

- Instead of varying any notes, try varying your ornamentation. Leave a roll out, find another place to put a roll, etc. And then see what you think those changes does to the feel of the phrase.

- Try approaching important notes differently. The easy place to do this is with “pickup notes” that get you into the tune, or tie the different parts together. Often times, these notes won’t even be notated in the ABC/dots. If the tune is in D, and the first note is a D, you might use a C# as a pickup (or maybe AC#, etc.) But instead of using C#, maybe try using E (coming over the top, instead of up from below). Or if you need a longer pickup F#E instead of AC#. Pickup notes aren’t the only place to do that.

- Try replacing ornaments with other notes. If you are doing a G roll, try playing GFG, etc. Or adding ornaments where you have repetitive notes.

- Try other approaches to a note, like a slide. Or delay an important note for a bit by playing the note below it and then hitting the desired note a split second later…

There are a couple of other exercises that really helped me with variation:

- Try playing a melody in a different time signature. Change a reel into a jig, or vice versa. That can give you a good idea of how well you know the contour of the melody as well as force you to make some in-the-moment decisions about how to get the phrase to sound right with a different number of notes. It can help you figure out which notes are really important to the melody, and it can also help you figure out how to play different notes to approach an important note if you’re needing to add a note to get from jig to reel.

- Try playing a melody in a different key. This really helps more with your familiarity of your instrument and familiarity with the tune in question. But can also give you a sense of playing the same melody differently. And it is a good step in getting your brain weaned off relying too much on the so-called “kinesthetic memory”. If you find yourself getting lost in a tune when you try to do a variation, then it’s likely that you’re still letting your muscle memory for the fingering pattern control the flow more than having your conscious brain control the flow.

@Richard: When I mentioned baroque, i was thinking about the way a harpsichord player would be given the “figured bass” and have to improvise a melody to it. That’s a level of melodic composition – making up strings of notes – above and beyond what is done in Irish traditional music. When I mentioned the jazz solo I did so because, well, again, there’s a level of melodic composition – making up strings of notes – above and beyond what is done in Irish traditional music.

The fact is when an Irish player introduces variety the second or third time around, he/she is still repeating the same phrase. If they’re not, that isn’t really Irish traditional music anymore. That plainly and simply is a different process to both the baroque improvisation (making up a tune to chords) and jazz improvisation (making up strings of notes usually but by no means always referencing the tune or chords of the Head) to which I referred. Jazz improvisers are expected to do more, melodically, than Irish traditional players.

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

“I’ve always thought of variation as being variation in ”the“ melody, and style as being how you use ornamentation, dynamics, how you treat note groupings, etc. ”

I don’t think the two are entirely separable. Of course, two musicians can play a tune with the same set of melodic variations, yet in two completely different styles. But many accomplished players are constantly varying the melody in some way - and the particular way they do it is surely part of their style. Some do no more that ‘microvariations’ (changing a note here and there, adding the odd note or replacing a few notes with one long note etc.), others will replace a whole phrase with another now and again, others still will play a tune as a constant stream of melodic variation, never quite taking the same path as any ‘standard’ version of the tune, yet somehow maintaining the shape and character of the tune throughout.

Re: The subtle art of Variation and Style

“Reaching this level of fluency with a musical instrument is a thing that happens over years”

"The number of young players with blistering technical facility shows this isn’t necessarily true.
What (usually) takes years is the experience to deploy musical resources tastefully and expressively."

Perhaps, but your technicalities are doing you no favors. A young musician who has no job and whose friends are playing trad is likely to spend hours playing the stuff every day, as well as going to lessons, and that’s not to mention that there’s a bit of competition for many with their friends, particularly in the Comhaltas regime. It doesn’t really matter how many years you play; it matters how you practice, and how frequently you do it.

Many adults I play with claim that they aren’t picking it up as quickly because their “brain is too full.” This is an excuse and should be treated thusly. If you played for several hours a day for every day for 6 months you would be a totally different player. Similarly, if you actively listened to trad tapes and transcribed several a day for 6 months, you would be well on your way to tasteful ornamentation.

Sure, there is some limit to how far you can go in terms of real dexterity, mostly relating to the physical limitations of your body and to a lesser extent, the mental restrictions. That being said, fluency is a result of hours playing and listening, not years since you first touched your instrument.
