What is it with bodhrans and guitars?

I know this is a well-trodden path on this site, and I hope I’m not just repeating stuff that has been said before.
I have no objection to bodhrans and guitars per se. I love playing with a really good bodhran player: it gives me a real lift and helps me to concentrate on the beat, lilt and rhythm, which I think is at least 60% of proper ITM. I play guitar myself, sometimes accompanying at sessions. A sensitive guitarist who knows what they’re doing can really enhance a tune.
This thought occurred to me recently. Suppose you’re interested in ITM, but you’ve never picked up an instrument in your life. You like the sound of the fiddle, and you purchase a violin. Would you assume that, because you had obtained an instrument, you are now a fiddler? Would you attend sessions, playing along to every tune, although you had not mastered the basics such as bowing the string to make an even sound, or fingering an accurate note? There may be some incredibly thick skinned people who have done this, but I imagine most would have the self-awareness to at least attempt to get to grips with some of the basics of playing their instrument.
With a bodhran, however, it seems to me that many people equate ownership with ability. True, it’s easy to strike a drum with a stick and make a noise - even small babies can do that - but in my opinion it doesn’t make you a bodhran player.
Usually, guitarists at ITM sessions can at least play the guitar - maybe not that well, but at least they can play. The problem arises when they have no real feel for or understanding of the music. I used to get very frustrated when guitarists would play the wrong key or chords, but I’ve learned to live with that. What really puts me off is when they try to accompany you but play in the wrong rhythm.
If I ever ran my own session again, I think I’d ban guitarists altogether (excepting those who played melody, and singers accompanying their own songs). I’d also give putative bodhranists an audition. 30 seconds should be ample to determine whether they can actually play or not.

Re: What is it with bodhrans and guitars?

“With a bodhran, however, it seems to me that many people equate ownership with ability.”

Since it’s a drum, less experienced “musicians” assume that the melody and harmony factors are canceled out completely and therefore giving the instrument a huge learning curve. A learning curve so large that they can learn everything about it in only a few moments. This of course is not the case, but to the untrained ear the bodhran is simply a “hand drum”.

They don’t understand how versatile ANY kind of drum can be in terms of tone. Because of the experimenting I did with my friends’ snare drums and toms, I new I had to treat the bodhran the same way when I began learning it. Pops, clicks, claps, booms, pows, and many more sounds that i’m discovering that can all be used stylistically. Not just “ticky ticky” or “papa papa”

“The problem arises when they have no real feel for or understanding of the music. I used to get very frustrated when guitarists would play the wrong key or chords, but I’ve learned to live with that. What really puts me off is when they try to accompany you but play in the wrong rhythm. ”

As for the guitar, i’ll use an analogy with language.

When a child learns a second language, he assumes that all he’ll have to learn is learn vocabulary and replace the words. He isn’t aware of the different grammatical structures or phrasing. He isn’t aware of conjugation rules and feminine/masculine setups. Or how is accent affects the foreign words he’s trying to say. So as it is with the imexperienced musician learning Irish guitar. This is the interaction between me and many guitarists i’ve met…

Me: Nice guitar playing, what kind of music do you play?

Guitarist: I play everything.

Me: Great! Do you now Irish music?

Guitarist: No, but I could probably pick it up…

*2 reels and a jig later*

Me: Sorry bro this isn’t working…

From my experience, they try to use the “4 chords” system, which simply doesn’t work because the melody isn’t written for the 4 chord system and isn’t restricted to the tight, nonvarying 4 chord system. Not only that, but the ABAB setup of many tunes calls for modal key changes which a guitarist won’t pick up on unless he can hear the chords within the melody. Even then he still likely doesn’t know the tune which still has to be learned even if you are just playing the chords. Then there’s the matter of the plectrum. If he doesn’t know how to accent the right beats, he’s out of luck. Since he doeasn’t know Irish music, he isn’t aware of the space between the notes.

Q: What is it with bodhrans and guitars?
A: Ignorance (not to be confused with stupidity or anything really bad).

Many perpetual wannabees are blissfully unaware or oblivious to what they are
really doing or trying to do, and have no idea how to get a mirror and

Look around you.
Listen.
You know who is playing WITH you.
They are listening to you, too.

"I do not know the evolution of that lovely missing link,
the banjo, into the Irish mainstream, but I am so glad
it found its way in."

For anyone who is interested, Joel Walker Sweeney is the person who brought the banjo to Ireland.

How the Banjo Got to Ireland--
As fate would have it, Sweeney joined the Virginia Minstrels in 1844 during the group’s first and only European tour.

Like it or not, this is the music Dan Emmett and the Virginia Minstrels (who started the blackface minstrel craze) brought across the pond a year earlier in 1843 - which introduced the banjo and two-handed bones playing to England, Scotland and Ireland (in that order).

The Virginia Minstrels:
Billy Whitlock (banjo), replaced later by Joel Sweeney
Dan Emmett (fiddle)
Frank Brower (bones)
Dick Pelham (tambourine)

The four Virginia Minstrels arrived in Liverpool on May 21, 1843. They toured Manchester and London, then disbanded in July. Billy Whitlock (banjo) had a falling out with the band and returned to New York. Frank Brower (bones) teamed up with Sweeney (“father” of the banjo) where the pair toured Edinburgh, Scotland and England. The Virginia Minstrels regrouped with Sweeney taking Whitlock’s place on banjo. The four played Dublin from April 24 to May 7, 1844. The group toured Cork and Belfast, then headed back to Scotland. By September 1844, the group disbanded again and all returned to the States except for Dick Pelham (tambo) who settled in England.

So if you don’t like the banjo in traditional Irish music, then you can blame Joel Walker Sweeney and the Virginia Minstrels. If you don’t like bones in traditional Irish music, I’m not sure if you can blame Frank Brower or not since he merely brought the bones back to the United Kingdom and Ireland, although played in a different style. And if you don’t like bodhran, then I suppose Dick Pelham might take some of the heat.

(Source: “Dan Emmett and the Rise of Early Negro Minstrelsy” by Hans Nathan. Chapter 10, pp 135-142)

However I’d like to share a few horror situations with said instruments which might (or, admittedly, might not!) reflect what might be part of the matter of this thread.

Picture the Kingsdown Vaults in Bristol, late 90s - a really lovely session welcoming venue. My dear friend, and brilliant guitarist, Pru drives me some 40 miles to catch up and have a tune with another lovely musician on the fiddle, Phil Dawson.

SIX other guitars AND SIX bodhráns arrive. Now begins the horror bit… they all played at the same time and they didn’t/couldn’t/wouldn’t listen to each other. I was sat next to Phil and could barely hear him. And this went on all night! Pru is a really sensitive musician, he listens to every note and shapes his patterns around the melody, just as many of you espouse here. Well he didn’t even get his guitar out its case. This mayhem Tower of Babel situation was relentless, in spite of their being asked to give one another a chance by sitting out and listening a while.

I know of some venues which actually limit the number of bodhráns; I find that sad, as there can be so much to be learnt from listening, and some beginners do listen, and that’s how they learn their craft by being with real people and musicians, and not locked away to practise and enhance mistakes in private. But I can see why there are limits.

Now fast forward to Hobart, Janaury 2003. Being new to the scene here, I hadn’t realised that many had made the pilgrimage to Georgetown for the Tamar Valley Folk Festival. I arrived at Irish Murphys (now defunct as a session) and FOUR people greet me like a long lost messiah!
“Oh great! Lovely to see you Brian, now we can have a session and get the beer tickets!” (IMs used to give out a wad of beer tickets to those in the session).

What’s the problem you might ask. One played shakey eggs with a shakey rhythm, one was a loud inexperienced bodhrán player, one was a very limited mandolin player and the other was a rock style guitarist. They didn’t have a single tune between them and they didn’t listen to each other, and I very much doubt how much they listened to what I played.

Sometimes now at our regular Hobart Session we get maybe three or four guitarists, three non-melody mandolins, maybe three bodhráns and the occasional djembe. All of them individually are skilled enough, but when they decide to do THEIR thing the end product is almost “white” noise which eclipses all melody in between. And that’s when there might be two flutes, three fiddles, banjo, whistle!

One of the guitarists likes to play G major tunes with as many B minor chords as substitutes as he can, or any A minor tune with The Am-G-F chord cycle, one tries to do as much reggae style as possible, another tries Arty McGlynn as rocked up as possible; all can be interesting in their own right, but all at once? Another Tower of Babel scenario. You get the image?

One lovely bodhrán player of 67 years, who is immensely experienced, automatically goes for a wander and chats to all the punters and her friends, and often says to listen to each other; too many bodhráns, she says, and you lose the tune.

Fortunately this doesn’t happen too often, but when it does it certainly does!

Maybe that’s why, sometimes inappropriately, that bodhráns and guitars attract such bad press. I hope that you read this with as much an understanding as I’ve tried to write.

All the best

Brian x
