Having second thoughts about “slow sessions”

I’ve always thought “slow sessions” would be a good place for people that are just learning the music, but after having slow sessions around for a while I’m not so sure. It seems like the people who are learning that come to the regular session learn the music better. They will show up as listeners for a while, and then come later with their instruments and gradually learn the tunes. The more courageous have their instrument out in case a tune they know comes up, but if we see an instrument we’ll usually ask them for a tune. I think that’s why others come as listeners at first.

When I compare the people who learn like this to people I hear from slow sessions, their playing seems a little more on the spot for some reason. I suspect it might have something to do with how they’re influenced. Also, I’m getting some bad feedback from punters who are in the pub on slow session night and it has come to the publican’s attention. Perhaps slow sessions shouldn’t be held in pubs if they happen at all. But I wonder if those folks would be better off joining us at the regular sessions since the quality of the music at slow sessions might not be as inspiring.

Of course there are some problems that can happen if a learner tries to dominate the session or make it cater to them, and they can be disruptive if they noodle, but all learners aren’t like that. My experience is that many of them are polite and have good common sense, and for that reason they are very welcome at the sessions.

Re: Having second thoughts about “slow sessions”

When I started out a good while ago, I attended a so-called slow session once. It definitely was not the place for me to be although I was learning my instrument at the same timel.
Reasons:
- extremely slow tempo learning new tunes and also playing them. I could learn tunes that much faster on my own at home. And playing the tunes too slow simply kills them for me. :-)
- no teaching or input regarding proper ITM style, ie. you’d just learn the bare-bones tune, no ornamentation, bowing patterns, etc. This also has the effect that e.g. fiddle players with classical background tend to keep playing in classical style.

It is mostly a session for learning tunes at a veeeerrry sslloooww tempo. ;-)
Also, since everybody there is more or less at the same level (i.e. no advanced players except for the leader(s)), there is really nobody to “imitate” or to choose as some sort of “role model”.
I was lucky to be invited to play with the “big boys” and found it extremely inspiring and motivating. I wanted to play ITM, and that was something I only could find at a real session.

However, there are a lot of people that enjoy this particular slow session and have been going there for years… (without ever “graduating”). Maybe that’s an important clue. Maybe it’s a low pressure environment among equals, whereas at a “real” session, they wouldn’t know that many tunes, couldn’t keep up the speed etc. and would feel somewhat pressured to practice much more than they are able or willing to and learn a ton of new tunes. Since they have been going there for so long, it doesn’t seem to me that they feel they’re missing out on anything and get plenty out of the social environment as well.

From what I know about those musicians, they are generally by far not as much obsessed with Irish music as those that attend the regular sessions in town. There are also some people who IMHO will never advance beyond the beginner’s level session (lack of time, obsession, enthusiasm, ambition, interest, skill…).

Where would they go if they didn’t have their slow session?
I think it’s great if there is a slow session in town, but I would always encourage anybody who is serious about ITM and willing to learn not just the tunes and the instrument, but the style as well to go to as many normal sessions as possible.

Re: Having second thoughts about “slow sessions”

Hello there

Quite recently, in my own nook of the woods, slow sessions have come into being.

First it was a regular session that attracted most of the experienced(or willing to get experience) players of the area, within a radius of 40 miles (yes, there aren’t many of them around …), a regular session so, at which it was agreed to start off with playing arranged sets at a reasonable pace, so as to allow newbies to join in.

After an hour of this, the regular session was allowed to start. Things are still this way there.

More recently another slow session appeared at a different ‘venue’, but it largely attracts the same players, who play the same tunes as they do at the first one, at the same tempo. This is deterring more experienced players from attending, as quite naturally “enough is enough” … so it’s unlikely that a regular session will still be held there, as reliable sessionists are reluctant to drive relatively long distances to repeat the same experience as a week previously.

Clearly, IMHO, such a ‘slow session’ tends to be a ‘non session’, as …

… there’s too much predictability about which tunes will inevitably be played : there’s little chance anyone will learn many, if any, new ones

… the systematically slow tempo is not appropriate to ITM, and players who don’t listen to it much on their own get the wrong idea of what it should sound like

… there’s only one leader, whereas at a regular session different players take turns starting new sets

Re: Having second thoughts about “slow sessions”

Jaypix, a lot depends on the format of the slow session. In my experience, for true newbies to the music, it helps to have an experienced player teaching and leading the tunes. And that person has to be very mindful of creating a *progression* both for each slow session and for the series of slow sessions overall.

- Each slow session can start with a warm up of the tune taught/learned the previous time. The leader can answer any questions and maybe teach some variations.
- Then teach a new tune. Make sure everyone has a handle on it.
- Then play through as many previous tunes as time allows. Try some tunes or sets at a somewhat faster pace to encourage people to work toward playing at typical session tempos.
- End by playing the new tune again, at least six times through.

The overall progression (from one slow session to the next and the next, and so on) can:
-For select tunes or sets, keep nudging the tempo up a notch until it’s basically at whatever the local norm is.
-Introduce more complex tunes—tunes with more than two parts, twistier melodies, particular technical challenges, etc.
-Explore tunes that sit well on some instruments and less naturally on others; figure out ways to make them work, e.g., “folding” on whistle, flute, and pipes; how fiddlers can deal with long open-string notes that are rolled on wind instruments; tunes with large leaps that may be easy on box or concertina, less so on other instruments, etc.
-Choose a tune to explore “doubling” (playing an octave lower or higher than usual).
-Play familiar tunes in different keys.

Personally, I think it works better to hold slow sessions out of the public spotlight. No point in annoying punters with endless repetitions of slow tunes, and no point in stressing out the musicians worried about how they sound. Fewer distractions.

Re: Having second thoughts about “slow sessions”

It’s a *slow* session. Some people call them “tune learning sessions.” Essentially the same thing. Yes, it’s a form of group lesson, usually led by an experienced player who also teaches. But in the format of a session.

You teach a new tune each time to build a common repertoire (that’s also based on what tunes are played at the local regular sessions). The rest of it operates much like a regular session, but at a slow pace and with time to talk about the tunes, ask and answer questions, etc.

A slow session that does not include learning new tunes doesn’t seem sustainable to me. People will either stagnate where they are and lose hope, or they’ll eventually get up to speed and go to a regular session instead.

If what you’re looking for is simply a session with a slow-ish pace, that’s just a session. Preferably with someone anchoring it who can keep the speed demons on a leash. But your OP raised issues that a slow-ish session doesn’t address. The approach I laid out does.

Re: Having second thoughts about “slow sessions”

Apparently, I’m misunderstanding your questions. But you mentioned newcomers who need to build repertoire. A tune learning session is a great way to do that, and to play lots of tunes, as you would in a regular session, at an easier pace.

By definition, newbies need to learn—tunes, skills, concepts, context, etiquette, etc. The people who can pick all that up by woodshedding at home and sitting in at regular sessions don’t need slow sessions. But many people progress more quickly and with a more well-rounded understanding of the music if they participate in the kind of slow session I outlined.

Also, you might be surprised at how many people play this music who don’t regularly sit in at sessions. It’s absolutely fine to stay at home and play alone, or with one or two like-minded friends. Sessions are about sitting in with a group and enjoying some craic with the tunes. But the tunes alone are good craic, too, played for their own sake. There’s more freedom, more expressive possibilities, playing alone in your kitchen. If you really want to get inside a tune, nothing beats it.

Which is why matt and I both mention playing tunes at a faster pace once people have them under their fingers.

And why it helps to teach new tunes, so people do in fact build repertoire, which enables them to join in at a regular session.

To play in regular sessions you need four basic things: good manners, basic competency on your instrument, a big enough bag of tunes that get played locally, and the ability to comfortably keep up with the pace.

A slow session that doesn’t teach new tunes and never picks up the pace isn’t much help.
