First Impressions

I know this has been discussed loads of times, but bear with me.

I don’t know any notes on say a mouthorgan, but could play any tune I know by ear, and still don’t know any notes on the mandolin or whistle.

I KNOW that the ability to read and understand music is a great help, but to a moron like me it somehow removes the mystery.
I see a fiddler in a pub and think “that’s great, she must have practiced hard”. Then I read some of these posts and discover there are patterns and such associated with music and therefore tunes, and I do not feel just as impressed.

I also believe my reaction is common. If you know nothing of the theory of music, musicians impress the hell out of you. When you realise they can simply follow scales, dots, ABCs and whatever, it takes something away.

Is it better, more traditional say, to be a “musical moron”, and avoid that loss of innocence?

Re: First Impressions

If there were no patterns in the music, and therefore in the way it is played, then it would be as meaningless as a tune “composed” by a computer using random numbers (an easy enough programming exercise if anyone has a few minutes to spare). Set dancing (which is the raison d’etre of Irish dance music) consists of intricate patterns of movement.
In music, the patterns are the composer’s “vocabulary”, and it is the skill with which he uses those patterns which determines the effect the music has on the listener. The same applies if for “music” you read “dance”, and for “composer” you read “choreographer”.
The lack of pattern is chaos.

Purely random music (no scales, arpeggios, defined rhythmic structure etc) - it’s called “aleatoric” - is technically difficult and unsatisfying to play because of the lack of pattern - and must be even more difficult to memorise for that reason. One or two musicians I’ve known who’ve had to play that music professionally say they subconsciously try to find patterns in the music, or perhaps even to try to impose rhythmic patterns - which shows that a lack of pattern is an unnatural creative state.

Re: First Impressions

I tell my students (ages 11-14) that there are really two levels for performing music, and few get past level one.

Level 1- you can play the notes correctly. Music is, after all, as we know, a series of sounds that follow patterns, and the sheet music helps us find these sounds. Many kids stop at this point.

Level 2- turning that series of sounds into something meaningful for us, the performers, and the listeners in the audience. At this point, the music has a flow, a rhythm, a feeling difficult to articulate, but brings meaning to all involved.

I also tell them that to be really educated musically and to play your instrument well, you need to develop your music reading skills as well as your playing skills. The ability to replicate what is written on the page is as important as the ability to play without any notation- “by ear”- by listening and using the patterns and techniques they already know. You can’t have one without the other, in my opinion.

Our school band and orchestra uses sheet music, and the jazz band (same kids) does not.

I would also say to my friend Bodhran Bliss that you already have the second standard in place (the performance piece) which is the more elusive and difficult to attain,. The music reading part is far easier, nuts and bolts stuff that it is.

Re: First Impressions

I think of this sort of thing as the difference between the familiar and the unfamiliar. When something is unfamiliar it seems mysterious and difficult, maybe even unattainable. But when it becomes familiar through exposure or study, it seems easier, perhaps even becoming second nature.

But “familiar” doesn’t have to destroy your sense of wonder or enjoyment. I’ve played these tunes for 30 years, and I can write them down in at least three notation formats, dissect their structure, talk about keys and modes and bowing patterns, draw an exploded diagram of a long roll, etc. But I still don’t have a clue how this music moves me so deeply, or why people can’t resist drumming their feet to it, or dancing. So the mystery is still there--maybe even deeper because it’s still such a mystery even after knowing many of the subtleties and “secrets.”

Re: First Impressions

i guess my reply addressed the “i just do not feel as impressed” and “she must have practiced hard” part of your opener, bliss. now i’ve been thinking about the “loss of innocence?” part……

no, i don’t think having musical knowledge and/or knowledge about patterns common to a genre takes away the magic, or that ignorance of this stuff is equivalent to “innocence.” reasons for this opinion being,

a) no amount of knowledge about music theory or patterns or characteristics will give you everything you need, nor will it ever reduce the music to a preset or rote formula….. and b) the “knowledge” only speeds up your apprehension of patterns and things. even if you have no formal knowledge, you would start to know much of the “patterns” from years of practicing and living with the music…..and most of all, c) the “magic” comes from in you. it is a flame that lights at the meeting-point between you and the music, you and the tune, you and the history, you and the tradition, you and your instrument. if that connection is there, the “magic” is always there regardless of formal knowledge. if that connection is not there, no magic, regardless of formal knowledge.
