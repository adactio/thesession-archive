The One-Form: Meditating on The Music.

One thing that has become abundantly clear over the years, is that we learn any given piece of music in at least two forms. We develop an aural understanding of the music, and we also develop a physical understanding of the music. At least, that’s the general process for ear musicians. Even still, when there is music that really speaks to us, there are a number of ways that we can dig deeper into it: Learning it in a different key, on a different instrument, re-arranging and improvising, escalating, etc. Though, one thing that has left me intrigued for a very long time now, is what I have come to call the “one-form”. The one-form is a single unified interpretation of the music that combines your aural understanding of the music with your physical understanding of the music; That is, having a complete visual picture of what playing the music looks and sounds like.

Sometime ago when I was much younger, an interesting thought came up: “If I really took my time and thought about it, could I recite every single note to one of my favorite piano pieces, with total confidence?”. It’s actually a really simple thought experiment ーa meditation even, if you will. In general, the question is, how well do you truly know the music that you’ve learned? This question is answered by visualizing the music playing on your instrument and assessing how accurate you are.

I’ve always thought of this as just a fun thought experiment, or an emergency meditation when I’m struggling with something. But now I’m left wondering if it’s actually a healthy and beneficial practice that should be adopted as a routine. To my experience, the music that I know best, I can recite at an exceptional level of confidence, if not with total confidence; Whereas music that I don’t know very well at all, even the simplest music, I wouldn’t be able to recite much at all, let alone with any amount of confidence. For example, I recently transcribed the reel “Lady Anne Montgomery”, and I play it on all of my instruments. A tune like this, I can visualize at speed and know that I am seeing and hearing the music correctly. I can recite it note for note. But a tune like “The Boys of Malin”, one of the first tunes that I learned, it’s much more difficult to visualize because I only know it on one instrument, and I haven’t spent the time with it that you get from transcribing. I could probably recite it, but it would be very slow in some places, and doubtful as well. Ironically, I don’t play it as well, even though I’ve been playing it for much longer.

Now, the question may be raised, “why would I spend time meditating when I could be playing the music?”, and that’s a valid question. If you already know that you know the music, then I would agree there may not be much reason to sit down and think the music out. On the other hand, you have music that, for whatever reason, we honestly never really developed to our own standard. This would be the music I would argue should be explored more thoroughly. And if we’ve played it 1,000 times, and it’s still not there for us, how much more can we expect to get out of it by just playing it more? Don’t even get me started on virtuosic music that requires everything we can give it. I hear classical musicians do all kinds of crazy things to get more intimate with a piece of music; Learning it backwards(I’ve done this myself), learning it in reverse, upside down, etc. because sometimes that’s what the music requires. If I can get something out of a simple meditation, even if it’s just a clearer picture of what exactly it is that I’m missing, then I think it’s worth the few minutes it would take to sit down and think the music out.

I’m also wondering if this is something that anyone else practices. Or even, if there are other musical meditations you practice. I think that what strikes me the most, is that at it’s core, this was one of the very first musical lessons taught to me; That is, considering the music when I’m away from my instrument. I was a child, so that’s not how the lesson was delivered to me; but looking back, having that awareness early has probably been the single most meaningful aspect of my musical development.

Thank you for taking the time to read this! I hope it’s relatable enough to inspire at least a short conversation.

Re: The One-Form: Meditating on The Music.

I think of this as utterly normal—playing tunes in your mind down to the smallest detail. I’ve done this my whole life as a way of savoring the tunes, exploring variations, and coordinating and reinforcing all the synaptic links (essentially, “practicing”). It’s akin to gymnasts, competition divers, and other athletes who “visualize” their movements right before actually doing them. Widely called “visualization,” that’s an oversimplification because the process engages all the senses, including proprioception.

You can do this in almost any setting, it can be silent, and a particular advantage of this process is that you can play the music at any speed—fast as a way to quickly rehearse a piece, or super slowly to dig into every last detail. It’s a highly adaptable and very productive exercise. Studies show that, if you’re an experienced musician, it’s essentially as effective as practicing with your instrument in hand.

I’m pretty new to music and don’t have confidence in my understanding of all this - but I’ll describe what it seems like to me. I see there being many valid ways to learn and play music, and the one I am focusing on now is two parts:
1) Learning to fluidly translate the sound in my head onto the fiddle (i.e. all aspects of technique, but coming at it from aural memory to fiddle rather than mental concept of the music (notes/ornaments/etc) to fiddle
2) Learning tunes entirely aurally so the sound and rhythm is in my body and I can hear it clearly in my head

This is different from the way I learned tunes at first, which was more like try to figure out all the notes and then develop a physical/muscle memory of playing through the tune.

There are some tunes I learned a while back and could not really play well then, and had forgotten the muscle memory of how I had played them when I learned them-- but the sound of the tunes was clear enough in my memory. I revisited some of these tunes and found I could play them right away - without even listening to them again - a lot better than I could when I learned them. There were even notes that I hadn’t had before that I quickly found from the sound of the tune that was still in my head. This makes perfect sense of course when I think about it, but was a pleasant surprise.

It’s become far easier as well to just play a tune I’ve never played before, as long as it’s in my head. The venn diagram of the sound in my head crossed with the sounds/techniques I am capable and practiced at making on the fiddle - modified by my ability to translate between these quickly - is the portion/depth of the tune I can play quickly with little practice. Of course it’s always an approximation of the tune as I heard it, for instance there might be bits I lack the capability for or haven’t built a clear enough aural memory of, and so my body naturally does whatever the closest technique approximation to my aural memory’s approximation of the sound is that I am capable of. As I expand what I’ve practiced and am able to do, the way that I play all tunes changes somewhat automatically. It also seems a bit easier for an old tune to naturally incorporate my new capabilities if I let it rest a bit (i.e. don’t play it) so the muscle memory of playing it fades some and the aural memory -> fiddle pathway can be what is primarily used. At least for me, it seems the muscle memory and mental concept of a tune fades far faster than the aural memory.

The aural memory -> instrument approach, to the exclusion of all else, I don’t think is contradictory to what you’re describing Jerone, but maybe a variation/subset of what you describe. For example, I will listen to one recording that I love over and over again, focusing on the sound - which is quite like meditation, and each time through my aural memory of the details becomes more robust, picking up more detail (it’s fractal, there are infinite degrees nuance to sound). In reflecting on this, I realize some of this is the aural memory that happens subconsciously, but I also sometimes do consciously notice particular bits and mentally connect those with a particular technique. It’s interesting to think about what portion of this learning is that mental part vs aural. It’s far more aural over mental now than when I began learning fiddle, and I’m not necessarily bent on getting to 100% aural, but it does seem to me that moving in the direction of more and more of it being subconscious/aural rather than mental is what I want for myself. Again - not that other ways are not entirely valid or even more effective for other people. I could even be wrong about this entirely, but this is how it seems to me.

Re: The One-Form: Meditating on The Music.

Philip, your posts get at how music works for me. It’s most fluid and fluent when my brain is in a nonverbal mode, which corresponds to your notion of “aural.”

I can bring “verbal” ideas into it, and that’s particularly useful when discussing a tune with someone else (e.g., teaching). But words and labels can be a distraction, and they often derail my playing. I play better (and learn tunes more quickly and durably) when the verbal side goes blank.

@Gromit—this process has long been called “visualization” as it’s used in athletics and sports training. But it’s much more about kinesthetics than just eyesight. The idea is to imagine in detail all the motions, body mechanics, and physical feedback loops of successfully performing a skill. For musicians, this includes not just imagining doing the mechanics of producing the tune but also hearing the the tune, in your mind’s ear.

Maybe it’s called visualization because vision is such a common, dominant sense in how we “see” things in our imaginations. If someone says, “Imagine you’re standing in a forest,” most of us picture trees and shade and maybe some understory plants. But it’s also possible (and worthwhile!) to imagine the scent of pine, the feel of soft duff underneath your feet and the coolness of shade on your skin, the sound of a breeze through leaves overhead or the call of a rook, the proprioception of stepping over a log, maybe even the tang of a berry on your tongue. All of that is included in the term “visualization.”

In the context of playing music, “seeing” may not be needed at all. The focus can be on the sounds and physical sensations of making the music. Although some people like to include visual references—seeing where their hands or fingers are and how they’re moving, seeing notation in their mind’s eye, etc.

I remember, at a session decades ago, asking a piper about a particular reel he played. He told me the name. Then, at night’s end as we were all packing up, he handed me a slip of paper. > I mean conceptualizing what playing the music actually looks like, with precise accuracy. A less visual example would be, as mentioned above, reciting all of the notes to your favorite tune.<

I couldn’t do either of those things, for sure.

I need to know what playing a tune feels like, not what it looks like. I never watch the detail of anyone else’s playing; unless they were on whistle or flute, it wouldn’t mean anything anyway. I switch between looking at their face and their overall body shape and movement.With a group, I see how they move together, how they fall into sync as they start playing.

As for knowing the individual notes, I couldn’t even come up with the tune to Happy Birthday. As a child, I learned Irish tunes by ear and it wouldn’t even have occurred to me they might be written down. For both the adults and children in our world, new tunes came from other players, the radio, a record.

I still can’t read staff notation, and although staff with tabs is okay, it’s an obstacle that I dispense with as soon as I can. I use it to remind me of the first three notes, and occasionally, when I’m learning something new and there’s a bar or two where my fingers won’t seem to go to the right notes by instinct, I use it to learn that section. Then I never look at it again until time’s gone by and I’ve lost track of those first three notes.

I don’t have conscious access to the process of learning or playing at all, and if I try to visualise music, I see nothing. But I can feel it, as movements, as tension and relaxation, as a running beat. If I had to conjure up something, it’d be my hand running over the surface of it: it might be smooth and undulating, or rough and gritty, or soft then hard, or a jumble of contours that threatens for a while not to resolve into a shape. Often Irish tunes end in an upward slope, where your hand slips off the edge into the air and then has no indication where to go next.

Some neurodivergent and autistic people have very acute visual recall and imagination that can run to incredible detail. I’m the opposite, where I can’t visualise anything I haven’t seen, and someone cutting their hair into a different style might mean I don’t recognise them until I see them moving. It’s all about movement, texture, and taste – I have synaesthesia, so my sensory wires are crossed. I taste textures, and sound appears as vague visual movement. But while I know I’m a cognitive oddball, it’s really fascinating to have insight into other people’s processes.

The prob with this is, you either have the tune (in which case meditation/recitation is unnecessary), or you don’t. And if you don’t have it, the only way to acquire it is by listening to it (at a speed where you can hear the individual notes).

If you “meditate” or whatever incorrectly (ie you imagine wrong notes) you then have to unlearn them later. The only way to properly get a tune is via input.

My silent acquisition, since I can read music, is to read sheet music and “hear” it in my head.
