Re: An acquaintance is quite adamant that ITM can’t be played on a piano accordion but only on a button box.

Trad music takes many forms. Here in Scotland PAs are rare in sessions, and often not particularly welcome. But they are more or less mandatory for ceilidh bands playing the same tunes. The difference is that sessions want to let the tunes flow to their own natural pulse, ceilidh bands need to overpowering oom-pah of a PA to keep the dancers stamping in time. So whether a PA is appropriate or not depends entirely on what sort of trad you want to play.

↳ Posted by Ailin on June 18th, 2017.

Re: An acquaintance is quite adamant that ITM can’t be played on a piano accordion but only on a button box.

As usual, let your ears be your guide. If it sounds like ITM then it is. Sometimes it can be a bit academic about whether a particular instrument is traditional. Does anyone fuss about whether bouzouki or guitar is traditional, even though they’re relatively late arrivals to ITM? If it sounds right that’s good enough.

Since any good living tradition such as ITM isn’t static but still evolving, I think it’s more of an issue whether an instrument is “legitimate” for playing ITM; and though some instruments are more traditional than others, it still comes down ultimately to the player. Here in the USA, it is very common for musicians to come into the tradition who are already steeped in other musical disciplines. Is it still ITM if a classical violinist or bluegrass player cannot properly deprogram himself/herself, just because the fiddle is a traditional instrument in Irish music?

In some ways it’s more of a challenge, and I would daresay requires a deeper insight into ITM, to make it sound right on an untraditional instrument - especially one that carries all sorts of baggage (much of which is deserved, to be honest.) I give kudos to folks like Michael Sheehy and Alan Kelly, who’ve figured out how to un-piano accordion the piano accordion. By that I mean, for example, keeping an unbusy, lighter more selective use of the left hand, using it only sparingly as a good button box player would do with the bass side, or a piper would do on the regulators. Players like that can carry the rhythm just fine with the melody alone, no oompahs to be found.

Ailin: As I said in my original post, traditional music takes many forms. PA is good for some forms, not so good for others. ↳ Posted by Jusa Nutter Eejit on June 23rd, 2017.

Re: An acquaintance is quite adamant that ITM can’t be played on a piano accordion but only on a button box.

Re: the oom and the pah being close together:
Yes, Mark M, you’re completely right, but it’s a silly argument (I play PA ITM). But that’s also true about most instruments currently in the tradition:
The Anglo concertina is laid out for playing in C and G in the English and maritime traditions; similar story for the diatonic accordion, which also has an oom-pah bass layout (mid-to-late 1800s entry to the tradition; the currently dominant B/C layout is new and keyboard-inspired). The “fiddle” everyone uses now comes from the Italian romantic ensemble and orchestral tradition (arched bridge, angled fingerboard, and romantic bow, not the traditional flat-bridged cigar-box shaped Irish fidl emphasizing rhythm and droning). The 6 string guitar was designed for Spanish music, with steel strings added by 20th century Germans. Even the uilleann pipes come from the highland piping tradition via pastoral pipes, for a very different setting than they’re now used in--the evidence can be seen on the adapter to a missing “foot” on the end of every chanter, betraying its origins. I’ve always been mystified that the button’s 40 year (+/-) head start on the keyboard gets so much press in a tradition that’s more than a thousand years old.

My two cents: Most of the instruments in Irish traditional music are fairly new arrivals from other traditions and have to be played with care to fit (fiddle can overpower an accordion any day of the week). They bear the marks of their origins, and pointing that out doesn’t mean much. Most of the oldest traditional instruments wouldn’t fit in a modern session anymore (ever try breaking out a sturgan? Won’t make you popular.). This doesn’t mean anything goes, just that traditions evolve. The dance between hold-outs and innovators is the heart of any worthwhile tradition.
