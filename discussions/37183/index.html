Non-Americanized Irish Americans?

I was on my way to play fiddle for a graduation party and got stranded just 10 miles outside of my hometown because my nearly 20 year old pick-up truck broke down on me. After my grandmother picked me up so I could make it to the dinner, she took me back out to my truck to see if we could get it started.
I also needed to get back to my hometown for a visit due to a family emergency and I already planned on staying a few days, so it was important we got it started.

After running out of water, and it failing to jump, we decided to call TripleA to have it towed. After waiting for nearly an hour, TripleA called us back and told us they would be yet another hour. After re-assessing the problem, I figured we should try to get it started while waiting, but we needed more water.

Now mind you, we’re in the middle of the country, around Fairview, Kentucky, maybe two or three miles west of The Jefferson Davis monument, a man known for his pro-slavery ideals. One of those kinds of places where everyone lives on farms and the neighbors live miles apart from each other. So the idea of walking onto someone’s property was quite intimidating, and it was also getting dark soon.

There was also a strong Mennonite community out there, and I always remembered my grandmother telling me they were kind and hard working people that were very likely non-racist. You’re garanteed to see several of them on their horses, carriages, and bikes. They always seemed friendly and courteous, so we decided to give it a shot.

There was a farmhouse in view, on a road off the highway. But it was far from the road. After walking about a quarter mile and noticing that the other 3 houses before the horizon were all another quarter mile away from the road, we decided to visit the first one. Getting closer, we noticed a pair of people but couldn’t tell if they were adults or children, so I called out to them to let them know we were approaching and peaceful. They slowly went around the house and out of our view, and now I’m terrified that we frighted them, especially after my sisters’ “Why did you do that!?” reaction… And noticing that they were in fact children, before they went out view.

We finally get close to the house and look around to see if anyone else is around. There was a sheltered field of green vegetables beside us that was completely empty. Finally, a gentleman comes from around the house. Behind him, his wife and all of their children. The boys in their overalls and straw hats and the girls in their dresses and bonnets.

Before I could even finish my greeting my sister goes, “Our car is broken down, could we have some water please?”. He says, “Yes, I saw that.”. He then gets a water hose by the sheltered field. He wore a straw hat, had bright silver/blue eyes, and had a long, blond, natural(unkempt) beard. He didn’t say much, but as I tried to make small talk, he made strong eye contect. He wasn’t very friendly and didn’t ask any questions, but was nice for someone meeting a stranger. I asked him what it was like living out there, away from the city. He said, “I don’t really know how to answer that, I’ve never lived in the city”. I immediately sensed a strong Irish accent in his speech, but being so nervous and stressed, I failed to ask him.

After getting the water jugs filled, we thanked him for his kindness, offered him money but he didn’t accept, then got on our way. As we were walking down their road, I look at my sister and go, “Dude, he had a really strong Irish accent…”. She goes, “I know! It was soooo cool!”. She then talked for several minutes about how pretty his eyes were and how pretty the girl’s dresses were.

My question for you is, could I have been hearing things, or could that man really have been Irish? My dad my be able to fix my car before I go back home, and I would like to visit them and share some music with them to thank them for their help and saving my week. But if they aren’t Irish, it could potentially be very embarrassing. So from this long-winded short story I gave you, from the location, to the lifestyle, to the way they dressed, what’s your assessment?

Re: Non-Americanized Irish Americans?

I live out in the sticks. I’m in south central Pennsylvania, which is an area that is culturally very similar to your part of Kentucky. Its why they call the wooded ridges around here “Pennsyltucky”.

When you get out of town and out here where we live, instead of a southern draw like what you hear out in the country further south, you can hear the flinty sounding, matter-of-fact, if-your-head-is-wax-stay-out-of-the-sun sort of dialect that sounds a lot like the Irish accent you hear in the movies.

if you wonder if they are Mennonite, look for signs of electricity, look for black or dark blue cars, or cast iron wheels on the tractor. There’s different sects, some can drive cars, some can use pneumatic tires, some can use electricity, and then some can’t. But straw hats, clothes drying on the line, no cars, and cast iron wheels on the tractor would give you a good hint.

Re: Non-Americanized Irish Americans?

There’s no such thing as an ‘Irish’ accent. There’s a ‘stage Irish’ accent, and a ‘Hollywood Irish’ accent. Neither of which you’ll ever hear from an Irish person. You’ll hear Dublin accents, Tipperary accents, West Cork accents, Limerick accents. So many accents, in fact, that you can sometimes place a person to within miles of where they grew up, based on the way they speak.

What you’re used to hearing as an Irish accent isn’t one. Never has been.

Source: Irish.

Re: Non-Americanized Irish Americans?

Years ago I read a discussion on the profusion of accents in Ireland written by some Irish academic. He had several key points. During the 800 or so years of English incursion into Ireland, the English Language itself was undergoing profound changes in pronunciation and vocabulary. So different parts of Ireland came under English influence at different times and got a different set of ‘tools’ as they adapted to English. This coupled with the innate cultural conservatism of the Irish give us a many-layered-cake picture of English as it was changing. Two Baronies that dated back to the Geraldine Wars spoke nearly perfect Chaucerian English into the 1700’s. Obviously the lack of good roads and poor communications contributed to all this. And did I mention Irish conservatism? A Kerryman would in no way wish to be taken for a Clareman!
