Scottish Traditional Fiddle - Grade Exams

Two years after moving to Yorkshire, I’m considering taking fiddle lessons again. I took ABRSM grades 3 & 4 a few years ago in East Anglia, but when I look at grade 5 (as something to aim at with my new teacher) I am daunted by the high positions and the esoteric scales, which aren’t much use to me playing traditional music even if I finally managed to mug them up - probably more difficult now that I’ve turned 70.

The syllabuses for Grade 5 in ‘Scottish Traditional Fiddle’ - whether Trinity or Royal Conservatoire of Scotland - look much more alluring and include several tunes that I already play, plus the skills required would be really beneficial. I think I could enjoy the learning process for this exam, and it would give me and my new teacher something to focus on.

However, I’m a very nervous candidate in music exams - my grade 4 was a ‘scrape’ because of bow shake - and I also don’t know how I’d get on if I had to travel to Scotland or take an exam remotely.

I just wondered - has anyone here any experience of these traditional fiddle exams? How did you get on, and do you have any advice?

Thanks in advance for any replies.

Re: Scottish Traditional Fiddle - Grade Exams

Riddell Fiddles have put plenty of student fiddlers (including a lot as mature as you) through the Scottish exams. For most it has been a not unpleasant experience.

From what I remember, there is still a bit too much emphasis on scales over, say, ornamentation. The theory stuff is pretty basic. I think there is some ear stuff at grade 5. You do get to play your choice from a selection of tunes.

Because of numbers we were able to get examiners to travel to us but, given where you are, you might have to travel. I haven’t checked but I would guess, given all that has occurred over the last eighteen months, that they offer examination over Zoom or an equivalent platform.

The geneaology tree of Scottish fiddle music--in Scotland, that is, not in Cape Breton--converged with classical, big-time. And some of the more vile (IMHO) aspects of the classical paradigm invaded Scotland’s traditional music culture. In recent years it’s been getting a booty-shaking blood transfusion from Cape Breton. But that classical paradigm is still strong for chunks of the Scottish traditional establishment.

Re: Scottish Traditional Fiddle - Grade Exams

No,no Donald, no offence at all, and certainly no apology required. You’re 100% correct in describing Paul as a “NE fiddler” - that is where his style is very much rooted, and he doesn’t play very much outside of that specific style, although I would say I have detected “external” influences creeping in to quite a few of his original compositions in the past few years.
The whole “style” thing has become very blurred over the years, and very, very few players these days - unless they make a conscious and deliberate effort - play in any one “historic” style. When I first met Alasdair - he worked in Aberdeen for a couple of years before moving to the USA, so that’s how long ago that was - his repertoire certainly consisted of a large number of Gow and William Marshall tunes, plus quite a few pipe tunes as well. Over the years, he has added to that, and of course absorbed and assimilated techniques from the many. many musicians he has collaborated with. I think he would be happy to be termed a “Scottish” fiddle player, but “Perthshire” I think is too narrow a geographical term to put him in, certainly these days.
I hope we can agree on this.

I apologise for continuing the side tracking but also agree that you, Fiddle Aunt, should just go for it. Goals can be a very useful motivation for helping to keep your mind active and skills honed.

As regards the “Conservatoire style”, I had been under the impressions that some tutors on the course specifically taught specific Scottish styles of playing and singing as “tradition bearers” or similar. Pete Clark himself, for instance, has (possibly still is) been a tutor there too.
However, it could be possible that when some students start to “dabble” in everything or try out new styles when they have been used to playing something different, they may end up playing a “homogenous mixture” for much of the time when they aren’t actually focused on a particular style at the time. e.g. when they are playing with other students and friends.

To an extent, I can be bit like that myself because, as an adult learner, I didn’t grow up playing music in any specific style or tradition although I obviously listened to a lot of Scottish music as a child.

Re: Scottish Traditional Fiddle - Grade Exams

Dear Fiddle Aunt, I admire your wish to take fiddle exams when you are in your 70s: I am of similar age, but would not be looking to take any more exams at my time of life. But I accept that it does give you goals and deadlines to work to, and a sense of achievement when you get your results.
I took Scottish Higher Music as an adult student back in the late 1980s, soon after we moved to Scotland, in a class of 16-year-olds, when our local High School was trying to boost its “community” image: in those days it was mainly “classical” based, though I do remember that there was a question about the Rolling Stones and Beatles in the written paper, which I thought (having not revised the subject) I might know more about than the question I did answer on Medieval anthems! There was a lot of theoretical stuff in the course, which has stood me in good stead since, along with the stuff I learned as a teenager while doing piano exams. It also did make me brush up on my piano playing until I could do a fair rendition of Debussy’s “Clair de Lune” - couldn’t hope to get anywhere near it now! The examiner seemed quite chuffed to have an older examinee to listen to - “You more mature people really feel the music” - he said.
Since then, it has been possible to choose to do traditional music in Scottish Higher Music - anything from Gaelic Song to Piping, to Scottish fiddle or Bothy Ballads.
While I have not done any exams since then, I have entered a few competitions and had the occasional success, but again there are some people who would say “Why would you bother to enter competitions?” and in some cases, “What value are they when there are only one or two entrants in your class?” I can only say that perhaps I have a competitive streak in me, and it’s a personal challenge, just as your taking Grade exams may be. It gets you doing more than you might if not so challenging yourself.
I wish you all the best of luck and enjoy your journey to doing these exams if that’s your wish.

Re: Scottish Traditional Fiddle - Grade Exams

I think for some people graded exams provide a means to improvement, with the target-setting involved. I think this is especially true for students learning their first musical instrument (in that they are also having to learn about music). Also, some students living in relative isolation find it difficult to gauge their progress and graded exams, to a certain extent, will perform that function.

My main reservation with exams is that you will always get a certain amount of working to pass the exam rather than working for real knowledge and improvement.
I remember being asked to assess a young lad who wanted help in achieving grade 8 guitar with Rock School. I thought, “He must be pretty good”, so I asked him to play me something. He said he couldn’t because he didn’t have his “book” with him. So I asked him to play me some scales and (simple) chords, most of which he struggled with. Eventually it turned out he had only passed grade 5 (but wanted to jump straight to grade 8). So I asked him to play me something that he had done for his grade 5 exam… you guessed it, he couldn’t because he didn’t have his “book” with him.
