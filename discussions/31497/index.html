Thought I was getting better at the whistle but…

Hi,

I’ve been playing various whistles for a year now. I’ve got the hang of most of the rolls and cuts and I’m even starting to get some snap into the A rolls after doing some ring finger excercises. Then I started listening to a few recordings of myself in the stabdard high D. UUgggh. The D whistle is so pitchy. Granted I am comparing myself to excellent seasoned players. Perhaps I’ll get some agreement here that the D is the easiset to learn but the hardest to master. I sound OK on the Low G, even pleasant, also on the low D and even on the high C, these are more forgiving, but the D is very pitchy. Is there some knack to playing it so that it sounds sweet withough pitchyness, which remains even after you’ve gotten rid of most of the squawk when changing octaves. I presume its about knowing exactly how much to use emboucherie and how hard to blow. I sound best on a Dixon high D trad, maybe the other high D’s I’ve messed with just aren’t up to snuff. Thanks for the help

Re: Thought I was getting better at the whistle but…

Try putting the mic in a doorway pointing into the room where you play, recording like this will capture more of the “room” and let you hear how others may hear you. Mic type and mic position can have a huge influence on the over all sound and not much of it good, so the best compromise to get an idea of how something actually sounds is to step back and capture as much as possible to find an acceptable realistic balance.

Playing too close to a mic will bias a recording by amplifying certain frequencies/sounds whilst others maybe lacking, thus skewing the sound of final recording. The ears of someone listening to someone else play (these skew what you hear too by proximity of the sound when listening to your self play) pick up an aggregate of all the sounds created, the sound from an instrument moves in different directions out from the instrument, it’s not uniform, so the recording field needs to be big enough to capture it. Then there’s the mic, regardless of type and quality, most have a directional bias which can aggravate proximity issues further.

Standing in front or behind (or positioning the mic so), to the side, up close or at the other end of the room, from a player will all cause the sound the listener hears to be differently weighted with frequencies.

So, to hear yourself as others hear you, step back from the mic and try and capture the overview, the doorway trick is a good one in this regard, as it trumps the directional bias of the mic, lets the sound generated mix in the room, and captures the sound as someone standing in the doorway might hear it.

It’ll sound much more natural and then you can make a better call on which bit’s of your playing you feel you need to work on. Could simply be that the way you’ve recorded yourself has clipped certain sounds boosting others, causing a miss representation.

Re: Thought I was getting better at the whistle but…

Yes, probably the recording, not the whistle - I’d like to add a few thoughts. Have a look at the waveform: when those “pitchy” bits appear, does the waveform hit its upper and lower boundaries (i.e. does it over modulate), so that parts of the wave are cut off? This can cause heavy distortion. Then you’d have to put the mic farer away, or down-regulate it until these over-modulations disappear. Also, most audio software have a normalisation function to, well, normalise extreme peaks. Afterwards re-amplify the track, or, even better, use a compressor on it. This is of course not the “authentic” sound of your whistle any more then (regarding dynamics, at least), but it’s common practise when recording whistles (and many other instruments, including human voice) for obvious reasons.
Recording the whole room instead of only the whistle is also a very good advice.

BTW, I think the Dixon trad is particularly mellow in the higher 8ve; this is a strong evidence that it really is the recording.

Re: Thought I was getting better at the whistle but…

And then there’s questions of the quality of the camera and the skill of the photographer. A couple years ago, a group of us hiked up Beinn Alligin, a mountain in the northwest. Most of us brought our little digital cameras or used our phones, but one guy (an exchange student from the Netherlands) had a one of those fancy digital SLR cameras with more than one big lens. When we took photos, we just pointed our little cameras or phones at whatever mountain we thought was pretty from wherever we were standing and clicked. But when this Dutch guy took pictures, he would often wander off the trail and stand about adjusting his camera. When the photos all appeared online later, the difference between Dutch guy’s photos and everyone else’s was striking. The colours were more vivid, and the landscape and people in it seemed more “real” in a way.

I suspect the same is true of recording music. Someone who is an expert in mics and recording can record an authentic, rich sound that gets close to “real life,” whereas your average recording numpty like me, with rubbish equipment who knows bugger all about it, does it in such a way that distorts the sound quality and fails to capture the essence of what makes it good.

Re: Thought I was getting better at the whistle but…

Listening to yourself play can be a very difficult discipline to achieve. As has been hinted at above, what our brain hears and what actually travels through the air may be very different. The problem is in ‘pushing aside’ (for want of a more appropriate description) the audio-illusion aspect -- i.e. what we think it sounds like -- and really making yourself hear the actual sounds coming into your ears.
Recording yourself helps to achieve this uncomfortably revealing state, and can at first be stultifying; but once you can do it, you almost immediately begin to actually play what you only thought you were playing, and recording becomes less of a horror.

Re: Thought I was getting better at the whistle but…

From my experience, whistles in sessions usually do sound awful.
They tend to sound like they are out of synch with the rest of the
instruments. That’s because many players have excessive and sloppy
ornamentation and they *are* out of synch. But also the way the sound
reverberates might have something to do with it too. Some players
overblow the upper octave, sending it out of tune and making it shriek
extra loudly. In fairness, many fiddlers are also sloppy and out of synch
but mercifully the instrument wants to blend in naturally. Whistlers and
fluters have to work harder on the blending part.
