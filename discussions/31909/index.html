Re: How many notes “per bow”?

Johnny, here’s what I think :

On the ‘Blue Head’ I’d go for D-U-D-U. Lovely melody!

‘Open Fence’ - bar 1, D-U-U D-U-D-U, same with the remaining similar bars. Bar 1, you’ve got a 2-string hop from 1st note to 2nd note, so a direction change is in order here.

For the other similar bars, you could try a :
D-u-D-U D-U-D-U. Note the bowing here - the small ‘u’ is an up-bow, but it’s a ghost note - so, eg at start of bar 2, D-u covers just the G note, sounded on the downbow, but silent on the upbow (just by releasing the pressure).

‘Bull Ring’ - I’d do pretty much the same bowing as for ‘Open Fence’.

Re: How many notes “per bow”?

One thing you might try for practice is to play an entire A or B part using a single bow stroke. Do it slowly and as accurately - especially with the left hand -- as possible. That can do you some good in several ways. One, you’ll tighten up your left-hand fingering so your fingers won’t depend on a bow change to keep the rhythm intact. Two, you’ll start to hear and feel where slurs can work to give you the best sound. And you’ll learn where you have to pay special attention -- in practice -- to smooth out string crossings.

And ultimately it should come down to what’s best for you, without worrying about style. If you feel comfortable (and sound good) with single-note bow changes, lean in that direction. If you’d rather longer bows with multiple notes, try that.

Re: How many notes “per bow”?

@ Gobby O’Gobbo -- I’d be surprised if there is a tune that you *cannot* play in single bow-stroke, given the various ways you have of separating the notes. Of course some of the tunes will not lend themselves (to say the least) to this approach; but it is certainly a useful exercise, in that it makes you divorce your left hand from your right.
The tendency (for me at least) when first learning a tune is to rely too much on change of direction as a way of separating notes. I try mostly to use direction-changing only as a way to cut short a note, or to emphasise the second note, or both; otherwise I try to use my left hand as much as possible, and using continuous bow-strokes is a good way to do it.

Sorry for the delay in responding
Yes, I did mean an entire A part or B part on a single bow stroke. And it has the effect that gam is talking about above.

You learn how to smooth out awkward string crossings, and how to put the rhythm into your noting fingers so that you don’t have to rely on bow changes for rhythm.

It’s not hard to do, but it means slowing your bow stroke waaaaay down, cutting out ornaments (typically) and playing at a decent tempo, but playing softly. Also, it means using the entire bow, end to end.

So it also helps your bowing by making it more relaxed.

PS - slowing the bow down while keeping a tone is something that classical violinists do. I’m not a classical violinist, but I like to go to their forums. I remember a few of them staging a friendly competition to see who could bow a B harmonic on the E string for the longest time. I tried it myself, and though I couldn’t hold it as long as they could, it helped immensely.

Also, keep in mind that this isn’t a competition, even against yourself. If you can get most of the A or B part before having to switch, that’s OK -- it’s still doing you good. But realize that it isn’t that hard to get the whole thing.

↳ Posted by Jim Dorans, JustAFiddler on May 27th, 2013.

Re: How many notes “per bow”?

Jmbu and gam describe a very nice idea. Certainly there are compromises as one creates the patterns you will use when you play a tune, including compromises for practicality (such as running out of bow). But it is not that hard to play an entire A or B part with one stroke. What you will want to focus on is the sound the bow makes -- when you stop and then change direction. Folk fiddle music has a bit of a gritty sound compared with the way most classical music sounds. And part of the difference is the effect made during the bow changes. Control over that sound is very important, and being able to choose when you want to add a bow change can make a very big difference in how you communicate with this music. I think the challenge of playing large sections with one stroke, and then adding the bow changes at the points they make the sound you want is a good idea. And, of course, there will always be compromises for practicality, but never forget the sound you are aiming for.

(And, by the way, the legato bowing techniques used for classical music also have their place in ITM.)

Re: How many notes “per bow”?

Just a final thought:

Don’t worry about being perfect. For a rest, you can even stop the bow if you want, but it’s not that important. For two of the same notes together, you can do as gam says, or you can use a cut to separate the notes. And the point isn’t to get you to use longer bows; it’s to help you feel the subtleties of the bow as you cross strings, and to help your confidence that you can cross strings any time you want. Not that you may want to or have to, because as Jim says, ultimately you go with your own style, which may be short bow strokes -- all this does is increase your options.

And it can help the left hand fingering. Watch how John Carty gets his rhythm mainly with his left hand. Again, not your style, but getting independence between left and right sides helps every style.

Try it for a half A or B part, say four measures, and see what happens. And don’t try too hard to get it to sound perfect; it won’t. But you may find it softening your bow hand or it may help identify where your left hand fingerings could be cleaner.

And what Fiddler3 says is true. If you want, you can then go from there to insert bow changes where you want them, to serve the music rather than the convenience of changing direction just to accommodate a lazy left hand or tricky string crossing.
