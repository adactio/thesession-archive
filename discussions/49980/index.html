Guitar Pedagogy.

I’ve decided that I want to learn a harmony instrument, and my instrument of choice is bouzouki. I don’t have a bouzouki though, so I decided to reconcile with the guitar and commit to learning it to at least a basic level until I get a bouzouki. I’m back on the Online Academy of Irish Music, this time, going through the guitar courses. The beginner course is pretty challenging for me because I only know 2/11 tunes taught, so i’m taking it extra slow.

In the meantime, I’m learning simple 4-chord progressions from my favorite songs, using Youtube tutorials to help with learning chord fingerings, to give me more practice material. One of these songs is in F# Minor. So i’m looking at these tutorials, and I notice something that, to me, is very bizarre. Even though they are teaching the song in F#min pitch, they are teaching it in Emin notation. They are using a capo to transpose, playing the chord shapes used in Emin, using and speaking in Emin notation, but are still playing in the original key of F#min.

Is this common practice in the guitar world, to consider a song by the chord shapes used rather than the [concert] pitch it’s in? Maybe this is just bizarre to me because I play concert pitch instruments, but it gives me anxiety(I say that in good humor). Would an experienced guitar player learning this song understand that it’s not in concert Emin? I mean, what’s even happening here, Moveable-C? I’m looking back on my experiences playing with guitar players and wondering if i’m just imagining having a lot of misunderstandings over key signatures. Whatever the case, I am once again astounded at the complexity of communicating musical ideas.

I would love to hear other’s opinions about this. Also, please feel free to share any other quirky things you can expect from learning guitar family instruments.

Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

This is indeed very common in the guitar world (Here Comes the Sun is played in “D” shapes with a capo on the 7th fret or whatever, Johnny Marr of the Smiths used “open-style” fingerings but with the guitar often tuned up to F# etc), and it’s particularly common for Irish music. On the bouzouki, it sits nicely around the key of D with no capo. You can still play a lot of chords, but it’s far more common to capo up, for example to fret 5 for G, 7 for A etc, because it allows you to allow the inherent droning quality of the GDAD tuning to come out, plus access to full bass lines up the neck. The same is true for DADGAD on guitar.

So, a luxury with these types of instruments is that basic chord shapes can give you a very large number of permutations if you have a capo (or long fingers, allowing you to e.g. barre a C chord shape (on guitar)).

A capo also allows you to play in keys that could be awkward on guitar, with lots of barre chords. Or it allows you to just avoid certain fingerings. Thus, you can easily play in A on a guitar with no capo, but if you feel at home with G fingerings, just pop the capo on 2 and off you go: playing in G but producing A.

Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

Capos are very useful when accompanying singers, who will have different pitches for the same song, and especially if it’s a fingerstyle accompaniment.

But for accompanying tunes I like to leave the capo in the guitar case. It’s very easy to accompany all tunes as if they are in one of D, G, Em or Am, but then all the accompaniments start to sound samey, using the same voicings, just at different pitches. It’s one of the major drawbacks of DADGAD - there are very few with the skill of Pierre Bensusan.
So for tune accompaniment I almost exclusively stay in Standard tuning with no capo.

Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

”Is this common practice in the guitar world, to consider a song by the chord shapes used rather than the [concert] pitch it’s in?”

Yes and no. A song might be played with the capo on whatever fret in order to fit the voice of the day. Then by some beginners because they have to (e.g. they might not know any other chord shapes). And by some advanced players because they want to (e.g. certain fingerings/patterns/runs/etc.).

For tunes, this is less likely. Example: a reel in A (e.g. The Reconciliation) will probably be considered ”A” even if the player has the capo on the 2nd fret for whatever reasons (e.g. more versatile chord fingerings, interesting voicings …).

Among experienced players, this is automatic. You’d think of F major as ”D shapes on the 3rd fret”, A major → G shapes on the 2nd fret, Eb → D shapes on the 1st fret / C shapes on 3rd and so on.

Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

Capos are very common in Irish backing and, for those of us few who engage in it, playing the actual tunes, and in bluegrass, folk, and sometimes in flamenco, but I don’t ever recall seeing one used by jazz guitarists. Personally I play a lot of ‘’D‘’ tunes in C/Capo 2, and almost all of my A tunes in G/Capo 2. This applies to my Irish music tunes as well as my bluegrass and old timey flatpicking tunes.
One thing to take into consideration with a capo is the quality of such. The elastic band types have the advantage that they can be moved very rapidly and thus accomodate key changes but they have a big disadvantage of not gripping all of the strings equally, especially on steel string guitars, leading to some strings buzzing and/or intonation problems. The more robust spring loaded capos give a much more even spread of pressure but need to be physically moved(as opposed to sliding). Be sure to place the capo right on top of the fret not in the middle of frets. This will minimise, even eliminate, any string bending occurring. You can sort of see the capo placement here in one of my clips(C/Capo 3 = Eb): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gVWP6fdtWP8
This page gives a succinct explanation of the various types of capo. https://www.theguitarjournal.com/guitar-capo-guide/
For what it’s worth I use a Kyser Quick Change capo. I think the ‘’Quick Change‘’ is a bit of an exaggeration, but leaving that aside it is of very robust construction and the rubber lasts a long ,long, time. To give you some idea, I purchased a Martin HD 28 in 2003 and part of the ‘’deal‘’ was that the shop would throw in a leather strap and a Kyser Quick Change Capo. The capo lasted about 15 years, the rubber was still in excellent condition but the spring broke. I carry a spare capo, the G7 brand, I believe made in England, and also an equally well constructed capo. Funny thing is that it gives a noticeably different tone to the Kyser, at least to my ears.
And just to clarify, I don’t have any connection of any sort to any of the products I mentioned.

I agree with Christ Stolz, DADGAD guitar is a good place to start. in many ways it’s a poor-man’s bouzouki (GDAE, GDAD, ADAD, etc tunings). to try doubled strings, you can get a DADGAD 12-string guitar. depending how much time you spend on it, eventually you will learn where every chord is on the fretboard, in every key, with every capo position. you can also explore “trick” capos that have cutouts for individual strings. if you see people play guitar/CBOM with two or more capos at the same time, that’s what they have. coming from your other instruments, piano and squeeze box, where each chord has a fixed location and fingering, you will discover that on guitar/cbom fretboard you can have “speed chess” competitions, “given 1 minute, how many Gm chords can you find”, “… with eyes closed?”. then there are “2 finger” chords, “1 finger chords”, “1 note chords”. you will have lots of fun with all this stuff. Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

One of the darker arts of guitar playing is learning how to MUTE strings so that they are not ALL sounding ALL the time. Quite often, when accompanying tunes with a pick, I’m only playing three note chords, but hitting all six strings. Generally, I will only sound the sixth and fifth string together if the interval is at least a fifth.

When starting out on guitar one of the first hurdles is working on the position of your fretting fingers so that all the strings, potentially, sound. Once you’ve mastered that you then have to work on the position of your fretting fingers so that only the strings you want to sound do so.

And for those with sensitive ears, the B string can be problematic, as the third with the G string sounds way to sharp. It varies from guitar to guitar, but I usually have my B and E strings slightly flat of concert (they’re under less tension anyway so have a tendency to play slightly sharper if you hit them hard).

Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

@jerone--

in DADGAD, you don’t “use” all the strings the way you do in standard tuning, because you have doubles or triples of at least one note. DADGAD wiz Dave Marshall explained it to me thus:

1. In DADGAD, you are only really fretting the four middle strings. The low and high D strings are either not played or they’re gonna drone.
2. You only really need two notes to back Irish tunes-- a dominant, and a 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th or 6th. (This is why all you really need is a 3-string zouk)

Yes, there are exceptions. But these are useful basics.

Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

On the subject of DADGAD and suchlike, while variant tunings can be fun, there’s a really good reason to be strongly grounded in standard tuning and drop-D, and that is the drop-2 trick. This is a jazz player’s trick which gives you a lot of cool voicings and inversions for 6th and 7th chords, on the fly. Jerone, you might find this especially interesting since the output of the drop-2 trick is always a four-string closed voicing, which sounds like it’s what you’re looking for. Since you’re a piano player, you can probably work it out from this sketch: pick your chord and inversion, say first inversion of an A7, C#-E-G-A. Move the second note up an octave, getting C#-G-A-E. This can be played on adjacent strings starting on the 6th, 5th, or 4th string, you just need to find the C#. This works, again, for any major or minor 6th or 7th chord, so once you know the fretboard well enough, this will allow you to do a lot of piano-like tricks pretty easily. If you’re in drop-D, which is the most useful tuning for acoustic guitar, it’s probably easiest to root these voicings on the 4th or 5th strings, since the lowered 6th is going to complicate things.

It’s also much easier to play tunes on the guitar in one of the standard tunings - Drop D or Raised E - than in most of the drone-oriented tunings, so that’s another good reason to play there.

But if you do want to play with different tunings, there’s two ways to approximate bouzouki tuning on the guitar. DGDGAE and DAEABE. For the latter, you’d capo at the fifth fret for your base position, giving you GDADEA. The main difference between the two is that the former gives you some added low end (a D below your bouzouki’s bottom G) and the latter gives you some headroom (the high A above your E). The latter can be nice if you’re playing tunes that do a lot of work around the high a. Both of them are good for picking tunes, though I’d call this a bit more advanced technique as you’ll generally be skipping one or the other of the whole-note string pairs, and it takes a bit of experience to make that feel natural.

Or, if you want to get straight on to playing bouzouki, you can do that easily enough by putting four strings on the guitar and tuning to GDAE. How you set this up is your call, but I suspect it’s easiest to leave the lower two courses of the guitar open and bunch the strings up at the treble end. Might also work to center the strings, leaving the first and sixth empty. You might get a bit of rattle from your tuners this way, sadly - some tuners don’t like being left empty. A rubber band will usually help in this case.

Re: Guitar Pedagogy.

Off topic, really, but that’s a cool way of explaining Drop-2 chords from Jon, above.

Strictly speaking, as I understand it, a drop-2 is when you drop the second highest note of a four note closed voicing an octave.
So G-A-C#-E becomes C#-G-A-E as a drop-2. But Jon’s work around keeps the lowest note of the chord the same, which, as an accompanist, you’re generally more interested in (than the highest note). So C#-E-G-A becomes C#-G-A-E (which can be fingered x4525x in standard).

I don’t think the same thing works for drop-3 chords, unfortunately.
A-C#-E-G becomes C#-A-E-G (fingered 9x798x) as a drop-3 (lowering the third highest note an octave), but raising the third note an octave gives A-C#-G-E.

Drop-2/drop-3 does my head in, but once you learn the voicings you can forget about the names!
