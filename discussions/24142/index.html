Re: just temperament: Why does it work?

Just intonation is not a temperament. Rather, it is intonation based on the physics of the overtone series.

Whenever you play a note, not only that note sounds, but a number of others as well. For example, if you play C2, you really are hearing C1 C2 G2 C3 E3 G3 and so on…. C and E together sound in tune when the E you play is in tune with the E in C’s overtone series. This is just intonation. The just major 3rd derived from the overtone series is roughly 386 cents above C, as opposed to the 400 cents of an equal tempered major 3rd. That’s why, if you play fiddle, you can play a major 3rd that sounds a lot better than the one on the piano.

The piano is very specifically detuned into equal temperament with 12 equal semitones per octave as a compromise so that musicians can play in all keys. Other temperaments before this (think Bach’s “Well Tempered Klavier”) used unequal intervals to put some keys closer to just intonation. Others sounded more dissonant. This is the historical reason why some keys were said to different characteristics, such as being warm, happy, or tense.

This is just one example of many of the problems involved when musicians really make an effort to play in tune and make the music sound good. And when the composer decides to modulate from C major to E major then another can of worms is opened …

Re: just temperament: Why does it work?

To expand on Jason’s answer a little - the reason the two systems differ is because the relationships between notes are derived differently. In equal temperament the notes of the scale are derived by subdividing the octave equally into twelve parts.
Since we’re talking about pitches, we’re talking about multiplicative relationships, a series of multiplications that wind up doubling at the twelfth step, thus, the ratio is based on the 12th root of two (the number which, multiplied by itself 12 times, gives 2). Start at A, multiply by 1 plus (12th root of 2) and you get A#, which in this system is identical to Bb. Multiply again, you’re at B, and so on.

The basis for deriving notes in just intonation is by a much “purer” relationship, based on integer ratios. This is the vibrating string example that you’ll see in every text book on music or physics: a string vibrates in “modes” which increase in frequency as the vibrating lengths get shorter. The integer ratios start with 1, then 2/1, 3/2, representing the unison, octave, and fifth, and so forth - if you play this out, you find that it’s possible to derive notes for each of the keys on a piano in this system, starting at any given key - but if you look closely you’ll find that the precise values of pitches you assign to each key will depend on where you start. So if you start at A = 440, you’ll get one set of values, but if you then start from the C that you derive that way, you won’t get A=440! Or in other words, there are 12 ways to tune a piano in just intonation to get A=440, where in equal temperament, there’s only one.

That’s the difference between the two systems.
Now as to why one “works” and the other “doesn’t work”, that’s another issue - the idea is wrong. Both are mathematical abstractions arrived at by idealists, it’s just that one is older than the other. The only pitch relationship that seems to be a primitive is the octave - at least, I don’t know of any system of music that calls two notes “the same” unless they’re in a unison or doubling relation, but there’s no other ratio or note value that has the universality. Beyond that, it’s what your ears grow up hearing. Sorry, but the “just” temperament is a tempered scale just as the equal temperament is.

Re: just temperament: Why does it work?

Just intonation is very specifically not a temperament. Temperament, by definition, involves compromising the ratios present in the overtones series, because just intonation doesn’t work particularly well (or is not terribly consonant) with functional tonality, especially if you want to change keys a lot. Musics that I know of that approximate just intonation, like Hindustani or Turkish music, are monophonic.

Re: just temperament: Why does it work?

By saying “just intonation works” I assume you mean that it sounds “right”. And that is because it does sound right.
When you sound a note (let’s call it the fundamental) a whole series of tones (the harmonic series) is produced, the pitch of each tone being a multiple of the pitch of the fundamental. When you play two notes together (an interval) and the two notes are justly intoned (can I say that?) then the tones in the two harmonic series match up better than if the interval is equally tempered.
I play guitar and, if I use a tuner to tune the instrument, I tune the guitar to concert pitch in equal temperament and then retune by ear to the D string. Certain of my strings are generally flat (B and top E) according to equal temperament but I just can’t stand the equal tempered major third between the G and B strings.

Re: just temperament: Why does it work?

Further to what I was saying earlier on this thread about intonation in string quartets there is a closely similar thing when playing Irish music on the fiddle (or any other music for that matter) - especially solo. If you’re in a major or minor key of A or E , for example, you will naturally play the B on the E-string spot on where the harmonic is - a precise fifth above the open string. If you’re playing in G that particular B won’t sound so good and you’ll automatically flatten it by a few cents so that it resonates with the corresponding harmonic on the open G. This sort of thing happens with various notes all over the fiddle in different keys, which explains why a tune played in one key can sound strangely different when played in a different key.
And I haven’t even got round to thinking about the role of the “trick” c-nats and f-nats in all this, as far as resonances are concerned, although there was a good discussion recently on other aspects of those notes (“space between the notes” #24091)

Re: just temperament: Why does it work?

Fiddles for ITM and classical are tuned the same - an A440 source (tuning fork, electronic, or a reed instrument), and the other strings in perfect 5ths up and down. What I was taking about in my first post was an exceptional requirement of a professional string quartet, especially in a recording studio, for getting the maximum resonances across all four instruments, so that the open C on the two lower instruments would cause a resonance in the open Es of the fiddles. You’d never tune like this in an orchestra or other ensembles because there are too many variables swamping such resonances.
What I described in my second post is really an everyday occurrence among the more experienced fiddlers and violinists across the whole field of Western music in general.
Dropping or raising the pitch of one or more strings by a whole step is a different issue, often done to make some tunes and styles easier to finger and bow, but yet again, resonance is a very important factor.

Re: just temperament: Why does it work?

“I obviously don’t understand the difference between intonation and temperament.”

Summary:
*Temperament* denotes a system whereby the intervals of the scale are adjusted or *tempered* to enable playing in more than one key with all intervals sounding acceptably intune.

*Intonation* refers to the relative tuning (or lack thereof) of the intervals within any scale, tempered or untempered.

*Just intonation* uses intervals derived directly from the harmonic series, so there is no *tempering* involved.
