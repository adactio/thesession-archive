There’re a lot of different ways of building a career that incorporates the creative outlets that make you feel more fully human. One needn’t, in my opinion, have a single end-goal carefully defined, anything short of which would feel inadequate. Rather, one can, when presented with choices, try to move in directions that build toward this sort of goal.

As one Buddhist writer I know put it, “I didn’t know where I wanted to arrive. I just had energy, determination, and a direction.”

To answer Greg’s original question: no, I don’t race out of bed every morning full of enthusiasm for the day ahead. But on pretty much a daily basis, I spontaneously but quite consciously express thanks to the Great Whatever for the opportunity to do teaching, writing, and playing that creates positive energy in the world.

I set that goal at age 19. It took several decades, but, as another teacher put it, “you should start where you are.”

Re: Do you live the fantasy?

I taught music full time for six years, in a little studio adjoined to a music shop in suburban Philadelphia. That was a wonderful job, and I enjoyed nearly every moment of it. I also taught fingerpicking guitar at a community college during that time. The only frustrations I ran across were students who really didn’t want to learn--kids whose parent were forcing them into lessons, or people bored with life looking for some magic bullet to suddenly make them talented and interesting. But for every student like that, there were a dozen who practiced every day between lessons, who asked probing questions, and who put themselves into the music, even as beginners. One of the most rewarding things I’ve ever done is teach someone to play an instrument who’d never played at all before and 6 months later spend an hour just playing music together.

I also ran a music store for a year and a half, while I was in college. I did all the selling, inventory, ordering, record-keeping, and some instrument repairs. Can’t say I was crazy about the business side of it, and it was hard to watch people choose cheap instruments over much better values that weren’t much more money. On the plus side, I got to play tunes with a wide range of people coming into the shop, and we stocked some nice instruments, which were fun to test drive.

I also worked full time in a shop making Flatiron mandolins for better than a year. Learned a lot about making and setting up instruments, and got to meet some great musicians who passed through. But the pay was crap, and OSHA wouldn’t have approved of the poorly venitlated shop jammed full of saws and plunge routers and joiners. The boss wouldn’t even buy dust masks for us--we had to pay for ’em out of our own pocket.

All three of those jobs kept me on my toes--always learning something new--so I enjoyed them. Maybe if was still doing any one of them full time (I still teach music off and on), I’d have burned out long ago, I dunno.

I’ve played music gigs over the years as well, tho always as a sideline, but I was a professional (non-music oriented) entertainer for nearly 10 years. That got old--the daily rehearsals, the self-promotion, travel, lack of control over your working environment, and the sagging feeling that the people who clapped and cheered the loudest were invariably drunk, stoned, or just organically clueless, so you’d wonder why you bothered to work so hard. I was a juggler, and it drove me nuts that people were more appreciative of someone juggling three tennis rackets (with tennis balls wired to the strings, cute, eh?) than they were of a well-choreographed, challenging routine with 3, 5, and 7 balls. Kitsch wins over art every time. Which is why Britney Spears is rich and Tommy Peoples isn’t.

Not to be too cynical, but I wonder how many musicians feel trapped in their careers, surviving on their talent even though they long ago discovered they’d rather _not_ be the center of attention, or that other life experiences (a family, home life, sobriety :o) are passing them by?

Re: Do you live the fantasy?

There’s an old saying about ex-patriate Scots (think of Sean Connery, Rod Stewart et al) that “they would do anything to be in Scotland, apart from go to live there”.

There’s no harm in dreams, I have plenty of my own, but the world is full of people who “would give anything to be (insert your dream here)” EXCEPT for what it takes to achieve that.

Around here we call it Torquay Syndrome. People say “I want to go and live in Torquay (english holiday resort) because it’s such a wonderful place”. But whenever they have been in Torquay they have been on holiday, in summer, with cash to spend and no commitments. Torquay in the winter with no job and no money and a mortgage and a baby on the way and your mother is lying/dying in a hospital 300 miles away is probably not so good.

You choose where to go in life. I found in early years in business that I could be much richer (in money) if I was a complete b*st*rd. I chose to be more kind, and less moneyed. I ended up where I am, with a daily struggle to make ends meet, but a very happy family and friends.

Thinking of what Johnathan said about his dream - it won’t just happen of its own accord. You have to find the village, the pub, the farm, the surgery. You might have to give up other things to have these, such as the ability to visit your family when an elderly parent is taken to hospital with a life-threatening condition, your friends, the culture you were brough up in.

I don’t mean to discourage, but there’s more to moving round the world for a vague fantasised ideal than many people realise until its too late.

LOL -- Poor Dave, he’s so misunderstood. No, DREAMS. It was an island enshrouded by darkness, and in the darkness, all your dreams came true. Not fantasies, not wishes, not daydreams of what you’d like life to be like. Dreams. The things that happen behind your eyes when you’re asleep. You know. Part of Dave’s point was that you, Steve, did what was necessary to make *your* fantasy come true, and that you accept the bitter with the sweet as part of the price of having not a fantasy, but a real life. Not everyone is going to see that bitter does come with sweet so clearly. Often, people are envious of or want a life that they’re not willing to do the work to get. Sometimes it’s just because they’re not sure what it is they have to do, sometimes it’s because they’re afraid of what that life would *actually* be like (rather than what they imagine it would be like, etc. ).

Mark’s point is that if what you enjoy about the music is it’s very casual and convivial nature, you should make sure that turning it into a business and changing the nature of your music isn’t going to kill what you enjoy about it.

Re: Do you live the fantasy?

I’ve lived what you infer as “the fantasy.” I worked for several years as a professional musician, playing usually 5-6 nights a week, on the road for weeks or months, and in several styles and many different places. The travel is exciting at first, but it quickly wears thin, especially if you have a loved one at home. The amount of work involved is immense, and in order to ramp up to a more national or even international level, it increases exponentially. Your sacrifices can include your physical health (playing injuries, no time or place to exercise), your emotional health (no SO, no friends, no routine), your career path (many are called, few are recorded, and fewer make a living), and even your pocketbook. I made a living, but I gradually felt like I had no life. When I got to the point where I didn’t even want to crack my case, I gave it up and went on to get a college degree and post-graduate degree in environmental planning and management.

Now, I have the best of all possible worlds. I have a great job that challenges me, but I have time enough for music. I have a lovely, patient spouse who is a wonderful dancer, a beautiful, spirited, talented child, and a great old home in a friendly, in-town neighborhood. I play with fantastic, friendly musicians who are easy to get along with and very talented, and I have a stable of really nice instruments I could probably not afford if I were a working musician (or at least the kind you don’t want to take on the road).

Are there roads less traveled, paths I never took, even regrets? Sure, who doesn’t have them? I could have continued playing music as my living, but I knew when I gave it up what I wanted and would gain. Each person has to work out these answers for themselves, but I would encourage anyone to shoot for their particular star. You only pass this way once…
