Re: what are the catholic origins of Irish traditional music

We don’t think of the tunes as being connected to religion. Even if a survey found most of the people who play traditional music in Ireland are from a Catholic background, it still wouldn’t make the tunes Catholic. It would be because a lot of Irish people didn’t convert to Protestantism when it emerged in the 16th century, so there are more people there now whose background is Catholic. Catholicism and traditional music is a correlation, but it’s not a consequence.

We might be at cross purposes, though. Do you mean protest songs? These are strongly associated with Republican (mostly Catholic) and Loyalist (mostly Protestant) communities, and you can work out which from the words. There are probably more Republican songs than there are Loyalist ones.

Most of the discussions here are about traditional Irish dance music. Some of the tunes are based on song melodies or have been made into songs, but most aren’t. Even if the tunes were somehow ‘Catholic’ or ‘Protestant’ (which they aren’t), you wouldn’t be able to tell by listening to them because there aren’t any words.

Re: what are the catholic origins of Irish traditional music

The tin whistle is a relatively recent instrument, but then so is most of what passes for Irish traditional music. Given the movements between Britain and Ireland are as old as the hills it’s a bit difficult to insert religion as a key factor (England was Catholic too). Probably you would look to the harp and song as the oldest Gaelic influences, with some influence of pipes as in most European countries (and maybe some influence of some Protestant clergy). Not much evidence of the harp repertoire being very religious though, although there’s a monastic influence in Gaelic modal melody and particularly in the use of just intonation and plagal harmony. Many of the dance types were imported anyway, with influences from Continental Europe, Scotland and England (polkas, reels, hornpipes etc). But as folk music people played on whatever instruments they could get, many of which were cheap imports or ex military. Not sure that answers very much

↳ Posted by Loughcurra on March 7th, 2024.

Re: what are the catholic origins of Irish traditional music

My understanding of this really comes from the BBC’s Folk Hibernia documentary. I honestly haven’t heard anyone else relate ITM to Irish Catholicism, so take this with a grain of salt. Since it was made by the BBC, it is likely an English take on the connection between Catholicism and Irish Music…

Folk Hibernia talks about the establishment of the Irish Republic and that, in seeking to establish an “Irish Identity” different from the English for the Republic, the Irish language as well as folk arts like music and dance were to create a cultural framework. Catholicism had been banned in Ireland for a couple hundred years, so it was a rallying cause for Irish Republicans. Many of them were Irish Catholic and sought to establish Catholicism as the state religion/moral framework of Ireland and engrain it into the new Republic, again to differentiate Ireland from England. The Irish Republic was seen (at least by those outside of it) as fundamentally Catholic in the early days and you can see the evidence of the power of the Catholic Church in Ireland.

As such, Irish Folk Music was seen as a source of pride and nationalism, as was Catholicism. The two became associated because the Irish Republicans tended to be Catholic and tended to take pride in and devote time to Irish folk music and dance. So Irish music came to be associated with Irish Catholicism.

Obviously this was mostly a thing in the 1950s through the early 60s as the popularity of Irish music waned. I think in some older circles there was probably still an association of Irish music and Irish Catholicism and many of the children who grew up in musical households also grew up in Catholic households. This would be much less prevalent after the folk revival in the 70s and the post-Riverdance worldwide explosion in the 90s…so it’s not an association one tends to make as much these days.

Re: what are the catholic origins of Irish traditional music

"Folk/traditional music tends to get linked to nationalism at times, which is a very mixed blessing: on the one hand, it can give it a boost in popularity and support; on the other hand, the music can fall under the influence if not control of politicos…
The music can even be associated with times everyone just wants to forget."

This is precisely what happened to Bulgarian folk/traditional music under the Communist regime.

The government, latching onto folk music as being distinctly and patriotically Bulgarian, pumped resources into supporting folk music.

Some parts were cool, like hiring troupes of folk musicians and dancers to perform all over the country in free exhibitions, and finding the very best folk instrumentalists, singers, and dancers and setting them up at Universities to teach. You could get degrees in these things.

Other parts were strange. They found the best instrumentalists and put them into a State-run “folk orchestra” that played scores arranged by classically-trained Bulgarian composers. The ethos was to “elevate” the traditional music, and some of these arrangements are a far cry from the pure-drop traditional music. (The same thing was happening simultaneously in Ireland with Sean O Riada’s ensemble.)

And yes to your last point, because the State-controlled Bulgarian folk music became associated in some people’s minds with the dark days of the regime.

Re: what are the catholic origins of Irish traditional music

I sympathise with Vincent. I don’t think we have done enough to explain how and why a bunch of long-oppressed peasants from a small island off Europe have managed to create, sustain, assemble and grow what is surely one of the most amazing and complex collections of traditional music in world history.

There have been attempts - O’Neill, Breathnach, Reg Hall etc - that attempt to cover places and periods, but we still don’t have a plausible overall narrative. How did our music survive the years of oppression when people struggled to feed themselves let alone acquire and learn to play instruments?

Which lack fortunately doesn’t stop us enjoying the music and partaking in it at our many levels. Hey, no Irish music and we Irish Flute makers are out of a job! And the same goes for performers, event organisers, teachers, collectors, publishers, session organisers, venues, festivals, instrument makers and repairers, writers, broadcasters, etc, etc. This is not just a collection of tunes any more, it’s an ecosystem.

Ask yourself. Supposing back in 1975, I’d decided to go into making Chinese or Indian flutes, or Latin American Quenas, or flutes from any other folk tradition? In far-flung Australia. Could I have survived? Could I have raised a family, moved down the coast, built a fine house and a fabulous workshop? Would I still be making flutes 49 years later, when I could be sitting back enjoying state funded retirement which became available to me 11 years ago? Would I even be playing Irish music? Would we be playing St Paddy’s Day next week?

Of course! This stuff is special. O’Neill expressed it as “a fascinating hobby”. I’d call it a lifestyle. Perhaps even a compulsion?

I’d love to see one of our universities - Limerick, I’m looking at you for obvious reasons - offer PhD opportunities to further research the history of the music and its participants with a view to pulling together the big picture. And then once we have the big picture, go on to fully populate every segment. This extraordinary cultural movement deserves no less.

Thank you Vincent for reminding us what a special phenomenon engulfs us.
