Re: Learning Accompanying on Guitar?

Just to add a few cents to the really important thing stated by Dave and clarified by llig, learning tunes first is the difficult way to start it that gets you more and more pleasure with time. If you start from the other end (learn chord structures to D, G and A tunes), it will seem easy in the beginning and give you more and more dissatisfaction (pain even) with time, until you discover that eventually you have to learn the tunes anyway.

It would be advisable to find a patient and (at least fairly) competent melody player and work on tunes together, it’s great for motivation, especially when you are a beginner and struggling with technique problems, and you can get a lot of useful information on the way. That also prevents you (hopefully) from “trying out new things” in the session surroundings, thus making your presence there much more welcome.

Another thing is- when e.g. a fiddle player shouts out ‘D!’, the tune may well be in D but the implied harmony does not start on the i chord. The key (or modal centre) generally is where the tune ends up rather than where it starts. In other words if our horse hair & cat gut torturer correctly shouts ouit D, you may find you need to start on e.g. an Em chord (or so-called E5 or E no 3rd dyad)

As you say in your post “ I understand it’s quite a complex subject”.
Also you don’t understand ( ? want to understand ? ) words like “myxolydian”.
Well, buckle your seat belt young person, cause it’s gonna be a bumpy ride with a steep learning curve.
Consider the fiddle player who shouts out “D ” because the tune has been written out with a key signature of two sharps, but it’s really in A because it’s myxolydian, ie it has a flattened 7th, so it uses the same notes as the major key of D, but starting, ie the key note, is A.
So your first chord will probably be an A (major ), and the next one possibly a G ( major ) ( and you’ve got to hear when to change ) and there might be a D ( major ) but maybe not. So shouting “D” has only proved the fiddlers’ ignorance and is no help to you.
Learning, either the tunes themselves, or how to read the tunes with your ear, are the only two ways of getting to a position of any competence. There isn’t a short cut.
Sorry.

“Also, many fiddlers and box players have no clue about chords and keys. They’ll say ”This is in D“ then launch into a tune in G” Bren

Well said. This is something the discerning guitar player learns with experience; Just because the melody player is good at playing tunes doesn’t mean you can depend on them for anything else. You have to lean to depend on your own judgement, listening is the key.

It’s not true that you need to be able to play the tunes to back them. I’d be more disparaging on that point but the f&^% filter seems to be working well today. You need to learn the tune to back not learn the tune to play, two different things. So you need to listen.

Take gam’s advice to begin with:

“Keep it as simple as possible, even if that means playing one chord all night. Concentrate on the rhythm, and don’t ‘try things out’ within earshot of the other players” gam

And slowly build up the tunes you can back. Listening all the time you’ll soon notice that there are indeed patterns to the tunes and once you’ve sussed the key(s)/mode (or your own way of expressing it) a certain tune is being played in, you’ll learn to find your way around it without ever having playing it before simply by paying close attention and listening. Some tunes are more esoteric and need to be specifically learned, most aren’t.

I fundamentally disagree with setting out to “learn” all the tunes. You’ll need to start out learning them (and truly you never stop learning) as individual pieces but the flaw in the “learn all the tunes” first plan is that the tunes all get different treatment by different players and therefore require a different approach from the backer. The biggest mistake a backer can make is to think “I know this one”, you have to listen and back according to whats being played that moment.

Sounds difficult, almost mystical, but it’s not. Just get out playing and in time you’ll learn the flaw in some of the points being made in this thread. Being able to get on a tune that you don’t really “know” is a combination of ability experience and listening. Knowing when not to play is as much a skill as knowing what to play, learning to trust your ear comes from “playing” not listening to the warped or conformistly warped agendas spewing forth.

So get out there KaateeMac, get twanging, get even!

Re: Learning Accompanying on Guitar?

there are chords for each key…it’s just a fact. There’s 7 diatonic triads for every key signature, not just three. If somebody is trying to play D G and A and it is not working for the tune, don’t blame the chords, He’s probably just playing them in the wrong order or changing at the wrong time. Or perhaps the tune only uses two chords or there might be a phrase in the B part that calls for a fourth or fifth chord not used in the A part. Tunes can change keys at different parts too, which the guitar player needs to be aware of.

I was taught that starting Earl’s Chair with a G was a faux pas, as opposed to the more acceptable B minor. But the tonal center of the A part is B, so either chord works, and it is fun to use both at different times when there are no other backers around to clash with, kind of freaks people out who think that there is only one way to skin a cat.
I know a guy who insists that the first chord for the Providence is an Eminor. But even though the first note is an E and that kind of works, I prefer to hang on to the A chord from the end of the preceeding section,
But while the examples people have used are somewhat ambiguous, I agree with llig that calling out that first chord is the best way to get a backer started right in on the next tune.
Capos have a place--I find capos quite useful when backing singers, and playing in keys with lots of flats. I almost never use one for tunes, however. I find that people who use capos a lot to change keys are using the same chord shapes for each key, which can get kind of repetitious. Of course, I know there are many people who find my mostly first position standard tuning chords kind of predictable and repetitious.
The paths to enlightenment are as numerous as those who seek it…

Re: Learning Accompanying on Guitar?

"No one said that it doesn’t matter at all.

But two different professionals said it doesn’t matter so much. There’s a vast difference.

Obviously anytime there’s more than one it starts to matter a whole lot more." green whistler

Got to be careful how you express those ideas though!

Only ever having been semi professional (well I was a pro for two weeks but I don’t reckon thats long enough to count ; ) I wouldn’t want you to take my word for it, but, this is how I’d describe it;

Because the progression isn’t cast in stone you don’t need to play all the possible chords to be playing the right ones. But you do need to be playing a rhythm that complements/works with, the rhythm of the tune. Thats a given.

So yes, the rhythm is fundamentally important in a way that chords are not. As in you’ll get away with a bum chord now and then, but not bum rhythm.

The right chords? Try playing the wrong chord and it soon becomes obvious the right choice of chord is an essential component also.

So I’d say on balance that both are of equal importance when it boils down to it. Despite the opinions of the couple of afore mentioned professionals.

But perhaps, in the context of this thread, it would be better to say: get your basic rhythm sorted before you venture out to join in with others, your choice of the basic chords needs to be reasonably good also, ie you need to hear them as they happen and are called for. Where as your choice of additional and possible chords can develop as you go, because you’ve the basics under control.
