↳ Posted by Tom Stermitz on November 2nd, 2017.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

You will find a need to simplify when you try to play a tune at a very, very, fast speed (even great players will tend to do this a bit when the speed gets out of hand). The other time you might consider simplifying a tune is if a great player finds that it is very hard to execute a particular section, this might be necessary if the tune was developed for an instrument very different than yours (so for fiddle, mandolin, CBOM, banjo players that might be pipes, box, or flute). All of that is different than playing different ornamentation than another player. For example, if Frankie Gavin puts in a roll I might put in a triplet (banjo). In general though, take the time to learn a good version of a tune and don’t simplify it.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

I’d say NO, generally speaking, although it’s OK to make your own arrangements at times but not as a short cut. You might even wish to make things more complicated!

For instance, if you are playing harp, piano, PA etc, you can change the “left hand” accompaniment to suit your own tastes and preferences but it shouldn’t be just to make things easy. Likewise, ornaments, drones, double stringing etc, etc are also a matter of taste and style.

However, altering the actual melody is a “No no” as far as I’m concerned. It should be learned properly, no matter how long it takes.

Of course, many of us find ourselves in sessions where tunes are played very quickly or where we haven’t quite learned them properly. With experience, it’s possible to adapt and either miss out the odd note or lengthen them to keep up and not fall apart in the tune. It can be a semi conscious act at times but it’s not something that beginners will find that easy. Quite often with the mandolin, I might even add extra triplets and so on instead of just playing one long note which might suit the fiddle better.
The latter scenario happens all the time but that’s quite different from deliberately simplifying the tune before hand.

Now having said all that, many tunes have different versions or settings already. Some are easier. You can take your pick but just be sure you are playing the same thing as everyone else.
Also, with many multi part tunes, some players may just play the first two parts and not the variations. Sometimes, this suits a particular set better..often useful when playing for dancing and so on. Again, it’s good to learn the whole tune though even if you don’t play it in its entirety all the time.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

All the things wrong with simplifying a musical phrase for the sake of making it easier…

1. You’re limiting your potential. Chances are, the phrase isn’t impossible, so simplifying it removes a learning opportunity and eliminates any growth. You won’t learn anything new or cultivate any new skills.

2. It’s lazy. Making good music takes the hard work of practice. If your destination is to be a good musician, simplifying the music you’re learning is a shortcut in the wrong direction.

3. Melodies are already simple, in that they are linear, single-note structures. They don’t need to be simplified because they are already inherently simple. Anyone could play it as extravagantly or plainly as they’d like, but the bones will always be the bones and nothing needs to be taken from them.

4. It implies that something is wrong with the music, when that is not the case. The music doesn’t need to change, it’s your understanding of your abilities that needs tweaking.

5. You could mess up the music. It’s not likely but it could happen. If something is so difficult that you feel the need to change it, doing it wrong could not only kill the rhythmic flow but also the melodic flow of the music. If the change doesn’t affect the music all that much then it wasn’t difficult enough to have to simplify in the first place.

6. You’re skipping practice and lowering the standard of the music. Why simplify a hard tune when you can just learn a simpler tune and come back to the hard tune later? Hard tunes aren’t supposed to be simple. Their complexity and musicality is what makes them special.

7. You’re confusing your current abilities with the instrument’s capacity. Is it really “hard on mandolin”? Or is it just hard for you because you haven’t reached the required skill level to play it yet? If it’s not actually hard on mandolin, then you just sold yourself and your instrument short.

If you like a tune enough to learn it, why change anything about it’s most basic structure, it’s melody? It’s already simple, and it can only get so difficult before it stops being actual music and becomes just a string of notes. The only reason to have to change anything would be if it truly is exceeding the limits of your instrument. In that case, find a version that fits for your instrument.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

Perhaps it’s nitpicky, but I need to point out that the title of this thread is not “simplify tunes to make them easier” but rather “change tunes to make them easier.” Two versions, one difficult to play and one easier to play, can be equally complex.

If you’re one of those saying it’s not OK, have you ever noticed that the same tune can have rather different versions on different instruments?

It’s always existed in ITM as far as we know, and it exists because each instrument has certain note-combinations which fall easily under the fingers which may be difficult or impossible on certain other instruments. It’s hard to say just where it being part-and-parcel of the unique style of each instrument leaves off, and where making tunes fall more easily under the fingers begins.

Not to mention that, at least in my opinion and in the opinion of many others I trust and respect, there’s really no such thing as THE tune in ITM. If you don’t believe me look at how many different versions of tunes are here in the TUNES section. There are pipe version which fall easily under the fingers on the chanter, and box versions impossible to play note-for-note on the chanter. Saying it’s not OK for a piper to play a version that’s pipe-friendly is ridiculous and flies in the face of the piping tradition. Is creating a pipe version “changing a tune to make it easier” on the chanter? Of course.

I change nearly every tune I learn to fit my personal way of playing.

BTW it seems to me that the standard session versions of tunes generally feel like all the idiosyncrasies that one might find in versions specific to any one instrument have be ironed out. It’s why I like learning session versions better than quirky one-instrument-specific versions.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

There’s a big difference between an experienced player coming up with a clever variation, and a less experienced player glossing over part of a tune that they find hard! Austin has expressed a desire to become a great player, but great players don’t tend to simplify tunes. Whether it was the composer’s intention or not to make a part of a tune into a learning experience, the player can certainly use it for that purpose.

I still remember when I was first starting out on banjo, I used to freak out when tunes went up to the high B, because the reach seemed awkward and annoying, and would always mess up my timing. I used to try to find ways to play the B in the lower octave. The Musical Priest was my nemesis! (Despite my screen name ;-)) But tunes go up to the B (and higher) a LOT in this music! I soon realized that if I was going to play this music, I had to become friends with the tunes that do things that I found awkward on my instrument…

The good news about playing mandolin is that the vast majority of tunes in this tradition are very playable on mandolin, since it’s tuned the same as the fiddle. So my suggestion will echo a bunch from above. Slow down the tune, find the part that you want to make easier, and then make it easier simply by working on that specific part until you can play it properly slowly, and then start to bring your speed up. No shortcuts. The good news is that once you’ve developed the ability to play the part that you struggle with, that knowledge will likely help you with parts of other tunes too. And eventually, things that seemed awkward at first will become part of your tool kit, and you won’t have compromised to get there.

Difficult tunes are often tunes which I haven’t fully grasped. The melody can be quirky itself, and it can be difficult to play on the instrument I have in my hands (and, indeed on any other instrument close to me). There are several options:
1 Make sure I “get” the melody.
2 Work on whatever passages I find difficult.
3 Try it on another instrument.
4 Leave the tune and play something else.

Whatever you do with a tune, you can never know beforehand how the next person will play it, if your version will match theirs, if you (or they) think it matters, if your version is too simple (or too ornamented). Be ready to adapt to whatever is played by people around you.

Here’s what I do if I’m faced with a part that’s challenging (on the fiddle). I play the offending phrase slowly, with single bows, until it feels “easy” and I actually like the sound I’m getting. And for me, the key is that I’ll be able to play the phrase without my stomach tightening when I get to the tricky part. That may not work for everyone, but it’s a key for me. It means I can relax and play the part well.

In the end, you should become your own best teacher. A good teacher may help you progress more quickly. But in the end, you’ll be your best teacher.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

Note: Teaching yourself doesn’t mean practicing blindly and unguided. Properly teaching yourself means studying, reading, watching videos, attentively listening, pretty much doing all the same things a teacher would tell you to do, but without their guidance. But to understand the fundamentals and basics, a teacher is crucial because they can isolate what’s causing you problems, personally, and give you what you personally need, on the spot, before you develop bad habits.

Also, experienced teachers think differently from beginner musicians attempting to teach themselves. I had several big problems with my technique that I had no idea how to fix after all kinds of experimenting, and my last teacher remedied them immediately. It took a lot of reworking my technique on my end, and without her, I would still be plagued by the issue. And the fix was as simple as me moving my hand position back on the fingerboard several millimeters and raising my wrist about a centimeter. That was it. And I never saw it because I just didn’t think that way. Not only did she fix my posture, but she also gave me several exercises to recover. Exercises that wouldn’t have worked without the change in my technique because the bad technique limited me. Bad technique WILL limit you. It’s not a matter of if, but a matter of when. That’s why good teachers are so important for beginner’s.

Learning Irish music on piano, I had to refer back to piano basics, because the fast and dense melodies required skills outside of my skillset. The music I typically played didn’t require me to move in those ways. I had to recover proper scale fingering technique, and even learn some new techniques, before I could learn tunes fluently and in time. Because I had already been trained in several of the techniques I could just refer back to them. If I hadn’t been, I would’ve had to have found guidance on the skillset because the techniques are too sophisticated for the average person to think of on their own. That’s what teachers, and books, and other resources are for.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

I’d agree with others here: don’t make any changes that alter the melody or you will be out on your own if playing with other players, maybe risking dysharmonic clashes even.
While playing on your own, slow it down until you can get the difficult bits right.
But if you are trying to play in a session where that tune is being played, and you can’t keep up, miss out some notes, but keep up with the rhythm.
BUT then go back and practise more until you CAN get all the notes in at the usual session speed.
Only after you’ve got all the notes in at speed can you start thinking about more complex stuff such as ornamentation: and even then it depends whether you’re going to be playing this piece solo - when you could do your own ornamentation, or with others, when you need to listen and fall in with what others are doing and not try to out-do them!
And yes, as Jerone says, get a teacher or at least an independent critic of your playing: they will hear things that you don’t, however self-critical you think you are, and be able to advise you in the direction of better playing.

Re: Is it ok to change tunes to make them easier?

An important lesson I learned from John Carty -- the notes can be changed, varied, modified, except when it changes the tune. Every tune has a basic set of ideas that defines the tune. These basic things can be really simple, even skeletal. Changing the melody to add variation or to fit an instrument is fine, as long as these core aspects are left intact. The rest is a matter of taste -- although most of the possible variation is simply not in keeping with the tradition.

And so, that leaves two questions: (1) what are these core things in a tune that cannot be changed? This depends on the tune. You can figure it out by listening to various musicians, on various instruments, from various places, and listening for the things that stay the same as each player puts their variations on the tune. And (2) what variations are consistent with the tradition (assuming you want to play traditional music)? The answer is essentially the same -- listen to the good traditional players, and see how they each made the tune their own by adding ornaments or variations.

Another consideration is with whom you intend to play the tune. The amount of variation is limited if you want the tune to sound well when played with others. Not everything needs to be note for note identical, but certainly certain variations will clash when played at the same time as other variations.
