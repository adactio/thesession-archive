Re: New composition

Well, here’s my approach on writing tunes although the tunes and songs I write are not in the trad genre. I practice a bunch of tunes or songs of various types depending upon my mood. In the morning, I’ll wake up to one or more of those tunes running through my head so I’ll go practice that tune some more. Sometimes though, what my mind has ginned up in the course of the four or five hours I sleep in a night, is not a tune I know or have been practicing. Sometimes it’s a derivation of another melody and sometimes (gleefully) - it’s something entirely new. That’s the one I attempt to harness and memorize, document, record, carve in stone or whatever. From that standpoint, I would say to refrain from trying to create something new because that implies that you’re forcing it. Go for a tune that results from an interesting mis-fingering while practicing or some melody you whistle and can’t get out of your head. Those are your inspiration moments. Try using those.

Re: New composition

I see what’s happening. You start getting snippets of things off of songs you know, and all of sudden, you are improvising something else on the spot. It’s a bit of sudden inspiration. But you also have to have the discipline behind it to work and work on it. Sometimes it works, sometimes it’s a wash. I don’t know your entire musical training and background, but I think doing quite a bit more theory study will help with your composing (I think I’ve said this before).

What I’ve done, that has led to successful completion of songs (meaning I’m satisfied that the song is complete and needs no major tweaks, and can be played for other people in public) is, literally, working on the song over and over again. My best song to date, which was played publicly by my daughter at a recital, took me 8 straight hours to completely compose. No stops except for necessities. I basically growled at everyone to leave me alone, barely ate, and stayed up late at night until I was satisfied. I’ll never be able to do that again, nor would I want to. But most of my completed songs have taken me days, weeks or months of work to hash out. I’ve also thrown out (or, more like archived) more songs than I’ve completed.

So, here’s a challenge. Complete at least half a dozen songs (you are mostly the way there already). Play them, look over them, see if there are too many similarities to established songs, and tweak them as needed. Then, record yourself playing them all and play them back to yourself to see if you really like them. Do they work for the established IT style? Do they go anywhere musically? Do they move you? Those are things you want to ask yourself. And feel free to ask technical questions as needed.

Re: New composition

I’d echo what Ailin says, and suggest a look at some other notable composers. There’s a really excellent collection of Ed Reavy’s tunes, Charlie Lennon’s book (and the double CD he put out a few years back: https://thesession.org/recordings/2652) are worth a look. I know Ailin’s point is to direct you towards a flute/whistle tradition, which is important and definitely a good place to start, but my point is that there’s more than one tradition at play here.

That sounds an odd thing to say. I should probably explain.

When you’re starting out, learning tunes, practising technique, putting in the hours (exactly as you and every other decent player has had to do), it’s absolutely normal for your brain to try and assimilate and extrapolate the information you’re flooding it with. Why? Because, in effect, you’re learning a language. One without words, but still with all the subtlety and complexity of any other language. So far, you know all of the phrases in 17 tunes, absolutely inside-out, and because you’re also listening intently, and you’re clearly bright and intuitive and creative enough to have an impulse to do so, your memory has started to ‘acquire’ the phrases from other tunes without being able to play them yet. This is all really great, and suggests that you have what it takes to be an accomplished player. I’ve been through something similar, so I know how this feels.

Of course, at no stage does this mean that you shouldn’t follow the urge to improvise, or to explore the shapes that the music makes. Good composition can come out of these things, eventually, even if we aren’t quite there yet.

Let’s compare it with a language student for a second, and let’s say they’ve learnt enough French to get by in a conversation in 17 different scenarios. They’ve done their study, learnt their phrases and worked on the pronunciation. Does this mean they’re ready to up-sticks and move to Paris? Nope. At the moment, they’re still listening to the French that they don’t yet know and trying to work out what it means, and if they’re bright and intuitive and have an enquiring mind, they might find themselves trying to work out how to say the next thing - but that’s where things fall down. It’s possible to try a phrase in French, but find that because the grammar isn’t quite right, the intended meaning is lost. Worse, it’s possible to say things that “sound” French, but aren’t even real words. We don’t want that.

I suppose part of it is context. There’s a huge amount left to learn, and not just tunes. What about regional styles? What makes a Galway tune different from a Clare tune when they’re neighbouring counties? What about the rich Leitrim-Roscommon tradition of great flute and whistle players? Donegal and Sligo for fiddle, sure, but there are strong flute traditions there, too. Why are tunes from Kerry so odd? What about Sliabh Luachra? Can you play a ‘box’ tune on a flute? If not why not? What about pipe tunes? What are the strategies for getting around the notes you can’t play? These are all things that you should become aware of - in time. Your ear will develop to recognise the different ‘accents’ that are available to you, too. After all, Marseille French is very different from Parisian French, is different from La Rochelle, and Brittany/Normandy is just WEIRD. Until your ear becomes sufficiently adept at prising them apart, that is. Then you can slip from one accent to another - and it’s the same with ITM. Adopting different a ‘character’ to your playing allows you to communicate with (i.e. play with) a far wider collection of people.

After that, there’s individual style to consider - all great practitioners of the music develop an individual style, but can you tell your Christie Barry from your Mary Bergin? Almost every musician goes through a necessary phase of ‘imitation’ on the way to developing their own style. One of the drawbacks of learning by ear, I suppose. You can’t help but pick up the accent of the person you’re learning from - I know I used to do that. A lot. Of course, out of that develops your own taste - for ornamentation, for variation, for particular ways of expressing the music, even down to which version of a tune you might play - and in time, a sense of your own style will develop. Not that you’ll play like that forever. Recording yourself is a great way to measure your progress, particularly if you don’t come across the recording for a few years… (showing my age a bit now). I’ll also do a lot to show you the difference between what’s going on in your head and what’s actually in the sound - my own playing rarely lives up to what’s going on between my ears. That’s the same as recording your own voice. “Is that really how I sound? I don’t think I sound like that.” Good practice.

It isn’t just about learning more phrases, (more vocabulary if you will), because a language isn’t just about the words. There’s the order of the phrases, the internal conversation that lurks inside every tune if you look hard enough. There’s idiom - the particular stylistic quirks that mark out a regional style, or a particular player, or even the composer (hence why I suggest Lennon and Reavy - two of the most easily ‘spotted’ composers around). There are the limits of the instrument that your playing, and (at any one time) limits to your ability, which will obviously change as your technique inevitably improves and your musical understanding grows. As I say, by the sounds of it you have the makings of a fine musician, possibly a fine composer too.

But… right now, your own musical self isn’t yet present. You have the kernels of some good ideas, and your tunes reflect this, but what you’re doing isn’t yet composing. It’s trying to understand the logic of something, to unpick its workings without having allowed them to become part of you first. It’s also testing the limits of what makes the music, and your understanding of it. Which is a really good sign. What I would encourage you to do, when you’re working on a phrase and it turns into ‘something else’, instead of making a whole new tune around that misplaced note or twisted phrase, try and turn it into a variation for now. Try and work it into the tune you’re playing, see if it fits, see if it still makes sense and doesn’t clash with the original tune. If it does, great. You gained something. If it doesn’t, don’t be disappointed. It’s just as important to learn what doesn’t work, so you’ll have gained something there, too. Creatively speaking, variation is probably more valuable than composition, as a skill in its own right, as it’s integral to playing with other people. A good player will almost never play a tune the same way twice, there will always be something new and unexpected, but it will still be the same tune. On the other hand, a less accomplished player might try a variation and turn the tune into something resembling jazz, which is most definitely not the idea. Not that I’ve got anything against jazz, but it doesn’t go down well in sessions, most of the time…

Anyway, crack on. Don’t be discouraged, but also, don’t be hasty. What you’re doing is ultimately going to be beneficial, one way or the other. As far as tune composition goes, the best advice I’ve got is to stick at it, but not to think that something that’s leapt into your head while playing something else is the finished article, not right away. Write it down or record it. Give it a few months. See if it still stands up. If it comes easily back to mind, if it still flows and feels ‘natural’ to you, it probably has something in it, if it doesn’t (or if you suddenly start to recognise different bits and pieces of other tunes mixed up in there) then don’t be afraid to discard it. There’s plenty of time.

Re: New composition

Further to my last post, I’d like to say that much of the joy in traditional music(Irish, Scottish, or whatever) for me is playing with others who love it as much and sometimes even more than myself. If you are relying mainly on your own compositions for your repertoire, you may find that you have very few people who are are either able or willing to join you in this process. Of course, you may prefer to perform solo or form your own band. That’s up to you.

Of course, when you are a respected composer and perfomer, other players will want to play your tunes but…usually… not until then.

I’m not suggesting that it’s necessary to learn every “bog standard” tune but it’s good to concentrate on those which are played at your local session or in the area. When you’ve been playing for many years(usually), having a large repertoire of traditional tunes is extremely useful as you can go into most sessions anywhere and be able to play for much(Though not necessarily all) of the night. Not so easy if your repertoire consists mainly of original compositions.

Thank you, Kellie. I’m not presuming that I know more about composition than you or anyone else on this site
who is pursuing composition. My intention was never to show (you) how to compose.
Much less begin posting compositions myself.

My intention is, and always has been, to encourage your success in becoming a well-rounded musician.
From my perspective I’m trying to be a good mentor by pointing out what I consider minor distractions you
may be having in the process of learning the tunes. Yes, you can apply those *accidents* to composition.
That is part of the tradition. At the same time I hope to encourage you to focus on your intention.
When your intention is to learn a particular tune; learn that tune. When your intention is to compose,
stay with it for *as long as it takes*.

Sorry for ranting,
