Re: How much would you pay?

The Stradivari in the Ashmolean is the one known as the “Messiah”, reputed to be the most perfect ever made - and now no longer ever to be played again. I don’t know who makes these decisions (there must be big money behind it), but don’t the fools* realise that the tone of a violin will deteriorate if it’s never played? Which is why that museum in Cremona makes sure its collection is played every day.

The major music colleges, and some private collectors and consortia, own a large number of fine instruments which are loaned out to the best students for a few years to help them with their careers, the instruments then being returned at the end of the period of loan to be passed on to the next generation. I don not understand why the “Messiah” could not be loaned to a top player for a few months every year, even if only for the recording studio.

It’s understandable that the very old instruments (usually pre-Stradivari) don’t get played much because they are now rather fragile and wouldn’t be suitable for the modern concert hall, but some playing of music of the period on those instruments would be beneficial to all concerned.

Although many famous paintings and other objets d’art are hoarded out of sight by collectors (as financial investments usually) there are others that must be carefully stored just in order to preserve them, but, as Ptarmi pointed out, very good copies can be looked at.

*fools and big money often go together, as we know from recent events.

In respect of Stradivari and his peers in that area it’s also worth pointing out (as a Member has mentioned in a private email to me) that Stradivari’s prices would have been high - perhaps too high for some musicians without financial assistance from a sponsor - but his paying customers in that prosperous area were rich merchants and the like, so his prices would have matched their ability to pay. From http://www.jose-sanchez-penzo.net/strad2k.html here is the entry about the “Messiah” Stradivarius, the one kept in the Ashmolean:

"Owners/players/dealers in chronological order:
Stradivari, Francesco and Paolo
Salabue, Count Cozio di (1775)
Tarisio, Luigi (1827)
Vuillaume
Alard, Delphin (1875)
Mr. Crawford, collector (~1894)
Hill & Sons - London (1931)
Ashmolean Museum Oxford (1938)
Remarks:
Stradivari’s favourite instrument, he never parted with, was also called Salabue. Luigi Tarisio, a famous collector purchased and kept it hidden from the world until his death. As people heard from this hidden instrument, they doubted its existence. One day Delphin Alard expressed his opinion that this instrument was like the Messiah, from wich you heard about but which can not be seen. Alard’s remark gave the instrument his name. Later the instrument was acquired by Vuillaume, who retained it until his death, passing it to Alard, who was his son-in-law. Hill & Sons presented it to the Ashmolean Museum Oxford, where it has to be kept for ever in a special prepared room."

Vuillaume was a famous French luthier whose instruments are now very much sought after.

Re: How much would you pay?

Another thought occurs. If the “Messiah” is still substantially as Stradivari made it, that implies it is one of those rare original baroque instruments that hasn’t been altered over the years to make it suitable for the modern concert hall. Even after being played in, it wouldn’t sound as we would expect a Strad to sound today; the sound would be distinctly less powerful and a little thinner, but with more articulation. Virtually all instruments from that period in concert use today and playing modern music will have been substantially altered - a longer bass bar, steeper neck angle, a longer, narrower fingerboard, a thinner and more arched bridge, a different sound post, a modern tail piece, and high tension strings. These changes together enable the instrument, in the right hands, to project over a full symphony orchestra to the furthest reaches of a 2000-seat concert hall. Few, if any, of these alterations to the “Messiah” would be permitted by an insurance company.

In 1993, the cellist Yo-Yo Ma was invited to make a CD of baroque cello music with Ton Koopman’s Amsterdam Baroque Orchestra. The cello he had at the time was the very powerful “Davidoff” Strad of 1712 that had once belonged to Jacqueline Du Pré. The problem was to get it to match with the baroque sound of Koopman’s ensemble. To do this, they removed the end-pin (not used in baroque cellos), so reducing the amplification provided by the stage flooring; then a thicker baroque bridge was provided, which acted in some degree like a mute. Then the steel strings were changed to the lower tension gut, and the tailpiece from the usual metal micrometer tuner assembly to one made of wood. Alterations to the internal bass-bar, the neck and the fingerboard wouldn’t have been allowed. Finally, a baroque bow was used. All this enabled Ma to get quite close to the baroque sound, especially when he found he also had to learn baroque intonation, which meant he had to play some notes at slightly different pitches to the equal temperament intonation he was used to - just like in Irish fiddle music.

I’m intrigued by the thought of changing the bass bar in an instrument, because this surely requires dissambling it to a major degree. Re-angling the neck was, of, course, done to a lot of instruments in the past, but wouldn’t actually change the whole acoustic pattern of an instrument in the same way as actual dissassembly of something that has been functioning ( or perhaps not, in this case ) as a viable and fine instrument for a long time.
Changing/replacing the soundpost, though, is a normal part of maintenance by a luthier.
And as to building a new one of this Strad, I have to say that people have been trying this for generations. Well, strads generally. There are so many intangibles in the man’s work that people still don’t know precisely what he did that made them so good. There are stories about the wood being boiled in sea-water, as a guard against the local virulent strain of woodworm; that the varnish is full of volcanic dust from a recent eruption of the local volcano, etc., etc., .
