Etiquette about instruments that you know are perfectly fine, but your buyer insists is defective

I’ve run into an interesting problem and wonder how others have dealt with this. When I sell an instrument, whether on eBay or otherwise, I know it’s not defective, whether it’s whistles or recorders, and I’ve only run into one or two in my lifetime like that. Usually what I sell is something that is uncomfortable for me to play, or I’ve bought something that I like better. But it works as it is supposed to. So what should one do, or how should one approach, a situation where a person who buys your instrument simply can’t play it, yet insists that it’s the instrument, and you have a general no-return policy? I suspect I may have to suck this up, but when I get the instrument back, I’m tempted to make a recording of me playing it, and send it the the buyer to show them that, indeed, it is playable. But I can get petty that way with people who seem clueless.

Re: Etiquette about instruments that you know are perfectly fine, but your buyer insists is defective

The rules are very differnet depending on whether you are selling the item or auctioning it. (on ebay that’s the difference between listing it for auction and putting a ‘buy it now’ price. Ebay don’t make it at all clear to sellers, but the legal position is very different in those two situations.)

If the item is up for sale at auction then it is ‘buyer beware’ - if it’s not what the buyer wanted that’s tough. The only time they can return it is if it is broken (i.e. not working at all) and you haven’t said so in the listing, or if you have described it wrong and it isn’t what you said you were selling. If it makes a sound but doesn’t sound as nice or play as easily as they thought it would that’s just tough. That’s why the prices on Ebay are so low.

But if you’ve put a ‘buy it now’ price on it then you are selling it retail, and all the usual distance selling buyers rights come in to play. Basically the buyer has a right to return it, for whatever reason they like, and any returns policy you think you have is over-ridden by their rights as a buyer.

Re: Etiquette about instruments that you know are perfectly fine, but your buyer insists is defective

As a Top Rated Plus “Power Seller” with more than 4,000 transactions on eBay, my seller account is configured to send a refund automatically for a defective or damaged item. In addition, any request to return a damaged or defective item is also pre-approved. Incidentally, around 20% of our sales are global. So far, out of 4,010 transactions we have totalled 2 refunds, and 12 returns.

eBay indicates that our return rate over the past 18 months is 1.12% (9 out of 802 transactions). That means we see around 1 return out of 100 transactions. We can always improve, but in my opinion with only 1 return out of 100, we are doing something right.

Usually, when a customer complains, it tells you there is something wrong with your product description. So our real challenge is to create a product description that eliminates the surprise when you open the box.

That’s why we love customer complaints. And we don’t get near enough of them. Why do I love complaints? Because I would have to pay a marketing company thousands of dollars to get the same feedback. Every complaint is a golden nugget of marketing information you can use to improve your business. So whenever we get a complaint, we take a closer look at our product description and see how we can fine tune it. As we improve our product descriptions, we get less and less complaints - and also less returns.

So if a buyer complains that your item is defective, take a deep breath, try to understand their problem - then fine tune your product description to address that specific issue - and even turn a potential liability into an asset. For example: “Players with large hands might find the fingering hole spacing a bit too ”tight“ for hot driving reels. But if you are an experienced session player with medium or smaller hands, then you will love the spacing…especially for tempos at lightening speed.” (I know absolutely nothing about whistles or recorders and made this up, but you get the idea.)

So if you want to reduce complaints and returns because of “defective” items, then create a product description that eliminates the surprise when a customer opens the box.

Best of luck with your listings!

Re: Etiquette about instruments that you know are perfectly fine, but your buyer insists is defective

I had a situation recently, I was selling an instrument that I haven’t played for a number of years. I listed it as honestly as one could including clear pictures of the serial numbers, the dinks and defects and I priced it to sell, very reasonably indeed.

I had a guy call me and ask if I would take even less than I was asking, AND, would I drop it round to his house! I said ok but absolutely no arguing or haggling or quibble-arsing about when I get there. He says, oh just one more thing, what condition is this bit in, what about these bits…. etc. So he had me taking close ups of all these different bits of the instrument and emailing them to him. In the end he says right he’ll buy it - no arguments.

I drop it round. What does he do? Spends about 40 minutes piddling about, umming and ahhing, turning his nose up at this little thing and that, putting it back in the case, looking at said case, taking it out again. I was about to say righto sunshine, enough of this, I’ve more pressing things to do than spend the afternoon pretending to admire the decor in your kitchen. But then he says “ok fine” and pulls out the wooblers. We shook on it, I said I hope you enjoy it, he says yeh yeh it’s great, and he’s all full of thank yous and best wishes as I’m leaving.

Week later…… phone call. He’s had an “expert” look at the guitar. It’s “not what I advertised it as”. I’m “guilty of fraud.” This is wrong, that’s wrong, yada yada yada. Wants his money back or he’s going to complain to the auction site and/or the police.

I assured him that I advertised the instrument honestly, in good faith, and that I’d never had any reason to believe there was anything untoward with the instrument whatsoever.

The fella wasn’t listening, there was nothing wrong with the instrument and very well he knew it too! He just decided after a week that he didn’t really want it.

It’s more headache than it’s worth sometimes trying to deal fairly with people.
