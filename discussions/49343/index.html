Re: 3/4 = 6/8?

3/4 and 6/8 represent different intents. All music is based on a repeating ‘beat’ or ‘pulse’, which is the click of a metronome or mechanical clock.

In music we group beats together, and the different time signatures tell you how:

- 3/4 represents music in which beats are grouped into 3, which if you were to step in time with a beat counting it ‘1,2,3, 1,2,3 …’, means that the foot that lands on ‘1’ alternates between left and right.

- 6/8 is used to represent music in groups of TWO beats, where each beat is a triplet of 3 eighth notes. i.e. a jig is essentially in 2/4 time, where each quarter note beat is a triplet of 3 8th notes. (This convention is called compound time, and is used because people don’t want to include a ‘dotted value’ in time signatures for some reason).

This kind of stuff becomes more clear when one learns how to dance. If one were to dance a waltz or mazurka, one considers one step per beat. While doing a ceilidh dance to a jig, each step is on each group of 3 8th notes.

Also the language musicians use to describe this is logically inconsistent and confusing. Some use the term ‘pulse’ to name what you tap your foot to, and ‘beat’ to refer to arbitrary subdivisions named in time signatures. Using that you could say that 6/8 has two pulses per bar and 6 beats per bar, but still it isn’t congruent as the word ‘beats’ in ‘beats per minute’ as musicians typically use that phrase, actually refers to the ‘pulse’ in almost all cases.

I.e. jigs (6/8 time) are usually played at something like ‘dotted quarter = 120 bpm’.

If you instead considered jigs as 6 8th note ‘beats’ then we would be typically playing them at 360 BPM which seems rather ridiculous, and considering it like that the note emphasis will tend to end up in the wrong places also.

Different types of tune tend to emphasize different beats, for instance in a 3 time bourree, the emphasis is on both the 1 and 3, jigs either emphasize beat 1, beat 2, or beat 3 (how jigs are written tends to vary exactly what beat is emphasized, swapping in and out of syncopations)

Re: 3/4 = 6/8?

I see a lot of 6/4 used when it should be 3/2, especially when there is an extended bar in a 4/4 piece (as in 4/4 + 2/4 = 6/4, except, in terms of time signatures, it doesn’t).

The convention seems to be that if the first number is divisible by 3 then the notes are grouped in threes, so 6/8, 9/8, 12/8, 6/4, 12/16, etc., are all played in groups of three (of whatever the second number is). So 6/8 is two groups of three quavers/eighths.
That convention also applies to 3/4, which one group of three crotchets/quarters.

Re: 3/4 = 6/8?

Technically, 3/4 is simple triple meter: simple becuase beats are dvided in halves (and quarters and….) and triple because there are three beats to the measure. 6/8 is compound duple meter, compound because beats are divided in thirds (and sixths and…) and duple becuase there are two beats to the measure. Non-technically, these characteristics make for very different dance types and differnet affects and evocations.

Re: 3/4 = 6/8?

music is not maths. 6/8 and 3/4 both have six quavers (1/8 notes) in a bar. 3/4 has three beats, each beat being divided into two 1/8 notes. 6/8 has two beats, each divided into three 1/8 notes. 3 x 2 and 2 x 3 both make 12 in maths, but two beats in a bar and three beats in a bar are not the same thing in music.

Re: 3/4 = 6/8?

I honestly believe that the problems people encounter with learning music theory are mostly because it is often taught very poorly, that many of the accepted ‘standard’ ways of teaching it, and even the way things are named, have no logical consistency.

For example, the standard way of teaching rhythms by ‘counting’ uses ‘1,2,3’ for 3/4 and ‘1,2,3 - 4,5,6’ for 6/8. This makes them look the same, when as discussed in the above thread, they are not. Personally I prefer using ‘1,e,a - 2,e,a’ for 6/8 as it better indicates the relationship to 3/4.

Many people’s introduction to theory is a well-meaning musician friend who has little experience with teaching, and this can lead to things getting explained poorly, or incompletely. Music theory is a lot harder to teach well than one may expect, or put another way, there are a huge number of ways that it can be taught poorly.

‘Techically’ Time Signatures ARE NOT FRACTIONS… they just look like one.
1.) The bottom number tells the size of the note that gets a beat.
2.) The top number tells how many beats to a measure (why I say beat & pulse really aren’t the same).

I find the following to be a better way of thinking about time signatures:

- The top number tells you the type of *note symbol*.
- The bottom number tells you the number of this symbol, or other symbols of equivalent duration, that will appear in a bar.
- How these symbols relate to the ‘pulse’ / ‘beat’ of music in actual performance is not told to you by the time signature at all, and is instead a matter of convention (it is somewhat indicated by note beaming in many cases).

To someone wishing to perform some music, generally the only thing that anyone actually cares about is the PULSE, and getting that in the right place for your dancers or listeners to follow. Within a jig the individual notes within each triplet often do not have the same time duration, as the first is often a little longer to clearly communicate the ‘drive’ in the music.

Time signatures also become even less useful in something like 5/4 or 7/8. The first represents music that is organized into an irregular grouping of 2 and 3 beats, such as the 5 time whaltz often considered as a group of 3 followed by a group of 2.

Things like 7/8 by comparison are used to represent music with an irregular underlying pulse.

In both cases the time signature does not tell you this at all, it needs to be worked out from beaming, listening to a performance etc.

If the music community at large could agree on a notation convention that eliminates the logical inconsistencies raised in this discussion thread, or else agrees to always clearly teach to learners that they are irregular, music theory would be a lot less of a ‘dark art’.

Regarding Irish traditional music, I don’t think that it is even possible or desirable to notate it with 100% accuracy, and even a single recording of a performance can not do so. The music is not ‘fixed’, different players interpret the same tune in different ways, changing how they articulate and ornament the music, introduce variations and even reorder the parts of a tune or add entirely new ones.

The playing style of irish music is really a ‘possibility space’, it can only really be described by a large corpus of numerous examples of the same tune, and a lot of practical experience playing in sessions.
