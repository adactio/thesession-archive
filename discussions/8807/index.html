The “right” to start your own tune.

Penfold posed an interesting question in the other thread which I thought was worth investigating further. When and where do you have “the right” to start off a set of tunes?
You can wait until you’re asked, of course, especially if you’re a visitor(you should). In other cases, I believe it’s a question of “going with the flow” and starting something off as and when you feel it’s appropriate. Good manners and common sense should prevail.
However, I’ve witnessed all sorts of scenarios where people(often they are very twitchy and frustrated) dive in with tunes and attempt to dictate or change the course of the proceedings. On other occasions, it’s not uncommon for musicians to “set up” at the next table or elsewhere in the bar and start their own sets whenever there is a lull. Even worse, sometimes when the original session participants are still playing!! The latter, of course, is really bad manners but visiting musicians should really respect what was happening there before their arrival and not even think of starting an alternative or secondary session, in any case. They should politely request to join the existing session and “fit in” there. If they don’t know the repertoire or it doesn’t suit them, then tough. The others were there first.

No doubt, you have all experienced this sort of thing. Any thoughts?

Re: The “right” to start your own tune.

At a fleadh anyone has the ‘right’ to start a tune but at festivals or established sessions you should probably suss out what the form is first.

Leppin into a paid gig at a festival could upset the paid musos and the festival goers - worth asking first.

As for an established session you need to suss out the repertoire. If you start a tune that is not known by the guys and gals you might find that you are seen as being pretentious or overbearing. I will generally ask if it’s OK to sit down and then listen a while, joinging in where I am confident of the tune. If you are good enough you’ll usually be invited to start a set and that is your ‘in’. After that it probably makes sense to check that the rest of the sess knows your set before kicking off.

If there is a couple of ye - lead and accompanying instruments then it’s probably OK to do a couple of your own sets - but only after showing due respect to the session first.

Having said all that - there are no hard and fast rules and not every session is as open as the one’s I generally attend.

Re: The “right” to start your own tune.

There’s a rhythm to how any session “works”, if it works at all. Some sessions like to have a fair gap between sets every now and again for people to talk and drink, others rapid-fire sets at some times and others not, some like people to start whatever they want whenever they want, others prefer people to suggest tunes to the group…it’s all good so long as it works for the majority of the session inhabitants.

Here’s a few more ways to see if you’ve (speaking to some mythical reader here, not anyone in particular) got the hang of this particular session’s rhythms correctly:

Watch the session leaders (or the people who are the de facto session leaders if there are no formal ones). If they look startled or interrupted in *any* way when people start up tunes, it’s most probably a “talk between sets session” or they prefer to start the sets or otherwise keep a bit of control over it. (And there may in fact be very good reasons for this. Or not. But that’s not the point.)

Listen to (and watch) what’s going on and who is calling the changes of tunes to whom. Is someone calling on various people to start tunes? (“What have you got on that thing, Paddy?”) Most transgressors of a session’s established rhythm simply aren’t watching what’s going on around them.

Try asking someone who is an obvious accepted regular (or the session leader) to tell you about how their session works.

Also, I shouldn’t worry TOO much about it. Everyone’s been a beginner at one point or another. Everyone at least understands (even if they might not have patience for it) that desperate impulse to want to play the few tunes that one does in fact know.

Oh -- and one thing that should be pointed out -- if you’ve arrived AFTER everyone’s been playing for a while, and you start up a tune and no one plays with you majestically moving through the thing all on your lonely, it may not be that no one knows the tune -- it may be that they’ve played it already, and you should have checked as to whether it in fact has already been played. :)

Re: The “right” to start your own tune.

There was a time where I would start tunes inappropriately, but it was unintentional and I did it out of ignorance. I was driven to because my repertoire was teeny and it insured that I would get to play if I started the tune. If I didn’t know the tunes being played I would wait for the first available opening and try to instigate the tunes I knew. If I wasn’t alone in using this approach it would result in a non-stop, monotonous diddly-diathon. This approach didn’t win me many friends -- only co-dependants.

The next phase I went through, after realizing that there was a problem with my approach, was to ask other people, “Do you know this one?” and I’d start the tune… but stop if no one knew it and joined me. This worked better, but I would abuse it by employing the tactic like a chain smoker. I soon realized that I was making the same mistake, but only with a little more sophistication.

Now-a-days I enjoy a relaxed session where there’s plenty of time between tunes for the air to clear and people to say hello and enjoy each other’s company a bit. I will often ask people if they know tunes before playing them, or use the snippet method, or I’ll just start tunes when it seems appropriate if that’s the MO of the session, but it’s for the right reason now… I think. I will either wait to be asked, or if I’m one of the anchors, I’ll be sure to leave room or invite others to suggest tunes before initiating my own. Because of my preference for a relaxed session I do find it irritating when my former self visits the session from time to time… but I try to be as tolerant and polite as possible the same way people were to me.

Not that my collection of tunes is that big really, but I think one of the values of having a larger repertoire is that you can relax more and enjoy the session because the odds are good that you’ll know the tunes being played and there’s no need to worry about instigating the few tunes you know. I suppose it’s a sort of pay-off for putting in the time, year after year, to build up a collection. I do enjoy a session that has a designated driver because I think it’s really nice to just be a passenger on the bus and enjoy the ride, but whether I’m the driver or a passenger it’s great to have a sense that there’s no urgency to start tunes. I only wish I could have adapted this attitude back in the days when I didn’t know that many tunes. But as they say – hindsight is 20/20.

Re: The “right” to start your own tune.

It’s a conversation between players, and if the better players don’t have their heads up their arses, they’ll let the less-experienced players play their tunes. And if there’s a lull, and you want to change the subject (e.g., play slip jigs after a full round of reels), then ask first, or wait a moment and play a few notes. Don’t just jump into things blindly, don’t shout, but don’t just sit there like a lump on a log. Speak up, or forever hold your piece (pun intended).

Re: The “right” to start your own tune.

High-strung, I might be wrong, but it sounds like you’re suggesting that when “less-experienced players” show up at a session, the “better players” should default to the “less-experienced players” and play their tunes… and if they don’t it means they “have their heads up their arses.” This reverses everything I’ve learned about good session etiquette. I’ve always been under the impression that when the less-experienced players show up and expect everyone to play their tunes -- they would be the ones who are considered to have their head where the sun doesn’t shine.

I think it’s good manners on the part of the “better players” to encourage and invite everyone to share their tunes, but everyone shouldn’t expect the session to cater to their level of playing… especially if they’re a visitor to that session. It would be just as rude if a “better player” showed up and expected to only have only their tunes played at a session that was started by the “less-experienced players.”

I believe, based on my experiences, that if you’re someone who doesn’t know many tunes and you want to be welcomed at a session, you should; a) ask if it’s ok to join in. b) be sure you aren’t taking a regular person’s seat. c) don’t start tunes as soon as there’s a lull. (best to wait until you’re asked) and, d) don’t annoy people by noodling along on tunes you don’t know.

I’ve been at some great sessions where really good (sometimes famous) musicians are the hosts, and watching as a novice, like myself, would show up and ignore one of the points I just mentioned. The folks who were hosting the session were very polite, but you could read it on their faces that they were annoyed. I’ve also seen novices show up who paid close attention to these points and I noticed they would receive far more encouragement from the hosts.

The bottom line is that you might only have the impression that the hosts have their heads up their arse because it smells so bad where you’re coming from. I think the better your attitude is when joining a session -- the sweeter the air will smell.
