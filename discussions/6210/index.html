Re: Pulsing Bow method like Denis Murphy

I haven’t heard the Denis Murphy recording, and Lady on the Island is a notable hiatus in my repertoire (memo - must rectify this anomaly asap), so I won’t comment on the specifics of this tune.
Nevertheless, there is a general point about a “pulsing feel” to the bowing with an accent on the first note of the bar. One common method of doing this is by giving a shade more time to that first note, lengthening it by, say, an eighth of its value, and playing the next note, or two or three, a little quicker so that the overall timing of the entire group remains unaltered. This, I believe, is the basis of the “lift” that is characteristic of so many good trad players. Another way is to make that first note louder without altering its timing, either by applying pressure or speed to the bow. This on its own can sound mechanical unless it is carefully controlled, but it can of course be combined with the method first mentioned.
On a thread here some time ago (which I am unable to locate at the moment), someone analysed the recorded playing of people like Frankie Gavin to show how they get the lift by minutely altering the timing of the notes. It seems that the detail of the altered timing defining the lift is characteristic of each player.

Re: Pulsing Bow method like Denis Murphy

2ndFiddle,
the problem for me is that I’ve never heard Denis Murphy, so I can only guess at the sound you’ve described. Unfortunately, guessing isn’t good enough if specific advice is needed. I can, however, make a suggestion concerning the singing quality of his e-string ornaments, which is: precise intonation, a responsive instrument that is exactly in tune and set up with good resonance. And a light bowing technique that doesn’t stifle the sound. If you have all these factors you can expect the g on the e-string to resonate with the open g, the a above it to resonate with the open a and a harmonic from the open d, the f# to resonate (although not a lot) with another harmonic from the open d, and the b on the e-string should pick up another harmonic resonance from the open g. Today, you’ll find that the Olive e-string has a particularly brilliant singing quality (too much for some fiddles, perhaps), and Denis Murphy might have been using a string like that.
If you very lightly brush an open string you’ll most probably get the first harmonic (the octave) sounding – is this what you mean? Sometimes that’s not what is wanted, but it can be used to advantage. For instance, a classical player doing a very fast scale passage downwards from a very high position through the octave can actually not “play” that octave note but move his finger to the next note below (so saving time). The skipped note will actually sound because it is the first harmonic of the string and is easily set off by light bowing.
Salt,
You haven’t given much background in your bio, but you describe your bowing as “intermediate” – which means a heck of a lot of work still needs to be done! There are limits to the amount of bowing knowledge that can be imparted solely by the written word, although Will Harmon has certainly pushed those limits with his excellent advice. Bowing control is the essence of fiddle technique – as the scrapings by any beginner will testify – and can ultimately only be taught fully by face-to-face teaching, so I suggest you have a handful of lessons (to start with) with the best recommended teacher in your area (he/she may be classical, but that doesn’t matter – bowing control transcends all the musical genres). Don’t expect progress to be rapid, because it is unlikely to be, but with a good teacher you should expect observable progress.
Bowing is difficult, and really takes years to master. Look at almost any fretted instrument player (banjo, mandolin etc) who takes up the fiddle. Left hand fingering isn’t all that much of a problem once they get used to the absence of frets and the fact that they are now personally resonsible for their own intonation (!). They will all testify that bowing is the problem they have to work at. In particular, I’ve noticed the number of ex-fretted players who find it difficult to play more than one note on a bow – a relic no doubt of the pattern of plectrum picking they have become used to over the years.

down on d

down on F

a long up bow on B AFA
down on B|d

down on e

Re: Pulsing Bow method like Denis Murphy

Many classical violinists in my experience are frightened to use the open e-string, and this attitude is encouraged by some orchestral conductors. I’ve never been clear in my mind why this should be so.
A few years ago I was at a classical string workshop and the tutor spent twenty minutes trying to teach and persuade the violinists to bow a clean clear note on the open e. Eventually, she succeeded (more or less), but most of them later reverted to type, using the fingered e on the a-string.
A year or so later I went to a workshop run by the Orchestra of the Age of Enlightenment (a prestigious European orchestra specialising in the music of the 18th century), where we did a lot of work on a Haydn symphony. The tutors talked about, and illustrated the use of the open strings whenever possible, and the cutting down of vibrato considerably, again to get that cleaner 18c sound. There was also instruction in the dynamics of an 18c orchestra and how to emphasise a note with an up-bow (instead of the commoner classical/romantic down-bow method). I’d say the workshop was successful, and the advice was taken on board by most.
Finally, I must mention that the tutors spent the first quarter of an hour in telling us about the importance of warm-up exercises (mostly stretching) before playing, and posture when playing.

Re: Pulsing Bow method like Denis Murphy

In the mid 19th century the classical world got bitten by the every note must have the same tonal qualities bug. The open E string, being the only unwound string by that time, has a distinctly different tonal quality. Playing the note fingered on the A “evens” the tone of the piece. Playing fingered notes on the E dampens its tendency to ring.

It’s this search for even tone (and volume) that brought us the Boehm system flute in that period with its “acoustically perfect” finger holes (finger holes of different sizes have different volumes and tonal qualities).

For players trained in this tradition *intentionally* making a quick stab at the open E string to get it to ring like a bell is a cultural taboo on the order of having sex with chickens.

Composers of previous periods simply used the fact that different notes on various instruments had different tonal qualities to add expression to the music. Particular notes were chosen not simply for their pitch, but for how they sounded on the instrument in question. Wnen teaching the modern classical player to perform these pieces as they were originally inteneded to sound it isn’t even enough to simply get them to play on open strings. Even when you accomplish that they will still, unconciously, try to color the tone of the note to be more “even” with the other notes, when, as per above, the entire intent of the composer in chosing to use an open string note was to take advantage of its unique sounding qualities.

We’re finally entering a musical age now where musical “ethical relativism” is being recognized and taught and the idea that mid 19th century practices aren’t necessarily the “correct” way is becoming, very slowly and painfully, more accepted. There are even some players now who are trained in the Baroque styles right from the beginning. They’ll never be able to work in a modern orchestra, but, foruntately, this trend goes hand in hand with the recognition that chamber music is not a “lesser” form. (Oona’s desire to maintain a career in classical music might be well served by forming her own chamber music group, sidestepping all the vicious shenanigans that has come to characterize the modern orchestral field).

I would not only agree about the importance of warming up, but if I’m ever so daft as to put up a web site about playing music I’ll probably devote at least a few pages to the subject, as it is not only laregely overlooked, but where recongnized badly misapplied, to the point of often causing more damage than the damage it was intended to prevent.

The suggestion that one perform the yogic “Cobra” position as a back stretch almost brought me out on the subject, and, the evidence suggests, has actually managed to do so in the long run.

Yoga, although taught at American and European YMCAs as “an ancient and gentle art of stretching” is anything but. Yoga is a *religious* practice of the overcoming the body’s limitations school of thought, and even though I’ve been a “yoga dude” for decades (trained by Indian masters, not some 19 year old chick at the Y who doubles on “Aerobics”) I strongly discourage its use for stretching. Many yoga positions are actually *intentionally designed* to damage the body in order to perform them properly, in order to demonstrate spiritual powers beyond what the ordinary person has. This is the same tradtion that has people pushing knitting needles through their cheeks to demonstrate personal enlightenment.

You *must* understand the idea of pushing needles through your checks as a holy act to understand and evaluate yogic “stretching.”

Never, *ever* do the Cobra except as part of a religious practice (or maybe if you’re a dancer) and in full knowledge of its implications. Just look at it, it does not stretch the back, it stretches the *abdominal muscles*, the only group of muscles in the human body for which stretching is *not* necessarily a good idea, and ultimately causes permanent damage to the vertebrae, reducing your overall structural integrity and certainly leading to lifelong back troubles is you learn to do it “right.” The spine is physically designed to *not* bend backwards as part of our adaption to bipedalism. A backwardly rigid spine and tight abdominal muscles are the only thing keeping us from simply folding in half at the middle.

If The Cobra is bad, The Bridge is right out.

If you wish to use yoga to stretch the back properly do the Cobra’s harmonically paired opposite, The Cat. The spine as originally “designed” for quadrupeds forms an *arch*, with all the structural integrity implied by that. Arch your back gently, while tensioning the muscles of the abdomen at the same time.

This is functionally and biomechanically correct.

*Do not* perform the “counter arch.” This is functionally and biomechanically incorrect and my cause spine damage. The Cat is a metaphor, we are not cats.

Unfortunately too many yoga teachers these days are badly trained by other badly trained yoga teachers and do not understand the genesis and original function of the positions they teach, let alone their actual biomechanical results.

Cross post:

“Strange but I read on some post here that that was recommended for getting the bowed trebles going good with the wrist.”

This is a very widespread traditional teaching method. Give it a try, you’ll have to get very good at playing with nothing more than movement of your forearm and little flicks of a fluid wrist. Crossing strings absolutely requires a fluid wrist

I’ve never thought of it as a way to “build up power”, but rather as an economy of motion thing, but maybe that’s what they mean by “power” as you’ll find you can produce loud and “powerful” notes with no more than a few millimeters of bow movement., which in turn brings right hand speed up to virtually arbitrary levels.

Any larger bow movements than this absolutely require a fluidly flexible wrist. You can’t “fake it” by waving your elbow about in the air.
