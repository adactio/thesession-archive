Re: How to play a tune

“Plus, I think the timing is more important for someone playing a rhythm instrument. Not saying that it isn’t important for the melody players not to know it, but I think that it is more important for the rhythm section to know it.”

In this music, *all* instruments are rhythm instruments.

You absolutely cannot play this music well, as it’s intended to be played, unless you can feel and understand the pulse and timing. Understanding *time signatures* may not be essential, but it’s such a trifling bit of jargon, there’s no excuse not to grok it. Besides, the beat is the fundamental way sound is organized into music--if you want to make music, you should likely have a decent grasp of how it’s structured.

The upper number is the number of beats per measure. The bottom number is the type of note that carries the beat. So in 4/4, there are four beats per measure, and those beats are carried by quarter notes. In 6/8, there are six beats per measure, carried by eighth notes.

Re: How to play a tune

To be clear, I’m not saying that an academic understanding of music theory is essential for playing this music. But I think some people forget that “music theory” is nothing more than an explanation of how and why music works. It gives us a vocabulary for talking about the music, right down to being able to distinguish between, say, jigs and reels.

And I’m not arguing against learning by ear and feel, or in favor of sheet music or abc notation. But Jonny Ward and a few others here seem to think learning basic music concepts is somehow antithetical to this tradition.

I suspect many if not most of the generations of musicians who created this music and carried it on understood very clearly how the beats in a tune are organized into groups phrases. If they didn’t, then we’d have hordes of “jigs” in 4/4 and polkas in 9/8. They also had to be clear about how many measures (read: consistent groups of beats) were needed for a tune for it to work well (or at all) for dancing. With the exception of the relatively small number of set dances, Irish music has hardly any “crooked” tunes the way Quebecois or Metis music does--tunes with extra bars tacked onto one part or another. Obviously, the people inventing and playing Irish music paid attention to counting out the number of beats and how they are organized into the parts of a tune. Irish reels are either 16 or 32 bars long, or come in more than two parts that still break out into multiples of 8.

Time signature isn’t “music theory”--it’s just shorthand for describing “counting out the number of beats and how they are organized into the parts of a tune.”

Re: How to play a tune

“I firmly believe that if some of the greatest exponents of this music had learned about time signatures and learned to read music they would not have developed into the original and brilliant players they are now.” --- big_tab

Just for the record, you didn’t say “learned FROM sheet music” you said “learned TO READ music”. The former is perfectly reasonable, the latter is a tough argument to make convincingly.

I think we can all agree that virtually none of the great musicians learned FROM sheet music. I do know a few very good, and very solidly traditional, musicians who do actually prefer to learn individual tunes that way (though they all learned traditional music in the proper context, from excellent performers and teachers). Still, it’s a rarity.

But the apparent assertion that learning the language of reading sheet music is detrimental to one’s ability to play traditional music is worth being dismissed without evidence.

The problem that seems to underpin the perennial vitriol around this topic is the fact that people (especially newcomers to trad) seem to mistake learning a TUNE from learning to play traditional MUSIC. We see someone ask for the notes to a tune, and we assume (with some learned prejudice) that the person in question is cheating, and has their priorities askew in choosing to learn the TUNE without using the opportunity to improve their MUSIC. The more dramatic among us would argue that the more this happens, the more active damage it does to the musical tradition itself (though how much damage a fiddler in Oklahoma, for instance, will actually do to the Irish tradition is another matter of debate)

I think we can all agree that Irish Traditional music is an aural tradition rather than a written one. But this particular thread seems to center around whether the ability to read sheet music invalidates the purity of the musician and the tradition, and I just don’t see any evidence that it does. In order to show that it does, I think you’d need to produce a convincing number of good traditional musicians who were ruined by learning to read sheet music in spite of having an otherwise solid traditional musical education, and I just don’t think it’s that dangerous.

While I wouldn’t argue that sheet music is necessary to “keep the music alive”, I really don’t see it as a damaging influence at all. I think the appearance of the radio and the popular music on it have had a far more deleterious effect on the “purity” of traditional music than sheet music ever has.

Re: How to play a tune

Prof. that’s exactly the point I just made above. Transcribing a musician’s playing of the tune after the fact shows how good traditional musicians pay no heed to many conventions of formal music theory, and also how poorly sheet notation conveys what music really is.

But you’re (well, tab is) arguing backwards from that to say that Seamus Ennis was less of a player because he could apply the straightjacket (warts and all) *after* he played a tune. That’s simply not rational.

Peter, I’m guessing you’ll be able to relate to a personal, purely anecdotal example of how this works. I teach this music and I do it aurally, leading a person through a tune phrase by phrase. Most people are helped a great deal when they are told, “This is a jig; we’ll learn the A part first and then the B part.” So it helps to have some utterly basic understanding of the form, even if you don’t know what 6/8 means.

Some of my students are happy with this as the sum of the lesson, but some want sheet music as a memory aid. I give it to them. It’s not at all unusual that a few will come back next time and ask why the sheet music is “wrong.” They point to pick up notes in the “wrong” measures, phrases that cross bar lines, rhythms and timings that are clear when played but appear no where on the inky page.

And I explain to them (again and again) that the page is *not* the music but just a way of inaccurately storing a half-arsed approximation of the melody. That music notation *never* captures the music, regardless of genre, and that you should only use it once you know how the music should feel.

Sooner or later, most people come around to understanding this, just as they understand that reading a book about Hawaii is not the same as living in Hawaii, and that if they want the genuine experience, they’ll have to get off their *ss and go there.

Music’s no different. Knowing that a jig is in 6/8 doesn’t put a straightjacket on my playing of it *because I know--from decades of listening and lilting and playing--how an Irish traditional jig should feel.*

In other words, my deep understanding of this music shapes how I understand music theory and how I see music notation, not the other way around. I sight read (as rarely as I do) as a traditional musician.

From my conversations with brilliant players of this music (Kevin Burke, Martin Hayes, John Carty, Liz Carroll, Verena Commins, Sean Smyth, Brian Conway, and more), it seems they understand this in a very similar way.
