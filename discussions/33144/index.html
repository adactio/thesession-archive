Playing with others in sessions

I’ve been playing Anglo concertina for some time now. I’m competent enough to play a number of tunes, but I’d like to play sessions with people, and aside from expecting everybody else to follow ME, I don’t really get yet how I can know what tunes would fit well with what chord progression.

I don’t have a background in music theory; is the understanding of this something that would come with theory, with experience, or so ething else? I’ve tried playing along to recorded looping guitar chords, but it rarely sounds “right”, and if it does it’s pure luck that it did.

So, do you blokes know how best I might go about learning the skill of playing unrehearsed sessions with others?

Re: Playing with others in sessions

Hi Jared,

There’s a fundamental misunderstanding in the way you are approaching this. In the kind of music played at sessions, melody has priority, you can have a perfectly good session without any chords at all (those are the sessions I like best). If you play well-known tunes from the traditional repertoire correctly, other melody players will play along with you without any difficulty. That’s how it works.

Re: Playing with others in sessions

Can’t really see a problem here. Just go along to one of your local session and play along with the tunes that you know.

Once they’ve got used to you at that session, lead off with one of your tunes. Just play the melody. Providing that your tune is reasonably well known, others should join in. If there are any guitarists or other backers at the session, leave the backing to them.

Hopefully, they should back your tune using suitable chords - but unfortunately that’s not guaranteed.

Re: Playing with others in sessions

“I don’t really get yet how I can know what tunes would fit well with what chord progression.”
Tunes don’t fit with chord progressions, the chord progressions fit the tunes -- if and when that situation arises. In traditional Irish music, you play the tunes. If someone else wants to play some chords at the same time, that is up to them, and needn’t make any difference to what you play.
If you are thinking of playing chords yourself at the same time as you play the melody, I would concentrate first on learning the tunes. Go to a session -- if they play a tune you know, join in. If someone asks you, play a tune or two and take it from there. Otherwise just listen, and if the chance arises, ask for advice -- people are usually willing to help if you show an interest in what they are doing.

Re: Playing with others in sessions

If I can pile on here, to go with what AlBrown and others have said, just listening is a great way to start. But to help you know what to listen *for* and to be clear, in an Irish traditional music session, everyone plays the melody in unison (except for strummers and beaters who’s presence is optional). So, if someone starts “Pop Goes the Weasel”, everyone plays “Pop Goes the Weasel” at the same time, in the same key. That’s the idea, anyway.

“Pop Goes the Weasel” is used here for illustrative purposes only, and is not an actual session tune. You will have to learn the tunes that are popular at your local session. But take heart--many of them will not be much more melodically complex than PGTW. You just have to learn them.

Re: Playing with others in sessions

There’s a few basic misconceptions that really need to be cleared up;
1) As defined above, an Irish session isn’t a “jam”, people will be playing the tunes, together. Chords and accompaniment are totally secondary, and non-essential.
2) Any chord “progression”may only fit the one tune it has been devised for. It may not even be the only, or the best, one for that tune.
3) What we play are tunes. Songs are the things with words.
4) Traditional songs, also known as folk songs,, are, by simple definition, old.
5) Anyone singing something they have written, to a badly-strummed guitar, is not a folk-singer, they are a singer-songwriter.

I feel better for that.

Re: Playing with others in sessions

I have been to one session locally - I was invited. I went the first time without my fiddle & just to listen, and intended to do the same the second time, but my inviter urged me to bring my fiddle & helped to ‘bring me in’ by playing tunes that he knew I knew - he is a very nice guy. However, I think it will take months of mainly listening before I can join in much.
There seem to me to be a number of problems in joining a session when you’re a newbie like me:
a) Getting to know people socially who’ve been friends for years before you came along.
b) Getting to know their repertoire - and their tunes may not often be repeated, so learning one at home, you mayn’t have the chance to play it again for yonks.
c) When you do know it, having the confidence to join in & not forget the tune suddenly because of nerves!
d) When you do know it, you might not be able to play it fast enough or
e) You might not know the version that they play. Smash the Windows has at least two possible B-tunes.
f) You can’t play loud enough to hear yourself plainly because that would be bad manners & anyway, you’d be drawing attention to wrong notes which means
g) You can’t quite hear what note you’re playing in a room full of musicians (over ten, for example) & then it’s surprisingly difficult to orient yourself.
h) You might find yourself one of four fiddlers, or one of five guitarists, so the sound is a bit ketchuppy & again, you can’t hear yourself…

My verdict - fitting into a session takes endurance, cheerfulness, humility, sociability, adaptability - oh, and it helps if you can play a tune. :)

Mollie
