dots and ears

As I have no previous musical background, I thought of learning the dots as I go along learning ITM. Due to which the no. of things to learn became 2 rather than 1. And due to this, learning each tune took considerable time. But a few days ago I set out to learn The Sporting Pitchfork. Rather than going to the dots, I went to the youtube lesson vids by Michael Eskin (who’s a member here too). And to my surprise I learned the tune MUCH more quickly by listening the slower version that he plays part by part repeatedly, with some ornamentation as well. And I feel that I will be more comfortable in learning this way, listening to slowly played tunes in parts and copying the same.

So my question is: Is it okay to ignore the dots completely in this process?

↳ Posted by Dick Miles on June 11th, 2011.

Re: dots and ears

I don’t think anyone is saying we should burn all the tune books, or that one absolutely shouldn’t learn tunes from them, but in my experience with those players locally who have primarily learned their tunes from books rather than by ear tend to:

1) Lock down a specific version of a tune, often times with the same ornamentation in the same places.

2) Learn a lot of tunes quickly, but only at a perfunctory level without much attention to detail or internalization of the tune.

3) Resist developing the skill of learning by ear or picking up tunes on the fly, often showing overt anxiety if asked to not read out of books in a session (yes, it happens).

4) Tend to have a limited repertoire over a period of years compared to those who take the time to learn by ear.

Until I started playing this music about 20 years ago, I was a classical musician and I remember the anxiety and resistance to getting off the sheet music. I remember that I thought it was impossible, but it was absolute worth the few months it took to make the transition.

I won’t for a second make the claim that I have a clue how anyone else’s brain works, I can only speak for what works for me, but what I do know is that 20 years ago I was absolutely convinced that it was impossible for me to learn tunes by ear. It wasn’t until I did the work to develop the skill that I realized that it is possible, in fact in some ways effortless to learn tunes by ear once you recognize your own learning patterns, even if you don’t understand why your brain works the way it does.

FWIW, here’s my system:

http://www.tradlessons.com/?page_id=135

Of course, what works for me may not work for you.

↳ Posted by Dick Miles on June 12th, 2011.

Re: dots and ears

I have spent a lot of time with dots in the past, mostly as a tool for understanding better what I was hearing on recordings. As a result, I have a good understanding of the basic structures of traditional music, which seems to escape some of the people I know who learned only by ear. I think it’s the same as learning a language purely in conversational terms, as opposed to learning the grammar.

The dots are still useful for jogging the memory, and for sharing the bones of a tune, but I always dispense with them as soon as possible. Nonetheless I have learned many tunes from dots - the secret being to know the music well enough to bring the dots to life.

In the OAIM fiddle lessons, ornamentation is so far being taught very location-specifically, but I found that once I had learned the techniques, it was second nature to start applying them elsewhere, and indeed to tunes I knew from other sources, including dots.

This argument in many ways is a false dichotomy - both have their place - but how many people could really play convincingly in *any*idiom without ever having heard what it sounds like?

The other thing that rarely gets a mention is that people learn in different ways. The usual distinction is between men who (supposedly) learn spatially and women who learn things as a narrative. I suspect it isn’t really that simple though. (Though it seems to work if you ask people how they memorise route directions).

In musical terms, learning by ear is perhaps more like memorising a narrative, whereas learning from dots is more akin to memorising spatial patterns. It’s clearly useful to be able to do both, but in the early days it was the spatial patterns that I found easiest.It may also have something to do with the instrument you play.
