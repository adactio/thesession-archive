Re: Record Yourself Practicing……equipment?

Michael is right to the extent that a recording of yourself is far removed from what you’re doing when you’re playing live. But I still think that recording yourself is valuable in that you can pick up nasty little tendencies that you didn’t realise you had. I discovered three absolutely crucial areas in which I was falling short when I started to record myself on minidisc. I won’t bore you with the details, but two things. First, you have to have decent recording gear. Cassettes just don’t cut it. Minidisc recordings have no hiss and no wow - good. A mic plugged into your PC is great if your fan is at least ten times quieter than mine. Extraneous factors such as these will prevent you from judging yourself properly. But second, and far more crucial, you need to have the critical wherewithal to be able to judge yourself. For example, if you have absolutely no sense of rhythm, neither will your recordings, but you’ll be blissfully unaware of it. You may need a critical friend but you may not realise it.

Re: Record Yourself Practicing……equipment?

I use an old style cassette tape recorder--works well.

And I do think that listening to a recording of one’s playing is highly valuable. My experience is that when playing I am wrapped up in the requirements of the moment, of creating and controlling the sound (timbre, pitch, dynamic) as well as making music out of the tune. Also, I think that wood flutes in particular have such a rich tone with many overtones, airy sounds, that the sound I hear while playing is not the exact sound someone hears when I play. Thus, the recording allows me to be a listener of myself, and to hear my tone and playing as it sounds to someone listening to me, not how it sounds to me as I play it. It’s a tough thing to do--painful at times, but can lead to the greatest progress as a result (pain is the touchstone of growth). I would bet that most people who play music either on an instrument or sing do not record themselves, and thus may think they sound great when in fact, perhaps if they heard themselves as recorded, they could hear more objectively what they are playing instead of they think they are playing. Case in point--the early auditions of American Idol and perhaps a few folks at my local…..

Re: Record Yourself Practicing……equipment?

Just out of curiosity, llig, when was the last time you actually listened to a recording of yourself, just for quality control, to make sure that you really do pay such good attention that you didn’t miss anything?

Yes, you need to be aware of what’s coming out of your instrument. But I often find recordings of myself somewhat surprising - in either a good or bad way. It’s like the difference between talking and listening to a recording of yourself talk. It sounds different, and it gives you more information than you had before - if not just from the fact that you have a second perspective of the same event.

Having said that, I’m not a big fan of recording *sessions* (although, I’ll occasionally pull out a recorder to grab a tune I’ve been wanting to learn). But a recording is a limited view of the live music, and is missing a lot of what makes a session good (or bad). How often have you listened to a recording of a session that you played in, and had it be a completely different experience than the one when you were there? Often, what feels like a great session may not sound as good when you listen to a recording. And vice versa. What really matters in that case is what it felt like live, not what it sounds like on a recording.

Re: Record Yourself Practicing……equipment?

A useful way of practicing is to listen to yourself, live, in real time, through monitor headphones as you play. This is particularly useful for fiddle players because the sound they hear direct from the fiddle is not what someone a few feet away hears.

What I’ve started using is a Sharp portable minidisc recorder MD-MT200h(S) that I’ve had for a few years, a stereo condenser mic (of which more later) that I’d forgotten I had and which I’d dredged up from the bottom of a drawer, and Sony MDR-CD470 Digital Reference headphones (comfortable to wear and a great sound) which has a decently long cord.

I put the mic on its stand on a table, connect it to the MD recorder, connect the ‘phones to the recorder, start the recorder and mic, and play a few feet from the mic. My wife tells me that what I’m hearing through the ’phones is pretty well what she hears when she’s standing next to the mic.

Using the ’phones in real time helps me to concentrate more on what I’m doing, to hear the tone quality, and to point up the (numerous) mistakes and glitches.

Now that mic - I don’t really know what make it is. It is a battery-powered (1 AA) SM320 stereo condenser mic with a 1/4 inch jack (therefore I had to get a conversion jack to plug it into the MD recorder). The only indication of origin is “B Tech” in very tiny print just below the business end. I’ve had it for very many years, can’t remember exactly why I bought it (must have seemed a good idea at the time), but couldn’t then get it to work with the tape recorders I had in those days, so consigned it to a drawer. Now, it works very well with the Sharp MD.

With a sound editor on my computer I’ve looked at the wave form of my fiddle recorded at CD-quality on the Sharp, and the frequency response appears to approach 20KHz - ok, so the 23rd position isn’t all that difficult :-) - but much more importantly, the signal/noise ratio seems to be quite good.

I haven’t been able to track down the mic on Google, but if anyone has any information (origin, technical etc) about it I’d be very grateful.
