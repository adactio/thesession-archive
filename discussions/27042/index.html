Re: Are tapes bad?

There are lots of problems with fingering tapes on fiddle. The one that jumps out at me is that they almost never end up in the right place, and even when they do, they gradually get nudged out of place.

You will be far, far, far better off if you just learn to use your ears.

Fiddle fingering is easy. To play in G, D, A and their relative minors and the Dorian mode tunes (which covers a ton of tunes), all you really need to know is where to put your index and ring fingers and land them there consistently.

Step 1: Look at the neck on your fiddle, on the treble side, up where the fingerboard runs into the pegbox. Three pieces of wood meet there. One is the maple neck itself. Second is the ebony fingerboard. And third is the small ebony nut that holds the strings in little grooves. Feel the spot where these three pieces meet--should be a bit of a “catch” there, a noticeable bump or ridge. Got it?

Step 2: Now that you know about the “catch,” hold your fiddle in playing position. Feel with the skin on the side of your index finger for that catch. You want it to hit at the base of your index finger, just below where the finger joins the hand at the first knuckle. With your thumb easy against the other side of the neck, you’re now ready to play. *Always* start by finding that catch with the base of your index. It’s your reference point for finding proper positioning for good intonation. (Eventually, this becomes a subconscious thing, but you want to set up with your hand in the exact same place every time in the beginning.)

Step 3: Since you’re a pianist, imagine there’s a key between the nut (the open string) and where you put your index finger. So there’s a half step in there, between each open string and the first note you’re going to play. When you bend your index finger down to hold its note (F# on the E string, B on the A string, E on the D string, and A on the G string), it lands with the same “black-key” space on every string. That should get you as close to the right spot as any piece of tape. use your ears to fine tune your placement.

Step 4: Ignore your middle finger for a while. Skip *2* keys to find where the ring finger goes. It’s a bit of a reach from the index, but don’t over reach. Really, your hand should just curl over the strings. Your ring finger lands about where it would if you weren’t holding the fiddle and you tried to touch your ring finger tip to the crease in your palm where the ball of the thumb folds toward the pinky. Again, use your ear to fine tune placement. That ring finger spot is the same on every string. It’s the high A on the E string, D on the A string, G on the D string, and C nat on the G string.

Step 5: Starting on the open D string, use your ring and index fingers and open strings to find the notes for Amazing Grace (in G major). The tune uses only open, index, and ring notes. Play the tune a couple dozen times, listening closely to your intonation. Play it on piano if you can’t hear what it’s supposed to sound like. Sing it in your head and find those notes on the fiddle. Don’t stop till you get it to sound like a reasonable stab at the melody. Pay attention to how your fingers *feel* when they’re in the right spots. Build a link between that feel and the sound of good intonation in your ears.

That’s really it. The middle finger notes are easy. Let’s look at the A string. For C natural, put your index finger in the right spot for the B note. Then put your middle down squished right next to your index finger. That’s C natural. For C sharp, first, find your ring finger spot. Then squish your middle finger right behind the ring finger. Lift the ring finger off, out of the way. Your middle is on C sharp.

Make sense?

For more help on finger placement and intonation, see the free video violin lessons from Todd Ehle: http://www.toddehle.com/

Re: Are tapes bad?

Another thing that can help. Get yourself to a mandolin. Find a friend that has one. Or go to a music store. You’ll need to hold it for only a minute or two.

Mandolin has the same tuning and scale length as fiddle. But it has frets. Every fret is a half step--a single piano key.

Put your index on the 2nd fret on any string. Notice the open 1st fret between your index and the nut.

Put your ring finger on the 5th fret. Toggle between your index on 2nd fret and your ring on 5th fret a few times. Feel the span between them.

With your index and ring fingers down on their frets, notice the 3rd and 4th frets. Those are where your middle finger goes, depending on which key/mode you’re in. On the e, A, and D strings, the 4th fret is sharp, the 3rd fret is natural. On the G string, the 4th is natural, and the 3rd is flat.

For now, ignore all the other frets. All you need to play 90% of Irish traditional music on fiddle are the open strings and 2, 3, 4, and 5th frets.

It feels a little different on fiddle, but looking at your fingers on a mandolin fretboard gives you an instant mental map of fiddle fingering.

Re: Are tapes bad?

Was I used to do a lot of when I first picked up the fiddle was playing double stops (two consecutive strings together) and just hearing for the intervals.

Start by tuning your fiddle, 5ths, that’s pretty straight forward.
Then use you ring finger and find the octaves, the open A string and the octave above on the E string, the open D string and the octave above one the A string, the open G string and the octave above on the D string. Shift your finger about until you have it bang on (no vibrato mind).

Then find your 6ths. the most useful one here is the Bnat on the A string. Play it with the open D string, and your index finger on the A string, shift about until you get it bang on. Then think of that 6th interval as the major third in Gmaj, sing the intervals in your head, shift around until you get it bang on.

And carry on with lots more shapes. And right from the start it’s important to get the high B on the E string. I know a lot of fiddle players with otherwise good intonation who still struggle with that one. Play the two open bottom strings then move to the open D and the B with your first finger on the A string, then try the B with your first finger on the A string and the octave B above with your pinky on the E string. Move your pinky about unyil it’s bang on, while checking you still have the B on the A string right by playing it with the open D string.

I quite often don’t touch my fiddle for a week or two and a quick run through these, takes less than half a minute, always gets my fingers back in.

A lot of people advocate scales, but I can’t be arsed with scales, they are just crap tunes. (I can stomach half a minute of abstract practice, then I’m straight into tunes).

Re: Are tapes bad?

All these responses saying you should just learn to play in tune are really missing the point. You need to get to the point where your muscle memory will guide your fingers to the proper position, which means you need to play in tune a lot to get there. Having tapes helps you get your fingers to the proper position quickly, especially when there are so many other new elements to pay attention to. Of course you have to use your ears too, but having two cues instead of one helps.

That said, as Will Harmon points out, tapes can slip, and having tapes incorrectly placed will set you back a lot. If an experienced fiddler or teacher can check your tapes on a regular basis that’s okay, but if you have to do it yourself you’re probably better off without.

Re: Are tapes bad?

I suggest you lose the tapes as soon as you can. It is all about two things. First and foremost, train your ears to hear the correct note. And second, practice a lot (using correct hand position) to develop the muscle memory so that your fingers find the note even before you hear it.

Regarding the first point, you eventually may notice that notes on the fiddle are slightly different than notes on the piano. Piano is an equal tempered instrument. You will find fiddle uses slightly different intervals. In fact, a single note (say C) can be a bit different for different tunes. This is also true in classical violin playing, but is especially apparent in ITM. So, there is no substitute for training the ears.

Good luck on the journey.

Re: Are tapes bad?

OK, with regard to “mastery”, you’ve knocked the nail on the head without realising it. We consider someone to have “mastered” an art when they reach a certain skill level. That skill level DOES vary according to the complexity of the task. We consider someone to be a ‘master’ when they are better than most other people at the task (ie. they have been practicing longer than most other people). I think it is fair to assume that the amount of time spent practicing is not dependent on what instrument you play. So when a violinist has done 10,000 hours he has done more than most violinist, so will be better than most violinists, and thus considered a master. Likewise, when an oboeist has done 10,000 hours he will have done more than, and be better than, most oboeists. So he too can be considered a ‘master’. It is not coincidence that the practice time for both to become masters is about the same, it is because that is how we set the level for mastery - the mastery level is dependent on the complexity of the task.

Your comments on effective use of time come about because we have been talking in shorthand - saying ‘practice’ when we mean ‘directed practice’, which Ericsson defined as “activities designed, typically by a teacher, for the sole purpose of improving specific aspects of an individual’s performance”. So time wasting and just scraping away don’t count. (Personally, I’ve been playing fiddle for more than 40 years, must have clocked up at least 30,000 hours, but I’m still no good at it, because virtually none of my practice has been ‘directed’).

And finally, I DO mean ‘it takes 10,000 hours to master the fiddle’. If you want to take issue with that, you need to find me someone who has reached ‘master’ level without putting in at least 10,000 hours. What I said does not mean that if you put in 10,000 hours you will automatically become a master. It means you cannot become a master without putting in the time.

It is an arbitary term, but it doesn’t just mean becoming adequately proficient, it means becoming one of the best proponents of the art. There are millions of competent fiddlers in this world, but only a handful of masters. The same for other instruments. Which is why it is not surprising that ‘mastery’ of any instrument takes about the same amount of time. Because ‘mastery’ isn’t a measure of practice time against complexity of the instrument, it is a measure of practice time against how long others practice.

Re: Are tapes bad?

I’ve tracked down a source for the “10,000 hours to master the fiddle”. It is on Prof. Sassenhaus’s website http://violinmasterclass.com, which provides on-line access to the standard of professional violin training you would get at the major music colleges.

The section on “How Much Should I Practice”, says (I quote verbatim from Sassenhaus):

"If you play for fun
Age five: 30 minutes
Age eight: 45 - 60 minutes
Age ten: 60 - 75 minutes
Age twelve to adult: 90 - 120 minutes

"If you want to be a professional
Age five: 30 - 45 minutes
Age eight: 90 - 120 minutes
Age ten: 2 hours
Age twelve: 3 hours
Age fourteen to eighteen: 3 - 4 hours
Violin major in college: 5 hours

"These are minimums - The actual time depends on you.
This is NET (NO EXCUSES TOLERATED) practice time.
Getting a drink, answering the phone, etc. does not count."

Add up the figures for the professional section from the ages 5-18 and 10,000 hours is there. That takes the student up to a full mastery of classical violin technique. I would suggest the college major 5 hrs/day on top of that is mostly learning the classical solo repertoire at the professional level and applying the final polish.

When I look at the syllabus elsewhere on Sassenhaus’s website I can well believe the 10,000 hours. That level of technical training is what professional classical violinists for at least the last two or three centuries have been doing (and still do).

BUT, very few of us here are classical professionals, or with aspirations to be so, at that level. We play for fun, so Sassenhaus’s suggestion of 1.5 to 2 hrs/day for teens/adults is reasonable and in fact accords with the experience of many of us who play Irish traditional music.
