Re: How to stay relaxed when playing at speed

I was just lamenting my frustration at this and their advice was to pay attention to consistency and rhythmic tightness. Sure enough, when I started slowing down with a metronome (like 50bps for polkas and jig triplets) I found that the tunes I was having trouble with tunes at sessions speed had actually little to do with speed.

Once I get something tight and really slow, I then speed it up by 10 bps at a time, with the goal of being 10-15 bps over what they’d play at a session. This then lets me have confidence in my consistency and be more relaxed.

There’s loads of other things about playing relaxed too, don’t get me wrong. I’ve been working on this but they weren’t enough or weren’t going to get me there alone.

Re: How to stay relaxed when playing at speed

I’m having the same problems. Two things that seem to be working.

The first is using a metronome, as Vechey said. My son is a jazz musician and teacher, and this is how he practices the most complex of pieces. Slow and precise and slowly increasing tempo - over days or weeks, not over minutes! As on-line guitar teacher, Justin Sandercoe, has said ‘Practice doesn’t necessarily make perfect, but it does make permanent’. Playing it right slowly gets you confident with the notes.

The second thing that appears to be working for me is paying attenion to your picking hand. Slowly working with jigs and reels and forcing yourself to play the rhythms DUD DUD for jigs and DUDU DUDU for reels seems dull, but it certainly seems to be falling into place for me. I was sceptical about the pattern picking until I tried it, but it’s forcing me to pay attention to the rhythm of the tunes, and once that is embedded in the brain everything seems to be less effort to play. Things I had difficulty playing accurately are coming easier. Of course there are times when you need to break the pattern, but the foundation seems to be necessary.

Neither of these are overnight fixes though!

Re: How to stay relaxed when playing at speed

I struggled with this problem on banjo for a few years. I think the root of the problem is that it’s in our nature to play *harder* when we want to play *faster*, just as we run harder when we want to run faster. But in the case of playing music with a plectrum, it’s very counterproductive, because you end up fighting against your own muscles and the momentum of your hand. What we really need to practice is economy of motion -- you want to make the smallest movement you can get away with that makes the desired sound. Banjos don’t require much force to get a good tone out of the string. Mandolin and bouzouki require a more forceful motion to get a good sound, but banjos can be played with almost no force. So I actually quit playing mandolin for a few years, because it was contributing to my heavy-handedness on banjo. (I have since gotten to a point where my techniques on the different instruments can be separated, and I play mandolin more regularly again. Although I can generally play banjo faster than I can play mandolin).

The way that I worked through the issue included a few specific things:

1. Make sure that your picking motion is good. The majority of the motion should be coming from your wrist, not your forearm or shoulder. Your fingers may get involved a bit in triplets and expression, but in general, the motion should come primarily from your wrist.

2. I started using picks that have sandpaper-like grips, which allowed me to loosen my grip significantly, which in turn leads to better relaxation of the entire hand and arm.

3. I started practicing playing as quietly as I could. It’s basically impossible to play quietly when you’re playing hard on banjo. So instead of trying to force myself to relax (which is kind of an oxymoron), I just purposefully practiced quietly. When I felt like I had found my groove playing quietly, I would work on pushing up the tempos of what I was playing *without getting any louder*.

Even after working through those ideas for a couple years, I found that I could do it pretty well at home, but when it came to playing in a session, there were so many other pressures, all bets were off! I would still occasionally find myself playing too hard again, especially if the tempo was beyond my comfort range. In those circumstances, I found that all I had to do was concentrate on playing quieter again, and that would have the effect of relaxing my hand, which in turn would allow me to play better at faster tempos.

Those issues are many years in the past for me now, and now that the relaxation is baked into my playing, I can play louder in situations that require it, without introducing new tension in my picking hand. So rest assured that you, too, can work through this!

Re: How to stay relaxed when playing at speed

I suppose I can relate. Playing guitar, sometimes I realize my left hand, on the fretboard, is gripping the guitar like it’s a baseball bat, and then I wonder why I don’t seem ready to play around the fretboard picking out notes quickly. When I’m fingerpicking tunes, the right hand is always relaxed and can only get fatigued or cramped if I play the same thing or fast precision picking too long. Easy enough to avoid that. I’ve wondered if this is a result of having grown up playing acoustic guitars that perhaps had strings set a little high, and to play a chord required strength. Plus, being young, I had to grow into the role.

So my problem is mostly the other way around; the fretboard is where I need to relax. I do think that on guitar I naturally gravitate to being more of a song inventor and rhythm/song structure player on the fretboard, and that suits me fine, I have never wanted to be a lead player, and speed doesn’t interest me, song feel and aesthetics do.

As for hand strength and endurance, hand and forearm exercises absolutely build endurance and a sense of agility, control and speed. Hand squeezers, barbells, and open-handed rapid squeeze/open repetitions help. Wrist curls with barbells help, with the palms pushing up and in reverse position with the thumbs lifting up. Do reps until you fail, with enough resistance so that you can do around 30 reps. Combine that with rapid open-handed hand squeezes (rapid open-close-open-close) and you’ll find the “burn”. Do that daily and results show up quickly. Then no ghost of Earl Scruggs will have the courage to come near you, for fear of being shown up.

Re: How to stay relaxed when playing at speed

Fiddle player here. Tension for me turned out to be less about my technique and more about my general fitness. What I mean by that is, even with proper technique, I still experienced a lot of tension because my general flexibility/mobility was so low. I had tight shoulders, a tight lower back, and tight hamstrings, and all of that affected my playing. It wasn’t until I dealt with these fitness issues that I was finally able to play without tension. And it didn’t take an unreasonable amount of effort, just a handful of stretches and some due dilligence.

I did end up learning one* strength training exercise though, which is the dead hang. Allegedly, it’s a phenomenal exercise for your shoulders and spine and is used for shoulder injuries in physical therapy(i’ve personally never had a shoulder or spine injury, so I wouldn’t know for myself); But to my experience, it helped me relieve tension in my hands by building my grip strength. Fiddle playing was always markedly easier and more comfortable after days where I practiced dead hangs. I’ve noticed a lot of improvements on piano too since practicing dead hangs, so I would recommend that specific exercise for all musicians.

Don’t fret my friend! I was afraid I would never be able to get rid of tension and always thought I just had the wrong impression about it. It took a lot of studying and practice for me to get to the bottom of it. I hope it all works out, everyone should be able to enjoy their instruments comfortably and confidently.

Re: How to stay relaxed when playing at speed

> And finally a question for other banjo players, do you find your right hand would get fatigued during a session, or is this happening to me because I’m playing with tension?

This sounds like a tension issue. I’d start with the grip - relax your grip on the pick until you find that you’re hardly moving the string, and then gradually bring in just enough tension to make a tone. That’s your baseline. Relaxing your right hand will also make your triplets work the way you want them to.

Another way to work on this is to practice your right-hand dynamics. This is tricky on the banjo because there’s a somewhat narrower range of dynamic variation than you get on a guitar or mandolin, but you can still do it. It’s basically an elaboration of Reverend’s advice above: you start by playing a tune as quietly as possible, focusing on keeping that volume constant throughout. Call that “1”. Then play the same tune through as loudly as possible, while still getting a tone that sounds a bit like music. Call that maximum volume “5”. Then identify a point midway between those, call it “3”, and find points 2 and 4 as well. If you work at playing tunes at a particular level, and then moving from one level to another, you’ll actually be working out a lot of kinks in your right-hand technique. At least, it worked for some of my students who had right-hand difficulties. Your mileage may vary.

Another thing to think about is whether your practice is helping you learn the tunes, or just the notes. Most of the advice here has been about different ways to move your fingers around. This is fine, but it’s not going to help a lot if the tunes aren’t in your head. This is why I often recommend “silent practice” - play through the tune on the banjo in your head three times. If that’s difficult, do it more, until it becomes easy. The reason this works is because if all you’ve practiced is some patterns of execution, you’ll be thrown off easily by anything that you haven’t practiced. But if you know the tune rather than just the notes, you’ll be able to follow pretty much anything.

Re: How to stay relaxed when playing at speed

relaxation is a function of confidence. on mandolin (and banjo), are you confident that your left (fretting) hand knows the tune? that the right (picking) hand knows the tune? that the two of them agree and are playing the same tune (together)? the test is simple, put the right hand in your pocket and play the tune just with the left (fretting) hand (use hammer-on, pizzicato). repeat with metronome at different speeds. repeat with the right (picking) hand. with metronome at different speeds. next try both hands together. with metronome at different speeds. you may discover that some tune phrases the left hand “cannot keep up”, other tune phrases the right hand hand is lagging. fix that. repeat for each tune you play.

to say the same thing: play a tune you know, with good technique, with confidence, relaxed, fast.

to say same thing differently:
- not sure where to put fingers (do not know the tune) -> kills relaxation -> kills speed
- know where to put fingers but they do not go there (technique) -> kills relaxation -> kills speed
- afraid to play loud, afraid somebody hears your mistakes (no confidence) -> kills relaxation -> kills speed
- can only play tune 10 times (no endurance, physical, mental) -> no relaxation
- relaxed, can play this tune all day? +5 bpm on your metronome, rinse, repeat.
