Tonguing vs throating in a flute.

Re: Tonguing vs throating in a flute.

Tonguing is used sparingly in Irish music. What you refer to as “throating” is actually also tonguing. A classicla player would refer to that as the second half of the “double tonguing” technique, which is a way to toungue passages that would normally be too fast to single tongue. So intead of going “tu tu tu tu” you would tongue “tu ku tu ku.”

Richard mentioned glottal stops above, and those aren’t so much of an articulation as they are a sort of accent. I know Conal uses glottals onnearly every note, but he scarcely uses them in place of a cut or a pat. You perform a glottal stop by saying “uh.” You should feel your throat close for a moment and produce a percussive stop.

I mentioned earlier that tonguing is not commonly used in Irish flute playing, and you would never hear it used as the primary method of articulation. Cuts, pats and rolls are the main way that trad flute players articulate. I think a good deal of the confusion about this comes from those techniques being referred to as ornaments(decorations of the melody) when they are actually articulations. In the same way that a classical player would tongue to separate two notes, a trad player will cut or pat.

Re: Tonguing vs throating in a flute.

‘tuh’, ‘guh’, and ‘kuh’ are all done with the tongue in different parts of the mouth. If by throating you mean a glottal stop, that’s done back in the throat, almost like a very gentle cough.

Like everything in tradition music, there is no rule, and you will surely find good players who only do one or the other - but the only sure route to finding your own style is to be able to do both well and interchangeably.

Re: Tonguing vs throating in a flute.

They all work to separate notes. A big difference is in the effect they have on the attack of the following note. Cuts produce a wee chirpy spike at the start of it. This can be more or less drastic depending on which fingers do the cut. Adding some attack on the air pressure (belly push, glottal stop etc.) will accent this even more strongly. Varied mixtures of these elements lead to the spectrum of sounds found from “poppy” piping through chirping birds to Conal’s “howling wolves on the forest trail at night” (you had to be there to hear him…).

One difference between glottal stops and tonguing at the front of the mouth is the air space between the stop and the flute. In t-tounging there is almost no air space between the two leading to a very precise on/off control of the flute’s sound. With the Glottal Stop there is a big space leading to a substantial pressure wave building up when the air supply is turned back on - with an associated delay and increase in the attack of the note. Both can be further influenced by belly pushing the air stream when attacking the note. Or, to put it differently - they sound different…

The tip of the tongue is probably capable of faster movement than the glottal stops, but both are very susceptible to training. And the tonguing can be made even faster by airing it with alternately using the tip and back of the tongue - effectively doubling the possible speed.

What is “right” depends on what you want to achieve and no one says that you have to always want to achieve the same effect. I personally find it cute to play a flute like pipes, but it is effectively omitting one of the major things that makes a flute a flute, and not just a poor man’s pipes. Pipes play on a nearly constant pressure and only have the fingers to work with. Flutes have that _and_ all the things you can do with your air. Half of flute playing is outside of and half inside of the front edge of the lips.

Re: Tonguing vs throating in a flute.

I want to offer a different perspective for your concerns. Irish flute playing owes much to the piping tradition, where the ability to use the mouth for articulation does not exist. Most Irish players do their articulation with the fingers via the various ornaments used to get a note started.

I’ve never thought of using “kuh” for a glottal stop as tonguing. When the term “tonguing” is used, it strictly refers to using the tip of the tongue. Whether “kuh” also incorporates the tongue does not alter that. So you can tongue, kuh, use glottal stops (via a soft cough) or even use the lips. Stop the air flow however sounds good to you. I use everything, but primarily slur for a nice cascading sound or use ornaments to provide separation of the notes, along with stylistic purpose.

Agreed. Wesley Mann is right when it comes to what tonguing or double tonguing is. Tuh is produced by the tip of the tongue against the roof of the mouth, Kuh is produced by the middle of the tongue against the roof of the mouth.

A glottal stop is a different animal and is neither Tuh nor Kuh. In “Arabic” you would write it ‘uh. As I said it occurs in Cockney “bu’er” for “butter”. The symbol in the phonetic alphabet is ʔ. It is produced by closing your glottis.

And if you want your flute style to sound Irish - avoid tonguing (tuh or kuh) and use glottal stops instead. When you have mastered the style you *could* add a bit of tonguing, like some players do, but it’s not necessary. If you keep using tonguing as a beginner you will sound too “classical”.

Re: Tonguing vs throating in a flute.

It’s one of the things that comes up in “classical” fluteplaying, that different languages often have different points of articulation (the linguist talk) so one must take that into consideration when using a French manual for example.

In English anyhow we have, for stops, the t/d point of articulation, and the k/g. (The difference between the pairs is voiceless/voiced). Both involve a part of the tongue making contact with part of the inside of the mouth and stopping the flow of air. There are also glottal stops which appear in the common American pronunciation of “something” which is often spelt “sumpin” but there’s no “p”, rather it’s /sum‘n/. Likewise the “bottle” /bo’l/ heard in many British dialects. Saying “oo oo” /u’u/ gives the notion perhaps.

Anyhow on Irish flute I use very little tonguing, and occasional glottal stops, the latter giving a very gentle articulation. On high whistle I do a load of tonguing.

BTW I should perhaps mention the so-called throat vibrato which is often heard with traditional folk singers (Irish British and American). It’s also the standard vibrato done on the Kena in Bolivia and Peru, and I’ve heard loads of Irish flute and whistle players use it too.

Re: Tonguing vs throating in a flute.

The following comments may or may not be useful and they are not intended to undermine anything anyone else has written here, but they are authoritative (I am a trained phonetician and taught phonetics at a university level for nearly forty years as well as carried out fieldwork on languages with a variety of stop positions, including velar, uvular and glottal stops as well as glottalized stops.).
(1) velar stops are made by raising the body of the tongue to contact the velum (the soft palate, which you can feel if you curl the tip of your tongue back until you feel the soft part). [k] and [g] are palatal stops. In English, and many other languages, [k] is noticeably fronter before front vowels as in ‘key’, ‘Kay’, ‘cat’ and noticeably backed before back vowels as in ‘coo’ or ‘caw’. Double and triple tonguing on the flute are made by alternating a ‘t’ or ‘d’ sound with a ‘k’ or ‘g’ sound.
(2) glottal stops are made by closing the glottis (the vocal cords) as when saying ‘oh-oh’ [o?0] or when coughing. In many languages glottal stops are simply another type of voiceless stop, like [p, t, k, q] (this last a uvular stop).
(3) glottalized stops (a.k.a. ejectives) are produced by simultaneously closing the lips [p‘], the supralaryngeal vocal tract [t’, k‘, q’] and the glottis. There is a little pop of air when they are released which is caused by the fact that when the glottis is closed for a voiceless sound it is raised, compressing the air inside the mouth. When the stop is released the air rushes out to equalize the pressure inside and outside of the mouth. Many languages have a series of glottalized stops along with a series of ordinary stops.

As far as Irish flute-playing is concerned, I believe most players who have described the sound describe it as being made in throat (i.e. in the glottis), although Seamus Egan in his Mod for Trad flute lessons describes it differently: ‘Glottal Stopping: You will notice that as each note is played, it sounds “separated” from the previous note. This is achieved by making a guttural “ K” sound at the back of the throat.’ A linguist would need to speak to Egan to see if his description of his own playing is accurate as it is difficult, without training, to be aware of exactly what is happening in the back of one’s mouth and the throat. It is quite possible that he is referring to a glottal articulation and using the letter ‘K’ because that is the closest letter in English to the sound. Also given the common occurrence of glottalized stops in the world’s languages (although not in English), it is possible that some flutists add an extra kick to their articulation by simultaneously closing the glottis (making a glottal stop) and the tongue/velum (making a velar stop or ‘k’), but I’ve not heard of anyone teaching it this way.
