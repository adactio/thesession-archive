Re: Seán Ó Riada

Not to put too fine a point on it, but European classical composers whose work can be seen as expressing the cultural identity of their country (much of it also revered as great universal world art), tend to spring from cities and regions with mighty classical music conservatories, institutions, pedagogy traditions, and dynasties going back centuries.

Most of that music was produced well before O Riada. Those barn-door-kicking radical modernists—and quintessential Russians--Shostakovich, Stravinsky, and Prokofiev were born decades before O Riada. Two of those guys were born in the 1800s, as was Finland’s Jean Sibelius, Italy’s Verdi and Puccini, Ravel and Debussy, Spain’s Albeniz, the list goes on. Ireland has an ancient and deep heritage of high culture, but when the names I just checked were producing, Ireland was a backwater crushed under the boot of oppression and suffering civil war, famine and the brain-drain of mass immigration.

You don’t just decide to create a classical-music cultural identity starting in the mid-20th century. You have to percolate and do your work. And to be fair, since Ireland has had a chance to begin healing from its traumas and experience enough prosperity to gather itself, there are some really interesting music adventures going on, including in “art music” such as classical and the contemporary/avant vein of classical dubbed “New Music.”

Re: Seán Ó Riada

> Why hasn’t Ireland embraced classical music the same way that the rest of Europe has?

I think you may be both overrating much of Europe’s embrace of classical music and underrating Ireland’s. The national broadcaster, RTE, runs a radio station that plays mostly classical music (Lyric FM) as well as two full-time orchestras (National and Concert Orchestras), and there are multiple conservatories around the country specializing in classical music. There are also plenty of composers who integrate traditional music into their compositions. As with any music, there are societal/cultural reasons why classical music has historically largely been found in particular parts of society (well-off, upper class, often Protestant/“British”), but that is true everywhere.

We often forget that trad is a fairly niche interest even in Ireland itself; if you stop someone on the street they’re more likely to be a fan of rock, hip hop, or electronic music than they are of trad. And the same is true of classical, even in countries like Germany and Italy that are seen as having a strong tradition. Classical music has historically enjoyed an outsized cultural stature in large part due to the prestige (and money) attached to it, but Taylor Swift has more people showing up to a single concert of her current tour than many orchestras see in an entire season.

You can look up John Field, Hamilton Harty, Charles Stanford, and Augusta Holmes to hear more examples of Irish composers, and there are plenty of more modern composers that incorporate trad sounds into their music, like Ryan Molloy does in “Luasc:”

https://soundcloud.com/ryanmolloy/luasc

"In 1936, the government, spurred on by the combined weight of clergy, judiciary and police, enacted the Public Dance Halls Act. As the late Breandán Breathnach pointed out in his excellent booklet, Dancing in Ireland:

"Intentionally or otherwise, country house dancing was not excluded from the scope of the Act … That it extended to parties in private houses when dancing took place is unlikely … [but] the local clergy and gardai acted as if it did and by their harassment they put an end to this kind of dancing in those areas of rural Ireland where it still survived. Breandán Breathnach, Dancing in Ireland, (Miltown Malbay, Co Clare; 1983), p. 45.8
Junior’s description of the desolation caused by the economic and social upheavals of the ’thirties is graphic and moving:

"So, they barred the country house dance, and the priests was erecting parish halls. All they wanted was to make money - and they got 3d. into every shilling tax out of the tickets to pay the government for tax. So the country house dance was knocked out then, and ’twas fox-trots, and big old bands coming down, and our type, we’d be in a foreign country then. “And so, a movement with the laudable aim of preserving and promoting the culture of the people of rural Ireland had, by a combination of mismanagement, narrow minded bigotry and, most importantly, a total lack of perception of the results of its economic policies on the rural population, virtually succeeded in destroying the traditional culture of Ireland. ”

Re: Seán Ó Riada

Ó Riada had to die young because he drank way too much. WAY too much. He drank himself to death. Why? Hard to say.

I’d put Ó Riada on the long continuum of people trying to make Irish vernacular music “respectable,” usually in the interests of political nationalism/identity. This was Bunting’s agenda: it was O‘Neill’s agenda; it was DeValera’s agenda; it’s CCE’s agenda. Various ideas are mobilized to advance this agenda of respectability: the idea that the music is fragile and dying, for example, has been advanced without stint for several hundred years, and yet here we are. This idea of deathbed fragility and rarity empowers the collector. If you actually read what O’Neill wrote you can see a great deal of indifference or even hostility to the music played by Irish people in chicago which didn’t meet his particular standards of correctness or wasn’t unique enough to be worth collecting. I talk about some of this in my book, but if you don’t believe me go to O’Neills “Irish Minstrels” on Google books and see how he wrote about piper James Byrne, pp. 320-323. Pay attention to his language. It’s all about trying to make the music respectable and orderly and productive, which is what Ó Riada wanted to do.

This is not to condemn O‘Neill, just to point out that Irish music is as much a political idea as it is a musical genre. You might love what Ó Riada did or you might not: my point as with O’Neill is not that it’s good or bad, but that it’s the product of a political agenda. That agenda is to take the music of “disreputable” or marginal people and make it the foundation of some idea of national character; also to make it a commodity via music sales and /or tourism.

Re: Seán Ó Riada

“I just think more could be brought to the table.”

And it is—people have set that table with an abundance of examples on this very thread. No matter how distinctive the genres are, music is music, and generations of people have been expanding genres and blending and “fusing” different types of music together longer than can be remembered. If anything, Irish trad is one of the more widely tapped veins for mixing blood types.

The OP, however, seems to be of the mind that Irish trad is stuck is some adolescent form, not fully developed, not as expressive, perhaps not even as fully musical, as other genres. (He might find more traction for this theory—and no doubt less pushback—if he posted it on theorchestra.org or jazznoise.net instead of on a forum dedicated to the music he holds in such low esteem.)

Many innovators are happy to Frankenstein Irish trad with other genres. No worries. Some of these experiments succeed to a fair degree (I would include The Brendan Voyage in that group). Some don’t. Either way, they have little effect on actual Irish trad.

Why is that? I’d wager that for any music to endure as a distinct genre, it must meet the criteria its adherents want and expect of it. In short, it must be complete, whole, not needing any further additives or to go branching off in some new direction. Surely, it will continue to evolve, but that’s a relatively slow, conservative process (even for “non-traditional” musics like jazz, rock, and pop).

Irish trad music is dance music, yes, but those roots don’t mean that’s all it is, or that it’s somehow less expressive, less melodically intricate, less harmonically open to interpretation, less rhythmically interesting, less intellectually engaging, less sophisticated, less compelling, less *musical* than classical or any other genre. To assert (as the OP has) that it is less, and that it could only benefit from sewing on limbs and organs from another body of music, reveals a lack of understanding of Irish trad and also of what gives value to any musical form.
