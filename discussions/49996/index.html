Re: Why (piano and harp aside) are there so few female backers….

As long as we’re questioning the premise, I’d also add the brilliant Nancy Conescu, guitarist from Portland, OR.

But to the original question, let’s assume the presupposition holds and that there are statistically fewer women playing strummy instruments than one would expect. I don’t know if that’s actually the case, and I don’t even know what sort of numbers I’d expect to find, so what follows is pure handwaving, but here are some hypotheses that you might consider worth testing:

Hypothesis one: There are more men playing Irish music than women, and the scarcity of women playing guitars and bouzoukis more or less reflects their presence in the music generally.

Hypothesis two: Men and women come to Irish music in roughly equal numbers, but more men come to the music with guitar experience, and hence have guitar available as an easy option, hence more men play guitar.

Hypothesis three: As in H2, men and women are equally represented in the music but women prefer (previous experience aside) to play melody instruments and men have a higher preference (again, disregarding previous musical experience) for guitars and bouzoukis.

Hypothesis four: Two or more of the above apply, and the observed result (again, I’m stipulating the observation) is a combination of these effects.

Personally, I’d want to know more about the effect I’m trying to explain before I started testing any of these hypotheses, but those are the ones I’d start with.

Re: Why (piano and harp aside) are there so few female backers….

This is a bit of a tangent, but in my experience teaching guitar I learned that hand size isn’t really much of a limitation, it just requires some posture adjustment. Some folks with shorter arms have trouble with larger and deeper guitar bodies, but this is mostly about the right arm, and there are lots of excellent smaller-bodied guitars out there.

If a person with small hands is having trouble reaching chords the problem is usually solved by raising the neck of the guitar so the fretboard is above their elbow. This puts their left hand at a better angle to reach the bass end comfortably. Sometimes it helps to have a footstool to get this posture right, since the angle of the strings relative to the pick also makes a difference, but there’s always a way to make this work. After all, a guitar neck is maybe two inches across - I’ve never seen anyone whose fingers couldn’t make that stretch.

Of course, if you want to play voicings that span a wide range of frets, smaller fingers might be problematic, but this doesn’t really come up in most playing, and there is always a workaround. (Unless you’re trying to play the Bob Weir part in a Grateful Dead cover band - that dude played some strange chords!)

Re: Why (piano and harp aside) are there so few female backers….

Interesting responses.

Basically, I think that a big reason is that many women still feel that they need to “prove themselves” and work harder to be accepted.

The term “backer” doesn’t always have positive connotations and, perhaps unfairly, suggests that it is an easier option or something you can do just to get involved until you master your instrument or learn to play melody.
Of course, that’s not correct. It takes great skill to learn to play accompaniment really well and one of the reasons why I prefer to be a “Melody Player” is that the latter is actually easier in the long run. :-)

However, that perception probably still exists among many budding players. While male players may feel quite brazen and just “go for it” as mentioned above, women are probably more thoughtful and likely to want to do things *properly*

Some examples have been mentioned but I don’t really think of them as “backers”. I prefer to describe them as good “rounded” musicians. I’m particularly familiar with the music of Anna Massie and Jenn Butterworth, for instance, and just to describe them as “backers” does both of them an injustice, Yes, they can do brilliant “backing” but they are much more than that.

Re: Why (piano and harp aside) are there so few female backers….

I know it’s a tangent, but to come back to the size question, I have taught guitar to people around 5’ tall, counting both women and kids, and I have not seen anyone who couldn’t play the guitar because of their size.

I’ve seen several comments asserting that there are people who can’t play guitar because of the size of their hands or bodies - in my experience, this is simply not true. It’s certainly possible, in mandocello’s story, that someone might believe that they can’t play because they’re too small, but a reasonably attentive teacher can clear this up pretty easily.

There are two issues to consider around a person’s size, with respect to guitar playing. One has to do with arm and torso size, and yes, large-bodied and even medium-bodied guitars can be awkward for players with smaller body sizes. This can be addressed to some extent by posture adjustment, but it’s also reasonable to just get a smaller guitar. (Reasonably-priced small guitars are widely available now, but Seagull made good ones back in the ’90s and they weren’t the only ones) I can guarantee that for any player, there is a guitar that they can comfortably get their arm over and play for as long as they like.

The other issue is hand size. As it happens, my hands are on the small size, as I know from comparing my hands with students’ hands over the years - when people told me they thought their hands were too small to play, we’d put our hands together, palm to palm, and they were seldom smaller than mine, and never by more than about half an inch of span or of middle-finger length. So there’s a data point there: out of probably a couple of dozen students I saw over my time teaching, I never found anyone whose hands were much smaller than mine.

The key to this is realizing that this has nothing to do with the size of the guitar body and everything to do with how you position the guitar. If you want to see how this works, and you have a guitar handy, there’s a simple experiment you can do. Just hold the guitar with the neck parallel to the ground, with your fretting hand in position to grab a G chord. Lower the guitar until your fretting arm is fully extended, and note the effect on your reach - it gets harder to fret the low E string. Then raise the guitar up, neck still parallel to the ground, until your fretting hand is about even with your shoulder (you may have to angle the neck slightly depending on how the body sits under your other arm). You’ll notice that as the neck comes up, starting around the point where your hand is even with your elbow, your hand naturally comes forward and puts your fingers into a better position to fret the strings, and the chord gets easier to form. This is why classical guitarists use a footstool to elevate the guitar, and why they play with the neck at a much steeper angle than most steel-string players use: it’s ergonomically more suited to the complex stuff that they’re doing.
That exercise is basically how you find the sweet spot for a guitarist - you get their fingers in position for a G chord, and you adjust the neck until they find that they’re not stretching and they’re able to come down on the strings cleanly. For a person with smaller hands, like me, that position is usually with the fretting hand somewhere between the level of the elbow and the shoulder. I don’t think I ever saw anyone wind up with their hand at shoulder level, mostly it was at elbow level or a bit elevated.

I go into this because I think people who want to play should be encouraged to play, and not told that their body is wrong. Anyone can play some guitar if it means enough to them that they’ll put in the practice during the early and frustrating bits. Once a student gets to their first chosen milestone (play a song, pick a tune, play a chorus or two over blues changes, whatever) they can learn anything they want, and at that point the main question is whether it’s actually what they wanted to do - some people come to guitar enamored with the idea of being a guitar player, but they find that the actual playing isn’t all that interesting for them, so they stop, and that’s fine. But once they pass that milestone, if they actually want to do it, they can do anything they want.

Any guitar teachers who are curious about this or find it hard to believe that what I’m saying is true, please message me and I’d be glad to talk to you about the techniques I used. Hell, if you have a student who’s having trouble I’d even get on a zoom call with them and see if I can help. I honestly hate the idea that someone might think “gosh I’d like to play the guitar” and is stopped by something as silly as the size or shape of their body, which really should not stop them. Partly I hate this because I think people should be able to pursue their creative whims without needless hindrance, and partly I hate this because so many of these people go and get ukeleles, and that’s just a damned shame.

I’m sorry if I’m hijacking the thread, but this is obviously a bit of a sore spot for me. Happy to take it to DMs or to another thread if anyone prefers.

Re: Why (piano and harp aside) are there so few female backers….

Hope these aren’t too sweeping of generalizations but I’m adding my experience to the reasons mentioned above. This is what I see most in my community.

Modeling - like attracts like. We have a strong fiddle community, primarily female in my particular cohort, which year after year attracts more female players. Most of them already play or are now learning fiddle and it seems like a bit of a vortex :) There are a few playing concertina or flute, but all the gals have wanted to join in by playing melodic instruments, probably because that’s what we’re all doing. One started learning guitar after spending time on fiddle after seeing the need. She’s way more versatile now.

Size of the instrument - not for playing as Jon debunked but for practical reasons. Objectively, lugging around a fiddle/concertina/whistle case is easier than a guitar case for a person of any size. Not that women are more averse when it comes to hauling gear.. I have two female friends who play cello and they literally get bigger cars and extra airfare for their instruments. They’ve gotten good at working within cello constraints for carpooling with friends & family. I’m sure plenty of men do that too. But on average the physicality of carrying the same weight/size of instrument will be more effort for the average woman than the average man. The two cellists are the anomaly when it comes to backers in my area and are also excellent melody players. So that dual ability further supports that women tend to play more melody and less accompaniment.

My mom picked flute in elementary and when I asked her why she said “so I could fit it in my backpack & keep my hands free to do other things on my way to school” … she was about 10. And that’s the best example of a little miss logical mindset I can think of for choosing instruments. If you happen to want to maximize for efficiency, why ever would you play guitar when you can play fiddle!?

> With the world of tunes a lot of it seems to be about the quality of your chops

Frankly, I think this is not at all the case in general. I know it’s true in some places, but the sessions I’ve played in and gone back to have mostly been social gatherings first, and people are made welcome more for social reasons than for some objective measure of their skills. A player who isn’t fitting in musically will usually get suggestions or other sorts of help (ie, playing their sets to help them get their legs under them, tipping them off to the tunes that are going to be played, etc) rather than a cold shoulder.

This goes for both players and strummers, by the way, but I think it’s especially true for strummers because the quality of their playing is almost completely irrelevant, unless it’s extraordinarily bad. This is because there’s no way for a strummer to add anything to a session. In a duo or trio setting, the accompanist and the musician can do some interaction, and the accompanist can actually be a musician. In a session, the strummer is like a fly sitting on a freight train - they might be moving along at quite a clip, but it’s none of their doing, and there’s not a lot they can do to make it go faster or slower or in another direction.

↳ Posted by Jimi Limpet on February 25th, 2025.

Re: Why (piano and harp aside) are there so few female backers….

Nothing really is too big or too small. One can always get used to an instrument or learn to adapt to it. On the other, we may also seek out or settle for something which we *think* might suit us better. Most, if not all, the obstacles V benefits are in the mind.

For instance, I find the tenor banjo to be a more “clumsy” and cumbersome instrument compared to the mandolin or even my octave mandolin which is actually larger in size. So, I tend to take it out of the house less. Same with my piano accordion and harp. Part of the reason for the latter two instruments is that I can’t play them quite as well, of course, and I also got out of the habit of taking them out after I had major surgery a few years back…I’m fine now but have just got used to playing these at home instead.

Also, I like to think I’ve got nice small dainty figures which I’ve always used as an excuse for not doing hard labouring work. :-)
So, I sometimes like to think the fiddle, mandolin suits me better but that’s nonsense. I can play larger instruments just as well when I can be bothered. It’s just a case of practice and slightly adapting my “fingering” where appropriate.

Conversely, I’ve seen and heard very large people play small “delicate” instruments very well. Some have huge arms and fingers but can still produce a warm, beautiful and sensitive sound.

So, while the size of an instrument and/or an individual may have a very small significance, it isn’t crucial and shouldn’t be really be an obstacle to playing well.
