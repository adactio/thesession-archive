Neurotype-aware learning / teaching

People’s brains are not all wired the same way — there are genetic differences from one person to another that predispose people to certain ways of approaching things, as well as being inherently better at some things than others. A individual group of biases is called a ‘neurotype’.

There is also a layer of learned behaviour on top of this from one’s parents and other people in their environment. Exactly where the line between behaviour patterns that are learned, and those which are due to someone’s neurotype lies, I don’t believe is currently knowable.

Humans have a tendency to perceive that all other people think and perceive the world the same way that they do themselves. However this is completely wrong, other people’s internal ‘mental process’ (the way that their mind operates, process the world, and how they internally experience this) can be radically different from one’s own. The following video gives insight into one person’s internal process:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oWIH4j1l9zQ

Being aware of this is critically important for effective teaching and learning. It means that the difficulty of a task will vary hugely from one person to another. For instance, one person may find playing by ear comes very easily to them, while for another person this task may be genuinely impossible, depending on how their brain is wired to process information.

Teaching has often been conducted in a way whereby a teacher develops a singular teaching approach and then delivers that to students largely in the same way. This is guaranteed to be ineffective for a subset of students, and is probably suboptimal for almost all students.

Moving beyond that mentality is a very complex topic, but the simplest advice I’m able to offer is to *listen to your student* and adapt to them. People seem to have an innate drive to learn and explore things in ways that align with their neurotype, which will come across as them asking for things to be provided in certain ways or trying to do things in certain ways.

For example, I personally learn best by understanding the logic behind something wholistically. I need to understand for example, the goal of practice and how to design exercise(s) in order to achieve that end goal. Giving me a book of pre-crafted exercises, or doing the equivalent via in-person teaching, is entirely ineffective. My brain innately rejects the exercises as worthless, because it doesn’t understand where they came from.

When I first started playing, I also had no idea how to approach learning how to play by ear because what I was hearing seemed to be completely intangible. I did not know how to relate the sounds I was hearing to an instrument, or even how to begin to learn that. I do now, but finding an approach to learn this that works took a considerable amount of time.

I also have no short-term memory, and breaking things down and repeated practice are critical for me to be able to learn anything. Either I get something into long-term memory, or I can not do it at all.

--

These are a few examples related to myself, but we really need to start paying attention to, both how we learn best as individuals, and for teachers to study how their individual students learn most effectively.

A big problem that we currently have is that Psychology has been obsessed with viewing all humans as exactly the same, and classifying all minor differences as ‘disorders’. I do not agree with that perspective, and it simply does not make sense for all brains to work the same, given how evolution works. Biology will vary brain wiring just as much as it varies other physical traits, creating innate biases towards behaviour patterns.

People are objectively different, and we desperately need to start accommodating for this.

> For modern humans - those of us who are well up Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs at least - what is the process that is selecting?

I guess for a species to survive it needs to develop as much genetic variety and ability to adapt to different conditions as possible. An animal that can only eat one plant species could be wiped out by a single season of bad weather, and if they all had the same immune genes they could be killed off by one infection.

Those drives have created lots of species with strong adaption skills, but we’ve really monetised it by developing sophisticated communication as well. Not only can we can pass on knowledge to subsequent generations, we can also assemble teams with diverse skills and aptitudes. One person can’t land a rocket on the moon; it needs a lot of very different minds.

People with different minds being able to survive and pass on their genes is all that drives it. Sometimes diversity is such that it makes a person’s life challenging or means they’re unable to live without support, but it is part of that natural variation.

The system, in the end, was unworkable.

Re: Neurotype-aware learning / teaching

Mark M, I couldn’t disagree with you more.

For example, the “circle of death” style of teaching often done at workshops absolutely doesn’t work for me.

This is the kind of teaching where the teacher sits in front of a circle of students, demonstrates complex techniques, and then goes around the circle asking each student to replicate what they heard.

If you are unlucky enough to be toward the end of the circle you get to listen to perhaps a dozen players, often those who self-evaluated themselves into a higher level class than they should be in and take way too much time, struggle to do the technique incorrectly over and over before you have your opportunity.

This completely and repeatedly scrambles my brain and doesn’t work for me.

For classes like that, I’ll sit out, take a video of the instructor and work on whatever skill is being taught on my own at home.

In comparison, I get huge benefit from “master class” style workshops where the instructor spends some considerable time with each student on some skill while everyone else watches.

It’s not about being a “slow learner” it’s often about teachers, because of time constraints, budgetary restrictions, or just not having a better alternative, having to teach in a style that works for many, but not all students.

Re: Neurotype-aware learning / teaching

"The teacher sits in front of a circle of students, demonstrates complex techniques, and then goes around the circle asking each student to replicate what they heard.

This completely and repeatedly scrambles my brain…"

I’ve attended many workshops where I can see people’s brains being scrambled by the fact that the instructor has developed an imagined construct of how they play that doesn’t match the way they play.

In formal music, so-called “classical” music, when students take formal lessons on their instruments they’re not only being taught how to play, they’re being taught how to teach. They’re being given vocabulary to describe every aspect of performance and a pedagogy, a methodology, which hundreds of years of teacher > student relationships has been proven effective for the vast majority of people.

But in Trad music and Folk music beginners often figure stuff out for themselves by experimenting on their instruments mimicking what they think they’re hearing their musical heroes do. Some of these people have tremendous raw talent and quickly become excellent players having never gone through any conscious learning process.

These people can find themselves put in the position of teaching workshops without a clear mental construct of their own technique or the required vocabulary to describe what they do. I’ve seen it happen where a good player, when put in the position of teaching, picks up teaching verbiage he hears other people use, and start using it himself, not aware that it doesn’t match how he himself plays.

I’ve been sat in workshops (especially uilleann pipes) where the teacher clearly describes to the circle a specific technique, but then plays something different, and continues to do so for the entire workshop.

I think “hold on now, he’s not doing what he says he’s doing, exactly what IS he doing?” and I watch his fingers and listen intently and figure out what he’s actually doing.

How many students in the circle have enough playing, teaching, and listening experience to do that? They’re confused, trying to make sense of what they’re being told and what they’re hearing.

So when the teacher says to somebody in the circle “now you try it” what is the student supposed to play? I’ve seen students play what the teacher SAID to do, then the teacher says “no, do ______ (the exact thing the student did)”. The students have to figure out on their own that they’re not meant to follow what the teacher says but what he does.

At one uilleann workshop as the class went along I was writing out, on the sly, everything the teacher played. After the class when the teacher had left I heard a couple of the students say that they couldn’t make sense of anything. I explained to them that he was verbalising one way, playing another, and showed them the sheet music I had written out showing what he was actually doing. The light bulb went on.

There’s an uilleann instructional video like that. It even comes with sheet music that corresponds with the way the teacher is explaining how he plays things, but doesn’t correspond with how he plays things. I transcribed all of his playing and gave copies to confused beginners.

I mentioned new (positive) perspectives concerning autism, because many of the people who use this website are of an older generation, and may not be aware of recent research. There is a paper called ‘Autistic peer-to-peer information transfer is highly effective’, which found, as the title states, that communication between ‘autistic’ people can be highly effective. The paper is here:

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32431157/

There is also a theory concerning what autism actually is called ‘Monotropism’, which the video I linked in a previous post by Meg (I’m autistic now what) explains very clearly. This theory states that there are different ‘neurotypes’ of humans, one group generally called ‘neurotypicals’, are polytropic, which means that they tend to focus on multiple things less intensely. Monotropic people (autistic people) instead focus intensely on one, or a small number of things at a time.

This research suggests that autism, at least the ‘high functioning’ kind, is a kind of human neurotype, a normal aspect of the species. That different attention styles would evolve is not unexpected, given that these differences can be advantageous in different situations.

We are also getting to a point where it is becoming possible to recognise if someone is autistic just from their communication style and related things. The following video is a great example of this:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CKIy_xvf_Jk

Humans have a long history of forming superstitions around things that they do not understand, and this is rampant surrounding autism. For example the following video, where Meg debunks a US press conference by looking up the actual research papers cited and points out numerous discrepancies.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sEIb-1IdDZQ

Meg has done many such debunking videos on her channel. She is a psychology student, and always looks up and cites numerous research papers in her work.

The use of autism as a social superstition is deeply concerning because there are many commonalities between this, and the ‘witch trials’ of the late medieval to early modern period. These resulted in the deaths of tens of thousands of innocent people - mostly women. Superstitions were held around them due to odd behaviour patterns which map closely to what we today understand as mental health conditions like depression. There is a lot of other background, and the YouTube channel ‘esoterica’ has many videos discussing it.

It seems to me that many of the problems faced by autistic people today are caused, not due to any ‘problem’ with the individual, but rather, extreme societal inflexibility (especially the education system), and a lack of awareness by both autistic people, and neurotypicals, of how the other neurotype communicates.

It is also worth considering to what extent the problems faced by people with these conditions are caused by society. I mentioned in an earlier post in this thread that I can not learn in the way that education systems teach. However, I know how I can learn very effectively (passive observation over a long timespan).

Over the past year I learned to sew clothing by watching people on YouTube sew a huge manner of different things. If I was to watch one video or take a class about something I wouldn’t learn anything, but that repeated exposure to a subject over a long period of time, I absorb things passively.

I can’t learn how education systems teach, yet I spend most of my time learning. This though begs the question, what other systems in society are causing artificial ‘disabilities’? Also, if autism is a neurotype, then what other neurotypes exist in the human species that are currently invisible, because we are not looking for them?

This was also my reason for posting this thread, as I suspect that music teaching approaches are artificially disabling some people, where a profoundly different approach would work better.
