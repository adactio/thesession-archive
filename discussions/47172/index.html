Re: Flute issues.

The pollen probably isn’t helping you. I feel your pain. Where I live at the moment, spruce pollen is heavy this time of year and bothers nearly everyone. Lots of post-nasal drip, raspy throats, sneezing.

Another possible issue is the flute itself. Is yours mopane? Some people react to resinous woods, though contact allergies (lips and fingers) are more likely than a systemic reaction that would affect your breathing or throat.

Embouchure is always a work in progress, and it takes time. Blowing steady, even, long tones up and down the whole flute goes a long way to developing the basic facial posture and musculature.

Re: breathing. Once your embouchure gets focused, playing flute takes no more breath than having a conversation. It’s more about support from the diaphragm than it is about gulping in lots of air. I found that it helped to stay relaxed on the inhale rather than hurrying to get as much air in as possible.

Best of luck, hope you get it sorted.

Re: Flute issues.

There are three issues that you present.
1. Almond oil. Get rid of it. There are no compelling reasons to continue with it if you think it’s effecting your skin. I have several flutes, and have, for the past 20 years used Jojoba oil. It’s not petroleum based like bore oil and it stays fresh for a few years.

2. Tone. (I’m a flute player). First, get a mirror and watch your embouchure - try to focus on the back of the tone hole - do this for every note in the 2 register scale. (Surprisingly, your lips will move forward as you go into the higher register - focus on this). Go slowly, and wait for the best tone before moving on.

3. Tunes. Don’t post a tune, asking for help, until you’ve memorized it and can play it confidently. Most of us here can play into a camera with notes or ABCs. It’s hard to assess your needs if you don’t know the tune, or haven’t developed a relationship with it. It’s just typing, and doesn’t express the real problems.

I’ve seen a few of your vids. It’s best for me when you know the material, and then I can agree or disagree with the interpretation.

Re: Flute issues.

If you can get a tone and then lose it after a few seconds, but it used to be better then:
1. Try another flute, or even a beer bottle - if the tone from this doesn’t deteriorate then you have some issues with the flute, possibly an allergical reaction numbing your lips, as you suspect. If this does deteriorate quickly then you need to work on your technique. Note, an allergy can develop over time, so you may not have had this reaction when the flute was new… If still not sure, ask another flute player to try your flute. If the flute has problems then there is plenty of advice on what those can be and how to fix them.
2. If you are sure the flute is ok from point 1. then you need to work on having enough air to play. Loads of stuff available online about how to do that. Make sure that you can actually feel lots of air leaving your mouth and if that is working ok and your tone is still failing then:
3. If your flute is ok and you are moving enough air, then you are not getting the air to where it needs to be and at the correct speed. Shape of embouchure. Make sure that you are actually blowing over the embouchure hole and not missing it completely (==>mirror). Look at the presentation of the flute to the lips (grip, angle, rotation et.c.).

Note that a bad tone generally is a different problem to an ok tone that you cannot hold. A deteriorating tone due to lip fatigue is much rarer than running out of air. Unless you are cramping the lips up to the point of muscle failure, which did not look like what you were doing on your video.

And please, do stick with the flute long enough to sort it out.

Re: Flute issues.

Ketil, my own experience of coming to flute after playing fiddle and whistle was that flute was relatively easy, not physically demanding. Yes, it took some time (months, not a year) to get a solid, consistent embouchure, but as I said above, the breath requirements are about the same as holding a conversation. (To be clear, I played in a Kevin Crawford-ish flowy style, not percussive like Conal O’Grada.) For me, getting a good embouchure was far easier than learning to manage a fiddle bow (which took years).

If you’re working “hard” to learn flute, you’re likely doing something wrong—working harder than you have to. I would encourage you to reach out to one of the many fluters now giving lessons through Zoom or Skype. Given your background with whistle and pipes, it will likely take only a handful of lessons to get your embouchure and breathing on the right track.

The main challenge with flute is that, once you’re playing well, you do have to play regularly to maintain your embouchure. But that’s a joy, not a chore.

Re: Flute issues.

I’d never making any generalizations about any of this, every player and every flute is different.

On Tuesday I was working with a couple of players on the differences between blowing a silver flute than a wooden flute, and really it came down to air flow direction, lower lip coverage of the leading edge of the embouchure hole, and increased air volume requirements if you want a “hard” tone. My suggestion was to spend the time required in front of a mirror trying adjusting the position of the flute and your lips and jaw, until you found the “magic” spot and then notice what you are feeling as far as the edge of the hole on your lip, all the angles so that you can recreate it every time you go to play. When I go to play, the way I pick up, hold and position the flute is always the same way, almost like a martial arts kata, to recreate the exact position every time that gives me the tone I want.

That being said, not every flute works for everyone, so when someone appears to be doing everything correction, but still has an issue getting a strong tone out of a flute, I suggest they try another instrument and see if anything improves.

My older Olwell boxwood has a somewhat squared oval shape. Compared two of his Nicholson models on Tuesday, mine had the squared oval, the newer one was more an elongated oval. Peter Noy flutes have a very square embouchure hole.

I really can’t effectively play the Peter Noy flutes I mentioned above, or the Tipple flutes with the classical style lip plate and square embouchure. They just don’t work for my anatomy without me fundamentally altering everything about how I play to deal with the wider airflow required. I’ve played with other players who have no problems with these designs. I can get a great tone from my slightly squared Olwell and my Hamilton headjoint on my antique Metzler, and pretty much any other flute with a oval(ish) embouchure. Square embouchure holes are almost always a non-starter for me. I ended up trading the Noy flute to another player for the boxwood Olwell I play today and absolutely enjoy.

Don’t always assume the problem is you, sometimes, the problem is just a mismatch between you and the design of the flute, and in these cases, changing to another instrument can make a huge difference.

Ketil, at least you’ve narrowed it down to a decision only you can make.

We’re all different, with different physiologies, but maybe my experience can suggest what’s possible.

When I was first learning flute, I quickly built up to playing at least one hour every day, rarely ever missing a day, really concentrating on my embouchure. Like you, I already knew a lot of tunes, so naturally, when I wasn’t playing long tones and practicing octave jumps, I was moving my tunes onto flute. Within three months, my embouchure was focused and steady enough to play dozens of tunes at session tempo without running out of breath or losing tone. Of course, my control over tone kept improving over the years, but I could readily play tunes on flute after just three months. No doubt it helped that at the outset I took a few lessons from a good wooden flute (not Irish style) teacher. She was a tremendous help in dialing in my embouchure, getting a reedy tone and a hard D. Through her, I learned that embouchure was a subtle thing, more a matter of finesse than “hard” work. I agree with Michael Eskin’s approach of figuring out precisely what works to get big tone and then picking up the flute and positioning it exactly the same way every time so you can replicate it every time.

I loved playing flute, how it felt under my fingers, the sense of singing tunes into the bore, thinking of phrasing as breathing, etc. It taught me a lot that transferred into my playing of tunes on other instruments. I miss it to this day (health issues forced me to quit).

Give it a good try, and best of luck with it. Let us know how you get on. And if it doesn’t work out, make the most of whistle and pipes. I’ve enjoyed all your clips you’ve posted.

Re: Flute issues.

If you’ve eliminated allergies et.c. then it is almost certainly running out of air. This has two basic sides:

1. Moving enough air to start with. The flute has a huge appetite for air volume, comparable to tuba and trombone. It is however at a relatively low pressure (compared to, say, clarinet or GHB) and with virtually NO backpressure to push against. This demands good breathing technique and singers can help you here a lot. Tensing up or “trying hard” is not really helpful.

2, Using the air efficiently - here you can look at the embouchure, tightening it up a bit, but this really is secondary to moving the air. I know lots of people with a really tight embouchure and a tiny strangled sound…

2b. Sounds obvious, but… Breathe in when you run out of air - actually do so long before then. Conal says you should never be running your lungs below half full in the course of a normal tune. Find the points in the tune where the breathing accentuates the phrasing and plan for more of them than you will actually need in a single run through - this way you can vary how you are phrasing the tune between different repeats.

If you are used to whistle then I share: When I play whistle (being used to the flute) I find myself in CO2 overload long before I have empty lungs - well over half of all the air I breathe in gets vented to the side of the whistle before I can breathe in again. Same when I play Clarinet. And that is when trying not to overfill the lungs!

Despite all the great advice you’ve been given, in your original post it seemed to me like there was a drastic change when you resumed playing. Your ‘before’ video isn’t bad at all and you ought to be progressing from that point, even if some backslide from when you were ill.

From what you described, it sounds to me that the flute is leaking even though you mentioned checking the flute itself. Have you done a suck-test on the headjoint? I think Terry McGee’s website tells you how. If there’s a metal liner inside the headjoint it could have detached, or a hairline crack inside. Look inside with a torch/flashlight.

If at all possible try a different flute to see if there’s any difference.

For tone, there’s a gizmo which has helped some of my learners and improvers. It’s called a “Pneumo Pro” and it’s a plastic thing with a blow-hole and 4 little helicopter-fans attached below that. It works good for woodenflute players. You look in a mirror and put it to your mouth, and try to angle your air-reed so that you make each little helicopter spin, one at a time. There’s 4 positions, way down for a bottom D, and 3 other higher positions. Kind of bypasses all the thinking we do for tone improvement, because you can physically see when you’ve angled your air correctly. It’s here:
https://www.blockiflute.com/Pneumo-Pro-_p_1.html

Good luck! - Lesl
