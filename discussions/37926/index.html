Re: Singing with a Fake Irish Accent

It’s all fake. You listen to The Rolling Stones or even The Beatles 60’s hits they’re singing with fake American, in fact Southern USA black accents. Rock and Roll came from America, so that’s what they emulated. It’s interesting to observe the accent change with the Beatles from their early rock n’ roll faux-Yank “Last naht ah sayed these wudds to ma gell” through to when *they* could call the shots and more authentically sing about “4000 howles in Blaackboorn Laancasher.”
It just goes with the territory (literally.) Paul Brady sang “Don’t come again” with his Ulster Irish accent, when it is clearly a Scottish song bursting with Scottish dialect words and phrases, “the bonny wee lassie’s answer”…“It’s there I widnae go”…“I will aye mind the girl..” and so on. Apparently Paul Brady learned the song from Eddie Butcher of Magilligan Co. Derry. My guess is it made it’s way across the North Channel/Straits of Moyle seperating Scotland from Ireland during, or subsequently from, the Planter days, to embed itself within the Ulster Scots traditional canon of songs, so Brady felt himself “allowed” to sing it in his own accent, despite the dialect vocabulary.
I used to sing a bit at sessions, a couple of Irish songs but mostly Scottish songs. My voice has gone a bit rusty these days though (thankfully!) If I sang an Irish song I would ham an Irish accent, with inevitable Scottish overtones. There was no other way to do it. If I sung an Irish song in a purely Scottish accent that would sound even more ridiculous than a Scotsman hamming Irish, hence the reason I focused on Scots songs. However, most of the good Scots songs are from the Buchan (Aberdeenshire) area, or at least they were preserved there - even the old Glasgow songs. So I’d end up singing Scots songs in a Buchan accent! You can’t win!
(OK, maybe not a Buchan dialect accent, that is very distinct, but with a slightly more “rural” Scots burr to the song than the drawn out Glasgow drawl…know whut ah mean, whaaaat?)
As for your American cadences, I’d say stick with them, because that’s what you are, but ham enough of an Irish accent to make sure the audience knows they’re hearing an Irish song. Don’t go 100% from out the back o’ beyond in Kilfenora, because people will know you’re making it up. But don’t overlay it with a pure American accent as people will see you as just another Yank singing Irish songs. I’m sure you will discern. The fact that you’re asking means you’re thinking about this.

I totally agree. I don’t really believe in regional styles, I think there is a spectrum of style, and each individual covers a band within the spectrum which will overlap with all sorts of other people.

But I do think you need to stay true to your personal style, just as I think you need to stay true to your natural accent. Supposing you were an Irish style fiddler, and you decided to put a set of old timey tunes in the middle of your act. If you take those old timey tunes and play them in your own style no one will think twice about it. At the same time, if you are an Irish style fiddler and you like old timey there is no reason why you shouldn’t incorporate elements of old timey into your personal style, or even develop a style that spans both. It is jumping out of your own style in order to simply immitate someone else’s that causes the problem, just like jumping out of your own accent to immitate someone elses.

Re: Singing with a Fake Irish Accent

@David 50, yes, the problem of Scots words for English speakers is one I face every day. You do naturally pick up bits of the language even if your accent doesn’t change. One of the first things my wife said to me (long before we were married) was how silly it sounded when I used even simple Scots words like ‘Aye’ and ‘Wee’. Since then I’ve stuck with ‘yes’ and ‘small’, but it still results in some very odd conversations - she says “Ken thare’s a waup on yon lum?” and I reply “Yes, I know there’s a lapwing on that chimney”.

There’s a good example of how you can overcome the problem in Eric Bogle’s Green Fields of France. In Eric’s original version one line of the chorus goes “Did rifles fire o‘er you as they lowered you down?” The missing syllable in o’er is essential to make the line scan, and it sounds perfectly normal either in Erics Scottish accent or in poetic English, but it doesn’t sit at all well in an Irish accent, so when Davey Arthur and the Fureys recorded the song they changed the whole like to “Did they sound the death march as they lowered you down?” which doesn’t make as much sense (they don’t play a march as the lower the coffin, they fire a salute) but it solved ther problem.
