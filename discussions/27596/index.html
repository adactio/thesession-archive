The Bitter Side…

It’s one thing to be able to play tunes/pieces/songs…
But it’s something else to be able to play your instruments.

I know twenty tunes, but none of them sound good(to me) because i’m still bad on fiddle. Sure i’m not as bad as i was a couple months ago, and i’m far from how bad i was when i first started a year and a half ago. But this instrument is ridiculously hard. I guess i’m still getting use to the strings. A little frustrated with all the extra skills you need to be able to actually play this thing.

I mean, lets look at the facts…

Sure, for piano, you play notes with both hands.
Yea, you play harmony and melody…
Yea it’s a rhythm instrument also…
And it’s Bass AND Treble…

The fact that it does this much makes it seem the hardest.
But to me, there’s one thing that makes it easier to learn than the majority of instruments. To me, the strings are harder than piano because you have to worry about tone, and with fret-less strings in particular, Intonation. For me the two things that cancel out Fiddle being easier than piano, is Tone and Intonation.(I heard that the French Horn is the Hardest instrument, Period. They say because the notes are so close together. Another “Intonation” problem aye?)

And the bitter side?
It takes time, no matter how hard you work.
Waiting on getting better.

Yea i’m glad that i know twenty tunes. I’m glad that i’m learning so fast. I’m glad that i even have a fiddle to play! But my own impatience frustrates me. Hopefully it doesn’t take too much longer.

Re: The Bitter Side…

20 tunes is plenty to be working with. I’d stop learning tunes on fiddle and concentrate on only learning technique, within the tunes you already know. That way you can concentrate exclusively on the technique, without having to try to remember what note comes next at the same time.

Do your tune learning separately on your piano (for anyone else I’d suggest singing/lilting or a whistle). Only transfer new tunes to the fiddle if they introduce a specific new technique, and even then get to the point where you can sing the tune through without thinking before you take it to the fiddle.

This is the quickest way to build up familiarity and technique with the fiddle, and you’ll find that once you have that familiarity, all the tunes you’ve learned on piano will come out on the fiddle, without you having to re-learn them.

Re: The Bitter Side…

>>“But a slight change of subject, is anything wrong with learning *just the notes* for tunes just because i want to play along with everyone else at our session? Learning a tune just enough to be able to fit it into a neat set”

Nothing wrong at all, if that’s what you enjoy doing. But it’s not going to help your fiddle playing at all. It may well hinder it, because once you’ve learned a tune that way it’s very difficult to un-learn it, so that you can play it properly.

And knowing more tunes won’t make you a better fiddler.

Playing a tune naturally and memorising a sequence of notes (or reading them) are completely different tasks, completely different thought processes. Once you can think through a tune and have it come out of your fiddle without really realising that you are playing (as I’m sure you can do with the piano), when playing is as natural and easy as humming, then sessions are a great way of learning new tunes, but they are useless when it comes to learning how to play an instrument.
