Is it ok to write new tunes? (Moving the furniture?)

Hello, I know I just started a thread, but when I was talking to the Whistle player that I mentioned in the other thread (the one I just posted) He said something that really stuck with me, we were talking about how I started playing and all that, but the thing that stuck with me is that I’ve only been playing a very short amount of time and that this tradition means a lot to a lot of people, and that basically some people would view me writing tunes as like me being invited into someone’s house and moving the furniture around. (something like that anyway) Also sorry if I made anyone here feel like that. Now don’t get me wrong the whistle player didn’t discourage me by saying this (He made it very clear that he doesn’t think either side is 100% correct) and he also gave this great story about how he knows/knew two men from Ireland (Sligo I think) who were around the age of 85 and 95 living in Chicago (again I think) still making it around to the sessions every Sunday I don’t know the exact day he said they go play. Anyway they were born in Ireland and raised on the tradition so therefore they had tons of great stories behind the tunes they play. I don’t know how they would feel about me writing new tunes, but the fact of the matter is that I don’t want to overstep my boundaries so to speak by adding to a tradition that I’ve only been a part of for a very short time. I love this tradition and the last thing I want to do is offend those who’ve come before me. If I haven’t stayed true to this statement in the past that please forgive me I just want to have fun playing tunes and though being social isn’t my thing most of the time I have to say that I’ve had great fun with the people in Ann Arbor as well as this site. I also know that I probably won’t be anywhere close to good enough to get into the University of Limerick in a year and a half. (The whistle player also said that the U of L is the way to go for making this a career) I don’t know I guess I just feel bad for coming into this tradition and “moving the furniture around”, so sorry for that.

I don’t know if any of this makes sense (as usual sorry if it doesn’t), but thanks for reading another one of my rambles. :)

Re: Is it ok to write new tunes? (Moving the furniture?)

Of course it’s OK to write new tunes. Everybody does it and in every musical genre. As at least one person mentioned (Reverend?) writing tunes and comparing them to the great tunes you can learn about the way this genre, or whatever one you’re writing in, works. At one time or another everybody draws something or paints a picture. Most of the time they’re just not really any good, modest at best. The hard thing to realize is that the combination of notes we think will change the world, will become tomorrows standards, the ones that are just “so damn cool”, will likely be forgotten before morning. If you dig far enough into the Session’s tune book you’ll see what I mean. So many tunes have risen on a steep arc, everybody played them, then faded like Bieber’s last, current, and future hits. And a few really have what it to takes to stick.

Maybe the best thing is to question why you want to write a tune. Do you have something you want to say? What is it? Do really think the tune adds anything to the world? Have you earned the right to say it ( I’ve been playing this stuff for 12 years now and don’t)? Is this tune something I want to hear? Of course not everything has to be “deep and soulful”. Most often the tunes we come up with are just neat little ditties. Write all the tunes you like, just don’t take them too seriously and, more important, don’t take yourself seriously. Whether what you write is any good or not will come out in time. You can’t force it. I’d avoid self-indulgence at all cost. That’s a musical and social dead-end. As others have said, learn to play, learn what this is all about, then fit yourself into the tradition. You can’t push your way in to it or bend it to fit you. Somebody told me that once early on and I’m for ever grateful. Thanks James Noonan, I hear you.

Re: Is it ok to write new tunes? (Moving the furniture?)

Gobby - yes, I knew that Niel Gow also published his variants of traditional tunes. The extent to which he did has been argued about, and Mary Anne Alburger, for example, thinks claims of plagiarism are often ill-founded. But it is inarguable that he contributed many original tunes to the tradition, which gained new life as a result of his playing and his fame, so my point stands - that it is okay to write new tunes, just as Gow did.

Tradition is a living thing & growth is always possible. If someone writes a tune radically different from traditional styles, that still may be a good tune, and I’d always encourage people to be creative. But if anyone loves the tradition, as I do, he or she will try to use the traditional idioms while adding something of their own, and trying very hard to make sure they don’t accidentally come up with a tune that’s there already but they’ve forgotten.

That’s a point to make - they were written down! This does stand to assist their preservation, and chances of being discovered and maybe taken up by players some long distance into the future. It’s a good idea, IMO, to write down one’s own comps in conventional music notation and keep them in a paper file somewhere, giving copies as and when to such other players as might request them or would seem clearly to welcome them. Re: Is it ok to write new tunes? (Moving the furniture?)

Kellie, you have a certain knack for attracting a bunch of attention in the discussions here. I can see a couple of reasons for that.

The first reason is that you have an infectious enthusiasm for this music that we all love. The last time we saw something like this in the forums started about 5 years ago with Jerone, LoM. Jerone showed the same kind of enthusiasm, and has continued his journey with the music, and now has 5 years of experience under his belt. I know that he has chimed in on a few of your threads, and he might have the best perspective of anyone here for what you’re going through.

The second reason that I can see for your threads attracting so much attention is because you bring up topics that people have differing opinions on, and that tends to get things riled up around here. Maybe less so these days than in years past, which may be a good thing. But some of the most important things I’ve learned from these forums over the years with regards to my own playing have been gleaned from the ashes of a big row, because the topics that people are most passionate about are often some of the most important topics. And having people work so hard to get across their particular point of view can be informative as to why they think those things are important. So there’s a lot to be learned.

To your original question, I kind of like the “moving the furniture” analogy, because I find myself feeling that way a bit with some of your threads about compositions. But the analogy isn’t quite right. Nobody “owns” this music (or maybe we all own it), so nobody should really have any grounds for feeling like their possessions are being threatened by someone who doesn’t have a right to mess with it.

Maybe a better analogy might be the public park where we all come to play frisbee golf. We’ve always come here for years and years, and we’ve used the old growth trees for our golf holes, just like the frisbee players before us. We’ve learned how to approach each goal by playing with the more experienced players. The more we play disc golf in this park, the more we know the nuances of the course - how the wind tends to affect things, what kind of discs to use for what situations, and the best approach to each hole. But now some youngster who really loves to watch disc golf comes along. He decides he wants to be a professional disc golfer someday, but instead of trying to learn the way that the rest of us learned, he decides that it would be easier if he just planted some new trees in the middle of the park to be his goals. In doing that, the youngster may be finding new ways to learn about playing the game, but in the process, he is potentially disrupting other players’ games and ignoring the advise of the more experienced players.

Do I think you should stop composing tunes? Definitely not. As I’ve mentioned before, I think that composing can be a very useful tool for learning more about this music. But the usefulness of those compositions is really for YOU to listen to, and try to make judgements about what sounds right and what doesn’t. The more you learn, the more you will develop your own knowledge of what this tradition is about. But there is no shortcut to becoming a good traditional musician. It’s all about listening, evaluating, learning, playing in different situations, and gradually finding yourself with deeper and deeper understandings of the nuances of the music.

I know I’m rambling a bit here, but I have one more personal note that I would add. It’s admirable to want to play this music as your career. But there are a couple of things about that - that goal would probably be difficult for you to pull off within the years before you’ll need to be making your own living. It would likely take you longer than that to become a good enough musician that you could fully support yourself with the music. Most of the people that actually do make a living with this music also have other professional skill sets. They are doctors, lawyers, plumbers, painters, teachers, software developers, or just about anything. And I personally know some full time Irish musicians that are fairly young, and in some cases, they’re struggling with what their futures will hold - it’s hard to have a steady relationship, buy a house, or raise a family if you’re always on the road. A lot of people struggle with their love for the music when it becomes their job, with the day in and day out grind of having to figure where the next month’s rent payment is coming from. And then, god forbid, what happens if you have an injury or some other circumstance that limits your ability to play music. Would you have enough money put away to make it through something like that? This is the reason that even the most gifted athletes are encouraged to go to college and learn a trade, because it’s difficult to guarantee a future as a professional athlete. The same might apply to becoming a professional Irish trad musician (and probably even more difficult becoming a professional Irish musician when you’re not from Ireland).

So my personal advice would be to learn a trade, while keeping the music as part of you. Then, sometime in the future, you could think about making it into one of the traditional music programs in Ireland. They’ll take you at any age. And maybe that will take some of the pressure that you seem to feel off for now.
