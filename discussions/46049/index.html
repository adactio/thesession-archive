The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

Hi! I know you’ll tell me to ask on Chiff & Fipple. I probably will at some point. But, first, let me ask my old fellow thesessioners. I have a keyless Delrin flute and I’m very happy with it, but now I have a chance to, finally, have my first ever wooden flute. The thing is: since I’m going to be getting my first wooden flute, and I already have a keyless (Delrin) flute, I think I’d like to buy a keyed one. And I can’t decide which keys so I think 6 would be the perfect choice. The problem is: the big names (Olwell, etc.) not only seem to be really expensive but, also, their waiting lists are years long! I’d rather avoid this. Originally my budget is just 1,000-1,200€, and therefore you’ll tell me that this is not enough for a keyed flute and that I should just get a keyless one. However, I might be able to add a few more hundreds of € to the budget.

So, to get to the point: to save me a bit of the investigation, could you summarize for me a little bit the usual prices and waiting times of the main popular maker? I might be wrong but it seems that Gilles Lehart might be the best option in terms of quality, popularity, price and waiting time. However, a 6-keyed D flute of his (€1,660) is more expensive than two of his keyless D flutes (€820) combined! Why is it that keys are so expensive??

BTW: if you’re interested in buying my barely touched MK pro in A (red), let me know.

Re: The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

Almost all of the popular makers are in the 18-24 month range for keyless and 2-3 years for keyed instruments. I wouldn’t rely on any information regarding availability and lead time other than direct from a maker at a particular point in time.

You might find this article interesting: http://www.francoisbaubet.com/p/making-keyed-flute.html

Most of the cost of a keyed instrument is time, craftsmanship not materials. 3-5 times the #hours per instrument for keyed versus keyless.

Re: The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

As with any craft-made custom/bespoke instrument the way to get one immediately is to buy a used one.

Also, I’ve tended to have better success, in the sound and playability quality of the instrument, with buying used, over waiting for years to have a bespoke instrument made. I suppose it’s due to the nature of handcrafted instruments inevitably varying a bit. Probably people will disagree, but in my experience only the very best makers have absolute consistency with their instruments, a superb example being Patrick Olwell. I’ve played quite a few Olwells over the years and they’ve all been fantastic.

In your budget range, why not get a delrin/polypenco/acetal keyed flute?

About the number of keys, I’ve only played vintage 8-key flutes and I’ve used all the keys at one time or other.

If I had to choose I think, for mainstream Irish trad music, the most useful are F (long and short) and G#.

If I had to choose between long F and short F I would choose the long, due to there being nothing it can’t do well. But people do learn to manage quite well with only short F. So two keys, F and G#, would be quite sufficient if we’re talking Irish traditional music. Bb and C natural can be played without keys, and some people with those keys choose to crossfinger those notes anyway.

Re: The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

I agree with Richard on both the second-hand market and (almost) the important keys.

The used instrument is a good way, as they are available “immediately” and if you would check Chiff and Fipple regularly, it is probable it will be way sooner than with the waiting time. They are usually a bit cheaper (well, except the very best makers where buyers are willing to spend more for skipping the queue, eg. Olwell). At least if you play right-handed, I play lefty, have waited 7+ years for my keyed Olwell and have bought used keyed flute during the fifth year of wait…(there were very few other “lefty” options during that time, but plenty “righty”). And you can resell without losing much if you decide another maker or flute (even by the same maker) suits you better.

For Irish music F and G# keys are important, I would too vote for long F rather than short if you need to choose because you can use it “always”, short F is tricky to use when playing sequence of D-F as you use the ring-finger for both D and F key. I use both long and short F depending on the situation but might accommodate to the long F only if needs be, not the other way round. The third key to get is C-nat. I have thought “why? it can be cross-fingered”, but the sound is better and C-nat note is frequent in Irish music. On the other hand Bb can be cross-fingered too and being scarce in Irish music, the key is not crucial. Eb key has not much use for the note itself but is handy for venting -> E note sounds better with pressed Eb key.

Gilles Lehart makes great flutes for a great price. You can order a flute from him with any number of keys and you can have other keys added by him later when you can afford it. He usually replies really quickly. I have keyless Eb and Bb made by him (btw both bought used) and they are brilliant, that Eb is my favorite flute, very very responsive.

Re: The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

I suggest raising your price target. Sometimes you can find a good deal, but flute prices are not whimsical, and the market is reasonably balanced between desirability and quality. Prices really do follow demand.

6-keyed flutes are (usually) more like $3,500, 8-key antiques (without compromises) are quite good by $2,000. You’ll probably be disappointed at anything, modern or antique, below the $1,500 level. One exception might be antiques that don’t play at A440. Are you playing with other people?

If you buy cheaply, then you’re likely to be disappointed, sell and buy again multiple times. Better to save for a decent flute, and buy once. (Which is not quite true, as most flute players still buy several times looking for the perfect flute!)

As to keys:
Eb, G#, now you can play the E-note with clarity, many common accidentals and the sharp keys of A & E.
F-long and Bb, and now you can play flat keys, C, F and Bb. Beware the pathetic left-hand pinky.

Depending on the flute, I disagree that you NEED the C-natural key, although most keyed flutes come with it. You already use cross-fingered C on your keyless, either OXX OOO or OXO XXX, and those work well on most flutes. C-Nat may be necessary on a large-holed flute to bring C# into intonation.

Re: The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

Thinking about ranking keys in order of importance/how often I ended up using them in mainstream Irish trad music, it might go like this

long F > G# > short F > Low C# & C > Eb > high C > Bb.

Now why would I have the footjoint C and C# keys ranked above those others? Because in truth I used them more often. I was playing quite a few tunes that go DCD or DC#D.

Add to that, I got into the habit of copying the fiddler’s thing of scooping up to D on the G string while also playing the open D string, by me playing a low C# gracenote to Bottom D.

As to the Eb key I was also in the habit of copying the piper’s “ghost d”.

I know these aren’t normal flute things but sorry I liked doing them. Now I play Low Whistle and I don’t have any keys at all, one adapts as best one can.

I will always recommend a Copley 6-key. The stretch on the top hand isn’t a stretch at all, and I can usually lip the flat C# in tune if I vent the G# and Bb; I’m not a fan of the long C although the two-piece body enables me to rotate the bottom hand holes toward me a bit and brings the long C into a more usable position. Re: The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

Another recommendation here to keep an eye out for a used wooden flute with keys. It’s the typical solution if you need to keep the cost down, and don’t want to wait a year or more.

I bought my first flute -- a blackwood Windward without keys -- as a new purchase, but it was immediately available through the Irish Flute Store here in the US: https://www.irishflutestore.com. That’s a good place to look for both new and used flutes that are available right now, and I believe the owner ships internationally.

I bought my current Aebi 8-keyed wooden flute used, on Ebay. That was taking a risk, and another risk by having it shipped internationally. However, I had done enough research to know the maker was a respected name, and the seller had a good reputation on Ebay. It turned out to be a great transaction. I was able to save a little money and get into a keyed flute more quickly than with a new order.

That said, I don’t recommend Ebay for secondhand flutes unless you have some knowledge about recent makers. Antique flutes are especially tricky unless you have a wealth of prior knowledge to evaluate the condition, and access to a good woodwind tech if the flute needs work. Buying a used flute from The Irish Flute Store is a safer bet if you want to be assured a recently made or antique flute is playable.

Re: The difficulty of buying (keyed) wooden flutes

First, you say you have a keyless delrin. I do/did too, a Somers. For not all that much Maurice Reviol added first 6 keys and later the other two. If you like, and can get the sound you like from the flute you own, that’s an option. Some years ago I thought a wooden flute ( a Grinter) would give me a better sound and make me a better player because it was , well, wood. It didn’t. Turns out the problem was me. Buying better didn’t ever make me better. The "take-away’ is to be honest with yourself. When you can play the hell out of the one you have then think about keys and wood. Too, remember that wood doesn’t make a good flute nearly as much as architecture and quality of construction.

As for wooden flute makers look at Tony Millyard. I have an 8 key Pratten (the 6 key would be more inside your budget) that sounds and plays wonderfully. He was delightful to work with and the wait was just a few months, even less if you want a Rudall (6) and he has some un-keyed in stock. The keys are post mounted, my preference, and very nicely done. You get to pick your keys and others can be added later. By the way, I wouldn’t have a keyed flute without the Eb.

Good luck. I wish someone had told me this years ago. I could have saved money and aggravation. Actually they did. I just was too stubborn to listen. It’s been a lifelong character flaw.

Wisdom there, Ross.

We get wrapped up in The Quest For The Holy Grail of flutes (or whatever instrument we play).

Sometimes we already have the ideal instrument for our own particular way of playing, but we’re oddly blind to it. Foolishly we cast it aside and continue the Quest, ending up with something that isn’t quite as good.

The ONE instrument I demonstrated any kind of perspicacity with was the Uilleann chanter I bought around 1980 from David Quinn, at that time regarded as the finest Rowsome-style Concert chanter maker.

I still play that chanter! I’ve never had reason to get another.
