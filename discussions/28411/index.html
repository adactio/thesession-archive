UIlleann chanters

A recent thread has brought a question to my mind regarading learning the pipes, and chanters.

There are keyed and un-keyed chanters my own being un-keyed, and my questions are these:

How tough might it be to make the transition from un-keyed to keyed?

Have any of our pipers here started with un-keyed and then switched?

What problems/challenges did they encounter, if any?

My main reason for asking is, I suggested a set of Daye pipes as an intro set for the member who posted the other day. Was I potentially causing problems down the road if the recipient of the pipes was going to want to play a keyed chanter? In other words, did I, in effect, recommend practicing mandolin as an intro to fiddle (which sounds like a great idea but does not always work out in the long run).
:-/

Re: UIlleann chanters

1. Depends on the chanter. If the maker has built key blocks on it, they can later add keys, although the downside is that drilling extra holes can alter the tuning, if the chanter was originally made without said holes. Mine has four keys but has a hole for a fifth, should I ever want it.

2. Yeah, I made that transition. My first chanter only had the Cnat key and the one I have now has Cnat, Fnat, Bb, and Gsharp. The only difficulty I had was with tunes I had already learned where I “faked” those notes, half-holing (in the case of Fmats) or playing around them. I *still* need to sort out McFadden’s Handsome Daughter, which I learned ages ago on the keyless chanter and dodge the Gsharps. The way I play it is a bit sh*t.

There is a bit of a learning curve in incorporating the keys smoothly into your playing. If you’ve been playing a keyless chanter for years, your hands have to learn a new motion in order to strike the keys in a way that doesn’t throw off the timing. It took a bit to get this. But it only matters on tunes where you use the keys. For all your squillion tunes that fit in keys the chanter actually likes, it doesn’t matter at all.

Re: UIlleann chanters

Unless someone invents a more efficient, easier to use g# key (and it’s been 250 years since the instrument was invented) I don’t think McFadden’s Handsome Daughter will ever be a stanadard part of the piping repertoire. That has to be the worst, most difficult note on the entire instrument to play consistently and not sound like sh*t.. I mean, C nat is enough of a challenge to keep a beginner busy for a while and so is Fnat. Bflat is actually pretty easy but it isn’t needed that much, so it’s a win-win. Which leaves G sharp. If you half hole it, you have to be very precise with a rather weak finger; if you use the key, you have to learn how to strike it with the thumb of your bottom hand without taking your fingers off the chanter. It’s very awkward and a terrible amount of effort for very little reward, musically speaking. If you want to drive a piper out of a session, fiddlers, just play tunes in A major all night. They’ll take the hint.

Re: UIlleann chanters

Oftentimes the key touches on uilleann chanters are placed somewhat illogically, not very convenient for any digit.

My 1970’s Quinn D 5-key chanter is a painstakingly accurate copy of a Rowsome, including the keywork, and the Bb touch is so placed that it’s difficult to reach with the LH little finger, so I reach round with my thumb.

In any case, I would guess that most pipers started off with keyless or one-key chanters on practice sets, then got fully keyed chanters later. I have a photo of Paddy Moloney as a young lad playing a keyless chanter on a practice set.

Yes sometimes there’s a bit of a transition going from a keyless & blockless chanter to a fully keyed chanter, because you have to get used to the feel of all those blocks. I’ve never known it to be a real hindrance to anyone.

Re: UIlleann chanters

I never felt there was any technical transition. I rarely use the nat key because I don’t like the tone of the keyed note, g sharp only occasionally (playing tunes like the Fairy Queen maybe), bflat is rare enough, c for the high note and occasionally for special effects. High D I’d play without the key but the key comes in handy again for the occasional rare special bit. Some keys are handy to vent when reaching for the third octave but that’s more a gimmick than anything else (I mean, it’s interesting to tip up to third G but never required in anything I’d play).

In all, the addition of high C easy G sharp, Bflat and if you like Fnat broadens your spectrum. Tunes in A still sound like sh*te over the drones so they’re not worth bothering with.

Re: UIlleann chanters

I don’t understand how tunes in A Major can sound bad against the drones, while tunes in A Mixolydian and A Dorian are presumably deemed to be fine.

All have the tonal centre of A, and thus have exactly the same relationship to the D drones.

Now, if your G# note is out of tune, it will sound bad, just like any other out of tune note will. The answer is to fix that note on your chanter, just as you would fix any other out of tune note.

Anyhow about the usefulness of the keys, I bought a 5-key chanter back in the 1970s which I still play, and I have used every key on it at some gig or other. (The chanter has long Fnat, G#, Bb, high Cnat, and high D.)

But I don’t use any of the keys very often, and 99% of what I play would go just fine on a keyless chanter.
