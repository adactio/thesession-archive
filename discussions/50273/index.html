How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

Added by Alannah Ní Thuathaigh on June 4th, 2025

How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

I sometimes sing things in French, Irish, or Middle English, and when I am doing this I simply pronounce it in a relatively textbook manner since these are entirely foreign languages altogether. In English I always sing in my own accent; I don’t like to change my accent whether it is for folk, classical, or contemporary music.

However, when I have songs in Scots - especially when they are a slightly less broad version of Scots - I don’t know whether I should simply change up the words/pronunciation to my own more standard dialect of English (very soft/not broad Hiberno English) - but then I feel that would be a bit disrespectful and also, sometimes the words would not longer rhyme in more standard English. However, if I try and sing it as people would pronounce it in Scots - it kind of just sounds like I am putting on a silly accent or that I am exaggerating my Irish accent. It is an awkward middle between being an entirely foreign language, and a dialect which is not my own.

Re: How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

@ Will Evans: I don’t think learning from paper comes into it, Grammar and vocabulary may come from paper, but accent doesn’t. Nor does it come directly from the ear: it comes from the accents you hear all around you in the first couple of decades of your life. Into your twenties and beyond you may be able to consciously override your accent temporarily, but it is extremely difficult to change it completely. That is why when people speak a foreign language you can almost always identify their native tongue. Your brain does a lot of processing on sound and language at the subconscious level, before you actully ‘hear’ it. If a frenchman learns English from you he won’t speak back to you with your accent, he will speak back to you with a French accent. That isn’t because he hasn’t yet mastered English, it is because that is how he actually hears you speak - he is exactly replicating the sounds he heard you make, but his brain has done some basic processing and slightly altered the rhythms and cadences to make it more intelligible to him. The same thing happens with instrumental music once you have been playing long enough to develop a personal style: your style will mimic the rhythms and cadences of the language you speak, and if you learn a tune aurally from someone from a different language area, although you may think you are mimicking him perfectly, but because as you heard him play your brain was already imposing your accent on what you heard, what you play back will unmistakably carry your accent to any third party listening.

Re: How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

It’s a tough question!

One aspect is a longstanding thing of pop/rock musicians, wherever they’re from, singing in a semi-American accent, often a semi-Southern US accent.

My wife and I heard Scottish people in Oban singing Scottish songs in a fake US Southern accent. (I’m guessing they also had Country-Western songs in their repertoire.)

It’s really tough for Americans singing English, Scots, and Irish English-language songs. On one hand singing them in American accents makes them sound like American songs, on the other hand doing fake English, Scots, and Irish accents can sound like parody, unless done really well.

I’ve heard Americans do a good job of going the middle path, the so-called Mid-Atlantic thing, sounding neither too American nor like parody.

With songs in the Scots/Lallans dialect it’s especially tough. I’ve known Scots who effortlessly slid back and forth along the continuum between “broad Scots” (practically unintelligible) and Standard Scottish English. Most English speakers don’t have that built-in ability and have hard choices to make.

It comes up every year at the dreaded American Robert Burns Suppers at which people who can’t pronounce Scots stumble through poems that the listeners can’t understand. With poetry you have sound and meaning working together, except at these events, where the poems are stripped of both. To get the meaning they’d have to be translated into Standard English, to get the sound they’d need to be read by either native Lallans speakers or by linguists. Only with both readers and listeners being Scots speakers do the poems have their intended effect.

Re: How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

As a Scottish (non-singing) musician, I have to say that I really like when people make the effort to retain the actual words, grammar and pronunciation of a Scots song - and not subsitute words and phrases in English because its easier for them and perhaps others to understand. Particularly with non-dialect words and phrases which, whilst they can be “translated” into English, then lose their original heart if not meaning and, at least to this Scottish listener, can slip into becoming twee pastiche. Substituting English words seldom produces an elegant solution ……….. It’s a talented person who can translate lyrics, poetry or prose into another language, yet retain the essence of the author’s original text.

So for the non-Scots looking to sing Scots songs, I would advise you to pay your respects to the poet by just singing the words as originally written. You’d be daft, disrespectful and destined to fail if you attempted to change the simple beauty of the lyrics of Violet Jacob’s “The Wild Geese”, James McQueen’s “Oh dinna bide ower lang awa”, or Robert Burns’s “Of a’ the airts”. It does require a bit of work on your part - so roll up your sleeves / do the hard yards, and you’ll reap the rewards.

I am similarly disappointed when I hear people taking short-cuts and flattening out the angular edges when playing strathspeys. The more complex and refined slow strathspeys by the likes of Marshall and Nathaniel Gow can lend themselves to being interpreted by classically trained musicians - and as such can work fairly well as “pieces”. But the older, simple Strathspeys need to be driven and have that fire within them that only comes alive when played properly. However it does take time to learn the specific techniques necessary to play them authentically. I warm to those who have, or are, making the effort, and even if their technique is short of perfect, I appreciate the time, love and dedication that they give to learning the tradition.

In relation to learning Scots songs, or in fact traditional tunes as a whole, the key is to spend time listening. It beats me how anyone can hope to learn to sing / play authentically if you only learn from reading lyrics / musical notation. By immersing yourself listening to the music and primarily learning by ear you have the best chance to be able to recreate the authentic sounds of the tradition. Of course that depends upon how good an ear you have, how well connected your ears are to your vocal chords / fiddle arm / fingers, and how much music you have in your soul …… I’ve listened to plenty trained, accomplished and “talented” musicians who wow the punters with playing at 125mph, but whose music, behind the disguise of speed and technical fireworks, is dead and lifeless: and yet some other musicians, (even learners who struggle with the mechanics of their instruments), have that indefinable quality that marks them apart - they play with soul and musical depth, silencing a room and evoking an emotional response in those around them. They are a joy, both to listen to, and to play along with.

Re Howard’s assertions that “No one knows what Old Lang Syne actually means, but they sing it anyway. The other verses are untranslatable and are seldom sung.” ……. Well, both those statements are quite obviously not correct. Five minutes on Google, Wikipedia or with a Scots dictionary will give anyone who is sufficiently curious the gist of the meaning of the title of the song and its lyrics.

Re: How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

In reply to ffiill, it’s important to distinguish between language and accent. Language is vocabulary and grammar, accent is how they are pronounced. Scotland has three, and possibly four, distinct linguistic groups. For the purposes of this discussion we can exclude Gaelic, an entirely different language with entirely different roots. That presents the same challenges as as singing in any foreign language. What we are discussing here is how to sing songs written in Scottish variants of English. Should you adopt a Scottish accent? If so, which one, and can you avoid sounding like a stage Scotsman? Can you still make the song your own? Or do you try to sing it in your own accent using language natural to you?

Most Scots today speak a version of standard English, albeit (as I said earlier) one with a lot of dialect words (but the same is true of other regions of the British Isles). They speak it with an accent (and Scotland has many accents), but once you get past that they can be understood by any English speaker.

Then there is Scots, also a version of English but one with roots in a different dialect of Anglo-Saxon from standard English. This is the language of Burns, and whilst other English speakers might get the gist of its meaning most will struggle to understand it fully without a dictionary.

Some claim that Doric from the North East is a separate language, but even the University of Aberdeen describes it as a version of Scots. Either way, it’s difficult for a speaker of standard English to understand without a dictionary.

A digression: I once had professional dealings with Japanese manager of an air-freight company based at Glasgow Airport. He spoke English with a very strong Paisley accent overlaid with his own Japanese accent. Whenever he phoned me I knew immediately who it was, but could not understand a word. I usually got around this by asking him to put it in writing so I could take it up with my manager.

Re: How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

“Real” is how you speak normally without thinking about it. Many of us have composite accents influenced by (for example) where we grew up, where we now live, our education. In Britain class is an important modifier. We may also speak onr way in formal situation such as work, another way in informal situations with friends, yet another way with family. Who hasn’t been accused of having a “telephone voice”?

For the the last 45 years I’ve lived 2oo miles from where I grew up. In England that is a vast distance linguistically. My southern friends think I’ve picked up a bit of a northern accent, which my northern friends find hilarious. I do notice that I speak a bit differently whenever I return to where I grew up.

The point is, these are all natural to us. Of course they were learned, but it was by unconsciously adapting to our environment rather than by conscious study (apart perhaps from those who take elocution lessons in the hope of improving their social status and employability).

To consciously imitate a completely different accent requires considerable skill. It requires intense listening to understand and then mimic the sounds and speech patterns in a way which sounds natural and effortless. Most of us don’t have that skill (even professional actors sometimes don’t succeed) and that’s when it sounds “fake”. The question then is whether it matters when singing a song.

If you were to sing a song in French, would you still use your own accent? Leaving aside the nonsense of dialect v. language, Scots is still just a way of speaking shared by a group of speakers, and they have an accent. If you speak that way of speaking, you adopt that accent, and if you sing in that way of speaking, learn to do justice to the accent. Yes, it’s fraught, and ripe for misinterpretation, for reasons far too interesting to go into here, but it’s not a fundamentally weird thing to do when you think about it for two seconds.

The linguist Max Weinreich stated that “a language is a dialect with its own army and navy” ……. “thus identifying the arbitrary and often political nature of the distinction between a language and a dialect. He suggested that the status of a language is frequently determined by political factors, rather than purely linguistic ones.”

There are many examples in Europe and further afield of countries which have colonised their neighbours and have used their own “standard” unifying language as a colonialisation tool to centralise and enforce control - both political and cultural. The Germans in the Baltic republics, Denmark and Sweden in Norway, numerous regional languages within France, Gaelic in Ireland, Russian in Ukraine (and in fact in many of the former Soviet Republics) as well as Gaelic and Scots in Scotland. This standardised “official” language was usually the language of schooling and thus of “educated” people and was seen as necessary “to get ahead” socially or for work. Howard mentioned class, (public) school education and elocution lessons, all of which are part of this in a British context. The “standard” language / dialect / accent was the voice of the ruling elite, the broadcast media, landowners, teachers, and of the educated. The native language / dialect / accent was perceived as being the preserve of the poor, the uneducated and the rural peasants.

Re: How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

I intend no disrespect to Scots, and I have made it plain throughout that it is a distinct variant which evolved from a different root from what has become “standard” English. I certainly did not intend imply that it is inferior, or simply a dialect of standard English. The two languages evolved quite separately and alongside one another from different dialects of Anglo-Saxon. However as an English speaker it is to me a foreign language - there are enough similarities for me to make a guess at the meaning, but to understand it properly I need a translation. I could have a go at imitating it phonetically (and might do so in private) but for a public performance that would feel to me as if I am putting on an act - it would not be me singing it. So I tend to avoid songs in Scots (and French for that matter) because I don’t feel I can do them justice.

The same goes for some English songs where the language is too distinctive and the accent too strong - Geordie for example. If the regionality is too important to the character of the song and changing it would do too much damage then I won’t sing it.

When I talk about substituting the occasional word I am talking about songs in English, whether from Scotland or regions of England. I will sing them in my usual accent, and (for example) will sing “more” rather than “mair”, and “if” instead of “gin”. I might alter a dialect word if it is not one which feels natural to me, or which might not be understood by my audience (although sometimes I might retain it and just explain the meaning). I might take verses from other versions. I am a singer and not a scholar, and my aim is to find songs which feel natural and appropriate for me to sing. That means using my own accent and language. This is after all what the original singers did, which is why there are often so many different versions of traditional songs.

You asked whether “something included in a ‘musical museum’ may have an element of preservation.” Yes, maybe there was an element of that. When the songs in the Scots Musical Museum were being gathered in the 1880s / 90s, it was all very much influenced by the earlier English anti-Scottish backlash caused by the Jacobite rebellions, with the subsequent “discovery” of Scotland and the Highlands by the likes of Boswell and Johnston, and its eventual romanticisation by Sir Walter Scott et al. There was a fear (which has been / is perennial) that Scottish culture was going to be subsumed by its more economically dominant and populous southern neighbour, and that we therefore had to fight to retain our sense of identity and culture. Hence Burns clubs, the flowering of Scottish fiddle music, piping etc. Of course in the early 1800s the Scottish aristocracy leapt on this bandwagon as a means of differentiating themselves from all the other Eton-educated landowners), dressing up in their Highland costumes, festooning themselves in tartan, bagpipes and tweed (along with the wealthy industrialists who followed Queen Victoria’s purchase of Balmoral to grab a bit of the romantic Highland action). All that faux tartan cosplay is still with us today.

“I am also wondering if the orthography (spelling etc) is aimed at a Scots or a Scottish English speaking reader.”
From what I have read, I am pretty sure the orthography is aimed at the Scots speaking reader. To do otherwise would be a whole lot harder, and perhaps to little effect. You occasssionally see pronunciation guides for English speakers, but as a Scotsman, I shake my head at how hoplessly inaccurate it is. A Scottish accent is really hard to imitate properly, and most people who try fall woefully short (think Mel Gibson in Braveheart, or any number of cartoon voice-over actors rolling their Rs and sounding very “see you Jimmy”). My favourite example is the film Highlander which cast a Frenchman, Christopher Lambert, in the lead (with an accent that was a mix of ‘Allo ’Allo and Scotty from Star Trek), an English wife who had been coached to sound pure dead Glaswegian, and Sean Connery, the only real Scot there, playing a Shpanish prinsh. Masterful.

Aberdeenshire Doric and the Scandi-influenced Orkney and Shetland dialects are utterly different from a Glaswegian accent, or someone from the Mearns, Fife, or the Borders ….. Before the days of being able to actually listen to recordings, the use of phonetics to “spell” the pronunciation looks like it was a fairly blunt instrument …….

“Is Hamish Henderson’s Scots the same as Burn’s? ”
Hmmm, probably no, not exactly. Hamish Henderson was hugely influenced by the Scots poet Hugh MacDairmid who constructed a new form of Scots as a way of forcing the issue …… From reading around the subject, looking at examples from elsewhere, this attempt to create a high-culture form of Scots complete with its own orthography, also happened in other countries faced with their languages and culture being subsumed by a dominant political / economic neighbour / occupier. So there are similar examples in Finland, Norway, Brittany?, the Baltic republics, Kazakhstan, Ukraine …… ….

Please accept that all the above is only my opinion, and someone better versed in this subject would give you a more accurate view of it all.

Re: How should I sing/pronounce Scots songs?

One thing that hasn’t been commented on is that accents are regarded differently depending on whether they are applied in one’s own language or in a foreign language. One of the difficulties of learning foreign languages is that they often have sounds that do not exist in the learner’s language, so a learner will tend to unconsciously substitute sounds from their own language. Many English people learning Spanish will order a beer saying “una cehveza, poh favoh” because in English they don’t pronounce R after vowels, so it’s a learning curve to learn to say ceRveza and poR favoR.

Despite such difficulties, most people regard it as desirable to acquire the (or a) correct foreign accent when speaking a foreign language. By contrast, adopting a different accent in one’s own language tends to be regarded as fake or pretentious and is rarely done by anyone except professional actors. But singing seems to be treated differently. In the UK most people who sing pop and related genres tend to adopt a pseudo-American or mid-Atlantic accent, probably because that is how they have heard the songs. And the trend seems to be growing - when I hear gospel choirs from London they seem to sing hymns with American accents consistently. That is something that doesn’t seem to bother audiences; in fact, most probably expect it. But to me it seems fake. Many years ago I was a singer in a bluegrass band and felt obliged to sing American, but gave it up when I decided it was fakery. I sing English and Irish songs using my own Scottish accent.

Aye, it’s a bit of a mein field out there. Which accents can a singer acceptably use without offending some other group / ethnicity / country? You would think that if the song is in the same language as the singer’s native tongue, then things would be (relatively) straightforward. But alas, that is not how the real world seems to actually pan out is it? Putting on an American accent for a rock or pop song is absolutely the norm, no matter where you come from. No-one batts an eyelid. But what about, for example, putting on a working-class accent if you are actually from a privileged public-school, middle or upper-class background? Mumford & Sons, Chris Martin of Coldplay or particularly Lily Allen with her Cockney (mockney?) accent come to mind ……. Trying to appear to the masses that you are really one of them (when you clearly aren’t) doesn’t tend to go down well ……remember public school educated Tony Blair putting on a mock estuarine accent dropping his t’s and h’s (bo’ull of woh’uh) in order to try to appeal to a wider demographic ……….

It has to be recognised that the boundary between what is seen as acceptable and what is not is always governed by the (ever changing) values of the wider society. In the 1970s The Black and White Minstrel Show was a Saturday evening light entertainment TV staple. It involved white British singers blackening up and singing songs whilst putting on cod African-American accents from the Southern States. Yup, really.

When I was a young lad starting secondary school in the late 1970s, our music lessons consisted of singing from the school’s song book (purchased no doubt 10 if not 20 or more years before). Some song lyrics were written to convey what it was assumed was the voice of an African-American. One such song I remember singing was “Old Folks at Home”:

"Way down upon de Swanee ribber,
Far, far away,
Dere ’s wha my heart is turning ebber,
Dere’s wha de old folks stay.
All up and down de whole creation
Sadly I roam,
Still longing for de old plantation,
And for de old folks at home.

CHORUS.
All de world am sad and dreary,
Ebry where I roam;
Oh, darkeys, how my heart grows weary,
Far from de old folks at home!"

What is relevant here, is that in the late 1970s, this (along with Robertsons “Golly” marmalade and a host of childrens books etc) was seen in societal terms as acceptable. Of course now this would be seen as absolutely unacceptable and as racist. Things change.

So what should a non-Scot do when looking to sing songs written in Scots? How to navigate this tricky tightrope without being insensitive or offending anyone? I have my own opinions, but I’m certain that I wouldn’t want to put myself on a pedestal to become the arbiter on this (or any other similar) subject.

However, by way of positive encouragement, I think Canadian singer Bruce Coughlan makes a fine job of “Ower Lang Awa’” his interpretation of a lovely poem / song written in Scots by James McQueen of Forres (1852- 1928).

As an aside to this, there has been much debate as to whether Scots is an accent, or number of them, a dialect or number of them, or a language. Here is the official view:
“Today, the UK government accepts Scots as a regional language and recognised it under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages.”

Having spent the latter half of my life living in Scotland, surrounded by friends who sing in Scots whether they are Scottish or not, I’m afraid I’m not going to change. Of all the songs I sing, I would guess that at least half of them are Scottish, with varying proportions of Scots words included. And I’m not going to stop playing strathspeys or pipe marches either, so there! I have spent at least 20 years as a volunteer organiser with the TMSA (Traditional Music and Song Association of Scotland) whose remit is to promote and sustain Scots music and song, as well as even longer with Edinburgh-based Scots Music Group. Only once in all that time have I been told to “sing in your own accent”.
