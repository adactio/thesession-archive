Are we “cheating” the composers when we learn to play by ear?

Before you all have a go at me, I ask the above question rather mischievously..
:-)

Comment has often been made here about posting “abcs” of tunes when there are published settings available for purchase by living composers. It is regarded by many people to be a little unfair although, of course, many composers don’t really object and are often quite delighted that their music is being passed on and shared.

However, surely “abcs” are just another way of passing on tunes in the tradition even although we might all agree that this isn’t the ideal way to learn?
So, is teaching a fellow musician a tune “by ear” or learning one yourself this way any less unfair to a musican who may rely on the sale of written sheet sheet to help earn a living?

Should we all purchase a copy or copies of the tune, in any case, whether we actually need them or not if we are are going to play it in public? (We don’t actually play from the music, of course)

As I say, this thread is very much “tongue in cheek” but my personal view is that good traditional tunes take on a life of their own and can’t really be imprisoned or “locked up” in books etc only to be played or passed on with permission. No matter how much some people might wish.

Re: Are we “cheating” the composers when we learn to play by ear?

Been there done that. See if Jeremy zaps by 2:30 PM GMT ;-)

Seriously though, notwithstanding a post a couple of days ago about applicability of various countries‘ copyright applicability to “The Music” (vis-a-vis my comment about Hal Leonard owning the copyright for the two major O’Neil collections) the question is interesting.

I would take the traditional view that the tunes that trace into the mist of the Irish Tradition are public domain (unless of course they are incorporated int o some current copyrightable work- specific arrangement like the some of things the Celtic Ladies and Gaelic guys do to pander to the Cruise Boat audiences.)

So ‘by ear’ generates no disrespect. That’s the way they were handed down. Given the historic circumstances, this music survived because they had to be carried from one generation to another ‘by ear’. I would offer that not handing them down ‘by ear’ is a diminshment of the tradition.

Not that using spots is wrong. But we need to keep the method alive as a tribute to the people who kept the music alive until Chief o’Neill and others could safely write things down without fear of the paper being destoryed for political reasons.

That being said. The new compositions create a responsibility of attribution. For traditional use in sessions and [ersonal play on one’s back porch, the collections I have seem recently- notably Liz Carroll’s really wonderful collection that she released in 2010 in Catskills- does not have detailed accompaniment or chords suggesting that there is some flexibility of interpretation as long as the ‘bones’ are respected, and that the tune is properly attributed (and compensated to Liz if it is recorded or done in any other for-profit venue). I don’t recall if it was ASCAP.

The OP poses two questions actually. And If Jeremy reads this, IMHO they are probably a very good, though a bit technical, discussion for those of us who wander the countryside looking for audiences to hear our humble scratching and squeaking out of ‘The Music’.
