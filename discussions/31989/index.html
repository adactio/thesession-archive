Re: ABC notation and a few good tunes to learn?

I would think that the best way to use ABC is to run it through a program, rather than trying to learn how to read it. If you can figure out how to select a tune, set a suitable speed and hit the play button, that’s all you need. The rest is just finding the notes on the mandolin and reproducing them. What the tune sounds like in ABC us no better or worse than what it looks like -- it’s the same information in a different form, that’s all.
The bottom string on the mandolin is G, with a comma, then you go up the scale A, B, C D E F G A B then repeat in lower-case letters, c d e (open top string, then continue up f g a b then add apostrophes c‘ d’ e‘ f’ g‘ a’ b‘ then c’’ if you can get that high.
Square brackets [ ] are used for enclosing sections to repeat, and along with a bar-line ( | ) to signify the end of a measure.

Re: ABC notation and a few good tunes to learn?

Hi BlindBard,

To sort of repeat what Gam said, early on, but in a different way that I hope might reinforce or make clearer, middle “C” on the mandolin can be found at the fifth fret of the lowest, bass G strings. An octave higher, the lower-case “c” can be found at the third fret of the A strings.

The “_” symbol means that the note that follows right after it is played flat. The “^” symbol means that the note should be played sharp. Also, the “=” sign means that the note is played natural, neither sharp nor flat.

You might also encounter the use of square brackets “[“ and “]” to enclose a group of notes and that means they are meant to be played together. For example, if you were supposed to play the two middle pairs of strings on the mandolin with no fingers down, it could be notated

[DA]

The square brackets are also used to mark the ends of sections, as mentioned above. It’s easy to tell what is meant by how the symbols are used in their context.

And a really good tune to play is

Rolling In The Ryegrass, in the key of D:
https://thesession.org/tunes/87

Hope this helps!

Re: ABC notation and a few good tunes to learn?

I wonder how your interface interprets ABC notation, BlindBard. Does it tell you if a letter is uppercase or lowercase? Because if it does not, that might be the source of some confusion. Upper-case “A” would usually be played on the open A string, but a lower-case “a” is usually played on the e string at the fifth fret. “A” with a comma after it is played on the G string at the second fret.

The way the notation works is that notes below middle C have commas after them. From middle C up to B the notes are written in capitals. For the next octave all the notes are written in lower case. The octave above that all the notes are followed by an apostrophe or accent mark.

On mandolin in Irish music you hardly ever go above the fifth fret except on the “e” strings where you might go up to the 7th fret and play a “b”. That’s a lower-case “b”. One of the things I like about Irish music is that it’s relatively simple, physically. It doesn’t have to be complicated or fancy. You can play it slow. You can play it fast. You can play it alone. You can play it with a rhythm back-up. You can play it with a band. And it sounds great each way if you play with feeling.

Re: ABC notation and a few good tunes to learn?

I don’t know if your reader differentiates between caps and lower-case, but the difference is vital if you want to read ABC.
The second fret of the G string is A followed by a comma, the open A string is just an A on its own, and the A on the e string is lower-case a on its own. The next a up if you could get that high is a followed by an apostrophe. The notes change from capitals to lower-case when you reach a C. Starting on your open G string and going up a fret at a time, the notes are as follows

G, ^G, (or _A,) A, _B, (or ^A,) B, then you get to C so there is no comma from now on. Next is ^C then the seventh fret is D the same as your open D string

crossed with Fidkid

On your D string, the first fret is ^D (or _E) then E F ^F G ^G A which is the same as your open second string

next is first fret _B B then the first of the lower-case letters c at your third fret
then ^c d ^d and e the same as your open top string

going up the top string you have f at the first fret then ^f g ^g a _b b then the first of your notes followed by an apostrophe c‘ at the eighth fret. You will rarely go any higher than that in Irish music, but the notes just follow the same sequence ^c’ d‘ ^d’ e‘ f’ ^f‘ g’ etcetera

It looks more complicated than it is. Basically a comma after a letter means it is an octave lower, and an apostrophe means it is an octave higher. The two octaves between are just the capitals CDEFGAB followed by lower-case cdefgab

If anyone spots any mistakes in this post, please point them out.

Re: ABC notation and a few good tunes to learn?

While I’m at it, a couple of comments on the statement that “the only way to learn this music is by hanging out with musicians that do this music and playing it continuously”

No question that is the best way. It assumes, however, that the person wanting to learn has access to those musicians on a continuous basis. This is only true if you live close by to musicians that are capable of playing and willing to share. People who do not have continuous access, either by distance or disability, need ways to bridge the gap, and for the rest of us, any tools that can make it easier are fair game. In a session, the top players will usually let you know the names of the tunes in that great set, but they may not have time to teach a beginner to play. Abc is just one of the ways to share that information around. Everyone has to start somewhere, and it’s part of the toolset that helps you up the mountain.

(end soapbox)

Then,around the age of 5 and1/2 you break into the cupboard where the instruments are kept while your da is out tinkering. Re: ABC notation and a few good tunes to learn?

“how fast-slow is this supposed to be played?” Play it as slowly as you need in order not to make a mistake. When you can play it through three times the way you want to, increase the speed a notch (there is nothing wrong with using a metronome, although it can take some getting used to playing with it as opposed to fighting it) and repeat. Once in a while play faster than is comfortable, in order to build up the speed muscles; but generally if you can’t play it slowly, you can’t play it, period.
There was a discussion about speed / tempo a few weeks ago with lots of useful (?) replies :)
https://thesession.org/discussions/31896

Re: ABC notation and a few good tunes to learn?

“tunes that are in the key of D major all use the D scale”
It is rather the other way round -- if a tune uses all or most of the notes in the D scale, the convention is to say that it is written or played in the key of D Major. While the convention can help to give you a good idea of the notes and intervals that will probably be found in the tune, it can be misleading, especially in Irish music, which developed separately from what is usually called ‘western’ music. Sometimes it is hard even to decide what key an Irish tune is in, there can be so many accidentals and ‘key changes’; and there is often encountered a tendency to shoehorn a tune into the key that has been chosen for it, for example playing notes sharp ‘because that’s how they are in the scale’. The best thing to do, I think, is to accept the recommended key along with a large pinch of salt, and be aware that the ‘usual’ conventions are a bit more lax.
