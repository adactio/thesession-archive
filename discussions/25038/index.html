Re: New PRS Session Tariff - illegal/unworkable?

Well if that was the case there would be no problem. Unfortunately, as you’ll see if you visit the fRoots site, PRS’s approach is that if one tune that they do control (AF, for example, is registered in the PRS database to both Jay Ungar and - wrongly, but that’s another matter - Sean McGuire) is played, then a licence IS required.

And they include not only new tunes which they control, but also all public domain material on the basis that the session may ‘accidentally’ (complex issue, see fRoots) play a ‘registered arrangement’ thereof. And it doesn’t even have to be an existing arrangement - it could be one registered in future and then claimed retrospectively.

We do need much clearer guidelines on what constitutes an arrangement because at the moment PRS are having their cake and eating it - and this is also something I’m trying to get sorted out.

Quite a few well known session tunes are in fact copyright to PRS members. OK, they may not want PRS to collect on these when played in sessions, but PRS thinks it should - hence why I’m campaigning for a ‘creative commons’ check box in the PRS registration process.This would allow tune writers to elect to collect only on ‘proper’ performances (i.e. effectively only their own gigs and recordings), and have their work treated as PD in sessions and/or when not money is changing hands.

Without this PRS can continue to hassle the landlords of sessions that take place in pubs without a normal licence.

The new tariff seems to be an attempt to resolve this problem, but in my opinion it does not.

Re: New PRS Session Tariff - illegal/unworkable?

Hi Mix

The idea is to have a tariff that allows some flexibility as a goodwill gesture if nothing else - and try to be fairer than the current system.

I’m suggesting that there would still be a licence, but it would be set at a rate to reflect an average across the board, and across the land. So the PRS would still have funds from the low tariff to cover royalty payments. (And they’d still get fees at the current rate from actual gigs and broadcasts).

I had a concerted go at Jeremy about attribution when I first joined this site but without any response. I’m even more concerned about MySpace and YouTube not having a field for displaying composers/authors because it erodes writers‘ rights. When someone uploads a cover of one of my songs it says, for example "The Violin by Joe Bloggs’ - implying he wrote it.

I’m trying to find a system that would work for tune sessions, song sessions, mixed sessions AND gigs, while still allowing authors and composers to get their just dues when they want them.

On the whole we can assume that most tunes played in sessions are either trad or ‘unofficial creative commons’, so all we need to do is formalise the creative commons aspect, and set a very low tariff to cover the few where the writer does actually want a royalty..

For songs you can generally assume that anything well known written in the last 100 years will be copyright, unless it’s by a local hero. Again, it’s a compromise, but I think we could settle on a rate that would average out across the board.

Posting an ABC of a copyright work might be in infringement (copyright also resides in books and sheet music separate to the music it represents), but it wouldn’t bother PRS as they’re only concerned with performance. The composer or publisher might sue, but it would be very unlikely and not a factor in this debate.

A minefield perhaps, but one with a nice safe motorway right through the middle!

I’ve now set out my suggestions in the fRoots thread.

Tom

BMI and ASCAP have easily accessible websites where you can search for their rates and terms & conditions and whatnot.

Neither of them is in the business of putting live venues out of business. However they do apparently hire freelance - I guess they could be called “investigators”? - who get paid by the number of new rate-paying venues they bring in. Which sounds reasonable, because there’s lots of places out there, and because expecting those venues to self-report is probably unrealistic. However some of those freelancers can be on the shady side.

Those “investigators” have no authority to shut any place down. Most they are authorized to do is report back to HQ. And the most HQ can do is - if the venue is big enough to be worth it, or if it’s a slow day at the office - send a letter, in which case all the venue has to do is reply that they have a Public Domain Only session once a week.

Now, if the venue is a bar that hosts cover bands - bands that play the music other musicians have composed - then yeah, the venue has to pay for the privilege of using those musicians’ work to bring in paying customers. Fair enough, I say. But that’s not what we’re talking about here, right?

If there is no composer or arranger then it goes to the record label.

As Jimi mentioned, the onus of proof is on you (or the establishment) to show that you haven’t infringed on any copyright. So guilty until proven innocent.

>> …a good proportion of what’s played has been written and recorded by living musicians - PRS collect royalties for them and that is how they earn their living.

Yes, but… These systems are not set up for trad music by any way you measure it. As mentioned, the only way any money could ever flow to the composer (or their estate) would be if every session reported every tune played and every setting was noted every time they sit and play. And even then, I have my doubts that any money would flow to trad composers. The way these systems work is that they use various ways to sample what music is getting played in public situations, because it wouldn’t be feasible for them to track every single piece of music played in every bar or pub in the country. So they sample what music is getting played in various establishments and use that data to extrapolate what music is getting played in general. Trad music won’t even scrape the bottom of the barrel to a point where any money would flow that direction.

I would be happy to be proven wrong on this, but I have never heard of a trad composer getting any royalties from their compositions, other than maybe from when another artist purchases a mechanical license to be able to record and release one of their tunes.

Performing Rights Orgs represent their members only. That is, if you are somebody who has composed a brilliant new slip jig that is being played at every session throughout the land, over and over and over again, BMI (for example) won’t try to collect a farthing on your behalf if you’re not a signed-up member of their organization.

So if your session plays tunes whose composer is known and alive and well, as long as that composer isn’t a PRO member your session doesn’t need to pay a PRO anything.

Reverent you are right. The bizarre way PRS works is they hire a firm of freelancers to collect license fees and that firm gets a cut which is why they’re so keen to collect money. The PRS itself disburses fees to copyrighted artists not on an individual artist basis but on the basis of an overall average of global artists’ sales. Not to do with how many specific times their music is played by professional tribute bands, local bands doing various covers, sessions etc.

I somehow doubt that many writers of “traditional” tunes do it to make money collected from sessions and pubs that let sessions happen. If they’ve recorded it, sure they want money from the sales of records. If a tune gets played in sessions it might increase their sales. Perfectly understandable.

In the UK pubs have to apply to the local authority for a music license for commercial (i.e. making money out of it) musical events. The regulations are clear that this doesn’t include the sort of trad music sessions we’re talking about, they want to protect traditional music. The PRS is a different kettle of fish and doesn’t care about supporting traditional culture. They just want to make money.

Mars bars aren’t worth very much but it is still stealing if you shoplift one. It is the law that if a business plays any copyright music in public they must pay royalties in the form of a PRS licence. If you don’t like it, don’t play in public.

The royalties for someone playing one of your tunes in a session may only be a few pence, but then your cut from a CD sale is only a few pence, royalties from a radio play are a few pence, a spotify play is a few 1/1000s of a pence. All those few pences add up and hopefully make a living wage. You are right that PRS distribution isn’t totally fair and favours the mainstream, but if that bothers you all you have to do is submit a setlist (online) and your licence fee will go to the composers concerned not into the general pot.

Re: New PRS Session Tariff - illegal/unworkable?

Mark M, you’re not wrong, but see my point in greenman’s new thread on this: https://thesession.org/discussions/50324

To whit: I know several trad musicians whose tunes are widely played and have been performed and recorded by “big name” bands. To a one, they are chuffed that their tunes are played in sessions, no further compensation expected. Some do ask for royalties when their tunes are used in commercial performance and recordings, but it’s not unusual that they ask only for attribution as the composer, in lieu of royalties. They would be unhappy to hear that PRS would demand a fee, on threat of legal action against the pub, for a session playing their tunes.

I have no qualms if a tune composer wants royalties—that’s their right. But in the trad *community* (thankfully, it yet remains a global but tight-knit community), it’s not uncommon for composers to willingly, even happily, forgo royalties.
