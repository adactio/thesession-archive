Re: Accompaniment is HARD

“And I would be interested to know, would you tend to back a tune you weren’t familiar with? or better to sit it out?”

I would never back a tune I was unfamiliar with. If you allow the urge to participate to cloud your better judgement, and join in on a tune that you can’t play, it could be embarrassing for you and frustrating for your peers if you don’t deliver. The best thing you can do when it comes to tunes you aren’t familiar with, in any sense or context, is to keep the practicing at home.

Now, the trick about accompaniment is that once you develop the musical syntax, and are adept enough on your instrument to play from improvising, you can familiarize yourself with a tune on the fly. However, that still requires *sitting it out* and actively listening until you’ve created your arrangement in your head.

In my opinion, the only difficult thing about accompaniment is learning to interpret the music, learning that syntax. That’s been my personal issue for self-accompaniment for tunes on piano, and interpreting what bass lines and harmonies flow best with the melody. Once you understand the syntax of the music, it writes itself.

Re: Accompaniment is HARD

I wouldn’t say accompaniment is hard but it’s not easy either.

The accompaniment is there to help tell the musical story of the tune by adding some harmonic context.
As I’ve said many times, to be a successful accompanist you really need to be able to hear (and understand) the (chord) changes. A rock guitarist or jazz saxophonist improvises melodies over set chord sequences. To do that well they need to be able to hear the changes. Similarly, the trad accompanist improvises chord sequences over set melodies. To do that well you need to be able to hear the possible underlying changes.

You will only get so far in trad accompaniment by memorising chord sequences for tunes off pat. You can do that without any understanding of how the chords fit. You need to use your ears. Too many people I know work out accompaniments (in advance) using their eyes by looking at scores and working out which chords fit particular note groupings. Often you end up with a progression that goes nowhere – it does nothing harmonically in enhancing the story of the tune.

There are some tunes that I have played over and over for which I will have a pretty settled idea of what I want to play – if it ain’t broke, don’t fix it. Generally though I’m thinking of the sound of the tune in my head and almost automatically selecting chords that I think will fit (there’s not a lot of thought going in to it -there’s often not the time).

Regarding accompanying tunes on the fly, in some traditions, for example Cape Breton, it is expected. One pianist there told me that the second time through the tune is there for you to correct any mistakes you made the first time. I find it quite stressful, tune after tune, but once in a while is ok if that’s what’s expected. A fiddle player once told me he liked my accompaniment because I didn’t try to accompany tunes I didn’t know – little did he know! Some tunes are easier than others. You can get away with a lot in reels where the underlying harmony is often ambiguous, but you have to be careful with waltzes where the harmony is usually more emphatically outlined.

Keep listening and practising. It’s a long journey.

Re: Accompaniment is HARD

One comment I would make as a player rather than a backer is that I’d far rather play with someone with solid, confident rhythm who occasionally picks a bum chord than someone who plays tentatively.

Some generic suggestions: make sure you know all the chord progressions in all the useful keys (ie starting with I-IV-V-I and expanding from there). Also knowing what to do with modal passages - specifically how to avoid making it sound like the relative major. Knowing your inversions and substitutions also gives you much more flexibility.

One other tip I have heard cited is to learn to listen for what the bass note “should” be, even when you just have a solo instrument playing.

For instance, a guitar can be strummed or picked, played smoothly or percussively, brought in to generate impetus or left out to create dramatic space, used to lay down a drone or to introduce harmonic variations, and so forth. It really doesn’t need to have all six strings hit at every beat.

Re: Accompaniment is HARD

I would just say less is more - you don’t need loads of chords, plenty of tunes work well over a single chord with maybe some shifting inversions etc. It also helps to keep the chords quite ‘open’, ie you don’t always need a third in there and rarely a seventh, keep it to fifths, maybe a second, that kind of thing… And keep the rhythm quite open too, you don’t have to strum away like crazy, and you don’t have to hit every string with every strum, if that makes sense. Light, open textures, both harmonically and rhythmically, are always good.

Re: Accompaniment is HARD

I’ve been extemporising accompaniment for all sorts of Trad music, including ‘Irish’, for some time now.
It initially takes a lot of critical listening, experimentation and practice.
Here are some random remarks.

First find which key/ mode the tune is in.
This is most important.
I find the key/ mode root by quietly playing a single note in the background until you find the one that appropriately works as a drone.
One can, in theory, use any of the standard triads (chords) of the mode and they will harmonise with the tune but chords outside this set may sound edgy (see comments re: flatVI chord in Dorian mode!).
Then it’s a matter of hearing where the cadences are in a tune.
It’s just like hearing the cadences in speech (I’ve attempted to illustrate this below, the asterisks are the cadences).
“Diddly diddly diddly, diddly diddly *doo, diddly diddly diddly, *doodly doodly *did”
That’s where one changes the chord.
Very often, simply playing the root (drone/ I) and the dominant (V) works.
With minor mode tunes try the i chord with the VII chord as a Dominant.
For the ‘softer’ cadences try using the IV chord.
This is the absolute basics!

There are some tunes that defy the simple system above. They are the mixed mode tunes and the ones with ‘strange’ (or unexpected) notes. e.g. the augmented fifth (D#) in the first part of Moving Cloud, most of the fabulous second part of the Wonderful Hornpipe, the dodgy Fnat at the start of part two of Chief O’Neil, the impossible to back phrase in part two of the Chicago Reel!
Also be aware of tunes that don’t start on the I (or i) chord (e.g. The Virginia Reel). The best way to check is to find which chord the tune ends on.
Never play a flat VI in any Dorian mode tune. e.g. in Edorian a C chord will clash with the C#s in the tune and make you sound like 70’s rock and suchlike old hat!). Try a A major instead.
Keep it simple.

Re: Accompaniment is HARD

Yhall House: “Never play a flat VI in any Dorian mode tune. e.g. in Edorian a C chord will clash with the C#s in the tune”

Never say *never*. I know I’m nit-picking, but the C#s are not there all the time – a clash only occurs when the C chord happens to co-incide with a C# note. If you know the tune well enough, it might be possible to use a flat VI chord as an effective harmonic detour without clashing with any melody notes. There are also tunes that waver between the Dorian and Aeolian modes, using both C and C# (for E Dorian/E Aeolian).

Re: Accompaniment is HARD

Accompaniment implies a secondary/subsidiary/inferiour role. Ok with that? Fine, works great in classical music: virtuoso violin soloist X accompanied by famous Z on piano, everybody knows who is the main dish on the table.

But in a music band situation, nobody says “John Lennon and Paul McCartney accompanied by Ringo Starr on drums and George Harrison on guitar”. That’s not how a band works, everybody has to pull equal weight.

Instead of thinking accompaniment, think of purpose to your playing. What do you bring to the table,
how do you improve the music.

So you are holding a bouzouki. Are you adding to the melody? (by playing melody, or harmony, or drones, or counterpoint?) Are you adding to the rhythm, the drive, the lift, make feet move? Are you providing a pitch reference to whistlers and fluters? Something else or additional? (I say: say “yes” to all of the above!)

nothing simpler than playing guitar chords to fiddle tunes (on whatever instrument, uke, om, bouzouki, balalaika).

purchase the three portland collection books and play the chords as written. you will like the result and you will be challenged to come up with better chords. get some other books with chords for slip jigs and waltzes. get the Vasen tune books to play Josefin’s Dopvals, get the Altan tune book for the Snowy Path.

It’s all hard. But it’s so fun when it works!

I don’t know if I’d say easier or harder, but learning tunes can be a more obvious path; you find the tune you want to play, then you learn it, and ideally you learn it from a nice version. Learning accompaniment (I don’t object to the term, since the melody could be played without me to great effect) is less obvious because it’s less codified. I think listening to and noticing the chordal players on your favorite recordings, then zeroing in on who your favorites are, and THEN trying to figure out what they are doing to make it sound like that is a wonderful approach. Though of course, some things are universal (knowing the keys, knowing tunes etc) the things that make a person sound like one player vs another can be pretty different, down to the tuning.

FWIW I’ve always hated the chords in the Portland collection; I feel like there are waaaaay too many. But your mileage may vary.
