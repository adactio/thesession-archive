Re: Dynamics within the Idiom.

I think the author there is talking about certain sorts of dynamics as used (or formerly used) in orchestral music, such as “terraced dynamics” and the gradual crescendo and diminuendo over large phrases and so forth.

Anyone who claims there is no “varying of loudness and softness” in Irish music has never watched a decibel meter while an Irish musician is playing. On the whistle and uilleann pipes various notes of the scale have unique and built-in volume levels. On top of that pipers do “barking” and “swelling” which intentionally introduce further dynamics. Fluters, fiddlers, boxplayers, and probably all other Irish instrumentalists widely vary dynamics to give rhythm and pulse to the music.

The volume level of the uilleann chanter varies so much in performance that when in the recording studio it is usually heavily compressed. Many pipers use a compressor when playing live on stage too, I did that for years.

Re: Dynamics within the Idiom.

There’s probably a lot of contextual stuff at play. I would say in a crowded session, or maybe earlier in the twentieth century a dance outdoors with relatively few musicians, the main emphasis is going to be on playing as loud as possible. Of course accenting certain notes (especially downbeats) seems to have always been important, but it may well be that dynamics in a larger scale sense (making long phrases louder or softer) was not a common feature of this music when its main function was to support dancing.
I would be amazed if someone suggested there was no dynamics at all to in the playing of slow airs, but certainly traditional sean nos singing doesn’t seem to make nearly as much use of it as many other styles of singing do.
Talking about the tunes as music for listening to, I’ve heard some trad musicians who make a great deal of use of dynamics (say Martin Hayes), but it doesn’t seem that common in older recordings I’ve heard. (Not altogether absent or banned or anything- just not common and often subtle where it does appear.)
Where I hear dynamics used it’s often in quite a regular way, maybe changing between tunes or parts of tunes etc… Rarely, maybe as part of treating repeated parts differently (you know, like ‘a part with some cuts/slides, then A PART WITH TRIPLETS’, but this often comes across as ‘classical’ to my ears.)
Breathnach’s (it was him wasn’t it?) take seems a bit absolutist to me, but there’s probably something to it.

This is all on a macro level though. I mean notes within a bar or a cluster of three/pair/group of four etc… clearly get played at different volumes by loads of players, and long notes or rolls often get ‘pulsed’ etc…

Re: Dynamics within the Idiom.

I’d agree with what’s been said above, re volume dynamics - yes, there’s a lot of use of them in the general musical line, and everyone who plays well uses them a lot (if they didn’t, then I think the ‘lilt’ and ‘lift’ would be lost). If you listen to some learners, who (eg on fiddle) have just perfected their timing and intonation only, the result can sound quite lifeless.

I use crescendo / diminuendo in some of my own comps (eg starting at one bar and ending some bars later), but I certainly wouldn’t use them in reels or jigs. I think it would sound a bit silly and very much out of place.

Re: Dynamics within the Idiom.

It can still happen a bit. To get a bit classical for a minute: Say some players might play the first bar of rakish paddy at a certain volume (let’s arbitrarily say mf). It’s mainly playing around that c natural, but the third bar is playing around with d and c sharp. It’s a jump up in intensity/tension etc so maybe they go up to f in volume.

But it’s a far cry from say romantic music where you’ll get all the way from pp to ff and throw subito fortes in all over the place and so-on. And it’s not really even all that similar to a baroque approach.

I’d also mention that in a session context it might have a lot more to do with the practicalities of playing together than expression. So people get quieter going into the next tune, because they want to hear the leader and make sure they’re playing the right thing. Or they play out very loud to steady the guy next to them who has slightly shaky rhythm etc…

Not a particularly good example Jim, the crescendo is fine, but the diminuendo has no place there, it makes it sound as though someone is fannying about with the faders. Re: Dynamics within the Idiom.

It depends what we mean by ‘the idiom’ or ‘the tradition’. Dynamics have, for at least half a century, been used by performing groups - Ceoltóirí Chualainn, The Chieftains, The Bothy Band, Lúnasa… Even if individual musicians not vary their volume level much, there is much use of arrangement to vary the overall dynamics. The same approach is used by grupaí cheóil in the fleadh competitions. Such groups may be a recent phenomenon in the history of Irish Traditional Music (and might by some be regarded as not part of the tradition at all) but they have, I think, profoundly influenced the way we hear and play the music today.

↳ Posted by Will Evans on June 4th, 2016.

Re: Dynamics within the Idiom.

Breathnach’s opinion that the use of dynamics was a marker of the ‘non-native’ player may have largely held true at the time of writing (although I am sure there were already a handful of players native to the tradition that were incorporating dynamics into their playing - perhaps by way of non-native influence). But I think it is more difficult nowadays to define what is ‘native’, since this music had spread well beyond Ireland and the Irish Diaspora; there are plenty of ‘non-natives’ (those not born into the tradition, irrespective of whether or not they are Irish in any sense) whose children have followed them into traditional music. There are ‘non-natives’ teaching traditional music - and ‘natives’ who have been concurrently influenced by commercial band recordings, or individuals like Tommy Potts, Martin Hayes etc. Whilst they may not be explicitly teaching dynamics as part of the style, they may still be unconsciously using it in their own playing, thus setting the example to their pupils. If it is already being passed on in this way, then can it not be said to be a part of the tradition? How many generations does it take?
