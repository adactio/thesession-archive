the joy of tunebooks…

…. or why the dots aren’t SO bad.

Last night, was leafing through the old King Street Sessions spiral bound tome with over 1000 tunes in it, & was so pleased to realize there are a ton of tunes in there I recognize but only sort of ‘half know.’ It was a great starting place, b/c then you go through your CD collection & listen & learn from all the different settings & then you come on here & see who has posted what, & voilá! You’ve got a much more solid & polished tune in your head. Plus, like William Shatner says, you can shop & compare BEFORE you buy!

Lots of musicians I know spend their evenings paging through dusty old tunebooks, the current fave around these parts is the Josephine Keegan tunebook, trolling for obscure jewels to add to their repertoire. Yeah, there are some stinker tunebooks out there, but when you reach a certain level, I think you can begin to weed out the lemons.

Yes, I do learn by ear, but aren’t tunebooks lovely sometimes?

Re: the joy of tunebooks…

The only tune I ever picked up by ear the first time I heard it was The Road To Lisdoonvarna, probably because of its simplicity, and the fact that they played it slowly and lots of times through. When I hear a tune I like and want to learn, I always seek out the sheet music and learn to play it from that. I am techologically challenged, so I don’t like carrying a tape recorder, and couldn’t fathom how one of those digital recording things work. When I play the tune from the book, sometimes I will find that the sheet music is somewhat different than what I heard and will stick to what I heard. Other times I find that the sheet music has some nice subtle differences that got lost in my memory, or were fudged by the people I heard it from, in which case I will keep what I read. Then I go out and play it with my friends, and the tune mutates some more, until it becomes a session-friendly, comfortable setting.
In any event, for me, the dots are a vital part of the learning process, and also for the refreshing my memory process when I forget bits and pieces of a tune I want to remember (usually something in the B part goes first).

Re: the joy of tunebooks…

The only problem I have with tunebooks is that the versions are sometimes off the beaten track. Even when you have a book like Josephine Keegan’s you’ll find that the tunes have gone through a filter of sorts and are changed slightly, or even dramatically, before they get included in the ITM lexicon of tune settings. Often a setting will be established by it’s appearance on a high profile CD and that setting will differ a bit from what’s in a book or even what the composer had in mind.

I try to zero in on the tune as it’s played by the crowd I like to play with. (or hope to play with) I will usually hear a tune I’d like to learn on a CD or by a friend. I’ll often ask my friend where they found the tune, and if they know I’ll try to fid the recording. If they didn’t reference a recording I’ll try to get it from them. I also try to learn the setting off of recordings from musicians who are part of a session scene I’m familiar with, or who’s playing I admire.

If I have the sources I’ve described above and find myself struggling with a passage of the tune I will sometimes explore tunebooks to see if I can find alternate ideas for how to play it that might fit my instrument and abilities better.

Yes, tunebooks are useful, but for me they only supplement listening.

So Emily has the King Street book!

I’ve never seen another reference to it. It’s funny, a piper friend brought that back to Phoenix from Santa Cruz about 4 years ago. It spread among the community of beginners here when we started an Irish Music Academy (basically group lessons on Irish music) and it’s now become the canon of an entire session scene in Phoenix. The just about ignore the recorded music and everyone brings their copy of the book to the session. Folks call out numbers and everyone opens to the page before a tune is played.

Even though all the advanced players in town disavow the tome, it’s stuck. So many of the versions of our common session tunes are very different, and the players who use it have a hard time adapting to different versions because they don’t have the dots.

I agree there’s a time and place for the dots, but there’s a lot of damage that can be done when people believe the dots more than their ears.

But I do agree, tunebooks, even that one, can be a great resource. I just thought I’d share my particular feelings about that book!

Re: the joy of tunebooks…

i learn mostly from tunebooks. but i can learn from sheet music. i dont recommend learning from sheetmusic. i recommend being able to learn from sheet music, it is very important. but it is not the best way to learn tunes.

i just learned an manglam ( https://thesession.org/tunes/1670 ). i spent about 3 days listening to it a couple minutes each day. then i tried to figure it out, and i was having trouble (i think maybe they might play in a different key on kitty lie over. b, perhaps?). so i looked at the sheet music, and found the version on the cd in the comments. and then i learned it instantly.

sheet music is good, but i dont think it should be your first thought if you have a recording available. try to learn from sheet music, and if it doesnt work, then the dots will be grand. because i can memorize a tune really easily (and sight read well), but i have never learned a tune quicker than that because i knew how it went in my head and i didnt know the notes. its obvious, but i dont go to sessions yet very often, so i usually just pludge through and find tunes i want to here and there. if you want to use dots, use them after you know the tune in your head, then the dots can show you the notes.

also, my favourite use for dots is one thing and one thing only, to give me the first note of a tune. the rest is easy after that.

Hi Emily,
I was actually in bands with both of the guys you mentioned, Afan with John Good (we were in a band with the piper that brought the King Street book back), and I’m currently in Blackwood with Sven (I’m the bodhran player, Kevin, our site is http://www.blackwoodband.com). We might have crossed paths at some point, though I don’t remember. If you were only in town occasionally I’m not suprised we didn’t meet, I didn’t go to the sessions often.

I don’t really know that I can say that the scene has improved, although there are a lot more people learning the music now. We’ve always suffered from having only a small number of really good/great players, and most of them are in bands, so it’s hard to also get them out to sessions when they’ve played all weekend in the pubs. The sessions are really great when we can get all of the pros together, but that takes effort and only happens every once in a while.

So what’s happened is that as more people have gotten interested in the music and begun learning (because a lot of us are teaching here), there isn’t an established experienced session to guide them (because the best session, at Rosie’s, is good maybe once a month when a bunch of us show up at once, otherwise there’s only a few people there). The beginners started their own session, and because there weren’t experienced players there enough they relied on the King Street book as a common reference. There are several players who are really passionate about the music and do as much listening and studying of the great players as they should, but many people just conform to the standard of the beginners session and thus don’t really learn the music.

On the other hand, it’s gotten a lot of people into the music, and most people at this session have improved quite a bit. Some of them I think will develop into really great players, but not because of their participation in the book-session.

I think the most interesting/saddest part is that Phoenix is a big town with plenty of good players, but people act as they would in a more isolated situation.

anyway, some of my random thoughts.
