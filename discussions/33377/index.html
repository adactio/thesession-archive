Drums at sessions

There was a time when I appreciated a good drummer at a session but these days I am caring less and less for it. I like it more when it’s in a band setting where we tend to play the same tunes more or less from week to week but at sessions it is different. Reason being sessions around here seem to be focused on variety, people are often bringing out tunes that haven’t been played in a while. Often times I am struggling to keep rhythm while playing along with tunes that I know but have faded in the back of memory and the drum is a huge distraction and I don’t appreciate having it at the session. Even if the drumming is decent or even fairly good to great drumming it just distracts me and takes away from my overall enjoyment. I am probably just a jerk though.

I think if your only instrument is drum there is a problem with you participating in *sessions* where the point is to share *tunes*. I mean what enjoyment does the drummer get anyway other than many opportunities to just twack along mindlessly all night long. You are not learning and sharing tunes so what is the point? Bands are different, I appreciate having a good drummer in my band of course. Our sets and styles are more or less static and being a performance it is just different. This will probably devolve into the same session vs. performance argument. Discuss.

Re: Drums at sessions

I can sympathize with being distracted when trying to listen and meld with the other melody players. Was the bodhran player just playing too loud in the OP’s example? I think the line of acceptance for bodhran in sessions can depend very much on whether it’s played softly as a background pulse, or clattering away in the foreground. A drum tuned so it “thumps” is also easier for me to ignore than one tuned and played so it sounds like hitting a cardboard pizza box, which seems to be a popular tone for some reason.

As mentioned above, a melody player picking up a bodhran for a few tunes is different from someone who considers it their main (only) instrument, and insists on playing on every tune. Some sessions focus more on social interaction than in providing an ideal environment for the melody players, and those are the ones at risk for drummer dominance.

I don’t think “learning and sharing tunes” is a good dividing line, because it applies to all accompaniment, not just drummers. A guitar player can get familiar enough with a tune to memorize or improvise chords to follow it. But few guitar players can actually kick off a tune set, because that means playing the melody. A few can, but I think it’s not that common.

I once attended a mixed Irish trad/Folkie session led by a guitar player, who would say something like “Let’s play Cooley’s Reel”, followed by looking around the room for a fiddler who could actually start the tune. If you can’t start the tune, then you haven’t learned the tune and you can’t share it. It’s a melody/backer distinction, not something unique to drums.

To me, the difference is that in a performance the musicians all know what they are going to play and how they’re going to play it; the drum has a place in the arrangement of the music.

Re: Drums at sessions

For me, the pinnacle of session playing is where every musician can hear and is listening to every other musician, and there is complete mutual communication, each picking up on the others‘ nuances. This usually only happens when there is a small number of players - say 2-5 (I don’t think I, myself, am capable of communicating - either verbally or musically - with more people than that at any one time). In this scenario, a good percussionist would, as far as I am concerned, be just as welcome as a good melody player (in theory, it would make no difference to me whether they were playing bodhran, djembe, congas, triangle or shaky egg, if they’re ’in tune‘ with the other players - but a ’tuned in’ percussionist would choose their instrument to suit the situation). There is potential for a drummer and a small number of tune players to create something greater than the sum of its parts.

In larger sessions, we are usually, to a greater or lesser degree, just playing along to, rather than playing *with* the other musicians. This goes as much for melody players as for chord players or percussionists. The difference here, though, is that, whilst the melody players all have the tune in common to connect their playing on some level, a drummer has little other than the most basic pulse to follow. All the tune players play with their own nuances (or lack thereof), which cancel each other out, so the drummer is left either to thump away monotonously or add their own rhythmic irrelevances - at best, adding nothing, at worst, being annoying and distracting.

Am I making sense?

Re: Drums at sessions

plantgnome writes: “Every session has it’s own written and unwritten rules, some accommodate tune learning, some are more performance driven.”

I would express this differently: some accommodate tune learning and others just want to play tunes. In other words, if your session was established to teach each other tunes then you would perform them differently than you do when you’re playing for mutual enjoyment. It’s enjoyable to play tunes slow and tolerate the clumsy playing of people trying to learn tunes--if that’s what the people having the session wanted, but it robs the session of the joy of performing the tunes up to speed and with a lift and good energy if tune learning wasn’t what brought the people having the session together in the first place. The lift and good energy only comes AFTER you’ve learned the tunes and you know how to play your instrument. The problems occur when people visiting other people’s sessions come with expectations that their tune learning is assumed to be tolerated. As a visitor you have to suss out what the people having the session are into doing. Some visitors think they’re entitled to learn tunes at sessions based on assumptions that there is some sort of rule about sessions that permits it. There isn’t…
