Re: Learning

Depends on what stage of the process we’re talking about. I almost never learn unfamiliar tunes off sheet music, it’s almost always something I’ve heard recently in a session, or have had in our recorded music library and I’ve listened to it for years. It’s already “in my head” by the time I sit down and learn to play it. At that point, it might take just a day or two to get the skeleton under my fingers. Then it has to be integrated into daily practice over a period of weeks or months before I’m completely comfortable with it, and able to kick it off at a session.

Saying that I’ve “learned” the tune in a day or two doesn’t really cover this whole process, including that initial period of getting it into my head, before I ever sit down to learn it.

Sometimes a tune will have an illogical pattern that defeats the normal process of learning a tune, where your brain tries to find patterns related to tunes you already know. Some of the Paddy Fahey tunes are like that, or Gordon Duncan pipe tunes like “Ian Greene of Greentrax” with that weird syncopated third section. For tunes like that, I have to isolate the problem area and hammer on it over and over, until it’s burned into my fingers and I can play it on autopilot.

It took me something like a month -- working on it off and on, allowing time for it to sink in -- before I could get all the way through Ian Greene at tempo. And I still have to remember to run through it every once in a while, so I don’t lose the autopilot programming.

At one extreme end of things I can sit down with a set of simple, predictable tunes I’ve heard before, play the track two or three times while plunking along on the fiddle, turn the track off and play them pretty accurately. Play them through a few times to myself and then play along with the track a few more times to make sure I’ve got it right.
I’ve now ‘learned’ a set of three tunes in maybe twenty or thirty minutes.
But playing them at the same speed as the recording I sound pretty bad. And in half an hours time I won’t remember how to start them.
At the opposite end of the spectrum, I can sit down with a track of some diabolically twisty reel being played at ninety miles an hour. Listen to it on a loop like a dozen times. Painstakingly work out phrases while stopping and starting the track. Go looking for another recording where they’re playing slower and not throwing in so many variations. Realize they’re playing a slightly different version. Get frustrated trying to figure out what’s going on in a section and come over here to the database. Sight read through four different transcriptions trying to find which ones are similar to the recording I’ve been working on. Finally get a hold on that bit that was escaping me. Slowly patch together those phrases and work out my version of the tune which I can now play slowly and shakily. (Except did I get that one bit right? Or am I now practicing a mistake?)
Ok back to the recording. No. It’s all ok. Play through the version I’ve worked out for, I dunno, forty minutes.
I’ve ‘learned’ a tune in maybe an hour. Maybe even two hours.
And in half an hour I won’t remember how to start it.
And if I try to play it at a session I’ll go right off the rails in about two bars.
With either of those examples, if I play the tunes to myself two or three times a day for maybe a week I’ll have a solid enough grasp of them to take them to sessions.
And now begins the process of a few months of playing the tunes which will really get them up to a nice level. Maybe with a random half hour in there at some point revisiting them in practice and seeing if I can find some interesting stuff to do with them.

Re: Learning

Scottsirish,

The thing is that if you only know a written version of a ScottsIrish tune, it is not necessarily the same version as that you will hear or other musicians will play at a local session.

Yes, you could learn multiple settings of the same tune “from the dots” and, theoretically, even with most of the subtleties required as long as the written score is very detailed but it still won’t guarantee that you will have learned the tune “as being played”. Of course, you would have to find such music for this purpose if it was actually ever written down or ask some kind musician to transcribe it all for you.

So, how much simpler is it to develop the skills of listening, picking up and learning all these nuances “by ear”? You don’t need to worry if your tune is the same setting or not as you can adapt to what is being played or learn “by ear” in a short time what is actually going on.

That’s the way traditional music is. Generally, we don’t get given copies of sheet music to “do our homework” during the week before we return to the session unless it’s a formal arrangement or, possibly, a “learning session”. Also, there is no national (or international) curriculum with approved or prescribed settings of tunes. Even on this site, there are several possibilities for even the simplest of tune submissions.

What a fraught and irresolvable subject! Personally, I’m confident that I ‘know’ quite a few tunes that I picked up years ago and can play at the drop of a hat, but find it increasingly tricky to commit newly-acquired ones to memory - though can join in quickly enough with lots of them in sessions if someone kicks them off. So is there a sense in which I can say I ‘know’ them even if I can’t straight off recall the first phrase or put a name to them?

I play from dots on a small tablet a lot of the time (tho’ try to limit doing so in sessions, and generally only introduce tunes that I can play by ear. So I observe the convention to a certain extent). The thing is, there are just too many good tunes . . . Maybe it’s only possible to memorise, retain, and put your own stamp on a limited number of them - a number that is likely to vary inversely with the total number of birthdays - though the desire to explore as many as possible doesn’t diminish.

As a rule of thumb, I suspect you can say you really know a tune if, absent-mindedly, you find yourself playing it on a different instrument or in an unfamiliar key for the first time and suddenly realise, ‘I’ve never done that before’. It means the shape and flow of the tune has taken precedence in one’s brain over the technical requirements of reproducing it. And then there are those that you used to play all the time but haven’t played for years: when someone starts them, they come flooding back into conscious recognition. There are tunes you know you know, tunes you know you don’t know, tunes you don’t know you know . . .

Perhaps the science surrounding the neuro-social-psychology of tune retention remains complex and not well understood. Further research needed. Meanwhile, we may have to make do with opinions.

“Can we say that we’ve mastered a tune if we forget the name of it?”

Yes! It’s great if you know the name, and even better if you can tell a story about the provenance. But that information doesn’t stick in the same area of the brain as the one where you play music. And it may not be remembered along with the notes.

Anecdote: Several years ago, I was attending a week-long workshop where one class was led by John Doyle on Irish guitar backing. He picked one tune to show us his approach, and it was really neat. We asked him the name of the tune and he couldn’t remember it. As the class wound down, the next class in that room was scheduled for Liz Carroll teaching fiddlers. She came in a little early towards the end of John’s class, and played the tune on her fiddle with him. It was great! And she couldn’t remember the name of the tune either.

Turned out, it was “Sean Ryan’s” or the Castle Jig. Needless to say, they both played the tune *brilliantly* together.

So it happens to all of us. And maybe especially as we get older. If you’re a regular at sessions though, it’s only polite to try to know something about a new tune you introduce. It can be a little annoying when someone plays a great tune and can’t give you any info for tracking it down to learn yourself.

That’s an interesting phenomena, isn’t it? I used to struggle quite a bit with not being able to play the tune well until I could remember the name. But it occurred to me that at least a significant portion of that struggle was just that I was committing so much brain power to trying to remember the name that it was distracting me from playing the melody. So I went through a phase of deliberately not trying to remember the name, and just play the tune. That worked great for me (and I would often have the name occur to me as I was playing it anyway). But the other side effect of that strategy was that sometimes the names got disconnected from the tune, and I became one of those guys who can’t remember the name of a lot of tunes that I play.

So in the last few years, I’ve been making a conscious effort to try to reconnect the names to the tunes, but in a less reliant way. And to complicate matters, I learn so many tunes in sessions that I don’t know what they’re called. (Because it can be really annoying if you ask the names of tunes after every set… And sometimes I forget to ask by the time the set ends as well…) Memory is a bit of a nebulous thing anyway. Like how I could tell you the name of a tune, but if you ask me the name of it, it will disappear into the depths of the memory, refusing to be recalled right at that moment…

But I guess if I carried sheet music around, I would be able to tell you the names (and *exactly how the tune is *supposed to go, too!) ;-) :-P

Ive been picking up tunes in sessions for decades , when they get really familiar I will ask the name then go away and find some settings that feel right . Not one of the tunes I can play in sessions could I play outside or start off . For me it’s a totally different process . Committing to memeory requires focused effort and conscious repetition probably from notation . As I can carry 40 odd tunes around in a small folder .
I’ve never seen notation at a session by the way .
I find knowing the name helps because it is like the label on a drawer , if no drawers are labeled then I could have put it anywhere and who knows what tune is in what box!!
I’ve no idea how many tunes I have and a lot of them I kind of forget I know , I can often start them and certainly play then but they may have sat in my brains for 35 yrs since they were last dug out and dusted off!! So I find the name very very usefull . The name helps bring the tune to mind . Sometimes i still need the first few notes to find a tune . But the name often comes first .
I have the great pleasure and honour of playing with some of the best musicians in Ireland bar none . And to be frank they are in the same position as me , but they often can’t find the tunes to start off , they have so many!!! , god knows how many after 50+ yrs full immersion !! :-) ! So I sometimes find myself the person to start tunes off because I can reach into a drawer and find old tunes we all know ….. I think it’s because I have most of the names! A good filling system !! (tune pal fills me in for ones I don’t have names for !! )
In general I’m playing my new tunes and tunes that I’ve learnt in the last couple of years and don’t really bother playing old chestnuts at home but they come in handy with the old timers . My go to set is predominately Scottish these days so it’s the old goldies we play together with my friends .

Re: Learning

Not very long. It can take as little as a few minutes of listening to several days or even weeks of drilling a more technical piece. I can get 75-80% of most tunes on the fly. I record some for later study, and I sometimes get a name and use a reputable tune collection to find some possible notation for the “gaps.” I can normally learn the recorded ones without need for notation. I am a good sight reader so learning from notation is easy, very easy for me as well.

Getting to this point does require effort. Some tunes will fight you in your efforts to learn them. Some may require technique that you must labor very hard to master. Every instrument will provide challenges…some more than others.
