Advice on practice tools for rhythm

A year ago I stumbled across a local session and was inspired. Having no previous exposure to Irish music I bought a mandolin from Amazon and spent the next few months muddling my way through YouTube tutorials until I could play well enough to sit with them. I now play weekly with the session that turned out to be comprised of several very capable musicians. Everyone is friendly and patient with me as I learn and it’s been a great experience.

Here’s where I need some help: every time I start a tune I find myself unable to keep up once the group takes over. I have about 15 tunes memorized so far and I play them on my own for fun every evening. When I play by myself I have no trouble but as soon as I try to play with someone else I find myself struggling to mesh with them. I suspect that it’s because I haven’t spent enough time practicing the timing of each tune.

I’ve tried playing along with metronomes and that has helped but i’m afraid that i’m still not getting the right rhythm out of that stagnant on/off beat. I tried playing along to YouTube recordings but those are usually way to fast for me and they’re tough to manage as they offer little continuity.

What I would love to find is some sort of metronome tool that will give me specific Irish rhythms at various speeds. For example slow Jig, slow reel, moderate slip-jig, ect… I’m looking for something I can practice over that will help me learn the correct rhythm for each type of tune.

Does anyone know of a tool like this? I’m also open to any advice. Learning this stuff has been fun; but I feel like my progress is slowing. I’m open to advice on any method for improvement.

thanks

Yes of course, more practice

But don’t be concerned about speed/First the swing and tonal quality then comes the speed as
a natural matter of course

Practice scales in the various rhythms of the tunes you want to play

Ditch the metronome - tends to lead to a stilted style/Foot tapping will follow naturally as your playing gets better - that’s a better metronome although some feel that that steals from the work required from actually playing and that the brain alone is fully capable and better than the foot

Get a slow down app like iLift with which you can slow to 50% of recording speed and even change
the key if needed. But don’t over do it playing along with the recording.

In the beginning lilt the song in your head as you play/or attempt to make up some simple lyrics where each note corresponds to a word or syllable.

Don’t just keep endlessly hammering away at the tune over and over- shake things up - try different methods - shake up your practice methods - diversify

When practicing a tune, don’t over do it. The neurotransmitters at the neural synapses being used
at some point diminish - you will notice this when the mistakes start increasing instead of decreasing -
Best to leave the tune for a while (sometimes for a day or two) and move on -

Over emphasize the strong beat at first - otherwise - with so much to think about - the all important swing may not get burned in - with time, the emphasizing the swing will be on auto-pilot -

I don’t play the mandolin - only the fiddle - all the above has helped this under-talented lover of the Irish music.

Re: Advice on practice tools for rhythm

One of the difficulties in ‘meshing’ as you put it, is that if you start off too slow, for example, you have to speed up, which in effect means that you play faster than the beat, so when you are level you have to slow down by exactly the right amount. It is easy to over-compensate, so most of the time is spent in accelerating and braking, trying to match speed, until the tune finishes (usually just as you’re getting the hang of it).
The trick is, instead of speeding up and slowing down, to make a slight adjustment to your ‘internal metronome’ so that it matches the tune.
This might sound like the same thing, but the difference is that in the first example you are trying to match individual beats or notes, and in the second you are regulating the overall speed of the whole tune. If you imagine running alongside someone, you can move your legs faster or slower to speed up slow down, which is the equivalent of playing faster and slower; but you can also adjust the length of your stride. It is not easy to do, and there are plenty of examples on YouTube of people whose internal metronomes aren’t synchronised.
Playing with a metronome is good practice, and does not restrict you in any way -- provided that you match your speed exactly. If you don’t, if you find yourself fighting the metronome, it will work against you; but if you can synchronise your internal beat, the metronome becomes your friend. In fact the tick can disappear altogether if you get it spot on, so that you stop hearing it until you get slightly out of synch again.

Re: Advice on practice tools for rhythm

Without hearing you play (as Tony said), it’s difficult to advise. It could be that the experienced players are coming in faster than the speed at which you started the tune. They shouldn’t do this. Or it could be that your timing is still unsteady and when they level it out, you find it hard to stay with them. I have experienced this from both sides. When you’re a reasonably experienced player, it’s impossible to lock onto someone who is wavering around the beat, so you end up finding a beat that’s somewhere in their wavering and essentially taking over the lead. But when you’re the wavery novice player, you’re still learning how to lock onto someone else’s rhythm and phrasing and can easily feel as though you’re falling on your face when you try.

In the first few years I was playing, I used Amazing Slow Downer a lot and would practice playing along with it at different speeds. Nowadays, I don’t do that so much but I like deliberately varying speed and swing of a tune (obviously at home by myself; don’t do this playing with others at a session!). Playing it fast and straight one time through, slow and swung another time, faster and swung a third time. Stuff like that. I have found that it has developed my ability to control and “lift” the tune.

Re: Advice on practice tools for rhythm

I respectfully disagree with Tom Connelly, above.

There’s no reason a metronome needs to lead to a stilted style. All it does is click where the beat is. If you’re style is stilted, that’s your fault - not the metronome’s!!!

You don’t need to set the metronome to hit clicks on the downbeat. Many jazz players definitely practice their ‘swing’ timing, for example, by using the metronome to click on beats 2 and 4 in a 4/4 time, for example (though this wouldn’t really apply if you wanted to do strictly Irish music.) Pat Metheny has a youtube vid out there somewhere demonstrating how he uses the metronome. Emily Remler (RIP) also demonstrates this on one of her excellent jazz/blues video lessons, which you can find online. I don’t think you can accuse either player of having a stilted style! (But part of that is using the metronome to click on the 2 and 4 like a snare drummer.

You can even try this… use the metronome to hit a 3-over-2 feel and try playing jigs!

I wish I had used a metronome more in my early years… I have a bad habit of rushing the tempo, personally, which I’m now trying to go back and correct, and it’s not an easy project.

Actually, I did try metronomes as a youngster, playing guitar, but had trouble because the ones I had weren’t loud enough! I couldn’t hear them over my playing. I guess maybe I was successfully playing on the beat with them. I can’t hear them over my violin, but I suppose I could use a mute. I could go to the mando, but I just thought of that now, typing this. <smacking forehead.>

Also, if someone has a desire to become a professional musician, it’s now pretty important for studio players to be able to play along with a click track. So if you can’t deal with a metronome, it’s going to hurt you.

Yes, this music lives and breathes. Bohola is great about controlled tempo changes and speeding up and slowing down between tunes and speeding up again, so it’s an exhilarating roller coaster ride. It’s a blast! But there is power, too, in strict time. Immense power. Remember that it is the Metronome of Truth!

It can be a terrific social occasion, sure, and that might be the reason I might join in, if the lulz are good. But chances are good at this point in life I’d sit it out until the herd thins a bit. Or a lot.

Also, there’s a difference between playing in a pub session and trying to sculpt an ensemble sound, say for recording or for concerts/gigs. Understand that my comments are more geared to the latter. Every pub session has its own logic, and some (most) are more social occasions than musical adventures. So take what I say with the according pinch of salt.

Re: Advice on practice tools for rhythm

"When practicing a tune, don’t over do it. The neurotransmitters at the neural synapses being used
at some point diminish - you will notice this when the mistakes start increasing instead of decreasing -
Best to leave the tune for a while (sometimes for a day or two) and move on -" Tom Connelly

“In the first few years I was playing, I used Amazing Slow Downer a lot and would practice playing along with it at different speeds. Nowadays, I don’t do that so much but I like deliberately varying speed and swing of a tune (obviously at home by myself; don’t do this playing with others at a session!). Playing it fast and straight one time through, slow and swung another time, faster and swung a third time. Stuff like that. I have found that it has developed my ability to control and ”lift“ the tune.” DrSilverSpear

This is an excellent thread for where I’m at playing-wise~ and I found the above advice especially helpful. I still use the slow downers, but less and less as my “hearing” improves.

I also play the mandolin and got an old Gibson. She’s loud. As my playing improves, I’m looking towards a tenor banjo. Also am gonna begin thickening my skin to be ready for the banjo player jokes. :-)
