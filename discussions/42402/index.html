Re: intonation

Definitely throw the tuner in a corner (don’t throw it away just yet). You have to be able to hear the intervals. Probably this is something you need to work on. Can you tune without the machine once you have the A?

Have you heard of ‘ringing strings’? At least I think that’s what some people call it.
I did not learn about ‘ringing strings’ from my first teacher (9–13) - my second teacher thought I was tone deaf. The idea is that if you play a note that has a matching open string, even an octave up, it should resonate. This a) tells you where you are (i.e. correct pitch), b) improves your tone.

I can’t simultaneously pass on advice on posture and hold, Sorry. it’s a long slow path but I hope you will keep at it and find enjoyment.

Re: intonation

Amen to Will’s comment. Once you know the strings are in tune drop the tuner.
Practice each tune slowly for a while, and every now and then if you have a first or second finger note stop and play it alongside the next open string up to hear the interval. If it sounds ok, all well and good, if not adjust your finger position until it does. Try as much as possible to do this without looking so that your muscle reflexes are fed by the aural stimulus and not the visual one.

You have already diagnosed a couple of problems which means you’ll be adjusting them as you go too. Think of the thumb as more of a prop than a grip. With a straight line from the forearm up through the hand this should
be tucked under the neck, and although probably more common in classical players, the left hand needn’t hold the fiddle at all if your chin position allows it. This means the left hand can be much more relaxed.

Keep at it! All the best.

Re: intonation

Carole, you should really only use the tuner to get the A in tune, like Alpinerabbit said. A pure tone tuner is preferable to a tuning fork, if only for the fact that it’s hands-free. You can’t sound a tuning fork, sustain the note and tune and bow a string all at the same time :)

You should then tune the rest of the strings - play A+E together, adjusting the E so it give a perfect 5th against the A. If you’re not sure, tune the E with the tuner, and then play the A+E together. It would be ideal if you de-tuned the E a little bit, then brought it up to pitch while playing the A, so your ear gets used to the interval.

Same drill for the A+D - perfect 5th apart, and finally for the D+G.

About not lifting the fingers to high - if you just play a simple scale starting on open D - so fingering will be

(1) D-string : 0,1,2,3
(2) A-string : 0,1,2,3

For (1), - keep each finger down as you place the next one, and don’t lift any. Now - lift all 3 fingers *as a unit* and play (2) - again, keep each finger down as you place the next one, and don’t lift any.

The idea is to use the minimum pressure to contact the string with the fingerboard. If you are pressing too hard, that might by why you are reflexively gripping with your thumb. Lifting fingers - only lift enough to clear the string - no more, otherwise it is just wasted energy.

On intonation, one of the aids I found useful were drones in various keys - really helpful to get your pitch true.

Here a link :

https://darolanger1.bandcamp.com/album/darols-drones-in-all-keys

If you want some really good advice on left hand technique, I’d strongly recommend “Basics” by Simon Fischer. I just looked at my own copy, and there’s a very good section which deals with the exact thumb problem you have, and there are actual musical exercises to help relax the grip. Photos too. You can get it from Amazon, or similar - it’s on Edition Peters, No 7440 and its ISBN IS 978-1-901507-00-3. It’s a life’s work :)

Re: intonation

I worry about intonation a lot, even after 40+ years of playing-- combination of high aspirations and modest abilities. So here are a few thoughts on the subject, for whatever they’re worth.

* It’s invaluable, as Jim said, to have some sort of pitch reference while you’re working on intonation. One simple way to do this is to play against an open string (either above or below the “melody” string). I often warm up by playing scales, or very simple/slow tunes, against each of the open strings. Listen for the intervals. Minor intonation flaws become much easier to hear and correct.

* This sounds kind of obvious, but make sure your instrument is *really perfectly* in tune before you start playing. Trying to practice intonation on an almost-but-not-quite-in-tune instrument is counterproductive. I’ve resigned myself to the fact that it can take a good 5 minutes (or more) to really tune my fiddle properly.

* I used to follow Jim’s procedure of tuning the A to a reference pitch and then tuning by ear from then on. I’m no longer so sure that this is the best way. First of all, the A string might “settle” into a microtonally different pitch while you’re tuning the other three strings (my tuner informs me that this is often the case), so using it as a reference for the other strings is problematic. Also, you want to tune the open strings to equal temperament, so listening for “perfect” fifths leads to inaccuracies (the inaccuracy for the G string will be doubled). I like the tuning app called Elegantune, which shows the pitch as a graph against time. Open strings never produce a completely stable frequency-- there are little microtonal wobbles at the beginning and end of the bowstroke-- and Elegantune helps you to filter out the wobbles.

* Keep the fingernails on your left hand well trimmed. You want to aim for consistency of finger position and to press the string down with the same part of the finger pad each time; even a few days’ fingernail growth will interfere with that.

Finally, I know you say your fiddle is well set-up, but…

* Get a professional to look at the set-up of your instrument, and see if lowering the action (i.e. distance from string to fingerboard) is an option. Usually this just means re-cutting the bridge. This might be controversial in some circles, since it’s possible that too low of an action could affect the sound adversely (?). But a low action definitely helps with intonation IMO, especially for anything fancy like closed double-stops. Frankie Gavin (who has killer intonation, even compared with other professionals) has said in interviews that he prefers a low action.

Re: intonation

You’ll hear lots of advice, and all of it is good. But don’t get confused or disheartened if you can’t do everything that’s mentioned here or elsewhere. Different things work for different people at different stages in their development. So try everything, and then focus on the suggestions that seem to be most helpful.

Drones are good, ringing strings are good, keeping fingers low (and relaxed) is good. I don’t like the idea of trying to match a tuner, because as Alpine said, being able to hear is the thing. Bringing vision into the equation takes away from your concentration on hearing.

I’ll offer a few other things:

First, work on getting your bowing to be even and to produce an even tone. Sketchy bowing can throw off intonation, so be aware of that.

Second, do practice listening all by itself. Play an open string and meditate on it for a few seconds or more, and get the feeling of being comfortable - meaning no tightening of the body - when you produce a nice tone.

Also, to help hearing, bow two open strings together and listen closely to them. It will be upsetting if they’re not in tune, so take your time, make adjustments, and see how close you can get them. Don’t rush - that’s easier said than done, but my point is you want to get comfortable listening to your tone, even without fingering a note.

The next thing to help your hearing will be listening for “beats” when you play two open strings together. If the strings are just slightly out of tune, you’ll hear a subtle pulsing of the sound - that’s the beats.. When the notes are perfectly in tune, that pulsing will go away. The closer in tune the strings are, the slower the beats will vibrate. Beats are easier to hear on the lower strings, so don’t mind if you have a hard time hearing them between the A and E. If you keep doing this, and listening closely, you’ll hear them eventually.

So all of that is just to develop your hearing, your confidence when you’re sound is good, and your comfort in producing a nice sound. Then work on the other stuff - scales, or whatever you decide to do.

Re: intonation

Another little angle here - after tuning, one of the other quicks tuning checks I do is to play the open E-string + the 1st harmonic of the A-string (at the point halfway between the nut and the bridge).

Then, repeat on the other strings - open A-string + D-string at 1st harmonic, then open D-string + G-string at 1st harmonic, so you’re hearing fourths instead of fifths.

It’s just another way of checking accuracy. I personally find that it’s easier to spot an out-of-tune sound on a fourth than a fifth, but I’m not sure how many would agree.

I’ve got (for me) the perfect method, and the quickest too - a tuner button on a small practice amp. Just hit it, and it generates a pure tone A440. The volume decays, just like with a tuning fork, and the initial volume depends on how hard you press it. It’s quick and easy, as you have both hands free, and can easily repeat the whole process very quickly. An expensive tuner, if that is all you used it for, and you can’t fit it inside your case :)

The human ear generally cannot perceive pitch differences below 2-3 cents. As I pointed out, your bowing has far more influence on the strings pitch than that. And you tune your strings to just fifths and you’re “in tune” to a just A: but now you want to play in the key of G. *Everything* is “out of tune” by this logic. > A cheap tuner can have an accuracy of -+ 3 cents. So it can read in tune, but 2 notes could b 6 cent out and still read in tune!!

The error is additive, not random. If A is +3 cents out, then E is also +3 cents out.

> when the tuner accuaracy is 0.1 cent

When the tuner accuracy is 0.1 cent, we call this marketing, because it is entirely irrelevant to what the ear can hear or the instrument can produce.

> Can you provide proof for that?

If you have time to sit through an introductory course on control theory, I guess. Tuners work by comparing to a reference oscillator. That reference is fixed.

> a violinist, with well trained ears can hear a lot better than that

It’s certainly true that a trained expert can hear extraordinary gradations. It’s also true that in practice those same experts accept a huge range of intonations as being “in tune” depending on physical instrument and musical context. And it’s also true that identical tones are much easier to perceive as being different pitches. I would be most interested to hear your 1 cent file with, say, a fiddle and flute.

And once more, I reiterate that your bow moves the pitch much more than 2 cents depending on what you do with it. In almost all instruments the operator of the instrument has far more effect on the intonation than whatever system you tuned it to in theory.

Hopefully you got something out of the posts before the conversation wandered off, as often happens in these forums.

I’m exactly where you are: playing for 2 years, struggling with intonation, lifting fingers, etc. Coincidentally, my last lesson focused mainly on improving my intonation. Granted I’m not a long time player, but sharing my experience may be helpful. Most of what’s been posted is probably really good advice, but wouldn’t help me in the least. In 2-3 more years, perhaps tuning the A and then tuning everything off of it, or some such, may work, but for now, ain’t happening. I just don’t know what the notes should sound like well enough. My teacher says use the tuner, as does yours, so I’m taking his advice and using it. Since I’ve started, everything he’s told me to do has worked so I’m not second-guessing him.

So, he has me playing the open string, then the first finger and really listening for the note/interval to imprint that sound in my brain. Then open, 1, 2. Then open 1, 2, 3, using the tuner occasionally to check my fingers aren’t wandering. Basically working the scale. I do this slowly 5 – 10 during practice, really listening to the sound. It’s teaching/reinforcing in my brain what the notes sound like and my fingers’ muscle memory where they go on the finger board to find those notes. Simple basic stuff. Then, he says, as I’m playing, first hear the note in my head and the finger will find that position on the finger board. At first I had trouble wrapping my brain around the concept, but it’s making sense now. Eddy Chen has a video of this on YouTube worth watching.

Then, he has me playing tunes a bit slower, first hearing the notes in my head and listening for them when I bow. If I’m unsure or feel I’m getting off, then use the turner to check (I have a D’addario Micro Tuner clipped on and really like it). As mentioned by a previous responder, make sure it’s a smooth steady bow stroke. I constantly get fussed at about this. Ugh.

Finally, “don’t chase shadows”, by which he means don’t stress over missing the note by a little and wiggling the finger trying to find it when playing a tune. If it’s really off, correct it; otherwise, don’t worry too much about it. I’ve been doing these exercises for about a month and he says I’m hitting the notes about 85% of the time, which in his opinion is pretty good for my level. Hopefully that means my ear is improving.

Good luck and if you come across something that works for you, please share back.
