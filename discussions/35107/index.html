Re: Flute Embouchure

Embouchure is the most transient and elusive thing I’ve ever encountered in life. Don’t be discouraged. The secret, I think, is in relaxing and NOT working too hard at it. Nearly every great flute player I’ve ever had the good luck to watch up close has convinced me that this is true. My embouchure is only good when almost every muscle in my body is relaxed. My grip is positive, but relaxed, my lips mostly closed with minimal airflow. Even the position I’m sitting in makes a difference. When I get a good sound with the least amount of air I’ll start increasing the airflow without forcing it. Relaxed control gets the best, most reproducible sound. Too much force works against you. It’s all wrapped up in the laws of physics.

Ya have to work at good embouchure. Just don’t work too hard at it. Also, it’s worth doing in isolation. Embouchure doesn’t just “come” by playing tunes. It comes from being systematically and purposefully developed through any number of exercises starting with long tones and intervals. When your embouchure is spot on, you don’t even think about it when you’re playing tunes.

Good luck…it’ll come.

Re: Flute Embouchure

I agree with ross faison, a very good post, worth reading and studying. It simply will take time to develop your embouchure, partly to train it to work correctly and partly to properly develop muscle tone and control, which must not be hurried or rushed. One of the worst things a new player can do is to overwork their embouchure, straining the muscles, so plan on daily practice but stop playing if/when your muscles feel strained, then come back to it the next day when the muscles feel relaxed. And playing long tones, lots and lots and lots of them for as long as one breath can last (always breathe from the stomach), and then going on to intervals including scales, arpeggios, etc., are excellent studies to develop embouchure control of the flute. And while you are at it pay very close attention with your ears. In time your embouchure will automatically follow your ears, a very valuable skill to develop.

And cactustune mentioned a very real point, that once you have developed your embouchure then you must maintain it, and the only way to do that is by DAILY practice. It is not necessary to play for hour after hour every day, but as a flute player you owe yourself no less than a daily warm-up, maybe for only a few minutes if you are in a hurry, but get into the routine of a daily warm-up. Playing a flute is more than just a physical game, for it is a mental game, too, and a daily warm-up serves both your physical and your mental needs. Way to go!

Think of a flute as being a bag of many little tricks, and in the beginning be prepared to make about a million adjustments, where sometimes even the smallest adjustment can make a huge difference. Practice thoughtfully!

A good exercise to try once you’ve got a decent note (something in the middle of the range, between low G and middle D): take the note that’s sounding good, then slur to the next note (up or down) and try to maintain a consistent tone. EMBOUCHURE WORK - a post from the Krantz Flute ListServe (some intonation issues described are only meant for Boehm flute players):

“It has often been said in the flute community that there isn’t a magic bullet. Some good news here: For some students, actually, including myself when I made the switch, there was and is indeed a magic bullet. Not for all my students, of course, but most. I personally changed my embouchure in one week. Bingo, done. It was the way it was presented. I’ve had students able to achieve the same using this method, but admittedly I’ve also had students take months. It seems to be partly a matter of how one uses the mouth when speaking and just general flexibility of the lips. That kind of thing is surely quite individual. Releasing tension is sometimes the hardest thing to do and it was exactly that that gave me problems. It was like saying to me to hold heavy books midway rather than totally drop them on the floor. Totally dropping I could do, so to speak, but I couldn’t do anything well with the explanation of ”you’re too tense; relax a bit." Going cold turkey - dropping the books to the ground - was what did it for me. So, in college, with Robert Willoughby, I was given the magic bullet. And, because we’re always listening for a solid, nice tone - and achieved as quickly as possible - magic was what I wanted and got.

"So, I rambled on and haven’t given any specifics!
I teach this embouchure you’re trying to achieve - a more relaxed one with center of lips now being the focus rather than tightened corners - via the same way I was taught: a magic bullet of puffing out my cheeks and doing tongueless attacks. Whoo, whoo whoo, hold. Slowly so you are getting a feeling each time that you are doing it as best as you can and not hurrying the process without really thinking it through. I suggest first using a finger for your flute; no tone expected from your finger so therefore nothing to really stop you from trying something. Blow, downward as mentioned in other posts, but allowing the air to go right into the cheeks. Tight corners gone, replaced by much more scrunching up in the center of the lips where the focus of the tone will now be. (What I missed in my teacher’s attempts to change my embouchure in HS was the fact that he kept saying to relax, be looser, but nothing was being told to me that I was going to replace the area of focus so naturally you either get no tone or revert to using corners.) The stream of air will go directly down with the help also of a slight overbite to direct it. But you will be literally puffing your cheeks. Next try with the flute, but try first a high note to give you a really good “blow”, lots of air pressure. What you will find is that, without any use of the corners pulling back at all, a fairly good tone will come out with very little adjustment. Keep trying without tonguing. As was suggested, put the flute up, place, blow. Put it down, redo the process. The higher up you go the less scrunching needed which is why you try the upper register first. The lower you go, the aperture is supposed to get bigger, of course, so therefore more attention to focusing with the center muscles, scrunching, forming the opening, takes place as you get lower. The somewhat ideal amount, being the average, will be around D2. You will find this note to be receptive to little changes as well as will show you what more bringing in of those muscles you need to do. I’m not saying it’s tight, by any means, but also don’t assume that this relaxed embouchure means that you are going to go limp. I think that’s the most common aspect of not getting the embouchure when changing from the smiling one. You ARE focusing with muscles; just different ones.

“With the use of the center muscles as your focusing area, forming a rather nice pressure directed towards the center, intonation in the middle register is also a huge improvement and statements such as ”You must lower the C#" leave your consciousness altogether. The addition of the frown (although I frowned with my smile embouchure anyway so that wasn’t a change for me) in the upper register goes hand in hand as well.

“So, I was pretty much all set with my sound all through undergrad - was quite satisfied with my projection, intonation, comfort and consistancy - until Nyfenger made a ”final“ adjustment of placing the flute lower with the addition of using more bottom lip folded up and around the teeth and the upper lip out more, sort of exposing the under side of the lip. Talk about magic. He was so excited by his own discovery for himself he blurted out right at the beginning of a lesson ”You gotta try this!!! Put the flute here, do this, do that, now blow!" I blew - and blew myself out of the water. It was an amazing moment for both of us. So I mention this because you’re not going to do it ALL in a few weeks and there will always be experimenting you can do achieve different nuances or dynamics or whatever the little touches can be when developing a colorful playing technique, but the basic change of going from focusing with the corners vs. the center can come fairfly easy if you eliminate ALL the corner stuff first by puffing - no tonguing to interfere - and then, as you get easier with that, add more to it along the way.

"Again, so many details probably not quite understood in email form but you’re probably getting a better sense as put together all the help you’re getting. I do just want to emphasize that I thought it was so much better going cold turkey rather than getting a little more relaxed gradually; hard to hold those heavy books in a middle position….

“There have been other discussions in the past that might be in archives, including the use of a smiling embouchure that simply isn’t tight. Not everyone uses this no-corner embouchure and they are fine players. But if you’re studying with a person who is asking you to change, as often occurs, then you owe it to yourself to try it all and then you can make an informed decision. All the better.”

Just to clarify, most of my post above is quoted from Adrianne Greenbaum’s website.

As for the Galway approach, it’s a good place to start. Embouchure refinement is the work of decades/a lifetime, but you need a place to start. He advocates blowing into the flute, which is what suits the wooden flute well. (In broad strokes, the silver flute embouchure tends to be more ‘blowing across’, whereas the wooden flute embouchure is more ‘blowing down/into’.)

As suggested above, spend some time with just the headjoint, so you’re not thinking about fingerings, posture, holding the flute, etc. (You can play a variety of pitches by covering or uncovering the end with your free hand, and you can even use your finger to make a slide whistle.)

Sorry to be so late to the party, but I couldn’t bring up the site for the last few days. I thought it was down, but apparently not. Maybe David Levine or Ben Hall had a spell placed on my computer (just kidding, guys!).

Anyway, I do have some concrete suggestions for you based on 40 years of Boehm flute and 30 on Irish:

1. Aside from tone, the hardest thing to master is staying in the octave you want. It’s like when a boy goes through puberty - you keep jumping back and forth between the low and high tone of a given note. Nothing but time and practice cures this. You won’t know what you did to cure the problem, but it will definitely go away.

2. Use your low G as your base of operation. It is the easiest and most pleasant note to play. When you get frustrated, your low G may well improve your mood.

3. As you read this, blow on your hand as you would blow out a candle. Notice the air is cool and the stream is strong. Now exhale on your hand as you would to create warm steam on a mirror or pair of glasses. Notice the air is warm and the stream is softer. The second octave is a blow and the first octave is an exhale.

4. I won’t tell you how to form your lips as this is individual and experimentation is the key. However, for Irish playing, I suggest dropping the jaw in a relaxed fashion as low as you can without compromising the embouchure. The cavity of the mouth does much to create the power and resonance of good Irish playing.

5. Don’t try to articulate the notes the way you do on whistle. Avoid using your tongue (you can decide later how much you want to tongue notes, but not now). Use your diaphragm, throat, and mouth to punctuate notes and make them fuller. Doing this will force you to use your embouchure to control the flow of air and you will instinctively find some of the postioning you need to develop your lip. The emphasis here is on how the embouchure not only allows you to play the note, but to create the specific sound you want. The air flow and embouchure must work in tandem to allow you to master the sound and the style you’re working toward.

Play on and enjoy the journey!

Re: Flute Embouchure

Loads of good advice above!

I played Irish flute for around 35 years. Yes at the beginning it was a struggle to get a decent tone.

I would practice tone (not tunes) for hours, in front of a mirror, watching the shape of my embouchure. If for a moment or two I got a better sound I’d remember the shape of my embouchure at that moment and work to repeat that shape consistently.

The best exercise for building strong tone I found, borrowed from Boehm classical pedagogy, was to play a single long note starting as softly as possible and then steadily increasing the volume until it was as loud as possible and then back to as softly as possible STAYING AT EXACTLY THE SAME PITCH the entire time. Do this starting at Bottom D, just doing Bottom D for maybe ten or fifteen minutes, then E, then F#, slowing working your way up to high B. Do this exercise while watching an electronic tuner making sure the needle points straight up the entire time. The entire point is to build strength and flexibility of the embouchure, and the ability to stay AT ONE EXACT PITCH at ANY volume level you choose. This exercise will do amazing things for your tone.

Don’t hesitate to take some lessons from a good local Boehm flute teacher! These people know exactly how to get beginners to get the best possible tone. The idea that Irish flute and Boehm flute embouchures are very different simply isn’t true. Any Irish fluteplayer who can get a great tone out of an Irish flute can pick up a Boehm flute and get a great tone out of it, and any good Boehm fluteplayer can pick up your Irish flute and get a much better tone out of it that you can, probably.

Tone is all about getting a very focused airstream directed at exactly the right spot, and SUPPORT. It’s the support side that Irish fluteplayers often aren’t taught, but Boehm fluteplayers are, which allows the latter to get superior tone. Open the oral cavity, open the throat, support from the diaphragm (well, not technically, but that’s how people usually word it).

About blowing across v blowing down into the flute, it is simply not true that Irish fluteplayers do it one fixed way and Boehm fluteplayers do it another fixed way.

Boehm fluteplayers learn to adjust the angle of their airstream to get the maximum tone out of the three octaves played on that flute. They do it by pushing our or pulling back the jaw. The exercise I explained above develops that exact skill: the way you keep a note at the same pitch while blowing soft and hard is by changing the angle of the airstream.

Re: Flute Embouchure

“the embouchure so widely used in today’s Boehm camp only dates to the later 19th century”

I don’t know what you mean by that. If by “embouchure” one means the shape of the lip-opening, that statement is untrue on two counts 1) the embouchure varies from flutist to flutist and therefore 2) various shapes are used by the players of every sort of flute, be it Chinese traditional flute, or Indian flute, or Boehm flute, or Baroque flute, or Irish flute.

I’ve spent over 35 years playing Irish flute and dabbling in Boehm flute and various ethnic flutes, and I studied Baroque flute at university, and I’ve taken formal lessons from Baroque flute and Boehm flute teachers, and spent much time observing fluteplayers from various cultures, and the thing is, a flute is a flute.

You create a focused airstream hitting just the right spot, and adjust the angle of the airstream to get the pitch and timbre you want, and support the airstream, for maximum tone on ANY flute. This is true on all flutes in all cultures throughout time.

It’s simply not true that Irish fluteplaying and Boehm fluteplaying, in regards to embouchure formation and goals, are fundamentally different. I played a recording of Matt Molloy for a respected Boehm flute teacher and she said yes, that is an excellent tone, just what she has her students strive for.

What is different is that in the Irish traditional fluteplaying world (like in the Jazz fluteplaying world) a much wider range of timbres is considered acceptable or desirable. While each orchestral fluteplayer sounds unique, yet there is a narrower range of what is considered desirable. In the Irish and Jazz fluteplaying world it’s not “anything goes” but a wider range of timbres are acceptable. Matt Molloy with his pure powerful liquid tone is considered superb, but players with a tone that’s rough, dirty, and raw can be admired too.

And of course a major difference between orchestral fluteplaying and Irish traditional fluteplaying is the use of ‘diaphragm vibrato’ but this is a separate issue from embouchure.
