Given that most of us learn our music by Ear, surely all we need here, in the Tunes section of this site, as an aide-memoire, are the first few notes at the start of each tune?

Re: Surely we only need the first two bars?

“That would save lot of space in the Tunes section…”

It’s not actual space you know. There’s plenty virtual space knocking about for the rest of the bars.

Also some of the tunes are very tricky, even for experienced players and listeners. In the absence of an aged fiddler whom you can ask: “what was that precise combination of notes?”, we have either slowing-down-the-recording software, or a handy accurate transcription. (Or blunder through as best we can, but it’s Irish music, who cares iff it’s right?…Right?) Why is music notation a problem? Surely the long transcription of Neillidh Boyle’s reel “The Moving Clouds” is pretty handy even to the most skilled listener. https://thesession.org/tunes/1091/comments
Surely less information is less good! The simplified pub music of today simply does not have the nuances of the traditional music, and if you don’t hear it down the pub, why not find a transcription of a traditional player?

You know, I think what we have here is more quasi-elitism and bullying of those not as technically skilled as the rest of us! This is just more of the typical: “Why are you asking for the notes? Just learn it by ear! I can, ‘cus I’m great!”

Learning by ear (first of all) is a pretty simple thing to get used to. (So beginners, don’t intimidated or bullied by these guys into thinking they have some secret knowledge.) Martin Hayes says that Irish music is really pretty easy to learn and to play (from a purely technique point of view), so he says “in Irish music EVERYTHING is in the style!” And “Learned-By-Ear” does not automatically mean traditional style.

As Whistleblower said on an old post (what ever happened that guy?):

“People think that it’s traditional music because it’s largely taught by ear. But the important thing is: What are they teaching? That’s the problem …it’s standardized Irish-music-lite!”
https://thesession.org/discussions/20786

Just because something’s taught by ear it doesn’t make it traditional in style. Comhaltas teaches by ear and is doing its level best to replace traditional Gaelic music with a revivalist pub music that is the identical in Ireland, America, England and everywhere else their virus has spread.

In Scottish fiddle music (even more so than in Irish music) there is an enormous wealth of printed music that is (increasingly) available, and people are bringing the old style of playing to tunes that have been long forgotten, and bringing new life to the tradition in virtue of what has been preserved for the benefit of future generations. In Charles Gore’s “Echoes of a Golden Age - Rediscovering Scotland’s Original Fiddle Music” the author marvels at the fact that many contemporary Scottish “traditional” fiddle players have no interest at all in the sheet music collections of fiddlers from the 18th and 19th centuries that have been recently republished.
http://www.footstompin.com/products/books/echoes_of_a_golden_age__rediscovering_scotlands_original_fiddle_music

But in Ireland the attitude to printed collections is even worse. There are many people in Ireland who have a complete distain for those who preserve traditional music by carefully noting it down. In Ireland the mistaken belief that classical music can be noted down perfectly but Irish music is resistant to any documentation. And like NedScot said on this forum before: “As is classical music the sheet music is only an approximation of what ought to be played, and as in classical music the ornamentation is essential to the music (not just an add-on). Yehudi Menuhin wrote that it is very rare for classical violinists to play exactly what is written on the page, but that it occasionally happens and that the results are always dreadful!”
https://thesession.org/discussions/17012

The fact is that some learn-by-ear fanatics think that the dumbed-down version of Irish music that they teach and that changes from week to week (and more often than not disimproves depending on how loud the pub is) is not as good as the old style of playing. The written record of tunes can be a way of stopping the decay of Irish music (obviously, in combination with a thorough grounding in the techniques of one of the traditional regional styles).

Just because the sheet music will not get you the whole way does not mean it should be discarded. To play Stravinsky you will need the technique to play what’s on the page, but you will also need a considerable knowledge about the correct interpretation and performance of 20th century Russian music. It can’t just read it off the page. Likewise in Irish or Scottish music, you need a considerable training in the authentic performance style before you can play any tune in a traditional way (though Irish musicians avoid this way of describing the process).

Just because something’s learned by ear does not make it traditional, or good, or worth listening to. And just because someone learned a tune from notation does not automatically mean it is not being played in the traditional style. If only aural learning could produce traditional music then Irish music is in trouble! If there is any merit to this thesis, then please list those tunes that (music readers) John Doherty and Neillidh Boyle learned by ear and those they learned from staff notation.

Re: Surely we only need the first two bars?

“Not all the good tunes get played in sessions I go to, so I can hear them”
Not sure what you mean there?

“Learning from the dots is a far more gracious way into a new tune than listening to a bloody midi!”

Learning from the dots is exactly the same as learning from the midi. Exactly, precisely, down to every last tiny detail of every single thing. The two formats are, by the very definition of the way computers copy and decode binary ones and zeros, completely and utterly identical. The information is the same.

What’s interesting though, is the way your brain thinks that the written form is more gracious. How can this be since the two are identical? It’s because when you read music, your brain embellishes the dry notes with preconceptions of how it should sound.

So here’s the rub. If your preconceptions of how the music should sound are correct, i.e. you are already well versed in the style of music you are reading, then all is fine.

But what if you’re not?

Re: Surely we only need the first two bars?

Dick, with all due respect, I’m not sure I agree with the point of your question at all. If it’s merely to do with saving space, it may have a certain validity. If it is to force people down the path of purely ear-training, IMHO it’s a little too draconian. I understand what’s behind the question, of course. Some folk can’t think past the written note and, as a result, play as if on railway lines, ie, they can’t get off - as in the case of many classical musicians in that if you remove their music, it’s like cutting off their hands.

But the ear too can be faulty - the Chinese Whispers syndrome (Did I hear ‘Tickle your ass with a feather’ or ‘Particularly nasty weather?’!!) So you can ‘mis-hear’ how a tune goes.

But surely the main fault with the ‘two bars’ approach is that not every tune falls into a similar pattern. Many I know take an unusual meander in the second part of the return. And what about multi-part tunes? Did that tune I heard last night have 3, 4 or 5 parts?

So what’s wrong with both approaches? If you look on the written note as merely a memory jogger (such as the names of tunes) and not gospel you can’t go far wrong. And as long as you keep your ear (and mind!) open for the tune ‘as played’ in a session, you can’t go wrong for either method.

I have used both methods both in teaching and learning. Some people take to the aural approach much more quickly than others. Others have to really slog at it. With experience it becomes somewhat easier but, even then, the written tune can at times iron out hidden wrinkles in the melody! So let’s not disparage one more than the other if either produces a tune played well - as long, of course, as the graduates from either school know that you can’t believe everything you see or hear!

As Groucho Marx once said, ‘What do want to believe - my word or the evidence of your own eyes?’

Re: Surely we only need the first two bars?

“What’s interesting though, is the way your brain thinks that the written form is more gracious. How can this be since the two are identical? It’s because when you read music, your brain embellishes the dry notes with preconceptions of how it should sound.”

It’s because written music looks,or *can* look, quite elegant, whilst midi files sound hideous.

But also, consider this: Notation gives you are given a visual representation, which you have to *convert* into music, either on your instrument or in your head - how well you do this obviously depends on how well you know the style, how good your technique is, how familiar you are with the notation system etc.. A midi file, on the other hand, gives you a very poor auditory representation of the tune, which scarcely qualifies as music, let alone traditional music - and it would take an extremely powerful imagination, or a lot of surplus earwax, to perceive it as such.

Of course there is no substitute for listening to good traditional players - least of all, a midi file. But the great thing about sheet music is that, whilst it may not be able to capture essential elements of style, neither does it impose any style (or lack thereof) of its own. Learning tunes by ear at full speed may be an ability that most of us can acquire in time, but it takes an exceptional musician to be able to pick up every nuance in this way (Llig - if you are such a musician, I applaud you, but most of us are not).

Daiv, why did you decide to not use sheet music in the first place? What was your reason then?

I’ve known plenty of people who stopped using the dots to force themselves to develop and improve their listening abilities. But they later continued to use the dots, just not so much for learning tunes.

I’ve never stopped using notation--dots, abcs, tablature, chord charts, etc. But I place a much higher priority on listening because music is so essentially aural.

Sure, notation can be a useful tool. I like being able to play from all the abcs and tabs online, and sheet music books, and scraps of paper handed me by other musicians. It also helps sometimes to transcribe an unfamiliar tune to “see” its underlying structure, or to keep track of really complex melodies or chord voices. As a music teacher, being fluent in many forms of notation helps me connect with students who want to understand how notation works. Still, when I teach, I teach first by ear and face-to-face playing. Using our ears and aural pathways is what makes music, well, music, and not literature or painting.

Re: Surely we only need the first two bars?

“Learning from the dots is exactly the same as learning from the midi. Exactly, precisely, down to every last tiny detail of every single thing. The two formats are, by the very definition of the way computers copy and decode binary ones and zeros, completely and utterly identical. The information is the same.”

Presentation is important - try looking at the ones and zeros making up an image file some time. The information content is the same whether you look at the bits or the rendered image, but one is a lot easier to make out than the other.
If someone doesn’t read music but can pick up a tune listening to the midi file, there’s a big difference between the two, regardless of the fact that the underlying representation for both is the same short string of alphanumeric characters.

Of course I’d agree with you that relying on either sheet music or a midi file to the exclusion of actual players playing the tunes in vivo is a gross mistake, and that both formats share some stark limitations, but learning a tune by listening to it, even if it’s listening to a generated sound file, is very different, cognitively speaking, from learning it by reading it from a generated page of staff notation.
