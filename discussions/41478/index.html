Re: Fiddle Advice

You don’t need to change anything on your violin to make it a fiddle. It’s the same thing. If you bought it from a luthier, he should have set it up properly for you. If you bought it privately, take it to a luthier to be checked over.

The first technique you learn is bow control, how to make a proper sound.
And the first position, including all flats and sharps, in tune.

I suggest you take lessons. Please do yourself that favor.

Re: Fiddle Advice

> I heard you have to do something with the bridge I don’t remember exactly what.

Short answer, no. Some fiddlers use a bridge which is less rounded on the top, so the strings are in less of an arch. This doesn’t matter in the slightest for a beginner.

> I also appear to be having trouble with getting rosin to stick to my bow.

That’s normal with a new bow that has no rosin on it. Just roughen the surface of the rosin (sandpaper or a knife will do it). Note that once rosined, a bow doesn’t need huge amounts. Experiment and see how long you can go before it obviously needs rosin - further than you might think.

Re: Fiddle Advice

As others have said you don’t change the bridge to make it a fiddle. But you do need to keep an eye on the bridge and keep it upright, it will tend to start leaning over time. Look at the fiddle square on from the side, the back face of the bridge (nearest the tailpiece should be in line with the inner notches on the F holes, and the back face should be vertical, at right angles to the top (which means the front face is angled back slightly because the bridge is tapered). If it isn’t right just grab the top of the bridge between two fingers and thumb and carefully pull it back into position.

Re: Fiddle Advice

Kellie, there are no easy answers. This is not like blowing into a whistle where the amount of air is predetermined by the design.

re: finger pressure. The absolute mininum needed to make a clean sound. Waaaaaay less than a mandolin. Like 10% of mandolin pressure.

re: bow pressure. This is a very individual thing. Think of bowing like breathing life into music. There is an optimum range to get the fiddle to sing, but even in that optimum range, there is a lot of variation. And outside the optimum range, there is also room. For now, suffice it to say that very little bow pressure is needed. Just the weight of the bow combined with a bit of weight of your hand/fingers will generally do the trick. (and of course, every fiddle is different in this regard). But you must get the bow hold under control before you worry overly much about pressure and all of that.

re: learning fiddle in general. This is a lifelong pursuit. You will most likely sound horrible for a year, and then barely passable for two. At the end of the second year, things generally click and you will start sounding decent. This has so much to do with not only the physical skills, but your brain needs time to learn and adapt to this as well. You’re younger than most of us, so your growth will probably be faster, but there is no easy path and no shortcuts. Persistent, regular, deliberate, focused practice is the only way to master difficult instruments. IMO, if you want to learn fiddle, you should really really really think about ignoring your other instruments for a couple years. Also, asking a few questions on the mustard board is not going to get you where you want to go. However, teacher and practice will.

re: your fiddle. From your questions, you obviously got a yours from a generic source, just like the mandolin. All of the advice you got in that thread about setup is even more imperative on fiddle. Get it set up so you don’t hurt yourself or fight the instrument. The action (string clearance at nut and bridge) is incredibly vital to playing properly and fluidly. Get decent strings.

p.s. Bach is the shiznit.

Re: Fiddle Advice

I’ve had a listen to your recording. You need to spend working on rythmn and pitch, the building blocks of music, before you will get anywhere with ANY instrument. Get a metronome and play with it, very slowly. Get a tuner and play against a drone so you can hear the relationship between pitches. You can’t make music without being able to hear rythmn and pitch.

On top of that, you need to learn the technique(notice I didn’t say ornamentation) associated with whatever instrument you are playing. Picking, bowing, breathing, hand and body position, whatever it is for the instrument you choose to study. Switching from instrument to instrument will not improve those things, only careful amd methodical STUDY. Not playing, not just practicing, but studying.

Once you’ve done all that, you can start to work on the music. And you have to study that too. There are no shortcuts. However long it takes for you to buckle down and start mastering the basics is how long it will take for you to be satisfied with music. Plenty of people never take the time and they wonder for years why they don’t sound like their favorite players and why they aren’t welcomed to sessions or bands.

In the past, you have expressed interest in playing music professionally. I will say this as plainly as possible: no one will hire you if you do not have a certain mastery of the basics of music, the technique on your instrument, and the musical idiom they are expecting you to perform. And that is the bare minimum requirement.

The solution is simple. Get a good teacher, listen to everything she/he says and put in into practice with all the discipline you can muster until that teacher tells you that she/he can’t teach you anything else. Then find someone who can and repeat the process.

Re: Fiddle Advice

“This is not like blowing into a whistle where the amount of air is predetermined by the design. ”

Pressure is predetermined by temperature and humidity as well as design. However there is still a lot of skill required to blow a whistle correctly: starting / stopping the airflow crisply with the tongue to get rid of the awful ‘kid on a recorder’ sound, then maintaining the pressure at a constant level over the duration of a slur.

Then once that is under control, the pitch of the notes is slightly dependent on breath pressure and every one is slightly different, so that has to be learned. The breath can be altered, both through phrasing of slur patterns and ‘pulsing’ to add drive to the music, assuming it’s a dance tune.

Beginners often perceive themselves as better than they actually are., which is caused by a number of factors:

*Inexperience, not knowing what to listen for.
*Surprise that one is able to do something, particularly if they have had prior bad experiences with it.
*Information overload, the conscious mind can only think about one thing at a time, until a task is automated by your subconscious it will ‘go out the window’ whenever you focus on something else.

Kellie, my man, something you need to know brotha’. Self-Awareness, on positive and negative ends, is one of the most virtuous and valuable skills, for any musician, in my opinion. If you don’t understand how you sound, you’ll misinterpret how you’ll be perceived by the audience. If you understand how you sound, you can play with confidence, and avoid attempting things that still need work. Now, you may be impressed by your progress in one day. But man, it’s only a day. One day’s work. And you won’t be getting much from that one day. It’s all the days, over time, that are going to matter. So instead of being impressed by one day’s work; Convert that impression with yourself into *confidence in your potential*. Being impressed with yourself will cloud your judgment of yourself, and maybe even make you lazy. And it causes you to miss all of the things that are wrong with what you just did. Being confident in your potential, on the other hand, will drive you to strive for greatness.

I don’t want to discourage you, or kill your confidence, I just don’t want you to make the same mistake I made. Rushing into it. Attempting to move too fast, and doing too much. Fiddle requires patience and consistency, and you’ll only build bad habits trying to move too fast. It’s going to take time. Work at it, and evaluate yourself every several weeks so you can monitor your progress. You’ll need it when you’re having a bad day and need to realize how far you’ve come.

After I got started, I took lessons for several weeks, and those helped a lot as well. I’ve been to other workshops since. I took my last fiddle lesson sometime last year, and only stopped because I needed to make budget cuts. All of those lessons have mattered.)

Re: Fiddle Advice

Kellie -

There are no shortcuts re: fiddle. I’ve been playing fiddle now intensively for 5 years, and I have been a serious traditional musician on a couple of other instruments for a very, very long time. Technique is extremely important with fiddle. You will rue the day that you find yourself being forced to unlearn bad habits acquired early on. The learning curve is long, and is some ways, forever. But the curve can be great fun. When studying GHB with legendary NY instructor Donald Lindsay, I drove 4 hours round trip every few weeks for over two years. It was worth every minute.

Although I now live in Vermont, I’m born and raised in Ohio. There are plenty of competent, excellent instructors in Cincinnati, Columbus, Cleveland and all points in between. When I was your age, and first immersed myself in traditional music, I drove. To Kentucky, to Indiana, wherever. Find a fiddle instructor right now, and drive. One hour, three hours, it doesn’t matter. You’re ready to get serious or not. Posting on the session will help a bit, but it won’t get you there.
