The Violin.

“Due to the apparent universal confusion about the difference between the Violin and the Fiddle, I have decided to tell people that ‘I play Violin in the style of Fiddle’.”

With the delightful vow renewal with the piano a few years ago, and the adventurous repertoire expansion I’ve had with the concertina the last several seasons; I’m ready to start my next big music project, this time, with the fiddle. The project is a skill expansion where I’ll not only be adopting one more fiddle style(Bluegrass), but I’ll also be learning violin styles. To expand my skill-set, I’ll be learning my all-time favorites from several of my music collections, including my beloved R&B and Modern Classical collections. Some of this music, I’ve been indulging in recreationally for over 25 years, and spans across a number of genres and cultures. It’s mostly song, but there is some classical and instrumental music too.

The quote above is mostly tongue-in-cheek, but I included it to contextualize my question. Do any of the fiddle players here play styles of music more suited for the violin style? From what I’ve gathered, how we distinguish violin from fiddle is by what kind of music we play; I’m thinking because different styles of music require different skill sets and that’s how the variable sounds are achieved. I’m curious to know what your experience was like as a fiddle player learning violin music. Or hey! What your experience as a violinist was like learning fiddle music! I’m excited, but also a little apprehensive. The pianist in me is hoping that it won’t be too much like learning a new instrument. I’ll never be able to say for certain, but I think I’m done learning new instruments. Not after what the concertina and fiddle have put me through with Irish music, though, I say that in good humor and affection :-)

Well, I guess I kind of “play violin in the style of fiddle.” I started lessons in elementary school and grew up playing in orchestras. Technically (bowing, fingering, intonation, speed) nothing in Trad feels hard in comparison to classical. But the magical lift, rhythm, lilt in Irish fiddling has been the challenge! Feels like a yearslong (lifelong?!) challenge. I know I still sound like a “classical stiff”…but over time slowly have gone from hating my attempts at fiddling to liking them once in a while. I have gone from being a sheet music learner to more of an ear learner, that helped. I can still hear the ‘violinist’ behind my fiddling, but I just had to accept that it’s going to be part of my personal style… instead of trying to totally erase it ( I don’t think I can).

Re the different skill sets- IMHO there is a basic set of skills required just to get pleasing sounds out of a wooden box with strings :-) …and then from there, the genres fork off into their own skills (i.e. improvisation for bluegrass, loads of sheet music reading for classical, alternate tunings and shuffle rhythm for Old-Time, etc). But due to the fact that it’s the same instrument, changing genres will never feel anywhere close to the challenge of learning a different new instrument!

That’s been my experience…
and look forward to hearing about your multifaceted music journey!

Re: The Violin

Re “Hearing the violinist behind the fiddling” - That is a good question, Jerone, and gets to the heart of it!

For my own playing, I would describe it as a lack of the creative melodic / stylistic variations that I hear fiddlers use…an even precise cleanliness about the playing (lack of grit)…overuse of expression (such as getting soft/loud/slow/fast within a musical phrase)…use of touches of vibrato…It’s easy to identify me as a classically trained player ‘putting on’ a different style, vs somebody who grew up playing in the tradition.

The mechanics (bowing, tone, fingering) and way of expression that I was taught, correspond to the sound of classical music. In order to fiddle, I need to adjust those…for example using ornamentation, and catching jig/reel/hornpipe/polka rhythms in the bowing. If I don’t actively use my Ear to override the ingrained habits of classical playing, it will come out sounding like Bach…( at one of the first sessions I tried, I emerged with an invitation to join someone’s chamber group … so much for my ‘fiddling’! :-)

Re “Ear” above - from years of listening to Irish/Scottish trad, I have an internal soundprint of how the music should sound…enough to recognize when my own playing doesn’t sound “right”. That’s when the work begins…

Interestingly, I have met many other ex-classical players in Trad (people who grew up playing silver flute or violin) but I have not yet met any fiddlers (except for you) who want to play more “classically”!

Re: The Violin.

I started off as a fiddler and have played Irish trad for fifty years, so that’s the language my fiddle speaks. But I’m a sucker for any beautiful or powerful melody, so I’ve picked up tunes from a wide range of genres. At one point, a top-flight classical violinist mentored me for nearly two years, and the skills I learned there enabled me to play pieces well outside the usual skillset of a purely Irish trad fiddler.

But my experience is the flip side of cancion’s in that my Irish trad sensibilities want to leak into everything else I play. When I play Bach or bluegrass, a censor goes off in my head, bleeping out every roll or bowed triplet I want to insert. :-) So I don’t play Minor Swing with Irish ornaments, but it’s uncomfortable feeling my natural instincts stifled like that.

For me, the biggest challenge in learning another genre was finding music that led me through a reasonable *progression of new skills,* rather than jumping into a piece that demanded many new skills all at once. I tried to be systematic about learning vibrato, shifting, and an array of bow techniques, and that takes time. Time (it turned out) that I’d rather spend playing Irish trad. (Bowing is a lifelong pursuit. Here’s a peek down that rabbit hole: https://violinlounge.com/violin-bowing-techniques/).

So I ended up dabbling in other genres. Enough to enjoy playing some of my favourite melodies, and to hone skills I rarely use in Irish trad (higher positions, extended double-stop riffs, some syncopated bowing concepts, etc.). But not enough to really pass as a fluent classical, bluegrass, or swing player, at least not to my ear.

None of that crept into my trad playing. But it did make me a better musician, with more expressive range.

Hope this addresses some of what you’re after, Jerone. No doubt Jim Dorans and others will have some worthwhile insights.

Jerone said :

“Do any of the fiddle players here play styles of music more suited for the violin style? From what I’ve gathered, how we distinguish violin from fiddle is by what kind of music we play; I’m thinking because different styles of music require different skill sets and that’s how the variable sounds are achieved.”

I agree that there are different skill sets for playing (eg) classical and Irish trad, and I suppose the main (left-hand) skill in classical is being able to shift position when required. Also (and this is not widely taught), the ability to decide which position to shift to (or not shift at all, but use a finger extension instead). Actually, this economy of movement can help in playing some trad tunes too, eg playing G tunes in A. Double-stops are often required too, and these are infrequent in trad, but copious in forms like bluegrass.

In classical, obviously there are more technically difficult bowings required (eg off-the-string, ricochet etc), and many forms of jazz require different bowings again (slurring 2 notes up, then 2 notes down), plus techniques like “chain bowing” too.

Now to my main point : the basics of a good classical technique will generally be required for any style of playing, and here I’m talking about how to get a decent tone from the instrument, regardless of the genre. There are great players of Irish trad both who went down the classical route, and others who didn’t, and sometimes you can tell, although both still produce an equally good sound.

Another surprise here is that most of the ornaments like cuts and rolls can be found in the classical style, although they’re normally just referred to as grace notes, or combinations of grace notes. The only difference in Irish trad is the way they are executed (the timing is very different), but there still is a requirement to be able to play them.

I started playing fiddle, self-taught at about 15 (I’m 70 now), and for years I scraped away, thinking all was well. Apparently it was, to listeners too, but at age 30 I went to to a highly regarded classical teacher for two years, and I learned an awful lot. He sharpened up my bowing and got me producing a far better tone too.

Hello briantheflute ! “So what is ‘the usual skillset of a purely Irish trad fiddler’?” Well, you asked gimpy, and maybe he will reply, but I’ll give my take on it for now. His statement could easily be misinterpreted, and I think you have done just that. In context, he is just talking about the skills that are required for other genres, such as shifting and complex bowing, much like I have described above. Danny could play well in any key, which worked well when during Irish Fellowship Club ceilis, a singer would come up and just start singing without a reference note. The piano accordion player Bill and Dan (plus the drummer Tony Keenan) could follow right along in any key. Dan taught me not to fear the flat keys, but I’m miserable in Bmaj. When I play with an uilleann piper who’s on a C, B or Bb set, I always tune down, though. The timbre of the strings is so warm. Playing along in standard tuning in this situation just doesn’t lay out right for the ornaments and double stops.

Danny said he had to learn to play in every key in the early 50’s when he was stationed at Thule AFB in Greenland and played with a joint Canadian/US country band. Years later after he retired he toured with Johnny Rodriquez. When Johnny introduced the band, he would say something along the lines of “over here on fiddle is Danny Cormier who grew up on Cape Breton Isle in Canada, a French-Canadian playing Scottish music who is now playing with a Mexican-American singing Country music. We can forget the words to songs in three languages.”

How I wish Danny was still alive to give me some lessons. He was a fiddler through and through with amazing left hand position technique and a power bow arm. He always carried two violin cases, one of which contained a bottle of whiskey and four shot glasses.

That is to say with the fingering and technique I should have been using for it at the start (I know, it is quite a convoluted journey :-) ) . However, on the flute I am less defined on the change in style, in that I might have to remind myself between the two, because at times I find myself in the frame of mind of playing the other style even though I am keeping to the tune properly. Everyone is different, but I think on fiddle/violin, being closer to guitar, that adding a skill-set or style should not be too confusing or dificult, and not be detrimental.

I’ve always followed a philosophy of “using all of your resources”. So where this is concerned, I would say it’s always a good idea to have a versatile toolbox. Ages ago here, so long ago that I can’t even remember if it was on one of my discussions or on someone elses; Someone said something to the effect of, “…it’s simply always better to be prepared…”. We want the music we’re learning to be either at our level(challenging enough for comprehensible input to work it’s magic) or below it. So, sometimes learning things that you may not use frequently can still come in handy. For example, I like a lot of EMaj tunes because I listen to Cape Breton music, and all of the EMaj tunes that I know require shifting into the 3rd position. And unlike tunes that just may have tricky bowing or are out of range by a note or two, I can’t run to the concertina for ease because EMaj tunes have tricky fingerings for C# to F# phrases(On concerntina, it’s a rule not to play adjacent buttons with the same finger consecutively, because you’re essentially jumping and it’s easy to trip over the buttons and lose rhythm and flow. The catch about the C# to F# is that they aren’t adjacent, they’re another row away but on the same finger! You’re not jumping from adjacent buttons, you’re jumping across an entire row). It took a lot of music hours to figure it out, but I finally sorted out how to pull them off with a technique I learned in my concertina lessons. But to bring me back around to my point, it gets to where you’re confronted with something that you haven’t had to face with the music that you already know. So it’s nice just to have something in the toolbox.

Re: The Violin.

Jerone, I have a corollary to your “be prepared” and that’s “it’s better to have options rather than only one way to play something.”

When I was still a newbie on fiddle, I was learning Farrell O’Gara’s and finding the opening phrase somewhat awkward, just the bit that goes |A,D D/D/D A,DFA|. Specifically, I was playing the bowed triplet down-up-down, and my bow hand wanted to continue that last downstroke onto the low A, again, but it felt like a clumsy slur on the string crossing. I couldn’t make it sound clean and crisp.

So I asked some other fiddlers how they played that bar. And I got every answer under the sun: slurring the down bow, using an up bow to catch the second low A, playing the triple up-down-up, omitting the triplet all together, omitting the second low A (e.g., |A,D D/D/D DEFA|), and so on.

Most of these hadn’t occurred to me (I was a genuine newbie), but the take-home message for me was that any of these options could work and one wasn’t necessarily “better” than any other. So it made sense to learn all of them. Doing so led to not just more options but an overall improvement in my bow hand control and agility.

Today, decades later, I use all of them when I play Farrell O’Gara’s, partly as little variations, partly as a reminder to not limit myself to one way of doing things.

The same “have options” mantra applies to broader situations that might draw on skills learned from playing other genres—learn tunes in more than one key, consider vibrato on a long note instead of reflexively doing a roll or triplet, etc. But I don’t want my style to sound out of place for Irish trad, so I have another corollary: Would Bobby Casey play it this way? Casey was rooted in West Clare fiddling, but he counted Michael Coleman as an influence and lived in Dublin and then London, sessioning and playing with musicians with a range of styles. To me, Bobby Casey is the model of inventive and playful fiddling, yet never straying from a traditional sound.

So I might use vibrato on a long note, but only once, as a one-off variation. And I might learn a tune in two or three keys, but only the keys that are commonly found in trad sessions (e.g., I play Calliope House in E, D, and G because I’ve encountered it in those keys at various sessions). Having Bobby Casey on my shoulder keeps me from borrowing too much from other genres while still being flexible and playful.
