Re: Bb, A, G, or F - most versatile mid-range whistle when playing with a guitarists and/or singers?

It all depends on the singer’s vocal range. Also remember the table of available keys for a whistle, bearing in mind the third key, A for a D whistle, does involve a half-hole for the 7th note;
D can also be played in G and A;
C………………………………F and G;
Bb…………………………….Eb and F;
A……………………………….D and E
G……………………………….C and D;
F……………………………….Bb and C.
Also remember that the KEY of a song does not refer to the RANGE of a song; you might need notes below the tonic ( keynote ) or well into the second octave, so playing the whistle of the actual key the song is in is not always the most useful exercise.
As an example, we’re currently working on a song accompaniment for a singer we hadn’t worked with before; she came to us with a demo recording set in G; we suggested that she was singing it far too low for the best use of her voice, and got her to take it up to C, which is not the best key to try to play an open-hole flute, which is of course basically the same as a low-D whistle for fingering. The low-G whistle works perfectly for this.

Re: Bb, A, G, or F - most versatile mid-range whistle when playing with a guitarists and/or singers?

Yes as people have said if you give a singer/guitarist a capo anything goes.

They might do the same song with the capo at a different place depending on how their voice feels at that moment- the song could be in Bb one day, B the next.

A whistler who is regularly going to be doing that really needs to have whistles in every chromatic key, so that you only need to learn a particular song once, wherever it feels best under your fingers, and then pick up whatever whistle you need to match the key the guitarist/singer is using at that particular moment.

It happened to a Boehm fluteplayer I know- she was going to accompany a guitarist/singer guy for a set of several songs. The songs were all going to be in G and C etc. Right before they were about the begin the first song the guitarist slid his capo up one fret and G became G#! C became C# and so the whole set went. Though she’s a good fluteplayer, playing in all those sharps really gave her a workout!

This wouldn’t have troubled a whistleplayer who had every key there in his whistle-roll.

Re: - most versatile whistle?

There are several generalisations, assumptions, & potential conclusions running through this thread.
One of the most prevalent is that a whistle’s key *determines* how suitable it is for playing under certain conditions. It may not be so simple. A high D whistle, for instance, varies depending on several factors including; the tune being played, the player’s awareness of their own range, breath control & dynamics, the bore of the barrel, the style of fipple & any tweaking of it, whether or not the player chooses a higher or lower octave at will (relative to a desired effect), the de facto ‘key’ * … etc.

Without considering the precise application (solo vs instrumental ensemble vs playing with a singer vs trad session) any conclusion is open to numerous skeptical questions.

Re: Bb, A, G, or F - most versatile mid-range whistle when playing with a guitarists and/or singers?

About Amazing Grace on a Bb whistle, of course each singer might have his/her own idiosyncratic range, but the Hymnal range (arrived at over hundreds of years of The General Public singing together) assumes a C-c core range. One-octave tunes are usually put in that range. Tunes that have a greater range push upwards and downwards equally from that C-c range.

In Hymnals, Amazing Grace is usually in F (range C-c) though a few Hymnals have it in G (range D-d). When one does Amazing Grace on the Highland pipes and the choir and congregation sings along, they always complain that it’s too low (key of Eb, range Bb-bb).

The common Hymn tune that requires a Bb whistle is SLANE (Be Thou My Vision, Bridegroom And Bride, and others). Every Hymnal I’ve seen has it in the key of Eb, range Bb-eb, that is, a full tone below C to a tone-and-a-half above c. This fits beautifully on a Bb whistle.

Amazing Grace was referred to a little while back in this discussion, and what key it might be performed in.
Amazing Grace is actually a Sacred Harp, or Shape-Note, setting of a text which is written in four-part harmony, to be sung unaccompanied, and is correctly titled New Britain, not to be confused with two completely different settings of the same text elsewhere in the same volume. The written key of the original setting is C, but this is purely a convention of printing the arrangements to avoid the use of ledger-lines, as when it is sung the pitch for the performance is set by a “pitcher”, ( no, not a jug of water, nor a baseball player ), to suit the voices of the assembled singers, and may not be anywhere near the pitch as it appears on the printed page.
Judy Collins, at one of the Newport Folk Festivals in the early‘60s’, heard it performed by a church group brought up from one of the Southern states to perform this almost-forgotten style of hymn-singing to the young audience, and the rest is history. As many bagpipers at funerals know to their profit.
Sacred Harp has had a great revival in recent years, with many sings occurring in other parts of the world, not just the US of A; England, Ireland….Poland, anyone ?
Check out Sacred Harp New Britain on youtube for how it’s meant to sound. The harmonies might surprise you.
Oh, and no religious alligence is expected or required; just singing loudly all day with a group of friends makes you feel good. It’s the endorphins, you know.
