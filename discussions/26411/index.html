Added by Rollmop on January 4th, 2011

Shouldn’t folk music…… move forward?

Contraversial subject I know, but having been involved in scottish and irish traditional music as listener and player for about 15 years now, there are some attitudes and understandings that still confuse me (forgive me if I am covering old ground- I read quite a lot of old forum posts but I didn’t see this precise topic).

I’ve not been reading this forum long, focussing instead on playing the tunes- but at a certain point I can’t fail to notice some of the odd comments. Surely folk music is music for the people, by definition, which develops and progresses as the people develop and progress- somewhat akin to language. However it seems that whenever a “new-style” tune is posted, or when the subject matter comes up in past forum posts, there will be remarks it being non-traditional and therefore not valid in some way.

While clearly people will have their preferences, is it not true (and indeed not really fantastic!) that naturally, the musical style should develop- whether it be the tune or the arrangement, if it also respects the musical traditions. I mean, we can all plod along playing Neil Gow tunes to a I-IV-V on-beat accompaniment until next century’s cows come home, but it seems to me that the really exciting thing is when the traditions of the past are taken forward into a new age, where people are different and their attitudes to music are different. Not to mention that this will help the style keep going and gain popularity amongst the young who will simultaneously learn a respect for the old traditions (n.b. this is definitely the case in my native Scotland, where folk has really “woken up” in the last 10 years). For me, as a Scot, it gives me quite a warm feeling to know that the excellent traditions of the past are being refreshed, updated, taken forward, rather than dying off.

One of the musicians who seems to keep coming up in the commentary is Mike McGoldrick. Two things on that- firstly, he doesn’t actually seem to me to be that revolutionary or shocking anyway (save the odd jazz horn section), and secondly, he seems to have a very great respect for the tunes and the heritage. But yet the comments I have seen have been odd- “definitely NO syncopation” or some snide comment about the addition of a more musically interesting chord in the accompaniment. If this kind of thing had happened in the classical arena, we’d still be listening to plainchant, without the “rich tapestry” of Baroque, Romantic and Modern music we have today.

All styles of art move forward, drawing on the past as inspiration and motivation, so why not folk music? Why seemingly more friction to change in this arena than any other of music I have encountered?

There’s room for innovation, for change, for fusing traditional music with whatever didjeridoo/Scandinavian/Tibetan mouth music/jazz that suits your fancy. You can syncopate it, you can write funky modern tunes in 13/5. Not only that, it’s not as if people aren’t doing this.

There’s also room for grumpy gits who like tunes played along more traditional lines, “just the tunes ma’am,” no fusion or tossery, and who like listening to Kitty Lie Over or old Willie Clancy 78s.

And of course, very few people fall solely into one camp or the other, so there’s room for everyone in between. At the end of the day, both serve a purpose. The roots are just as important to the music as new stuff, if not more so. The best innovators are usually well immersed in the fundamentals of the music.

It really gets my goat when people have a moan about how much friction to change there is in trad music, as if the music is breathing its last breath because there’s just not enough forward musical innovation happening. That’s patently BS. There are plenty of musicians taking the music in new artistic directions. And at the same time, plenty of people listening to the old recordings and trying to incorporate that into their playing.

Let the conservatives be conservative and let the innovators innovate.

Re: Shouldn’t folk music…… move forward?

No, seriously. It is changing, it’s just that it’s changing quite subtly, and in ways that are very difficult for outsiders to discern. The people who suggest that Mr McGoldrick is ‘revolutionary’ tend to be those whose understanding of traditional Irish music is limited.

It’s more interesting to look at Comhaltas. While many people point the finger at the organisation, making accusations that it is ‘hidebound’ and intolerant to change, the increasingly standardised style of fiddle playing which it encourages at a competition level, for example, is a recent innovation. It’s important to remember that tradition is largely invented.

One of the things the more conservative in the tradition object to is the frankly lazy process whereby ‘innovation’ consists in appending stylistic constituents from other types of music to traditional Irish musical forms, and calling it something new. There’s nothing innovative about combining different forms of mediocrity. It’s the small steps, leading one at a time to something new, that are truly innovative, as well as lasting the distance. The giant leaps into other forms of music generally become dated very quickly, just as so many new -style compositions are instantly forgettable while the tunes on which many of them are based have been around for quite a while in some cases.

Re: Shouldn’t folk music…… move forward?

I would suggest, in answer to the original question and earlier replies, that the people who think that folk music needs to advance, and they themselves are plainly the people to do it, are the ones who are going to disappear so quickly as their ideas become out-moded and irrelevant, and the people who are just sitting there playing the music will still be there, doing their thing, and carrying it on, which is the primary reason for its survival for so long.
Cecil Sharpe defined the three criteria of folk music such;
1) Continuity. People keep doing it.
2) Change. It does change as well, as time goes on.
3) Selection. Some parts of the repertoire get lost, dropped, forgotten, as time goes on, because they do not have sufficient appeal and interest.
The important thing in this discussion is to remember that items occurring under condition (2) may be subjected to condition (3) when they are found not to satisfy over a long period, but are merely fripperies irrelevant to the general body of material we choose to play from.
I would suggest that most of the ideas being promulgated under the banner of moving forward are likely to evaporate in the long-term, and the good and relevant music will maintain its bedrock support.

Re: Shouldn’t folk music…… move forward?

I think that ever since the music has become “globalised”, there have been offshoots of all forms of traditional music into “current styles”, particularly when creating recordings for an audience - piano vamping in the 20s, folk rock with a guitar, drumkit and bass in the 70s and 80s, celtic new-agey stuff in the past 20 years. Jazz syncopations and displays of virtuostiy in the past 15, excursions into balkan rhythms and things like that in the past 10, etc. etc.

On the other hand, there have been “incursions”, most typically of instruments - some of which have been better welcomed and accepted than others.

I think the role these excursions and incursions serve is to “draw the links” with whatever is happening at the time, to draw people in, explore whether new elements are worth incorporating. My limited knowledge of this is that the core of the music emerges pretty much unscathed (or, as Nikita puts it, “without anyone being aware of the changes”). It’s as if the new things (the only one I can think of offhand is cranns on flute) were not added on, but had always been there.

I think that, whether we like it or not, the music will move forward as the “average” of what its players play - and the only influence we can have is to play what we want to play - possibly even decry things we dislike and explain why, but with the knowledge that we’re just part of a greater whole which will pretty much bumble along without even noticing our effort.

On the other hand, even if I agreed that we ought to be pushing the music forward somewhere, I don’t really see where it ought to go. It’s music for dancing jigs and reels. And jigs and reels exist to be danced to the music. If the music had somewhere to go (intentionally, as opposed to following the “random” forces of evolution), where could it go without taking dance with it? And in the current state of ITM, does the dance seem ready to go there too?
