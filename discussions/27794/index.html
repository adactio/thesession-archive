Re: fiddle intonation in traditional music

I wouldn’t worry about the difference between ET as the tuner indicates and perfect fifths as should exist between adjacent fiddle strings - the difference is only 2 cents. It’s the thirds where the biggest difference lies between ET and JI (something like 13.6 cents).

If a fiddleplayer or fluteplayer plays with something inflexible and authoritative in ET, eg an accordion, they have to adapt or be seen to be “out of tune” (the accordion can’t adapt).

Fortunately, if most of the notes are in tune, most listeners will be perfectly happy. It’s when most notes are out of tune that most listeners will register a problem. Slower music gives the listener more time to recognise the errors, but more time for the player to react and fix them.

Terry

Re: fiddle intonation in traditional music

Picking up on Nicholas’ point (which came in while I was typing!), under acceptable acoustic conditions (i.e. when you can hear the fiddle), it’s probably easier to tune perfect fifths by ear than by tuner. The typical tuner meter will have a resolution of a few cents.*

So it makes sense to get “into official tune” by using the tuner, then improve on it slightly by ear.

*Strobe meters by comparison can resolve to around 0.1 cents, but will still be “out” by 2 cents because of the ET-JI difference.

Terry

Re: fiddle intonation in traditional music

I would be really curious to hear a major 3rd that is intentionally sharp of ET - mine are mostly flatter (playing flute).

And I would argue that yes, 5ths (and therefore 4ths) must be in tune - but only those which are in relation to the root - or occasionally, those which are played consecutively.

3rds and 7ths (and also 6ths and 2nds) are pretty mobile, depending on the most important relation they are in. Take the f on the e string. It can be a perfect major or minor 3rd to a D root. But it can also be in various perfect 6th (or “inverted 3rd”) relations to an A root, or to an open A string in a D tune. Or to an open A string in a G tune (which again allows for other possibilities). Or be the 7th in a G tune (very flexible) or the 2nd in a E tune (flexiblish).

On a droned instrument, such as pipes, you either have to be in tune with your drones - or deliberately choose to clash with them - both options are valid.

When adding ET instruments to the mix - there are supplementary constraints. You can move towards - or even match - ET. You can also choose to clash while staying with your drone. Or you can just clash with them. The “badness” of this will depend on the complementarity of timbres (e.g. guitar and flute - the timbres are so different that out of tuneness won’t matter all that much), and the duration of the note. In a typical dance tune, 5 hz is not going to be a noticeable dissonance (the difference beat will be at ~5hz, with is 20ms).

Long story short, provided your ears are not overly formatted (mine find piano chords unpleasantly dissonant), there is a tremendous flexibility in temperament, which I think the best musicians exploit.

erm, ok. I think ceolachan is right on with rhythm and timing. some notes really are more important than others, depending on the context of the rhythm and which notes are accented. I think mostly too, that most people’s choices for accents are deeply rooted in actual physics of the notes which tend to have certain relationships with one another.

For example in Common Practice Period music there is a common ornament called the neighbor tone which can occur any time a note repeats. The neighbor tone is similar to a cut but only occurs on an actual neighbor tone. so if you ornament two G notes you can put an A or an F between them….and this is my point. Regardless of key signature that A could be natural or flat, and the F can be natural or sharp. I find that this rule (which is actually just an observation) is also true for most traditional music. There is no reason why playing this tone extra sharp would negate or significantly change it’s meaning in the music. If it gradually arose that out of taste that fiddle players play this 7th tone sharper than what most fixed pitch instruments were designed for it’s because contextually it sounds right. Fiddle players are always listening for that perfect place to put their fingers (it’s a huge part of learning to play) so it’s understandable that over time we’ve come to different conclusions about pitch (subconsciously) than the makers of flutes accordions and pianos have come to.

It has to do with the muscle mechanics of playing the fiddle as well. When I think of what a minor second, or a half step feels like played on the fiddle I can feel my two fingers rubbing against each other as they move independently. Perhaps I am overcompensating for playing wide minor seconds in the past by pushing them extremely close at times, especially when the lower note is leading into the higher note (which is either the tonic or a 4th or a 5th to the tonic)

Re: fiddle intonation in traditional music

>>“If you don’t tune a fiddle to perfect fifths it won’t resonate properly”

What happens when you put a finger on a string, so that they are no longer separated by perfect fifths? Does it stop resonating properly?

>>“Violinists use pythagorean tuning for melody. ”

I think if you watch all the clips in the ‘definitions’ bit you’ll realise that what he is saying is the exact opposite - that violinists don’t just use pure pythagorean, that they’ll jump to just for chords of thirds or sixths (either double stopped or in harmony against an accompanist) and to equal for unisons with equal tempered instruments. And sometimes even when playing solo they’ll change the intonation to add expression. In short, we just play what sounds right.

It’s all about compromise. Think about playing D F# D on the D string. If you’re just playing single notes you can play the pythagorean third, and it will sound nicely in tune. But what happens if you double stop the A string with that phrase? Because two pythagorean thirds don’t add up to a perfect fifth, if you stick to the same F# intonation, then the chord between the F# and A will sound awful. So you have to adjust the intonation according to what is going on around the note, and not only to your own double stops, but also to keep the harmonies right with other instruments.

>> “What happens when you put a finger on a string, so that they are no longer separated by perfect fifths? Does it stop resonating properly?”

Not if the instrument is of high quality, set up properly, and played in tune. But if the violinist plays the stopped note out of tune it won’t resonate -- or at least not as well. Resonance is physics, not magic.

>> >>“Violinists use pythagorean tuning for melody. ”

>> “I think if you watch all the clips in the ‘definitions’ bit you’ll realise that what he is saying is the exact opposite - ”

You have missed the critical word “melody.” He is saying that the melody line is pythagorean, and the other notes are just. I’ve actually seen him give a demonstration where he, along with the help of a student, derives both pythagorean and just notes and scales, and then play passages of music using the derived values.

The musical passages do not sound “in tune” when the melody is played with just intonation. Your example points out one of the differences between fiddle and violin -- in violin playing the second violinist plays the A on the D string, justly tuned to the F# (without resonance ;-) ) Or if it’s a solo work the violinist plays the A on the D string and the F# on the G string.

It does not surprise me that a scientist (Loosen) can find a group of young professional violinists who don’t play in tune. I’ve heard more than a few myself. ;-) Anyway, here’s a study anyone can do -- take a recording of a top violinist playing Bach (or other solo work for violin) and study the pitches.

As Weejie indicates, there is a disconnect between how many violinists play, and how they’d like to be able to play.

The use of electronic tuners (or should I say; the over reliance on electronic tuners) with guitars and other fretted instruments, in relation to trad ensemble playing, comes with it’s own problems as you indicate above.

When tuning up I tend to start off with a tuned A, from either the aggregate of the fixed instruments present or an A from a tuner (I also tune from a harmonic provided I’m not too far away to begin with and depending where I am). But thats as far as electronic tuners can really be relied on to get you in tune with everybody else, too many variables (of which there are some enlightening explanation above, especially that regarding blown reeded instruments, I take it all back, honest ).

So tuning is not a constant, rather a variable and that much I’ve learned over the years. Playing in a duet I would first tune to the gizmo and provide an A for the fiddler and then adjust my tuning slightly over a couple of sets to them, usually the G & B strings (on guitar). In sessions it’s more esoteric and often a “wetter” average as often the variation in the melody instruments makes it impossible with the hectic nature of session playing, smaller more orderly sessions usually make for tighter over all tuning.

Tuning a fretted instrument on it’s own with a tuner isn’t an exact science either, intonation, pressure you finger the notes with versus action height, string gauge, etc can all have a slight effect. The old adage often trotted out: “you can’t trust an electronic tuner”, isn’t strictly true either, rather I think you’ve got to be mindful of the variables and avoid over reliance. Very handy in loud environments and a handy tool to have for a host of other purposes.

Fiddlers tuning to a tuner isn’t something I’ve seen much. Decent fiddlers (and I hate saying this because the buggers use this even when their wrong, barsets, and usually when their drunk or otherwise being unreasonable) tend to have a better ear, well good ones at any rate.

Fiddle intonation, being a variable, varies from one player to the next, but there’s nothing like getting a few fiddlers together to persuade them to conform to one another’s sense of intonation. Indeed I think this is something we all do instinctively and has something to do with the way the brain works. Some of it’s conscious, some, possibly most of it, is subconscious. But I’m more than prepared to consider that it could also be learned, as in the examples sighting classical fiddlers.

It’s the same process at work with how we perceive the intonation of an individual fiddle player. I reject the argument that we’ve all been “conditioned” by our exposure to modern music, but rather our perception has more to do with what the brain is telling us, the subconscious, is acceptable or not. There are some very interesting (to me at any rate) explanations as to why this should be in other posts above. Particularly, the emphasised note in relation to the tonality of the notes in between. That and the brain joining the dots between the emphasis and the other less emphasised notes of the passage. Certainly it hasn’t escaped my attention that some notes which are obviously a little flat or sharp don’t necessarily affect the over all perception of being in tune with one another. Sound waves are three dimensional after all and so there’s a fair degree of possible colour for the brain to interpret and make acceptable as the various sounds merge together.

It also seems like the tune flows well with F natural on the low D in the A part and f sharp on the high e in the B part. it even sounds quite nice If I use any tone between F natural and F sharp consistently in both parts. I wouldn’t be surprised if fiddle players were proven to be lax about the definition between these notes, because it often doesn’t affect our ability to play solo but only with others.

In regards to the post above about having a separate note for say… D# and Eb….I would have to agree with this. In the truest sense those notes exist in entirely different context in distantly unrelated keys. Their real values in contest over time should measure as different notes , but I don’t know if anyone has done the study by comparing real use of these notes in context scientifically.
