Re: ABC Keys

For ABC notation it doesn’t actually make a difference because it displays key signatures that have 2 sharps in (D major, E dorian, A mixo, B minor) simply with 2 sharps. So you don’t actually have to put the right mode in as long as you have the right number of sharps

So your question really is just about learning modes.

What you will find is that in Irish music the key signatures that dominate are the 1 sharp keys (G major, A dorian, D mixo, E minor) and the 2 sharp keys (D major, E dorian, A mixo, B minor) C major is used a bit and also there is the odd use of 3 sharp keys (A major/F# minor/B dorian) and the odd use of more flatty keys like Ddorian and F major

Re: ABC Keys

Stay a simple fiddler. Not only is it simpler, it will help in a more intuitive understanding of how and why these tunes work.

The standard system of key signatures was developed for a whole different kind of music to diddley music and though you can make it work for diddley, it’s a bit square peg and round hole kind of thing.

Basically, most diddley music is all down to tonal centers and scales. You’ll hear talk of it being “modal” music, but this is not very accurate. All this means is it uses more modes than your average music. And this does not cover the tunes that have gap scales, tunes that have changing scales, tunes that have changing tonal centers and tunes that have no discernible tonal centre.

A lot of the harmony suggested in diddley music is subtle and ambiguous and I think it’s very important to retain this ambiguity, it’s part of what makes the music special. But what a lot of people end up doing as part of their square peg and round hole thing of learning how to notate the music is loose that ambiguity. For example, if you have a tune with no third, you have to make a decision when you write it down what the key signature is, and that means deciding whether the third is major or minor. Then some strummer will come along strum chords with that note in them. This kind of thing irritates the heck out of me.

Re: ABC Keys

A lot of tunes have a final note that isn’t the tonal centre. The modal system developed for chant didn’t allow for that - what has developed for diddley music is kind of hybrid system (maybe due to Bartok more than anyone else) where the tonal centre is partly determined by the melodic formulas the tune uses (as in chant or Middle Eastern music) and partly by implied harmony (as in Western tonal music).

Mostly it works, but you are always going to hit the occasional unclassifiable puzzler. That in itself tells you what you need to know - if you are thinking about chording or arranging the thing, be careful.

Re: ABC Keys

Another way to look at understanding how the exact same notes can make up different keys/modes is to understand the patterns of scales, as steps. A major scale goes like this:

Root note, whole step up, another whole step, then a half step up, whole step up, whole step up, whole step up, and then a half step up.

That’s the “do re mi” pattern that feels natural to us. So if you think of a G major scale, it goes like this:

G A B C D E F# G
R W W H W W W H

The second line shows the root, whole step, whole step… thing.

But what if I were to start playing that same sequence of notes on the D instead of the G?

D E F# G A B C D
R W W H W W H W

It’s exactly the same notes, right? But the whole step, half step pattern has changed! So it’s not a major key/mode anymore! It actually “flattens the 7th”, but the rest of the pattern is the same. So this makes it D mixolydian.

Here’s a couple other pages that might help (or might confuse you further) ;-)

http://www.bandnotes.info/tidbits/scales/half-whl.htm
http://www.slowplayers.org/SCTLS/modes.htm

Re: ABC Keys

hussar: I don’t think you quite understand this music thing do you?!
What strummers have to do with it is: because they play other notes by way of accompaniment along with the melody, they have to select these other notes appropriately. This generally means choosing the same ones more or less as the melody (“although not necessarily in the same order!”). This collection of notes is called the key or mode. Combinations of these notes are called ‘chords’ or ‘dyads’ and as you know strummyfing botherers, by default, have a working knowledge of these groups of notes called chords. Other notes outside the key or mode can be chosen and blended in with accompaniment but this is usually outside the conventions of this particular music. It is not inappropriate in all sorts of other music!

Re: ABC Keys

I think this will address your OP in a straightforward way:

Play a Dmaj scale from D to d and back down. Now play the same scale but go from E to e and back down. You just played a Dorian mode (E dorian in this case). The notes are the same (two sharps, key of Dmaj) but the sound is different because the tonal center is now in E, where you started and ended. There is a different mode for each note of the scale…

In the key of D:
D - Ionian (I)
E - Dorian (II)
F# - Phrygian (III)
G - Lydian (IV)
A - Mixolydian (V)
B- Aeolian (VI)
C#- Locrian (VII)

The ‘tonal center’ may be ambiguous but generally a mode has a certain flavor. So a tune can be in both Dmaj AND A mixolydian, for example, and both have two sharps F# and C#. Same notes but different tonal centers

Furthermore, the sixth step of the scale (B - aeolian in the key of D) is also called the ‘relative minor’. Also with two sharps, its tonal center will be B and if you play from B to b and back down you’ll hear a “natural minor scale” or B Aeolian.

More important to understand the concept then memorize the names of the modes. Or just forget about it and play…

Re: ABC Keys

Jack, I agree that once you understand the theory, it can help you understand why tunes that do some “un-obvious” things might feel a bit strange. So I’m not knocking the idea of learning the theory.

My point was that if you’re initially approaching the tune from a purely theoretical standpoint, thinking that “this tune is in this key”, it can hinder your learning of the tune when it departs from the “norm”. If you approach the tune from the standpoint of the tune first, instead of trying to fit it into a box, then you are paying attention to the important bit first. The tune is what matters, not the theory, and a lot of these tunes don’t fit particularly nicely within the theory.

The OP was talking about needing to know the theory to be able to utilize ABC. I suggest that, instead of struggling to learn theory so that you can use a written notation to learn tunes, concentrate on learning tunes trusting your ears and your intuition.

And there is a multitude of software out there that can turn ABC notation into audible notes, where the software takes care of the theory for you, and plays the correct note for what is notated in the text file. I’m certainly not advocating learning this music from a midi file, but if you require the file as a memory aid, that might be easier than trying to do the translation in your head.

ABC gives you the option to customise the key signature by sharping, flatting or naturalising individual notes.

For example, if you write K: A mix it will display as 2 sharps. It may be easier to indicate that this isn’t the key of D if you write K: A =G - this displays as a key sig with an F# a C# and a G natural. It looks like (and is) an adapted key sig of A maj and makes it easier to recognise that the tune is based around A rather than D.

Re: ABC Keys

Tawny a double sharp is a note raised one whole step or two half steps and is usually marked with an x type of mark. This however has almost no application to Irish music as our key or mode changes (to the extremeties that would require it) are separated by parts so you can just change the key signature from part to part in order to avoid the terrible double accidental marks.

In the diatonic world C double sharp is better expressed as a D natural despite the fact that the true meaning here is different. In order to keep the meaning consistent, find the best key signature for the part which minimizes extraneous accidental markings as a whole.

A circle mark as discussed above would be used to indicate an unused note in the tune or part, so it would be written in the signature only, you would never see it in the body of the tune because it isn’t played, so it isn’t notated either.

if the only value for G (regardless of octave) is sharp in the whole tune, I think it’s best to indicate this in the signature only. If one of the parts doesn’t use G, the transcriber should use their best judgement whether to indicate any value for G in the signature.
