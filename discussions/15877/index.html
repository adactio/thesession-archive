Re: Practise what you Preach

“listening, staying home and studying the tunes”

I never turned up to a session not knowing a single tune.

“stay quiet if you don’t know the tune, don’t noodle around a new tune ( not even quietly).”

I hold back from ‘preaching’ this for that very reason. I unwittingly did all sorts of despicable things in my early years of attending sessions. I probably still have annoying session habits that I haven’t yet become aware of. Also, the problem is, there can be different definitions of noodling: Does it mean feeling you way around the contours of the tune and familiarising yourself with it or does it mean playing notes in the key of the tune, in the hope that some of them might co-incide with the tune? Or just playing notes randomly? We read comments on thesession.org about picking up new tunes on the first hearing and how it is something you can learn with many years of experience and exposure to the music. It might take some people (probably most of us) 15-20 years to achieve this, but a few people can do it within a year of picking up an instrument (alas, not I). So, should we be discouraging them form participating in a session when they can make a worthy contribution to it?

Re: Practise what you Preach

See that’s why I find the whole learn by listening thing kind of falls apart. There are two (and a half) ways I can listen in my modern-day life.

1) Listen at the session. The session is 3 hours twice a month. I forget how most tunes go before I get home. It’s going to be a very long time before I’ve memorized what people play at the session itself.

2) Listen to CDs. This is better but there’s a tendency for recordings to feature musicians who have altered the tune a great deal either through excessive ornamentation or out-right changing the tune completely. Many times the tune isn’t even in a key I can play with my flute or whistle. Still, I can memorize how the tunes go somewhat and get familiar with them.

2a) There’s the slow-down software method. Some recordings are so awful slowed down you can barely tell what they are playing. And it can be frustrating to try to figure out how to loop a small section of a tune. I had to write my own software to do that.

So, I end up resorting to what all of you have resorted to. I listen all the time, I listen at the session. I try to get recordings from older sources or more traditional sources and try to leave out the fancy recordings. But when I hear a tune I really like, I look up the dots, try to memorize it, then put the dots away and try to make myself sound more like the recordings. I try to sing it, too to get familiar with it.

It’s not perfect, but I’m not all that proficient at the instrument to begin with. And I find it’s not completely hampering my ability to learn by ear because as I get better at the instrument itself I don’t need to look at the dots for very long and I can start learning to play variations that I hear others do. I also find that I can pick up some easier tunes on the fly at the session as I get used to where my fingers go.

The thing is, this is what others have done and it worked for them. So why should it be so different for me?

“Fair enough Georgi, but how, if you aren’t directly taught by a competent musician face to face, the traditional method, are people supposed to learn how to play?”

“See that’s why I find the whole learn by listening thing kind of falls apart.”

Sigh, I didn’t want this to become yet another “dots vs ears” argument, but here goes:

The “learn by listening thing” does not fall apart.

It’s the One True Way (well maybe right after learning in person).

Really. I’m not kidding.

Yes, you can get something from sheet music, but it’s not better. It might be easier at first, but it’s not better. The problem with sheet music is not that it’s worthless (it’s actually quite useful), but rather that it’s just not as good as listening.

The real crux of the issue is that people seem to think that learning tunes is the goal. No, getting better at learning tunes is a better goal, and the BEST way to do THAT is by ear. If you don’t have Paddy Canny to visit for lessons, then developing your ear is all the more important. If you can’t hear it properly, and nobody’s around to tell you how to do it, what makes you think that a paper version of something that nobody ever actually played will help? Sure you might get the basics of one more tune, but what are you missing?

Bring a recorder to your session, and record any tunes you don’t know. Then listen to that recording over and over again. Hum the tunes as they are played, and try to play along with the recording. Slow it down on the computer if that helps. The first several tunes are the hardest to pick up. The better you are at learning tunes by ear, the easier it becomes to get better at it. If you keep it up, you’ll find yourself playing tunes that you never realized you learned. When you do, if you miss the odd note, that’s not a good thing, but it’s not a horrible thing either. Just try not to miss it again. Session music is seldom perfect, but you should always be trying to make it better, rather than worse.

My personal approach for dealing with my frustration to participate and my compulsion to noodle wouldn’t be practical in this day and age; I took up rolling my own cigarettes. Some of the tunes were so complete in my head that I could almost get through them from memory alone. When they came up at the session I found I could play along and hardly miss a note.

I have based my technique for learning tunes on these experiences even up to this day. I get the tunes solidly in my head first, and then my head teaches them to my fingers. I still sit out tunes I don’t know when I’m at the session and allow my memory to begin working on them.

Visitors that frequent our sessions begin to pick these up and join in after a while, but sometimes it will just be the three of us playing. The way I try to balance this is to ask visitors and others around the table to suggest medleys or start tunes they want to play.

When I’m visiting other people’s sessions I rarely start any tunes myself and mostly I’m just there to enjoy whatever tunes everyone else is coming up with unless I’m being asked specifically.
