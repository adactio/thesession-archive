How much does sounding pretty matter?

Hi all, I’m a long time lurker first time discussion initiator.

My question is about what aspects of a cheap fiddle are actually a problem in ITM. Specifically, my fiddle lacks the capacity to be really pretty, like, in a violin kind of way (confirmed by experienced fiddlers). How much of a problem is that? Please note that it is set up fairly well, is easy to play, and does not squeak or shreek. It is loud, somewhat resonant, and pretty fast response. I have an easy time getting my rolls to sound snappy and clear, and it doesn’t seem to pose any quirky technical impediments. But, it is not very good for making pretty, dynamic, classical violin type music. The tone has been described as nasal, though I have gotten compliments on its tone in loud crowded jams. The folks I play with are really kind and constructive and if an instrument’s tone was a problem they probably wouldn’t say anything.

Saving up for a nice fiddle would be financially challenging, but possible. Am I missing out on anything really important with this cheap fiddle? People often talk about really nice fiddles that are “warm,” “resonant,” “bright”, “dynamic,” etc. But then on the other hand, there are folks who shrug and say “nobody would sing Bob Dylan songs in an opera singer voice.” I’m currently inclined to go with the latter sentiment; please feel free to confirm or refute. Thanks!

Sorry I don’t have the means to record it with sufficient quality for listeners to tell what the tone is like in real life.

I’m not a fiddler, so take this with a grain of salt. I think it really depends on what you play. Do you play solo a lot? Do you play airs and waltzes often? IMO, “pretty” is overrated in this music. There is a segment of our population that thinks the music should be all harps and angelic singing, which is fine, but I tend to go more for gritty, aggressive tunes with lots of lift and drive. And a fiddle with some volume and snap sounds OK to me, especially for sessions. One of the best compliments I’ve ever heard a fiddler give to another fiddler was when he said “his playing is filthy”, meaning gritty, organic, and driving, instead of smooth, polished and pretty.

And the fiddle is only part of the equation. Physical technique can play a big part, and a bow can make a difference too…

IMO the only thing that matters with an instrument is if it creates the sound you want it to make. Music is about your expression. If you want to make a certain sound, develop the technique and find the instrument that generates that sound.

Of course, other people are more concerned about making the sound that other people like to hear, and want some one else (fellow performers, audiences, teachers) to decide what is the desired sound.

Re: How much does sounding pretty matter?

It is entirely up to you.

As a folk musician, I tend to believe that the music is secondary to the folk. Music is such a personal form of expression. Someone will always be there to tell you what and how to play, but music is an expression of your soul and only you can decide how much sounding pretty matters.

Some people would rather be impressive and get all their rolls and arpeggios and triplets or whatever. Sometimes this happens at the expense of rhythm, which some deem a cardinal sin. Some people sacrifice the fancier stuff in order to lead a boring set that everyone can play in unison. Some people improvise at every opportunity. Some people never do. Some people like clean new strings and bright cheery whistles. Some people like the sound of rusty old reeds or E-strings slightly out of tune. Some people go apeshit for pipes and harps. Some people leave the room if they hear spoons or an accordion. Everyone’s got their own ideas about what is good and what is bad, and everyone is wrong depending on who else you ask.

Do what you like. Play what sounds good to you. Trade instruments. Talk to everybody. Listen to everybody. Play with people you think sound amazing. Play with people you think sound not so amazing– they will inevitably teach you something about your own playing. Play in a stairwell. Play on a windy beach. Play in a gorge. Play in an airplane bathroom. Just keep playing and keep listening. You will never stop developing your sound. You get to choose what sounds good, and that is what matters.

Re: How much does sounding pretty matter?

[*what characteristics would you expect close to your ear for a good fiddle that will sound good and project well to the audience? *]
@Will, as to your question, there’s a wide variety of sounds that you could hear at chin level, and from my own experience of playing high quality instruments from time to time, here’s what I’ve found.

The absolutely ideal violin with a great power of projection will sound fairly quiet under your ear, even when you play it hard. You’ll still easily be heard clearly 30 feet away (assuming no other noise).

Some top violins don’t sound so quiet to the player, but they still do project well. The common thing I’ve found with both of these types of violin - the response is instantaneous. You can hear the ‘scratch’ as soon as you touch the string with the bow. Same with the left hand - the body is so resonant you can hear the sound of the note you are fingering, even if you’re not actually bowing it.

It’s hard to tell the projecting power without a listener, but you can get an idea if you try a good violin in a shop with a proper audition room. These rooms are fairly large, with lots of wood, and the right size so that you can hear (to an extent) some of what your listener hears, by way of acoustic early reflections.

On a cheap and cheerful, regardless of what you yourself hear, if you play a short phrase, then immediately stop and mute the strings, you won’t hear much after that. With a top violin, you’ll hear a lot of the early reflection sound, or ‘echo’.

Top instruments are also extremely unforgiving too. I’ve learned that the hard way :)

The ‘cheap and cheerful’ fiddle might still have the sound that you like, and that persuades you to buy it. By all means go ahead, but I’m only pointing out its limitations when used for ‘performing’.

Re: How much does sounding pretty matter?

>>“A difference in sound? That would seem odd, as violins are not priced by sound.”

Hang on there… That might be true in the esoteric world of old master violins where, just like old master paintings, the price is dictated solely by rarity and desirability. But as soon as you come down out of the clouds and start looking at the ordinary fiddles that people buy to play, you find there is a direct correlation between price and sound quality. And the reasons for that correlation are pretty obvious:

If we look first at new builds, starting at the top you have hand built instruments. With these instruments there is a huge amount of work that goes into getting the sound right - things like plate graduation and bass bar tuning. None of this work is actually visible on the finished instrument, but it is essential to produce a good sounding and playing fiddle.

From there we start coming down the price ladder. In order to reduce the price manufacturers have to reduce the amount of work that goes into the instrument, so they start cutting corners, starting with the invisible stuff you can’t see on the finished instrument. They stop paying a skilled craftsman to graduate tops individually, and set up a CNC router to carve them all to the same thickness. But wood is a very variable material, a hard piece of wood needs to be carved thin to let it ring, a soft piece has to be left thicker to give it enough strength. So to make sure that even the fiddles made of the softest wood don’t fall apart, they set the machine to carve every soundboard slightly thicker than it needs to be for the softest wood, which is way too thick for the hard stuff. The result is that machine carved fiddles NEVER sound as good as the more expensive hand made ones.

As you come further down the price range, more and more corners get cut to reduce the price, and the sound quality gets worse and worse. There is a common misconception that you can ‘strike lucky’ and buy a cheap fiddle that sounds as good as a much more expensive one. It’s rubbish. The reason that a cheap fiddle won’t ever sound as good as a more expensive one is that a lot of the work that is needed to make a fiddle sound good simply hasn’t been done on the cheap instrument.

With old instruments pretty much the same applies. Some were cheaply built and poor sounding when they were new, others were make to a higher standard and consequently better sounding. And that is how they will be priced on the dealer’s wall today. There is an exception though: there are some cheap old fiddles that at some point in their lives have been reworked - someone has taken them apart and put in the extra work to graduate the plates and adjust the bass bar as on a more expensive model, in order to improve the sound. That work is invisible - outwardly those fiddles look identical to their un-reworked brothers, yet they will sell for significantly more. Proof that there is a direct correlation between sound and price.

There are a few other factors (such as prettiness and labels) involved, but by far the biggest factor in setting the price of a fiddle is the sound. And there are always compromises to be made. Few fiddles are perfect, and in the price range we are talking about all have flaws of some sort. For the same price you might find two fiddles one of which has great tone but poor projection, the other has poor tone but good projection. Which you prefer will be a matter of taste, but what is absolutely certain is that if you want good tone AND good projection, you will have to pay more for it. Because the price of fiddles is directly linked to their sound.

Re: How much does sounding pretty matter?

Re outwesht’s post on the “law of diminishing returns”, I often wondered what the price tipping point was.

For top players in top orchestras (not world-famous soloists, many of whom are loaded anyway), the figure of £15000-£20000 seems quite popular. In one of the ‘blind’ tests (there have been several) where these players were given both modern instruments around that value, and fine old Italian ones too (including a 6-figure Strad), many couldn’t distinguish one from the other. They knew they sounded different, but the success rate in correct identification was poor.

So maybe £15000-£20000 is a figure not to exceed in buying a violin, for those highly skilled violinists who previously hankered after Strads and Guadaninis, and are not particularly well-off.
