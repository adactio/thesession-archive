Re: Guitar and other Instrumental Backings for Tunes

Always leave plenty of holes for the music to come through. Remember, accompaniment is backing, not fronting.
There have been a lot of questions in the past about harmonic accompaniment; if you are analysing a tune, a bar, a triplet, the nub of the matter is picking out the significant notes that indicate the relevant chord, and ignoring the less significant notes that are merely grace notes and progressions. For instance, if you are analysing a triplet, the first note is the most significant, the last note is probaly the next significant, and the middle note is probably the least significant; you might now find a chord that fits in with at least two of these three notes. On the other hand, playing along with a tune on the fly, as it were, you might come up with a simpler and easier accompaniment; the ear, and the fingers, sometimes lead you in a different direction to the path a carefully analysis may take you; neither way is wrong.

Re: Guitar and other Instrumental Backings for Tunes

Yes, I agree Pete about the backing not fronting scenario. I normally research the backing of as many tunes as I can. I’m not a bad reader ( not brilliant but not bad) and study the tunes that way, as well as listening. I’ve found some of my more interesting backings are where I could just about play the tune through the respective chord shapes. I do my individual touches below the register of the tune in the bass line, utilising 1st and second inversions
of the standard triads and sometimes quick diminished chords in the cracks as lead ins. Its a fascinating hobby, not easy but the possibilities are infinite. Of couse this particular style works best on reels in major keys or hornpipes.

Dear Al Brown. My version thus far is:
A Part
/ G / G ma 7 / Am7 (G bass) / Am7 (G bass) /
/ G / G ma 7 / Am7 (G bass) / Am7 Gbass) /
x2

B part
/ Bm Em/ Bm Em/ Bm Em/ C6 /
/ Bm Em/ Am / C#m7b5 Cma 7 / D sus /

x2

I’m only developing this but the key for me is to leave the third string open and play G maj 7 with the F# on the fourth string instead of the top. Leave the top string open. That way you get a fixed bottom note but a descending line in the mid.

I am open to embellishment and constructive criticism.
I am yet undecided as to whether the Bm’s wor in the B part.

The Banshee A part, I play pretty much straightforward: G, Am, C, Am, D (and sometimes when I am feeling like a rebel, D7 at the very end). The B part I have found in books with Em chords predominating, and also with Am chords predominating. Which gave me the idea to play it differently the first and second times through. So I play it with more Em the first time through, and with more Am the second time through. Unless of course, another backer is around, then I listen and watch carefully to make sure we blend well.
I have noticed more and more that I play around with different things at different times. I just played with a “newbie” backer, good guitarist with mostly bluegrass background, and had real trouble playing things the same way through each time we went through a tune!

Trad . Although I have suffered with nerves as outlined there is an additional technique that I’ve used in that situation
when I haven’t had the courage to fight the nerves physically. Now remember we’re talking about the guitar not the fiddle.
The technique is to mentally accept your playing is rough and
milk it . You don’t battle the muffled notes you welcome them.
You don’t scorn the bad foldback sound you use it as inspiration. You imagine perhaps what would have Missisippi John Hurt done in that situation if he was a trad backer or picker. He would have not cared . By the end of the the 1st bracket he would’ve had them in his hand anyway. I only use this sometimes but it has worked. It has stopped me from falling to pieces.
