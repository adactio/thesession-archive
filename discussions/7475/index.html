One possible negative consequence of the approach is that a school works on a mass basis. It develops a curriculum which is applied to all students. As such there is a tendency for the students to be noticably similar in their approach. A kind of a regional style with the “region” being the school.

Even in forms of music centered around improvisational skills, such as jazz, it is often possible to listen to someone play and figure out which school they attended, and even which teachers they had.

Whether or not this is a Bad Thing is largely subjective, but it might stand as a warning to listen to the advanced students and graduates of any particular school before choosing to attend.

Re: Irish Music Schools

Although I “teach” Scottish music myself, and think that things like summer schools are great, I admit I have grave doubts about the kind of musician being produced by longer courses in Irish or Scots music offered by colleges and the like.

Of course they attract a number of pupils who are brilliant before they get there, and still brilliant when they leave. But I think it’s a pretty rarified and artificial environment for fostering traditional music at it’s best. In some cases people tend to emerge with a lot of “classical” influences, but lack the depth within the tradition to apply them sensitively, for example.

It’s also reaching a point where having a piece of paper from some such institution is being seen as a good reason to hire someone to work on funded projects in “the arts” etc. This can lead to people who have relatively little experience (in some cases) within the tradition they are actually teaching/promoting being seen as leaders/examples.

I meet a lot of people working in “the traditional arts” in Scotland who have a lot of knowledge and even talent, but are quite self promotional and sometimes quite gimmicky in what they do. They seem to exist by moving from one funded project to the next and frankly I can’t see much true connection between what many of them are doing and what I consider to be traditional music. Strangely, though, I notice them having a subtle influence on it.

Call me old fashioned, but stuff like this worries me.

I’ve no idea whether the situation is similar in Ireland or not. I’d be interested to hear the thoughts of our Irish contributors.

Re: Irish Music Schools

Kris,
As you you probably know, I’m just as interested in Scottish music as Irish. In fact, I know a lot more tunes.
I agree that summer schools, workshops etc, and short courses are a great a idea and I’ve (as an adult)have taken advantage of these myself. Also, it’s good that the music is getting taught more at schools. However, I also get concerned by the idea of having so many University and College degree courses in traditional music. Of course, I don’t grudge them the opportunity and there’s been many talented musicians on the scene who have been through this system.

My main reservation about this system is similar to your own. It just doesn’t seem a natural environment in which to learn the music. The danger is that the students will emerge with a style of playing and/or repertoire which is “approved” or stipulated by the course organisers and, ultimately, quite a narrow focus. I’m often heard some of these students in sessions and they can play beautifully. Quite often, though, I’m unfamiliar the bulk of the material(OK, so that’s fault :-) )they play and it’s fairly obvious that this is what they’ve learned on the course. However, when others in the company start to play session standards, they don’t seem to know these.
Of course, I’m not suggesting that everyone should just learn a “lowest common denominator” of material. Far from it. However, it’s just that their repertoire often seems a bit isolated from what else is going on and what the rest of us have learned the traditional way.

Having said all that, I know many good examples of musicians who have been through this system and have subsequently widened their repertoire to include not only the standards but a variety of different material. So, probably, the grounding helps(or at least doesn’t hinder) in the long run.

It’s just that most us learn the better known stuff first and not the other way around. :-)

Re: Irish Music Schools

Personally I am cynical about Irish music degrees and what not. Firstly it isolates the music from its natural social element - getting one’s music from old players who kept the tradition alive, the stories and personal anecdotes associated, interacting with musicians in a casual environment. It gives me the impression of a very sanitary approach to learning traditional music.

Secondly I am not very comfortable about the music being formalised in cirriculums, standardised and etc. I feel that those are harmful to the health of the music.

Granted this is the perspective of someone who’s never attended classes for such degrees so it is one sided. However if I had the opportunity, I would rather spend 3 years in the right parts of Ireland mingling with old musicians and doing something else on the side, than 3 years in a Irish music college.

Re: Irish Music Schools

“it isolates the music from its natural social element - getting one’s music from old players who kept the tradition alive, the stories and personal anecdotes associated, interacting with musicians in a casual environment.”

Sorry, but I have disagree with that! Firstly, who do you think we’re learning the tunes from?! For instance, I’m hoping to start the Limerick trad degree in september, and I know for a fact that some of the lecturers and visiting artists who do masterclasses are legends.

Secondly, where are you likely to find so many old musicians in one place (apart from the festivals)!? If you’re doing something else on the side, e.g. working, then you can’t be travelling around the country to a different place every day to meet more old musicians.

Thirdly, being in a group of similarly passionate people surely cannot be denying anyone of the ‘social’ element, and, I believe, all students are encouraged and even expected to go out and play in sessions and at festivals etc.

The ‘old musicians’ who kept the tradition alive: surely everyone who’s playing trad is keeping the tradition alive!? By teaching people irish music, and giving them qualifications and a good basis to make a career from it, aren’t the schools doing plenty to keep the tradition alive? Don’t get me wrong, I respect everyone who plays trad, and I love playing with the masters - the best thing anyone ever said to me was John Joe Tuttle telling me I had “some music in those fingers” at a session once - but it has to have a future as well.

Finally, if you limit yourself to learning from ‘old musicians’ then aren’t you missing out on some fantastic opportunities to learn from great young musicians!? Just because they’re young doesn’t mean they haven’t entered into the spirit of trad and don’t know the stories behind the tunes etc. For instance, would you pass up the opportunity to learn a tune from, say Jesse Smith, for the sake of sitting in on an old dude playing diddly diddly!?

Obviously this is just personal - but I can’t wait to start at Limerick and can’t imagine anything I’d rather be doing.

Cheers, Tize.

Also, reply to what John J said - I can see your point, but I think most of the people who, for instance, are doing the Limerick Degree, will be at a playing standard where they have developed their own style and have their own repetoire as well. What the course is seeking to do is help them along with that - not to change anyone’s style or ‘approve’ their tunes, but to further them still.

For instance, in workshops with Siobhan Peoples, Paul Bradley and Liz Doherty, I’ve learnt plenty of fantastic tunes, and the teachers have suggested variations and techniques, in the sort of ‘well this is what I’d do’ respect, but then encouraged us to put our own style to it. This is, I reckon, most important - you’ve got to personally enjoy the music, and to me it’s all about interpretation, and how I feel the tune should be played within the tradition, not just playing someone else’s interpretation of it. I reckon most schools do, (and if they don’t they should ;o) ) encourage this approach to it. If students don’t take it and just learn as if from dots, then that’s their fault.

Re: Irish Music Schools

Yes - there is (IMO) much more value to a course that would imcorporate many visiting artists and masterclasses (and field trips and lots of exposure to field recordings and archival material) with musicians who are “great” for a variety of reasons. A musician might be “great” because the are a successful recording/touring artist - old or young, or because they are recognised as a respected proponent of a particular style or instrument.

What I fear is happening, though, at least over here in Scotland, is that these courses are mainly taught by acedemics. Some of these individuals are also deeply rooted in the tradition, many less so, and even many of those who are seem to prefer to capitalise of their knowledge of how you can reshape the music using classical influences and technique etc. The reality is that by the time these individuals salaries are paid and other expenses met there is often little left for bringing in the much needed visiting artists, etc.

We are reaching a point in history where Irish and Scots music is something people choose to play by choice rather than heritage. That is a fact, like it or not. So I suppose it’s inevitable that there will be courses devised to prepare people for their “career paths” just like in jazz or classical music. Perhaps in time, the music produced by this group of players will be seen as a completely seperate strand. Only time will tell.

Good luck to you, Tize, in your studies. I would love to hear how you are getting on from time to time, once they begin.
