Your own tunes & session etiquette

Many people write their own tunes, but I think it’s not considered good etiquette to start playing them in a session, especially if the session is new to you, and you are still “scoping it out”.

I write my own tunes, and I wouldn’t normally play them unless asked. But, what about starting a set with one of your own tunes (no more than two rounds), then following with another couple of tunes that others are likely to know, and be able to join in on?

I did this at a Glasgow session last week when visiting, and it seemed to work OK.

What are your thoughts on this topic?

Being fairly shy, I generally don’t push my own tunes on others, especially strangers. I do play a couple of my own tunes at local sessions, as the first tunes in sets, but that’s because they’ve been accepted by the other players in the sessions. Etiquette comes down to respect with the people you are playing a session with. Less to do with the fact you wrote the tune but everything to do with which tune works to start a set & how will the present company respond. Point is all the possible scenarios happen in real time. You just need to approach starting a set w/your composition with respect & responsibilty to the others as far as session etiquette is concerned.

Re: Your own tunes & session etiquette

“What is the difference between playing your own composition that no one has heard before, and playing a tune from “the tradition” (O’Neill’s for example) that no one at YOUR session has EVER heard before? In my opinion, there is no difference.”

Not everyone is good at picking up a new tune immediately, on the fly. If you’re introducing an established trad tune that the group just hasn’t heard before, there’s a good chance they can find one or more recordings by trad artists for reference when learning it at home. Same thing for dot learners, as with your O’Neill’s reference. That doesn’t happen with your own composition. Ear learners would need to ask to record the tune for reference. Dot learners wouldn’t have a reference unless you submit the dots to the database here, which is somewhat discouraged.

Anyone in the group who hears the new tune and wants to invest time in learning it, is not going to be able to travel to another session and play it, on the chance that someone there might know it. With a tune that’s already “in the repertoire” there is no-harm-no-foul in taking a chance to see if someone knows a tune when you’re asked to play a set.

In the right session under the right circumstances, I think there’s nothing wrong with an established member playing their own composition as long as the group is cool with it. I’ve seen that and enjoyed it. A very high-end session may also have more players who can pick it up easily with no other reference than the person introducing it. But otherwise I think it’s not really the same as introducing an established tune that the session group just hasn’t encountered before.

Re: Your own tunes & session etiquette

Sure, you can just launch an unfamiliar tune (your own or not) at your session, but you might get more traction if you:
- share the abcs and a link to https://michaeleskin.com/abctools/abctools.html so people can plug the abcs in and see staff notation and hear a midi playback (with variable speed).
- share a link to a YT, FB, or other recording of the tune.
- record yourself playing the tune and share it with your session mates.
- encourage people to record the tune on their phones—offer to play it slower two or three times through.
- host a house session and offer to teach the tune. (In more casual settings, you may be able to do this at your regular session.) Even if you host just one or two players, and they like and learn the tune, you’ll have critical mass at your regular session.

Sharing tunes goes both ways: if you want people to learn tunes you like (or that you’ve penned), put the effort in to reciprocate, learn their tunes. This is simple courtesy, but it also helps you jibe with other players in your circle. You’ll end up with a Venn diagram of tunes with a large common core and a sense of musical taste that agrees at least as much as it diverges.

When visiting an unfamiliar session and you’re invited to start a set, I’d leave my own tunes out of it. You can suggest some tune namess to see if they’re known in that circle. I usually play sets on the fly, unpremeditated, however, so more than once I’ve ended up on a tune that no one else knows. No harm then in glancing around the circle—“Anyone else know this?”—and if there are no takers, I’ll “hup” after the first or second time through and segue into another tune, aiming for something more widely known. As often as not, when glancing at the other players, at least one will say, “Don’t stop!” or “Keep it going!” and then ask afterward for the name of the tune. In the old days, they might quietly finger along to pick up the tune on the fly. Nowadays it’s more typical to see people reach for their mobiles to grab a quick audio.

Re: Your own tunes & session etiquette

ITM is a living tradition. That’s one of the sources of its good health and vitality and appeal. We didn’t “close the book” once O’Neill completed DMI 1001/The 1850.

New tunes. New instruments. New ways of interpreting the tunes. New styles of playing. But most importantly - new people, young people, new acts.

There is a huge body of repertoire from the 20C and now 21C which are unquestionably now legitimately part of the living stream and fully in the idiom of the tradition.

There may be some awful tunes. Or weird ones which have no place within the fold. But, in general, the tradition is the players - and the players will ultimately determine what survives and what, deservedly, perishes.

I love that we are strong and confident enough to adopt and incorporate Newfoundland, Breton and Finnish tunes into sessions. Even some Scottish :-)

As to the wisdom of playing self-composed tunes at a session where you’re not familiar - brave. But sure, nobody died. And at worst there was a 90-120 second period which was a bit weird. We should be able to handle that. And if the tune is actually good, perhaps it’ll get taken up somewhere.

“Performing” at an unfamiliar session is definitely a risk of being showboating. Great if you’ve the chops to pull it off. Gnothi seauton, meden agan; as the Greeks would say.

So much of the commentary here seems to assume that any “new” tune at a session is only acceptable if it would be accessible elsewhere in some form so that an interested person would be able to learn it. This sounds transactional as if the only value for a tune is its proven history. How does any tune get created then? Before O‘Neill’s who could ever determine if a tune was new or not. Maybe new tunes were created all the time before the modern era of recording. I’m just trying to imagine a group of musicians in 1910 or so from different parts of Ireland who may have met up in New York, Boston, or Chicago perhaps. How did they make music together? I can only imagine what it would have been like, but maybe because of regional stylistic differences in the playing, everything they each played sounded like a brand new tune to the others, even if it had the same name. Maybe that was a golden era of composition when musicians created their own tunes in order to be able to play something new on a regular basis. When these tunes were written down by O’Neill and later on recorded by the likes of Morrison they became fixed “compositions”. This caused standardisation at the expense of originality and regional stylistic variations. So now, thanks to all the new technology, a musician can travel all over the world and play Cooley’s Reel or The Kesh Jig nearly exactly the same way everywhere. That’s a really cool thing about standardisation but it is a double edged sword that stifles creativity at the same time. Someone pointed out above that there are many current groups creating amazing new compositions and I agree with that. I’m guessing that the musicians in those groups are probably busy playing and practicing with their band mates and not debating on social media about whether or not it’s OK to play a new composition when they are visiting a new session.

But re comments or questions about “traditional repertoire”: It’s not the latest thing that came to you in your parents’ basement last week. Please, put it on Youtube, put on your social media, don’t bring it to a trad session.

But re Paddy Fahy, Sean Ryan, etc. Yes, their tunes have a known source as opposed to so much folk music with no known individual creators. But one of the wonderful things about folk music is that it has a ruthless and very efficient if wholly informal Darwinian weeding function. In which tunes that are memorable, riveting, arresting, unforgettable, heartrending, catchy, joyful, irresistible such that people want to play and dance to them, gain currency, and are passed on. Paddy Fahy, Sean Ryan, Paddy O‘Brien of Tipperary . . decades later some of the tunes they composed, many, many years ago, became part of the tradition because they had one or more of those qualities. Rather than subjecting me at a traditional session to the stuff you’re coming up with in your parents’ basement . . . let alone asking me to pay for a recording full of it . . . Call me in ten, fifteen, twenty years when this has happened with even one of yours. But pardon me if I don’t hold my breath.
