Sally… if you know exactly what everyone else is thinking at all times, more power to you. It’s a very impressive skill. However, I’m pretty sure that, from moment to moment, I don’t even know what _I_ am thinking. Maybe if you were around you could help me out.

Knowing what everyone else is thinking is particularly difficult in a session. In my experience, people often have grimaces, grins, or blank expressions on their faces as they’re playing. Sometimes they appear to be looking at me, but they’re probably not. I’m not convinced that they’re aware of their expressions, but I am pretty convinced that, most of the time, they don’t have anything to do with me. I reckon that if they have something they want to tell me, they’ll tell me. If I suspect that they might have some issue with me, and if I suspect that they might be sitting on it, and if I’m curious about what they’re thinking, I’ll ask them.

The key is not to take things personally. If you’re doing something that offends others, and it’s is within your ability to change it, and you want to change it, then change it. If you can’t change it, you can’t; there’s nothing you can do about it, and it becomes the other person’s problem by default.

What do the fiddler and the accordion player know about what _you_’re thinking?

If the other fiddler is really contemptuous of your ability to remember tunes, what do you propose to do about it? Work harder and earn her respect? Or consider that respect is something to be accepted, but never expected; received, but not demanded--and ignore her attitude?

If the accordion player is playing bum notes, don’t presume a reason for it. Maybe he thinks he’s Dermot Byrne, or maybe he thinks he’s Jesus Christ, or maybe he can’t hear himself at all. I would suspect the latter. But what he thinks of himself is beyond your knowledge, not your problem, and you can’t do anything about it anyway. The bum notes ARE your problem. If you don’t like the bum notes, you have two choices. First, you can leave. Second, you can ask (politely) that, for the sake of the people who are still learning the tunes, could he do you a favour? Since he’s an experienced player, could he use his expertise to play the melody really cleanly and help to reinforce the tune in your own head?

You’ve say that he started playing to demonstrate his superiority, and you’ve presumed that he would stop because he was afraid of admitting his inferiority. Did he tell you this verbally, or did he write you a note? If offered a choice between conscious undesirable behaviour and oblivious undesirable behaviour, assume oblivion until real proof is available.

Zina, I respectfully disagree with your suggestion to Conan to give someone The Look--lest the recipient misinterpret that and perpetuate this whole cycle. The Look won’t teach him what’s what. “I think I’d prefer to sit this one out” would be nice, clear, and direct, and leave no room for confusion.

---Michael B.

Re: musical superiority complexes

WRT the thread on “musical superiority complexes”: thought I would reply, as some references have reminded me of other comments from other forums. Some of this fits with comments from Michael B and others.

Warning: this is a long post, because the discussion has focused on some issues that I think are simultaneously complicated and important. Apologies for the length: as a session leader, participant, and founder, I promise that I’ve thought hard about these issues.

As a session leader and session participant for a lot of years now, and someone who has taken time to build slow sessions as entry-devices for those new to the music, I’ve observed that often the greatest difficulty comes not in acquiring the physical—or even the aural—skills or the range of repertoire necessary to participate, but rather with the complex and different social dynamics of a true trad session. Trad Irish sessions have historically not been run like other, more democratic musical meetings with which they may seem to share surface characteristics.

Please note that this is explicitly not a comment about Sally’s experience, whether she did right or wrong, nor indeed is intended as any kind of response to her or other’s comments. Sally, I’m sorry you had a lousy experience; there’s no denying that some people sometimes act like jerks in session settings.
Rather, these are some anecdotes about how complicated—and alien—session social dynamics and musical values can be for those unfamiliar with this particular tradition.

Don’t forget: whether we like it or not, in many ways Irtrad is a meritocracy; those who have been doing it longer, do it better, or have more tunes, often receive, and deserve, special attention. Who are you better going to learn from?

If I’m in a session and a monster player comes in, and even if the session then is transformed from being more of a “sharing” environment to being more of a “listening with awe” environment, then I’m grateful; at least I got to hear what can be done with the music.

When a visiting player comes to my teaching session with better chops, or better settings, or tunes I don’t have, I step back and let that person teach--because ultimately I serve the tradition, and the tradition’s standards of excellence.

Here’s an anecdote about “The Look.”

I was recently visiting a city in the Southwest which has a long-standing trad Irish scene, but in which, on the basis of my past observation, real master players either aren’t present or don’t come out to the session. At invitation of locals, I and my bodhran player visited their pub session, where there was a lot of sight-reading, playing of fixed arrangements, and “improvised countermelodies” going in. These are things which, in my opinion, don’t serve traditional music or traditional performance aesthetics very well. And the problem with that, for me, is that propagating such “anti-traditional” behaviors prevents other people from seeing, hearing, and feeling the incredible musical and social experience which the more traditional behaviors can create.

At one point, one of the local players, not knowing a tune I was playing, started playing an “improvised countermelody” to the tune—which, let’s face it, in a modal diatonic idiom is very easy to do; it’s in fact a lot easier than either learning the actual tune, or exhibiting the self-control to sit out.

Apparently, I gave this local player some permutation of “the Look,” because he commented on it to a mutual friend. My friend was kind, and said to that person in reply, “look, Chris really cares about the tradition, and he has very high standards, and he believes that people should put in the time to learn to play the music in the traditional style.” The mutual friend didn’t say “no, he wasn’t really sending ‘the Look’.” Instead, he offered another interpretation, another perspective, on what “the Look” might mean, and from what priorities it might proceed.

BTW: I was not conscious of sending “the Look.” I’m usually extremely conscious of the vibes I send when I’m leading a session, but in this case I certainly was not intending to send negative messages about the person. Evidently I was sending negative messages about performance choices he was making right then.

And you know what? The person receiving it knew why I sent “the Look,” and later acknowledged that he knew playing an “improvised countermelody” was not really what one should do in the absence of actually knowing the tune (it obscures the main melody, usually clashes with the main melody’s rhythmic “lift,” and usually clashes harmonically as well). Based not on arbitrary or subjective opinion, but rather on an accurate assessment of what the music has traditionally valued, such conduct doesn’t serve the music very well.

I certainly intended nothing personal with “the Look,” and in fact was truly not conscious of sending it. But I did (and do) believe that there are ways of playing which either serve or detract from the music, and I do believe that those who want to lay “traditional Irish music” should respect the tradition enough to learn their tunes, learn the performance practice, and sit out when they can serve the music better by listening than by playing.

That said, I would certainly (though regretfully) acknowledge that a good bit of social behavior in session settings is screwed up by individuals’ personal investment in what should be, in one way, an impersonal activity.

Sometimes people measure their sense of self-worth in a hierarchical way—figuring that having more tunes than someone else, or having got one’s tunes from more obscure sources, or having played in sessions with “famous guys” somehow makes them “cooler,”—or on the basis of participation—that playing less and listening more somehow makes them less “valuable.” It doesn’t make them “cooler,” but darn it, it does make them more authoritative in the tradition. It’s when either senior or junior players mistake a rather “impersonal” dedication to musical quality for a “personal” assessment of an individual’s worth that these situations become personally painful.

There are people in this music who are elitist or exclusive—but on the other hand, some times what outsiders or entry-level players see as elitism is not directed at people, but at the caliber of the music-making going on. If I go to a session and players are present who can play rings around me, I sit out--not resentfully, not self-consciously, but out of a sense that truly serving the music calls for different kinds of contributions at different times—and that having the maturity to sit out is as much a contribution as is playing along.

Ultimately, a traditional music—by definition—is about seeking self-expression within the aesthetic parameters of that tradition. This can be a deeply self-expressive music, but it is “traditional” because it has handed down not only a repertoire but also a set of aesthetics and procedures. In that respect, traditional music is “impersonal”: its traditional contexts (like sessions) do not prioritize “self-expression” at the expense of received aesthetics.

If a musician wants to prioritize “self-expression”, in the absence of a significant time spent learning the tradition, then that’s fine; but s/he should not play a music so dependent on virtuoso physical and aural skills, on having learned a shared repertoire, and on understanding the idiom’s aesthetics and procedures.

A senior player who plays with less advanced musicians certainly can help them along, certainly can adapt her/his playing to the more modest skills of the other participants. But I’d say one shouldn’t presume either (a) that s/he must so adapt or (b) that if s/he plays variations, different ornaments, and a more complex or beautiful setting, s/he is intending to “prove that he’s waaay superior”. In some situations, I’ve done that myself: for an entry-level player—which I’ve also been—it is important to hear what can be done with the simpler tunes.

Someone said that the “real good players always are friendly and generous.” I wish it were true, and certainly some of the finest people I know are also some of the finest players. But it’s not necessary always the case. Sometimes great players are bastards. More significantly, sometimes what we perceive to be personally obnoxious, “superior,” or divisive behavior from a more senior musician is not that—but rather an impersonal, and sometimes even fierce, dedication to quality music-making.

Someone else said “when playing with less experienced players, you are absolutely right in that it’s only fair to keep the tune simple.” I believe that—but I don’t believe that playing a more complex, more effective, or more inspired setting “smacks of arrogance otherwise.”

Sessions, in my opinion, are wonderful places for learning—but they are not necessarily wonderful places for teaching. I’ve been in sessions in which one or another senior player, for whatever reasons, seeks to teach a more junior player, and you know what? It often brings the session crashing down. Why? Because a session should (in my opinion) always seek to serve the music.

People in sessions should be able to play to the height of their abilities, because then the music is better, and the music being better serves all participants better, whether they are seniors who are challenged to play at that level, or juniors who are lucky enough to be present and hear what can be possible in the music.

This does not mean being exclusionary, or “elitist”, or arrogant, or demeaning to others. It does mean that, in playing a traditional music, one’s ultimate responsibility should be to the tradition. It’s bigger than any of us.

And just so it’s clear, I’ll reiterate some opinions I expressed at the beginning:

* I regret that Sally or anybody else had a demeaning or demoralizing session experience;

* I don’t believe that senior players need to be elitist;

* However, I think the social dynamics of a session—the tradition’s meritocratic, not democratic, priorities—are often pretty alien to those of us who grew up outside it, and can send deeply confusing messages;

* Learning “session etiquette” (not a term I like) requires just as much effort as finding one’s way into any other foreign language or set of cultural behaviors: it’s never a bad idea to keep quiet, observe, learn as much as possible while intruding as minimally as possible, and accept that it’s okay to be a novice. If you moved to a foreign land, would you expect everyone in all circumstances to speak the local language only at your very limited level? Wouldn’t you think that, at least some of the time, you should sit quietly and hear very eloquent native speakers convey what *can* be achieved with the language?

* Ultimately the priority, in my opinion, is, by playing or not playing, by teaching or not teaching, by standing up for ourselves or “checking our egos at the door,” is to serve the tradition we have inherited.

all the best, and in service to the music we all care about,

Re: musical superiority complexes

Well said, Chris. I happen to agree with you pretty much on every point. But it should probably also be said that a group of players can make their session whatever they want for whoever they want, and the level of the music played and the kind and authenticity of the music played is up to them.

The trouble only comes in when a group of players‘ standards of “good music” clash with another group of players’ standards. At which point, I always tend to follow the lead of whoever’s session it is, as I personally see that as only polite. If I don’t like or can’t accept those standards, I can always disappear to the bar or out the door or (especially as I may not even know what their standards are) I can simply listen and enjoy their music for what it is. I don’t think of that as elitism, either. It would be the height of arrogance for me to walk into an OldTime jam and expect them to conform to my standards of anything. I expect them to give my own session and my version of music the same respect.

I’ve lost players from our session because I expected them to hold to our rules and standards. That’s their choice, and if they choose to see me as elitist and uppity, there’s not much I can do about it. But if they want to play at our tune learning session, they *will* have to follow our etiquette. (Which includes not playing new tunes too fast, not playing off sheet music, being willing to learn the new tune we’re learning, being supportive of the other players, not indulging in self-aggrandizement, being aware that lovely orchestrated harmonies aren’t really part of the tradition, etc.) If they won’t, it’s my job to speak to them about it, which is not any more fun to do than it is to have happen to one.

I know I’ve given The Look. I’ve gotten The Look. I mean it when I give it. I pay attention when I get it. (I also decide whether I think The Look was deserved or not and decide whether to take it to heart or not.) I’m also sure that people have thought I was giving them The Look when really I was realizing that I was playing a different tune on the B part. :)

LOL -- not at all, Noah, and I expect you probably already know that. Chris is quite right -- some of the very best players can also be downright bastards when they want to be. But they can also be very nice when they want to be. This makes them absolutely no different than the rest of us, I think. :)

I’ve told this story more than a few times, but I guess I’ll tell it again -- I once talked to an extremely famous player who told me that he’d attended a session, and, while he was glad that the other players loved the music, that they were doing something better than staying at home and watching television, and that they were enjoying themselves, he himself wished that he wasn’t there.

The moment the words left his lips, I resolved that my goal was to be good enough at this stuff that I would never make a better player wish that they weren’t there because of my playing.

I’ve since been told that that sort of attitude is what makes a beginner welcome -- not because one is creeping about being humble humble humble, but because it makes one more aware of how other players’ standards of music may drive them and the session. You don’t want to be the sort of player who has to play *every single tune* despite *fill in the sin here* -- ie: not knowing the tune, not being able to keep up, not playing up to the standard of the rest of the players, etc. If you’re the right sort of beginner with the right sort of attitude, with good players you’ll usually (but not always, and you can’t expect it) be encouraged to play the speed you can play and they’ll play right along with you.

Almost every person on this bulletin board we call The Session will tell you of attending sessions at which they quietly put away their instruments and listened, because if they’d joined in, they’d have been lowering the tone. Those are magic times, if you’re willing to see them as such, to be sitting in the midst of great players playing their guts out.

Chris

Enjoyed reading your comments, I would agree with most of what you said. You did mention some of the points I made earlier; I should have qualified them by saying that in many’s the case it depends on the type of session, number of people present and number of regulars around as to whether or not it’s appropriate to, for example, show someone a few bars from a tune they don’t know. It certainly wouldn’t be the case, in my experience, that many sessions have fallen apart because of a quick tutorial. Likewise, it would strike me as odd if a very good player who was in with a bunch of beginners played at their highest level for the duration of a session. Obvously if the player was a reg, and the others were blow-ins then it could be forgiven but otherwise…. definitely not. Most (but not all) “Master” musicians would check out the ambience of a session and gauge whether or not it’s appropriate to “show off”, or worse still hijack the session. I disagree to a certain extent with the point that sessions should serve the music first and foremost. No matter how much we might want it to be the case, I don’t think that was the aim at their conception 40 or 50 years ago in London, and I’m doubtful that it is now . Quite simply, people get together, play a few tunes, have a bit of a laugh and generally enjoy themselves. If wonderful music ensues then great. But it’s not the end of the world if it doesn’t. “Bad” sessions are what make you appreciate those once-in-a-blue-moon nights where everything gels. Sessions have become the mainstay in the preservation of Irish Traditional Music but I feel that this is a role which has been thrust upon them. People are free to create sessions in which the music is taken very seriously indeed - if that’s what floats your boat. And of course most people tend to want to play with musicians of the same calibre or better. But that’s not always possible, and even if it is, it’s not always particularly enjoyable. I’ve been stuck at the odd session with a few po-faced oiks who admittedly played excellent music but created a session less atmospheric than the moon. If you look after the people first, the music will generally take care of itself.
