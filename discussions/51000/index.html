What did the dots do?

It is an amazing accomplishment that Chicago’s Chief O‘Neill put down the dots for over 1000 tunes in the early 1900’s. Countless musicians since then have benefited from his work. However, at the time, did he take into account musicians who learn music by ear and rarely if ever consult notation to learn how a tune should sound? Because the dots cemented the tunes into a form that was interpreted by music readers, ear players were kind of left out of the process because from then forwards the music became standardized by the music notation. From that point, music notation sort of dominated the future transmission of the knowledge of the tunes. (This was doubly reinforced by audio recording technology.) I’m just imagining a possible situation where O’Neill may have listened to a player who knew many tunes but did not use notation at all. The chief wrote down what he heard and no one knows if this was actually what was played. Also, did Chief O‘Neill make up his own tunes? Who knows? I’m guessing he must have put at least one of his own tunes into the books. It worked out fine some would say, because the tunes are amazing. But what was lost by putting the music down as a fixed sequence of notes on paper? This is not classical music where 99%+ of the notes are fixed. So what did the dots do to the music? They did a lot. But there was a cost. Thinking about this has also made me wonder…Are there any actual recordings of Chief O’Neill’s playing? It would be interesting to hear him playing something that he put down in notation so that the recording could be compared to the dots he wrote down. He must have been a great musician. In Chicago, there is a statue of him playing the flute so probably that was his main instrument. I learned a lot about this remarkable Irish immigrant from watching this documentary…https://www.thirteen.org/programs/chicago-stories/wttw-documentaries-march-17-2014-web-extra-francis-oneill/

When I started learning the tin whistle, about 30 years ago, I was a very talented sight reader. I’d been a classically trained trumpet player. I quickly bought O‘Neill’s 1001 Tunes, but I never found reading the dots to be very helpful since I didn’t know how Irish tunes should sound. Re: What did the dots do?

It is nearly impossible to represent all the nuances of the performance of this music in notation, and even if you do write out every cut, tap, slide, bend, intonation variant as is done for detailed transcriptions of, for example how a specific piper might play a tune, you’re still capturing a specific performance, and the same player tomorrow will play it completely differently.

What I’m saying is that essentially all of the common tune sources, whether arrangements on this site, or O’Neill’s are just very rough approximations of tunes, with the details to be filled in and variations introduced by the player.

In your original post, you imply that writing down the tunes somehow broke the process of conveying the tunes by codifying them with notation.

That’s only true if one treats them as the source of truth regarding the tune, which nobody should be doing, ever.

Instead, they are just the framework of the tune, or perhaps a high level sketch of a particular performance, but really nothing beyond that.

Re: What did the dots do?

Adding to what @Will Evans said. Irish music as an oral tradition was on the verge of extinction. At least this is what Chief O’Neill thought. O’Neill stated explicitly that his work was “undertaken for the promotion of a patriotic purpose.” He believed that Ireland’s ancient music expressed the essential soul of the Irish people. He once wrote, “The music of Ireland is all that her oppressors have left her.”

As it has been pointed out, Chief O’Neill couldn’t even read the dots himself. So I doubt very much he intended his book to be some final pronouncement on how any particular tune should be definitively played or represented. This wasn’t an academic exercise. It was a response to a crises and a hope that better days are still ahead for these diddly notes.

Re: What did the dots do?

What O’Neill did was to record how the tunes were played (note-wise) by those around him at a specific time.

But the way tunes are played evolves over time (just like the human species does). Every so often the interpretation of a major player will cause a tune to mutate. Look at that Paddy Fahy reel that starts on the low G and how Martin Hayes (I presume it was him) changed the way the B part was played (which just about everyone follows now, but it ain’t the way Paddy played it).

And even if you do learn a tune from dots, your personal setting can evolve consciously or subconsciously over time. There have been many occasions where I have learnt a tune from a recording, which usually involves some transcription, only to find years later that I’m not playing it exactly the way I learnt it. Sometimes the changes have come about because of a mistake (or mutation?) which makes the tune better for me.

Re: What did the dots do?

In the “why” questions, we should ask who was buying these transcriptions, and why were they buying them?

A lot of music publishing in that period was aimed at the amateur musician - we’re still in a period where for most people, music was something that happened live, and if you wanted music in your house, you had to play it yourself. (This goes way back, of course - Beethoven cranked out a lot of pieces for amateurs to play, and lots of, if not most chamber music, as I understand it, was written for people to play at home, not for performance) For someone with a violin or a piano around the house, this would have been access to a body of tunes that were otherwise not very available, and I suspect that these, rather than the traditional players, were the main customers for O’Neill’s collections.

Another market would have been the professional musician working to entertain the public. A working violinist would have had a mix of classical and popular material, and I can imagine that these collections would have been a godsend for a violinist or a flautist trying to put together a performance that would engage their audience.

Even though O’Neill’s stated intent was to preserve a tradition, and even though over time they took on a canonical position in the music for a lot of players, I doubt very much that traditional musicians were originally the main market for these collections.

As far as fidelity to performance, it seems perfectly obvious that no transcription, of any sort, can ever be even close to a faithful representation of a performance, and I don’t think any musician would ever expect that they would be. This is why symphony orchestras have conductors and principal players in each section to decide on the interpretation of a score for each performance, isn’t it? And this is why anyone who likes the Cello Suites will have multiple recordings of them, and strong opinions about those recordings - Casals and Starker and Edgar Meyer all play the same notes, in the same order, but with very different effect. So I’m not too bothered by the idea that O’Neill might have somehow broken Irish music by writing it down. It’s shown itself to be tougher than that.

This of course is nothing new. Names are not strongly tied to tunes, we’ve all experienced that “what was the name of the tune I just played?” moment, and the converse “Saddle the Pony, that’s a great tune! How does it go again?”. So some of this will be down to people just coming up with the wrong name, or forgetting the name entirely and making one up. This is probably why so many of the tunes are named for local personalities, like Tuohey and Jackson and Mayor Harrison, the mayor of Chicago at the time, and Sergeant Early, who was a colleague of O’Neill’s on the police force - they needed a name, and those names were just lying around for the taking.

Then too, you had people from all over Ireland ending up in Chicago, who would have come from relatively insular musical communities back home, and probably would have had very different names for the same or for very similar tunes, and different tunes for the same or similar names. So it’s not surprising that some tunes and some titles got conflated or swapped around or otherwise confused.

In the specific case of The Maid Behind the Bar, I have at least one recording of the familiar tune of that name, under the title of “The Haymaker”, so that might be the older title of it. I’ve been told that the Maid Behind the Bar was an inadvertent abbreviation of Kiss the Maid Behind the Barrel which came about when someone tried to print the longer title on a record label and the ends were cut off. Not sure I buy that version, but it’d certainly explain why there are similar tunes with those two names.

I was witness to an example of just how little impact notation has on the music played at Irish Trad sessions.

In the early 1980s, at a Highland pipe band competition, I heard the band led by Neil Dickie play his composition The Clumsy Lover. In 1983 he published it.

Maybe a year or two later I heard some Trad Irish musicians playing it…well, sort of playing it!

I was amazed by how fast the Tradition had chewed the tune and spit it out, eliminating parts and mixing up the order of those that remained. I was talking to somebody about it, saying that the composer had published the tune only a year or two earlier. They looked at me like I had two heads- obviously the fact that the composer had published the tune was absolutely meaningless.

The “real” tune is whatever version is played at your session. How that version came to be is irrelevant.

Actually the same process, but to a lesser degree, also quickly happened in the Pipe Band world itself. Though (as best I remember) they kept the parts in the same order, the various bands that played the tune generally distilled it down to four parts. I believe it was the 78th Fraser Highlanders who recorded the most musical version, having the most impactful parts in the most impactful sequence.

Re: What did the dots do?

“Do we have evidence that specific trad Irish players c1910 were sightreaders?”
Yes (James O’Neill being one - Frances writes about him in one of his books, as well as other musicians)

There’s some incorrect information and assumptions in this thread, unfortunately:
- As has been pointed out, Frances O‘Neill was not the scribe (James O’Neill, no relation, was. Fortunately there’s even a book about this, coincidentally called “The Scribe”). That said, Edward Cronin actually transcribed for Capt. O‘Neill first. Also, while F. O’Neill wasn’t fast enough to scribe tunes live, it’s likely he was able to read the music (at least somewhat)
- O’Neill’s first collection published was in 1903 - the Music of Ireland. The 1907 Dance Music of Ireland was a direct response to the desire for musicians to have a cheaper publication of dance tunes available. I agree with Michael Eskin that these musicians likely all knew these transcriptions were bare bones and to be used as either a memory aid or just a starting point
- The tunes are *not* attempts to capture a performance, and in fact there was a committee formed to help decide if the transcriptions correctly captured the tune (not the performance). The committee fell apart due to strong opinions (well, there’s more to it than that, which Nicolas Carolan has described elsewhere)
- The 1903 Music of Ireland includes sources listed - F. O’Neill did try initially to attribute who the tune was transcribed from, but this was abandoned by the 1907 edition to a slew of difficulties (among them, maybe: anger / hurt feelings about the “wrong” person being cited, inability to be certain where it was learned, and maybe an attempt at obfuscation as to the number of tunes lifted from earlier collections?)
- That said, it sure looks like James O’Neill did try to capture what he heard players doing, including adding hints at the variations they employed, while keeping the tune succinct (ie just the one time through) - look up Eliot Grasso’s thesis about this, which is fascinating and informative.
- J. O’Neill also did his best to capture traditional intonation while putting it into classical notation standards - it’s worth comparing the 1903 Music of Ireland with the 1907 Dance Music of Ireland to compare when tunes have changed key signature. This is particularly noteworthy for tunes in A modes - Often in the first they were note as A major and the second as A dorian, while in practice they were likely actually A mixolydian, which can thankfully be corroborated with recordings such as the Ward cylinders and other early recordings (ex Patsy Touhey)
-F. O’Neill clearly did not like to leave tunes unnamed, and there are countless examples where he (or others) made up the name (which he writes about freely - there’s no attempt at deception). I believe he, like many of us, treats the name of the tune as a mnemonic to remember the tune, or a label, rather than as some intrinsically linked feature.
-It’s very incorrect to say that making wax cylinders required going to New York to Edison’s recording studio: both Touhey and Capt. O‘Neill had their own wax cylinder recorders. O’Neill’s is at the Ward Irish Music archive along with the Wax cylinders he recorded (in Chicago). Touhey advertised that you could order one-off recordings from him. The main reason O’Neill didn’t just record every musician is that the technology for portable recordings simply didn’t exist at all when he started (well before the 1900s), but a secondary one was that the goal was just to catalog the music of Ireland.
-Editing to add that James O’Neill was not Scottish (where does that erroneous idea come from?) - he was from County Down.

I personally believe that the music has changed quite a lot from the wax cylinders in the Ward Archive, for what it’s worth. But compared to the difference between traditional music and rock music, I’d agree that it’s not that dissimilar…

Re: What did the dots do?

Adding this to illustrate the original goals of the collection:
"After many years of intercourse with all manner of Irish musicians, native and foreign born, who were addicted to playing popular Irish music with any degree of passable proficiency, I began to realize that there was yet much work left for the collectors of Irish Folk Music. Many, very many, of the airs, as well as the lilting jigs, reels and hornpipes which I had heard in West Cork in my youth, were new and unknown to the musicians of my acquaintance in cosmopolitan Chicago. Neither were those tunes to be found in any of the printed collections accessible.

The desire to preserve specially this precious heritage from both father and mother, for the benefit of their descendants at least, directed my footsteps to Brighton Park, where James O’Neil1, the versatile northern representative of the great Irish clan, committed to paper all that I could remember from day to day and from month to month, as memory yielded up its stores. Many trips involving more than twenty miles travel were made at opportune times, and I remember an occasion when twelve tunes were dictated at one sitting.

Originally there was no intention of compiling more than a private manuscript collection of those rare tunes remembered from boyhood days, to which may be added some choice specimens picked up in later years, equally worthy of preservation. Drawing the line anywhere was found to be utterly impracticable, so we never knew when or where to stop."
Capt. Frances O’Neill, Irish Folk Music, A Fascinating Hobby
https://billhaneman.ie/AFH/AFH-II.html

Couple points to add:

O‘Neill’s brother-in-law, Barney Delaney, also had an Edison phonograph. A few of the cylinders in our collection are believed to be duplicates of ones O’Neill and co. sent to various people around the world. I’m guessing they must have put two machines together, horn to horn, to record the duplicates.

Wax cylinders could only be played a few times before the sound degraded, so they likely were not slowing down the players to learn tunes like we do today. John McFadden was reported to have great skill in learning tunes by ear, so this story from O’Neill about them learning “Lark in the Morning” is a good example of how they used cylinder recordings to pass tunes around:

"This tune was first published in O’Neill’s Music of Ireland, and the story of
its recovery and preservation needs no apology for its presentation here.
James Carbray, a native of Quebec, but now a resident of Chicago, when
studying music in his young days picked up some fine tunes from an old Kerry
fiddler named Courtney, long settled in Canada. Many years later Mr. Carbray,
amiable and accommodating gentleman that he is, recorded them on an Edison
phonograph and forwarded the rolls to Sergt. James Early of the Chicago police
force. It didn’t take John McFadden long to memorize "The Lark in the Morn-
ing, " and we may be sure it lost nothing of the blas or graces at his hands in
transmission to Sergt. James O’Neill’s notebook, and subsequently in its final
setting herewith submitted." Irish Musicians and Minstrels, p.431.

It’s possible O’Neill and co. were wiping the cylinders down and re-recording on them which was standard practice at the time since the cylinders were so fragile in nature.

To learn more about the story behind the Francis O’Neill Cylinders, check out our recently updated blog about the history: https://wardirishmusicarchives.com/WIMA-Blog/Patsy-Touhey-Cylinders-in-the-Dunn-Family-Collection.htm

Re: What did the dots do?

I’m the author of The Beat Cop, for what its worth. There are two recording of O‘Neill playing, which are the archives at UC Dublin. It’s O’Neill on tin whistle, Edward Cronin on fiddle, and Cronin’s nephew Tom Kiley on the mandolin, which is basically inaudible You can hear O’Neill speaking at the beginning

https://spokeshave.net/O’Neill1.mp3
https://spokeshave.net/O’Neill2.mp3

O‘Neill as mentioned had his own cylinder machine for recording and made a lot of recordings, some of which were lost. He relied a great deal on Edward Cronin, who could read and write music. James O’Neill was, as also mentioned, from Belfast by way of County Down, and he was comfortable with the dots. Francis could read music to some extent but was not fluent. He quarreled with both men over “the matter of keys [signatures].” He then began working with Selena O‘Neill, no relation, who had a formal degree in music and was an accomplished prize winning player, although if you hear her she will sound, I argue, charming but not “authentic ” I have a link to her recordings on this page of the companion website to the book: https://thebeatcop.com/page/community. O’Neill was very enthusiastic about her playing.

I think his publications had a bigger effect in Ireland than they did in the US, partly I think because people involved in managing/collecting in Ireland, like Breandan Breathnach, saw his as a “fellow traveler” and an example of respectable Irish cultural nationalism. O’Neill loved the community of Irish music but was always complaining about the bad habits of various musicians--drinking, not being reliable, quarreling etc. He always said that his written tunes were just a framework and that the tunes lived in playing, and in variation.

He was a fascinating and complex person who lived quite a remarkable life in addition to his impressive and at that point unprecedented collections
