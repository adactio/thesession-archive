Re: Time to develop the “hard” Irish flute tone

No, I don’t agree with you at all on that AB, because much as an experienced dancer can probably learn a new style of dance much easier than a non-dancer, a woodwind player can more easily adapt to the requirements of a new instrument than one who has zero experience in what is physically required.

As a classical flute player, you have a lot of strength and distinctions in the shape of your lips and mouth, as well as airflow control and use of the diaphragm and abdominal muscles for tonal support.

Someone without the woodwind training would not a clue about or even know how to begin to describe what they were struggling with.

The challenge was identifying the required changes and then consistently applying them, plus strengthening the lips in new ways to adapt to the new requirements for the style. It didn’t take long to figure it out, but it did take some considerable time to physically be able to sustain the new style for more than a tune or two. Even now, if I don’t play flute for a few months, it takes me a week or so of daily playing to be able to get through an entire set without my lips turning to jello.

Just my single case: after about 12 years wooden flute I effectively gave it up because I still sounded like cotton wool. Several years later I gave it another shot, and committed to at least three sessions of 10-15 minutes EVERY day; perhaps I missed 1% of days. The curve was this: after 6 months, on the point of giving up for ever, I started to get flashes of a decent D. After a year I was getting a tolerable tone quite a lot of the time. After two years, though there was still lots of room for improvement and lots of need to practise, I felt it was time to loosen up on the 3X15 routine.
Lucky ones may well find it much easier, but yes, you may well be looking at years.

Re: Time to develop the “hard” Irish flute tone

Pat, I’m guessing that your students are not necessarily immersed in Irish music and flute playing, that to some extent they are newcomers to the tradition?

A lot of traditional musics (plural) have what you might call hard edges, things that deliberately colour the sound or technique - for example the very strong dot/cut effect of the Scottish strathspey, which is relatively unusual in a global context, or the hard D of the Uilleann pipe, and so on. I’d include that type of flute tone in such a list.

So perhaps what I’m trying to say - and this is an idea, not an assertion - is that your students aren’t yet comfortable leaning into that sound, that it seems strange and even unpleasant to them. Perhaps some listening homework - I’m a great fan of giving students a few minutes of music and asking them to listen to it daily for a week or two, which can be helpful in broadening the horizons beyond the type of bands that may have brought them into the music.

Re: Time to develop the “hard” Irish flute tone

I am no expert on traditional flute, but as a modern flutist and a baroque flutist, I practice long tones in which I gradually adjust the embouchure - often in front of a mirror so I can watch what is happening - to change the tone color. One exercise that is particularly helpful is to push a note *just* to the breaking point where it goes to the first overtone, then pull it back and see how long I can balance it on that edge.

One other issue that nobody has addressed above (unless Terry addressed in his article, which I have bookmarked to read later) is the design of the flute. The cut of the embouchure certainly has an impact on how hard a sound you can get. In the age of Covid-19 you may be reluctant to do this, but trading instruments has always (pre-pandemic) been a quick way to determine if the issue stemmed from the instrument or the player.

Re: Time to develop the “hard” Irish flute tone

We shouldn’t forget that the flute can enforce the hard tone approach. If you had an “improved era” flute (Nicholson, Rudall Rose, etc) or a “faithful copy” by a modern maker, you had little choice other than to adopt the hard low notes approach. If you didn’t, the said low notes sagged dismally flat (which I termed “Flat Foot Syndrome”). I’m not talking a few cents, but sometimes more than 50, ie more than a quartertone down. Early apologists tried to argue that this was clear sign of the makers using a different temperament, or tuning to an older pitch standard, but none of these arguments stood up to scrutiny. In all temperaments and at all pitches, the notes with the same name (eg “D”) should be in tune with each other!

So when Irish players started taking up old conical flutes, they would have struck this problem, and, rather brilliantly, determined the solution. Nowadays, with well-tuned flutes, we can choose to use the Hard D approach or not. It’s important to note that the “blowing down, jet offset” method is not analogous to “lipping up”. It works by rediverting energy from the flat fundamental into the in-tune partials. Using the same blowing technique on a well-tuned flute won’t drive it sharp.

But the choice of flute could also drive you the other way. I started back around 1970 on the Boehm flute (where would I get a wooden flute in Canberra back then?). And, typical of the time, the flute assumed that the player would be using the “blowing across” approach. But I taught myself at first, and naturally adopted a “blowing fairly downward” approach, as this seemed to me to produce the best sound. When I felt I was getting somewhere, I decided to take some lessons. The teacher was horrified that, even with the slide hard in, I was playing hopelessly flat across the whole range, and started each lesson with “lipping up” practice until I could get the flute up to pitch. I hated the tone it produced, but recognised that I did need to be able to play in tune if playing in a band, which was my aim. So I unlearned blowing downwards, and had to relearn it later. Which is the reason I then wrote the article on Getting the Hard, Dark Tone!

(I did cheat a little by sawing about 3mm off the end of the head tube! Now, that’s better, said the teacher….)

Terry MCGhee, you have touched on a truly sordid era in silver flute making. It is seldom spoken about, but true nevertheless, that many student model flutes, and even more scandalously, some ¨professional¨, were made with inaccurate scales from the mid 1930´s to about the mid or late 1970´s. It wasn´t until the so-called Cooper, and Deveau scales were adopted that many (most?) student and mid to upper quality stopped having distorted scales. When you shorten the head joint to bring the A into tune at 440hz, you throw everything else out. James Galway had some amusing anecdotes about this, and was one of the professionals that worked with Albert Cooper to create a more accurate scale.

Re: Time to develop the “hard” Irish flute tone

AB, I think her approach is solid, but I don’t completely agree with everything she says, probably because as she says herself, everyone’s mouth is shaped differently.

I do engage my upper lip as described, but I don’t have to look down my nose to do it.

To get the hard tone, I both widen the airstream using my lower jaw pulled back, edges of my mouth pulled back and used the engaged upper lip to direct the airstream downward.

Also, I also cover quite a bit of the blowhole with my lower lip, about 30%.

The rest of it really is about diaphragmatic support of the air column to get both the pressure and volume of air required to sustain the tone and provide the foundation for breath pressure based rhythmic emphasis. As I’d been trained in diaphragmatic breathing since I was a child, it’s something I do without thinking about it and not sure how I’d teach it to someone, because I breathe that way quite often even when not playing wind instruments.

Very interesting discussion on flute tone, because it includes people who have arrived at the tone they like in different ways. I watched Leslie’s video and tried to put into practice what she said. It did improve and steady the tone when I tried to actively follow what she said, but my tone wasn’t awful before.

when I moved from the whistle to the flute I was only about 14 so I found the breathing hard to start with (I tell people it takes about 3 months to be able to get through a reel properly, if they ask, but obviously stamina carries on increasing as you grow up). I only realised much later how much proper embouchure brings to the party because you are using your breath more effectively. I remember being told to imagine I am blowing down a straw, so as to narrow the airflow.

During pandemic, I’ve played a lot at home, on my own in a room with good acoustics and I have to say that I’ve been able to improve the tone outside the combined volume of a session. Maybe I need to get a little personal monitor amp and a ‘secret service’ type earphone to maintain my tone (and er, tuning) during sessions. It also doesn’t harm at all that I’ve just had my flute fully serviced so now all of the air I put into is is used more effectively.

I did have one surprise when I worked hard on embouchure with a mirror; my airflow was about 2mm to the ‘downhill’ side of the centre of the embouchure and when I consciously adjusted its placing, it improved the tone even more. So now I need a monitor amp and a mirror…..
