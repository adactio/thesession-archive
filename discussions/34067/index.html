Re: Analyse my ornamentation please (I promise not to delete the video)

Yeah. You *think* you’re doing a triplet on 2-3, but you’re not. It wouldn’t work if you were, but you’re not. ;-) What you’re doing is basically playing a quaver followed by two semiquavers and then onto the next beat. It’s not the same thing as a triplet at all, and sounds a bit like, but not quite like, some old English tunes. It doesn’t really suit Irish music.

You also need to watch that you get your beats right. Playing the triplets was throwing you off the beat quite a lot of the time. I wouldn’t suggest practicing all that number of triplets in tunes, and I would say not to bother at all with those ones that you think are on 2-3. They’re not idiomatic in this music. Well, I can’t think of a tune they would go with, at any rate. Throw in a roll every now and then. The odd cut, maybe.

Re: Analyse my ornamentation please (I promise not to delete the video)

I started writing yesterday, but didn’t have the chance to finish. I think everything I was going to say has been said but, to summarise:

1. The only type of ornamentation you use is the single note bowed triplet. There is nothing *wrong* with that but there are many other ways to ornament a tune.

2. The triplets themselves sound mostly good to my ear - nice and tight, with all three notes sounding clearly. A few of them don’t come off so well, but that is just a matter of practice.

3. Your timing is off in places; you cut some phrases a little short and bring in the next phrase early (e.g. at 0.30, 1.07). Since this always seems to happen just before a triplet, it is reasonable to assume that it is the triplets that are throwing you off. Try taking it at a slower pace and counting the pulse as you play; if you can’t hear where the problem is, use a metronome.

4. The placement of triplets is mostly good, but your attempts at putting them across beats 2-3 (e.g at 1.57, 2.07) is not idiomatic (at least, I have never heard any good Irish fiddle playing where triplets were used in that position).

5. At this stage, you can cut the number of ornaments by half - or three quarters. You are displaying the child-with-new-toy syndrome. ;-) When we learn a new trick, we want to use it all the time - I imagine most of us here have been through it at some stage. But to put in that many triplets without detracting from the tune, you need to be (i) 100% on top of the tune and (ii) 100% on top of your triplets - and even then, most players wouldn’t choose to put in that many.

I hope that helps. I think you have the makings of a decent fiddler.

Re: Analyse my ornamentation please (I promise not to delete the video)

This is going to be brutal so it won’t surprise me at all if you choose to dismiss my advice, and others rush intemperately to your defense. But allow me this. I’m the fifth generation of a family of trad musicians, have taught trad for about forty years now, not the occasional student here or there but hundreds and hundreds over the years. I teach at Milltown and the All Ireland, as well as UL and UCC. I’ve taught several of the musicians you aspire to play like. Most years I have adult students in workshops and summer schools that are much like yourself, with little if any ready access to exemplars but mad keen to progress. Some have been “playing” trad for twenty or thirty years. Even after that length of time most just don’t play trad “well” (and I accept that judgement has a subjective element). As with any worthwhile art form, trad is terribly difficult to master; the simplicity of the tunes and the relative lack of technical demands hide a subtlety and nuance in effects and musicality that are easy to overlook if one is not brought up with it. It’s a music of small things.

The harsh reality is you’re making all the same mistakes made by most people that try to learn trad without ready access to exemplars. They all stem from your haste to get to what you think are the interesting bits (playing ornaments, playing like Johnny or Tara etc). As a result, you’re ignoring fundamentals. Again and again your videos/recordings exhibit the same problems:

a) You can’t reliably keep a simple rhythm. As a result you can’t add a pulse, let alone a generate lift;

b) You have problems with musicality. You have little sense of the musical line. Essentially you’re just playing a series of notes rather than a tune;

c) More specifically to trad, you don’t seem to understand how dance tunes work and basic “question and answer” phrasing. You drop whole measures;

d) You seem to have picked up some ideas about trad, that to paraphrase Pauli, are not even not right, they’re not even wrong e.g. that a Donegal fiddle style (not that one exists save in the most simplistic attempt at classification) eschews rolls or is limited to a certain tunes!

This is all terribly negative, I know. Even musicians born and reared in communities where many play and the music is a commonplace talk of mastering the art as lonely, dark work, something that can drive a man mad, certainly help turn one to drink.

You’ve perhaps bailed by now, dismissing me as a xenophobic trad diehard, tutting away and shaking my head at your cheek in trying to steal my precious. But if you’re still reading you might want to try the following:

i) Learn to lilt. It really is the silver bullet. It’ll help with a), b) and c) above. Internalize tunes and play with them under your breath before you try to put them under your fingers;

ii) Stop trying to learn tunes that take your fancy. Most trad musicians teach essentially the same fifty to a hundred tunes to beginners for a reason: they’re melodically simple, and have obvious phrasing and rhythm patterns that are easy to manipulate to generate pulse and lift. Again, this’ll help with a) and b);

iii) Stop trying to learn and introduce ornaments. It’s far too early. If you can’t generate lift and musical interest in a tune played plain, adding ornaments (as evidenced by your recording above) is only going to make things worse;

iv) Stop trying to emulate particular musicians or play in a regional “style”. It’s like trying to paint like Rembrandt without knowing how to prepare a canvas or mix paints;

v) You seem to have Matt’s book. Why not stick with the tunes and sequencing in that? At one to two hours a day for five to six hours a week it’d take someone in your situation perhaps two years to master the material;

vi) Then perhaps pick up Seamus’ double CD “Tunes for Practice”. Arranged in common sets, he plays tunes first plain and then with simple variation and ornamentation, all at an easy pace. There’s easily twelve months of study there alone;

vii) Take any opportunity to learn to set dance. It’ll do wonders for a) above.

Learning music, to whatever standard, in whatever circumstances is a great thing. Lots of practicing and listening are the watchwords given to beginners on this board. If only it were that simple. The real trick is knowing what to listen for, what to practice, how to practice and in what sequence.

Good luck.

Re: Analyse my ornamentation please (I promise not to delete the video)

If diametrically opposite is very similar then fair enough. ;-) Anyhow my advice is and has remained consistent over the last few decades;
That is to learn to play the tunes simply with verve , excitement, good tone and timing , drive and lift and that none of these rely on any ornaments which are a stylistic choice.
That beginners learns the tunes in a simple manner and only start to incorporate instrument specific ornaments when and if appropriate.and that this is a logical development of an organic process which can not be skipped.
That by skipping these foundations a superficial style can only result.
That its about finding a voice and expressing oneself musically in a simple , consistent and balanced manner and only then building in more advanced technical tricks and turns , so to speak, Just as a Piobaireachd develops from the ground to its ultimate variation only to return at the end to simplicity.
That this excessive focus on aspects that differentiate trad from the rest of the worlds music fails to realize that its the aspects that draw us all together that are paramount, the similarities!!
Yes, as a piper or a fiddler there are decades of understanding to come, fine shades of tone and technique rolls and cranns ,doubles and trebles what -have you but they amount to nothing without a musical style that revolves around the fundamentals I enumerated above , upon which a person can build a serious musical identity .
