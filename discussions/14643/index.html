Guitar, choice of instrument for lazy people everywhere!

Yeh i know it’s been said before..well I’m saying it again!
Guitarists, learn the tunes, then play them. Don’t be lazy! Who wants to “back”, anyway? If you really want to strum 4 chords all night, learn some Bob Dylan songs. (and you’ll have 1 chord left over!).
why are guitarists so lazy? go to a session and listen to the melody players and the guitar guys..the amount of work put in to play what they r playing by these two respective groups is probably in a ratio of 40:1, if not more. No exaggeration.
Put it another way, the amount of practice time it took me to be able to “back” to a level where i was welcome to join in my local session was roughly equivalent to the time it took to learn my first 3 reels. Again absolutely no exaggeration. Every guitarist here may hate me saying this but they know it’s the truth. (those that can play at least 3 reels that is, which lets face it is a minority).
Guitar is my favourite instrument. In rock, blues, classical, jazz music I can listen to it for ever. But 99.5% of sessions I’ve ever been to I’m blocking it out so I can enjoy the music..it’s there with the clinking of the beer glasses, pool table etc. oh that also includes bazouki players, and the guy i know who wheedled his way into sessions purely on the basis he had a slightly unusual similar instrument (a mandola i think he called it or somesuch).

I’m allowed to say this cuz I’m a guitarist myself so can’t be accused of being opinionated, old fashioned or tunnel visioned, or of deliberately being contrary just for the sake of it…….. ;-)
………And they’re off!!

Re: Guitar, choice of instrument for lazy people everywhere!

The time it takes for you to get to an appropriate level to go into a session and back to the point that no one tells you to push off, with all due respect, has nothing to do with learning how to accompany. The art of playing a passable backing in a session pretty much only requires the guitarist to know what key you’re in. Then for 75% of the tunes, it’s a safe bet that you’ll get away with the tonic for two bars, the sub-dominant for a bar and the dominant for a bar, and everyone will be too focused on themselves to really notice what you’re up to. NOT that I advise doing it, but, well, I’ve always wanted to see what would happen if I tried that one on.

To be able to accompany someone in a manner that actually advances the tune and creates interesting harmony requires knowledge of the tunes, which takes a lot of listening to acquire. If you’d like to listen to some skilled guitarists working as accompanists, I’d recommend you Ian Carr, Chris Newman, Ian Stephenson and Marc Clements for starters. Certainly, they rank amongst my main influences, so if you’re looking to understand the other viewpoint they would mark probably as good a place as any to start.

Personally, I spend a great deal more time working on and refining a chord sequence that I was going to use as part of a band than I would need to learn to flatpick the tune - although to truely absorb it into my own repetoire and style I’d have been playing it for months. However, I do spend a fairly large percentage of my time working on expanding my flatpicked repetoire and developing variations on tunes I already know.

Lazy and unmusical…

I posted this in Feb 2007…

I seriously think that guitar playing has ruined all music over the last sixty years or so. It is a combination of (i), (ii) and (iii).
(i) It is mostly used for playing chords on and accompanying the voice or other instruments actually playing a tune (not just ITM by the way!). Because one will often learn chords first, many never get on to learning scales and tunes. Because of this, the majority of guitarists (unlike the generation of musicians before- including guitarists) have failed to learn “dots” and basic theory. Most can barely read a chord chart proficiently, let alone explain the function of a minor seventh flattened fifth. (And computer based composing/ recording does not help!)
(ii) It is very good and easy for playing the dreadful BLooZ, encouraging even the player who has being strumming and “licking” for years to endlessly churn out old hat and clichés and never progress onto other more challenging, grown-up or melodic forms.
(iii) The amplified version caused all other instruments and voices to become inaudible in concert for nearly a generation (or until half decent fold back was invented), leading to a preponderance of tuneless over-loud guitar based nonsense and a relative dearth of other instrumentalists and forms.

Hakanozel, I’m not going to even try and play armchair psychologist, but your subsequent post, and your references to being “embarrassed” or feelings of “insecurity,” do suggest a certain mindset that, perhaps more than most, colors your impressions and observations about guitarists in Irish music.
This is not to condemn you for your views, certainly not when you encourage guitarists to be more conscientious about what they do. It’s simply that your approach to the whole topic seems to be couch in that renowned paradox, “I would never want to join a club that would have me for a member.”

Look, you are no doubt right to say there are many melody players who would be perfectly happy without backers around. All _I_ know is, the melody players I’m around most often say they appreciate the presence of a good guitarist or other kind of backer; they talk of the extra “lift” or drive an accompanist can give a tune, how his/her presence can inspire their own playing. They are not given to some subjective relative measurement of the “work” another musician has put into his or her craft.
A fiddler I’ve worked with over the past couple of years once told me she envied me as a guitarist. I told her I was rather envious of her, and her ability to explore all those ornaments and other subtleties of the tunes which are so characteristic of this music. But she said, essentially, that as a guitarist, as a rhythm player, I was at the “heartbeat” of the music.
It was one of those arguments which no one tries to win, and are so gratifying to have.

Anyway, best of luck and enjoy the music.

Strayaway, where can we hear your air? I play some airs and other tunes fingerstyle as well and always like to hear other tune players. Do you find fingerstyle tune playing or backing more of a challenge? I find backing is harder, but I enjoy it more too. When playing solo you are not trying to interact with anyone else, and you know what notes or nuances come next. In backing I try to be right there with a melody player, to the point of sliding a note when they do, stopping a split second when they do, stressing a note when they do, knowing the tune in my head, and having to hold it there while my hands do something else (and I am generally not chording except as a stress point). Yet it is the most exhilerating and intensely good time I can possible have. I love tweaking the accompaniment this way or that according to what the melody player at hand is doing, the intense concentration on them, etc. It’s a thrill like no other.

Curious… do people often ask you to play that air or other tunes at a session? Or do they just barrel ahead with the tunes? I find it depends on the session. Some will ask for a tune every time the circle comes around, at others they just pass you by and call for the next melody player to start something, even though you are a melody player tooand they may very much like your playing, they don’t think about it!
It may be too low key and relaxing for them, it’s very much like a harp sound. I generally have to retune into another tuning so it’s a bit of a chore and I don’t mind being passed over, and I worry about breaking the high E going back up several times during an evening. So honestly I am happy either way, and do enjoy backing more and just go along unless asked to specifically play something.

>you say you envied the fiddler her ability to explore the ornaments and subtleties of ITM. Isn’t the underlying assumption here right at the root of what I’m saying? Why CAN’T you explore the rudiments? What is it about guitarists that they think their instrument is capable of less subtlety?!

Hakanozel, I will certainly strive to do so, i.e., “explore the rudiments.” It is a most worthy goal.
However, as I noted, the fiddlers, flutists, et al, I hang about with _value_ my playing as an accompanist. So doesn’t it make sense that I should spend a significant amount of my time refining and improving my skills at backing, e.g., working on my strumming and chord inversions?
I also spend a significant amount of time practicing my singing and the accompaniment I use for it. Singing is not only something I enjoy, but people continually ask me to do it, so again, I feel motivated to focus on doing it as well as possible.
And I devote time to other instruments: bouzouki -- which I _do_ use mostly for melody -- and bodhran. Again, I like playing them, and people seem to like what I do enough that they encourage me to play them.
Now, let me clarify the statement to which you referred. When I told my fiddler friend I sometimes “envied” her, it had as much to do with her instrument of choice as what she played on it. You are absolutely right in saying a guitar has subtle qualities, and that there are guitarists who are capable of exploring and showcasing these in delightful ways.
But just as the guitar has elements a fiddle cannot duplicate, the reverse is true, and that’s what I was getting at: How wonderful, to me, the ability to lay a bow across those strings and run one’s fingers on what seems to me an impossibly small fretboard to produce such sounds.
Sure, I could go hellbent trying to approach my guitar-playing as if I was a fiddler. And there’s nothing that says I can’t make steps in that direction. But ultimately, given constraints of time and other resources, I have to set priorities for developing those aspects of my music that are likely to be valued most, whether by other musicians or by people who listen to me play, and that therefore I will use most often.

But thanks for giving me stuff to think about.
