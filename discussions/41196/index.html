Re: Recording music

Your problems probably aren’t down to equipment, but positioning. If your microphone isn’t in the right place the most expensive kit in the world won’t give you a decent recording.These days even the cheapest kit is good enough for what you are trying to do (although you will get better results with an external mic rather than the built in one in the laptop). Audacity is free, simple and capable of recording at studio quality on your laptop. Add to that a USB condenser mic (preferably stereo). Let your wallet choose which mic you buy, anything over about £30 will be good enough for what you are trying to do. And then it’s all down to positioning.

Microphone positioning is a black art, and you haven’t given enough information for us to give any concrete advice - are you recording a solo instrument? if so what instrument? or an ensemble? in your bedrooom or a concert hall, or a pub with background noise?

Assuming you are recording a single instrument the first thing to do is find the position that gives the most natural tone. Many instruments, particularly strings, are very directional at high frequency, so the low frequencies go out in all directions and the highs only cover a certain area, so walk around the instrument and listen until you find a position where you are getting a natural sound without too much low or high content. Move up and down as well as sideways - A high mic position usually sounds better than a low one. There are other problems that come in if the mic is close to the floor, and about head height often gives the best sound.

If you are working alone at home you can do things the other way round - put the mic on a stand in the middle of the room, listen on headphone and walk round the mic as you play until you find the sweet spot.

The next job is to find how close to put the mic. Putting the mic close to the instument gives a strong direct sound with less reverb from the room, moving it away gives more reverb. So working along a straight line from the mic position you’ve found to the instrument move the mic back and forwards until you get a good balance between the instrument and the reverb.

Finally think about where the mic needs to point. Study the polar plot for your mic and get to know which directions it is sensitive and where it is deaf. Very often it is more important to point the deaf zone at a strong spurious sound source (another instrument, monitor speakers or a wall that is giving a strong echo) than to point the mic directly at the instrument you are recording.

AT 3035 (used, £65), into DBX286a preamp (used, the original US version, £100), into Focusrite iTrack dock for iPad (new, £130), running Cubasis (about £40). Total cost was about £340 (excluding the iPad of course).

Re: Recording music

Well worth trying to acoustically treat your room as far as possible. Choose a room that has a lot of soft furnishings in with as few hard surfaces as possible i.e. not the kitchen or bathroom. A good trick is to drape some duvets over the door to the room (or alternatively over wardrobe door) and stand in front of it. The duvet(s) act as an acoustic baffle, absorbing sound waves. Ideally you’d be hanging soft thick drapes on all walls, but that’s probably not all that practicable! If you’re going to buy a microphone, buying a super-cardioid (or hyper-cardioid one) might be a good idea, as they only record what is directly in front of them, so minimising the amount of reflected sound waves bouncing off your walls.

Re: Recording music

Further to postings above about the acoustic properties of the room in which you’re recording . . .

I was thinking I needed to get more expensive mics to take my recordings to a better quality level (I mostly use condenser mics in the £150 - £200 bracket) and I tried out a couple of well-known brands at twice that sort of price. The difference was noticeable, but marginal. I then put a large rug on the (wooden) floor and hung some thickish curtains over the windows: this resulted in a marked improvement. In particular, it eliminated any ‘bathroominess’ and resulted in a more intimate sound that was truer to the real voice of the instrument.

Opinion: Top-class equipment is no doubt essential for the best recordings, but for general purposes it’s worth spending a bit on managing the room acoustics before splashing out on more expensive kit.

PS: The other thing to do before spending lots of dosh is to experiment with mic positioning. I’ve found that it matters quite a lot. Record the same thing 3 or 4 times with mics in different places: make a note of which is which, and then listen back to them the next day when your ears are fresh.

Re: Recording music

“I was thinking I needed to get more expensive mics to take my recordings to a better quality level (I mostly use condenser mics in the £150 - £200 bracket) and I tried out a couple of well-known brands at twice that sort of price. The difference was noticeable, but marginal.”

Yes, similar experiences here. More expensive does not necessarily mean ‘better’, just ‘different’. I know from trial and error that my singing voice will sound better via a dynamic microphone such as an EVRE20 or Shure SM7 or Beyerdynamic M69 or M88 than it will through a posh Neumann microphone that costs thousands.
When it comes to stringed instruments, I like a microphone that can capture the attack, the sharpness, the brightness: and an expensive mic is no more likely to do that than a cheap one. That’s why studio engineers have lots of mics and know their character. Say a fiddler turns up to record and they sound screechy as hell; stick a dark or neutral sounding dynamic mic like an SM7 or RE20 or even a ribbon mic on em.

To echo the comment above - making your room sound better to start with pays many more dividends than buying an expensive microphone. Otherwise all you’re doing is getting an even more precise hi-definition realisation of a crummy sounding room!

If you really don’t want to spend much money at all, here’s a bottom-of-the-range microphone that might well do a better job than a £1000+ mic in a really crappy sounding room:
http://www.tbone-mics.com/en/product/information/details/mb88u-dual-dynamisches-gesangsmikrofon/

It’s a cardioid dynamic mic. Dynamic mics are less sensitive than condensers, and won’t pick up nearly so much of whatever ugly resonances are bouncing around your kitchen.
