You Are All Welcome To Our Culture!

Dear Friends,

May I hereby invite you ALL to be an unconditional part of ‘OUR’ culture. Let me explain this (my latest bizzarre outpouring):

I was born in Balfast. My first experience of (what can quite reasonably be classed as) folk music was Orange marching band renditions of traditional tunes being belted out as the bands marched passed the top of our street on summer evenings. I liked it. I liked it so much in fact that I tried to persuade my poor mother to by me a ‘band stick’ (the band leader’s baton that is often twirled and thrown with great dexteriy at the head of bands in parades). This was an unusual request coming as it did from a young catholic boy brought up in a largely catholic area. But she got me one and took of the red, white and blue ribbons, bless her (the colours were just colours to me).

It hadn’t dawned on me at that time that I was ‘supposed’ to consider the music the preserve of another group, that it was almost universely considered a mark of seperation between me, a child, and others of a different religious/political/social orientation. Such is the gift of young minds, their clarity should be an inspiration to us all. I still credit this experience as a contributor to my musical existence today.

Much folk music in Ireland has been usurped by political elements at times in our history of course. But the resilience of the stuff is such that, time and time again, it will resurface with the appearence of something that is at once new and contemporary, and something that never really went away or appeared from somewhere else. It constantly proves itself to be never ‘orange’ or ‘green’ for too long, of one or the other. This is due to a drawn out process of inspired reworking using internal AND external influences, and creative composition and experimentation along broadly established aesthetic lines.

This process is a very, very special and unique one in the world of music. Some say the process has ‘broken down’ with the advent of faster and increased communications, some will say it is no longer relevant (because it is not moving quickly enough to react to modern cultural Ireland). My own view is that it is changing at an accelerated rate alright, but there is still a diversity of movements in it that continue the main gist of what has always been there in that they are borrowing and learning from each other: there are conservative movements who revel in existing aesthetics and attemp to communicate through inspired personal playing AND regional styles, then there are those who consider introduction of new themes and motiffs to be of more importance, there is the localised ‘grass roots’ music, and there is the big touring acts and everything in between.

It is my beleive, given the recent changes in cultural and political Ireland (multicultural population, parity of esteem for population in the North etc.), that it is important to consider the nature of the music up to now, how it has proved an open resource, how it has transcended our worst times intact and renewed, and that it holds an important message that may see us through future bad times.

If we could all look at what we have through the clarity and curiosity of a childs eyes then I think we would have little to worry about. History will prove the music to be ultimately harmless in its adaptability, no matter what we throw at it, although I think it is wise at this stage to throw no more at it.

This music isn’t really ‘mine’ or ‘ours’ of course (we’ve borrowed too much of it, even though we’ve contributed a lot in the mix!). But in the spirit of everything that ever moved us along as human beings a little bit can I restate that, no matter who and where you are, you all are very, very welcome to it.

It soars above and below all attempts to sanction it off, and so it belongs to none, and all.

Regards,

Harry.

Re: You Are All Welcome To Our Culture!

I don’t equate ITM with something as geo-political as the English language. I don’t know how you’re defining “cultural property,” but what I mean is that it clearly is music from Ireland developed by Irish people.

When I strive to play ITM I’m listening to the way Irish people play, and study the history of it as it relates to the country of its origin… Ireland. If I learn it from someone outside of Ireland who isn’t Irish, I would do so because their source for it relates to it’s origin i.e. mastering it as it is played in Ireland. On the other hand, if I learn it from someone outside of Ireland who has reinvented it -- it won’t be Irish music anymore, it will only be a derivative.

Just about every kind of music has borrowed from other genres, and as Harry pointed out, ITM is no exception. But what has developed into ITM did so in Ireland or with the Irish people, hence the name. If someone outside of Ireland plays music that borrows from ITM and yet another genre is born -- it won’t be called Irish music anymore, it will probably get it’s own distinction. If that new distinction becomes known and is associated with the country or culture it develops in -- it will then be the ‘cultural property’ of that new country and it’s people. This doesn’t mean no one else could play it, or play it right; all it means is that it’s the cultural property of that country.

My hunch is that Harry was inviting everyone to feel free to play ITM, and that Irish people have no jurisdiction over it. My query here was inspired by another recent thread where the concept came up of reinventing ITM outside of Ireland. I just wanted a little clarification from Harry and his thoughts on that subject.

Re: You Are All Welcome To Our Culture!

Culture isn’t property. People don’t own or possess culture, they belong to it by participating in it.

And cultural labels are almost always too small for the human realities they’re pinned to, designed to separate based on hair-splitting differences rather than acknowledging commonalities and cross fertilization.

In short, if you want to play “Irish,” you can’t just be influenced by the Irish. You have to integrate the same outside influences they’ve long looked to. In fact, integrating outside influences is one of the defining characteristics of this music (if you’re of the mind to try to define such things--personally, I’d rather just enjoy playing music).

I too would like to hear what Harry has to say.

Re: You Are All Welcome To Our Culture!

Hey all,

Couldn’t resist getting in on this discussion. The exchange of musico-cultural capital between Ireland and the rest of the world – and especially North America – is a long-standing point of interest for me.

Let me preface this by saying that I don’t mean any of my remark to be antagonistic. This thread has the potential to be massively informative and productive, so I wouldn’t do anything to stymie it. Also, Harry, I have to say that your posts are quite captivating. It’s a rare delight to see a combination of intellectual acuity and incisiveness leveled at this topic.

Encomiums aside, though, I have to say that I found this particular post a tad idealistic. I certainly appreciate the sentiment you infer by suggesting a socio-cultural transcendence as an intrinsic quality of what goes by the “working title” of “traditional Irish music.” I am also certain that it does in fact posses that quality. Having seen first hand the amazing and undeniable variety of personalities, attitudes, histories, and social, cultural, and even racial backgrounds possessed by the community of people who play this music, I can’t deny that it must have some powers of pan-cultural attraction and fascination. That being said, however, I can’t help but suggest that, because of the varied commercial interests that now hold significant influence over the dissemination of Irish culture, and especially Irish arts like music and dance, those cultural phenomena will be only as “adaptable” as the forces and flow of capital allow them to be. As long as there is a mint of money to be made performing “Irish” art forms, and so much valuable cultural cache in “Irishness” (and anyone who has seen Riverdance or *shudder* Celtic Woman knows what I mean), no Irish cultural phenomena will ever be allowed to be inclusive. Like it or not, traditional Irish music, song, and dance are cultural capital now, in every sense of the word, and the Central Bank of Irish cultural capital is in Ireland. And because it is a form of capital, market rules apply. Right now, I believe the market unmistakably declares that “traditional Irish music” is part of a commercial package, part and parcel with an accent, an appearance, and an attitude. Of course, all of these “aspects” Irish culture are stereotypes to an extent; but they are still expected by those legions around the world who consume Irish cultural capital in all its various forms of expenditure. And so long as any practitioner of those arts does not conform to consumer expectations of that art’s packaging, he/she the practitioner will never be truly believed to be fully part of the community. In short, I believe that musical transcendence is limited and curtailed by the cold, hard fact of how profitable Irish culture is to the Irish. To offer a crass reduction of the point, there’s a standing joke here around Boston that the quickest way to get inside a pair of panties is to have an Irish accent. Crude, but it illustrates what I’m trying to say.

The long and the short of that is the idea that the profitability of Irish music as a cultural commodity breeds suspicion and intolerance of those who don’t fit into the consumer framework (Americans, for example). That sounds a lot like I’m saying that the music makes Irish people discriminate against non-Irish in a musical setting; it doesn’t, necessarily. It does, however, make them competitive in my view, and competition is by definition an exclusivist phenomenon. So long as Irish culture remains a potent source of profitable cultural capital, there will be those who seek to protect what has for a very long time been at most a monopoly, and at least a powerful hegemony. And that, I think is an equally powerful source of division in what might otherwise be a truly transcendent musical form.

Maybe I’m being pessimistic and grumpy by suggesting this theory, but it is only a theory, and only mine at that. I’d be more than happy to hear from anyone who wants to contest it. Again, I’m not trying to pick a fight. This is just an idea. Feel free to go after it with an axe…

Regards,
James
