playing by memory

playing by ear is hard to learn, i can understand a lot of musicians dont. i am learning now. i dont find it very difficult tho, but i find it takes some work. the hardest thing for me to do is find the first note, but if i’m given it i do pretty well. also, of course, in a session environment, you’d be a fool not to look at someone’s fingers, but whatever. the first time i ever really did it was at irish fest milwaukee this year , and i picked up at least good 4 or 5 tunes (of course, i forgot them within a day or two, but thats not the point).

but, playing by memory. i grew up with trad. all the music i play outside of band is either trad or original compositions (kinda trad influenced mod classical stuff), both of which are done entirely by memory (i dont even write down my original compositions, which could be a bad thing. especially if i dont play them for a year or so i have to figure them out again).

i started taking flute lessons last november or december or something, and was having trouble with a solo piece for a solo competition at my school (the entertainer by joplin) not only because of the syncopation, but “something else”. i soon figured out it was because i hadnt memorized it. which then meant i couldnt play it well, even though i have been reading music for as long as i’ve played my flute.

the piece for my recital, scherzo by gary shocker, was infinitely harder, more syncopated, and has a range all the way up to a 4th octave d, but i pulled that one off without a hitch. the girl who was waiting for her turn while i played the entertainer 6 months earlier attested to how much better it was, a whole world of difference. part of why i did better, is cuz i approached it differently; i would never move onto the next note unless i could play all notes before it, and in time, and i of course had it memorized. i did use the sheet music, just in case i forgot it on stage, but it was just a back up, and i didnt really need it.

everyone always says, “thats fine for ITM, but you cant memorize a mozart symphony”. why not? james s. wilkins, jr, a student of theobald boehm, could hear a tune and play it back after first hearing. not because he was especially gifted, but it was what boehm had taught him and expected of him. and not an irish tune, mind you, but flute soli, long and complex, and all classicky. i believe i read he knew learned at least 500 flute soli by the time he was done studying with boehm.

many an irish sessioner could play easily a couple hours of music off the top of their head if you told them which tunes to play or gave them time to come up with the tunes. i’m sure even some could play for days in a session if it were physically possible. beethoven’s 9th symphony is only the length of a single cd. why then, cant a classical musician memorize something like that? sure, its not easy, but the pieces themselves arent easy. if they were to practice memorizing tunes, or had been taught from the start to do it, i bet that after they fully learned how to play a complex classical piece they would have it all but memorized.

Re: playing by memory

disclaimer: like i said, i have never learned a symphony by heart, but that is beacuse i have never played one. i know positively i would memorize it, because of how i learn music. i am learning two pieces right now for my audition band at school, and this is how i go about it:

1 get right notes
2 get right rhythm for above notes
never move on to next note without both above correct
3 play by memory what just learned

if i cant play it with my eyes closed, i dont consider that knowing it. i do it measure by measure (if its a hard passage of 32 second notes on a beat or 2, i make sure i can play them both by memory before i move on). of course the first time i look at a piece, i dont have it memorized. and sometimes i might play through a large section before memorizing it, but i always memorize. because, otherwise i dont know the music. i can play it, but i dont know it. thats just how i do it.

so yeah… it might be a bit unfair for me to tell people to memorize classical (i speak several languages, and an old hobby of mine was to memorize digits of pi), but then it would be unfair of me to expect people to memorize ITM too, i guess. because its the same memorizing anything the world, its just how you look at it. make connections with the stuff, look for patterns, create meaning and familiarity where there isnt any.

and besides, even if it is a lot of work, memorizing a piece really gives you so much more freedom. you can practice anytime, anywhere, and can focus more on your tone, rhythm, the notes you’re playing, and musicality, because you dont have to look at a sheet of paper.

but, its the way our society has changed. orators in rome and greece used to memorize their speachers, and had complex systems for memorising them. now we have note cards, and word for words speaches. ironically, we now have more tools and tricks and devices for memorizing and learning than we have ever before, because we have taken unearthed many of the tricks and even hidden secrets of memorization from the romans to the hindus (who memorized their holy books for 4000 years without writing them down or changing anything) etc, to using these and new breakthroughs to create new methods that are the children of the old methods (like harry lorayne’s link system, built on the loci: sophrology, built on hypnosis (tho no hypnosis is involved, they studied the human minds capabilities under hypnosis and figured out how to get them without it) and yoga, autogenics built on eastern meditation, and many more). i think that some day we will start using these systems and others like them more widespread, but it takes time for a society to accept that you can completely rechange the way you think and learn.

Re: playing by memory

Classical concertos, and other solo works are, almost by definition, usually technically demanding at a high level and require great mental and physical stamina and concentration for the performance. To get such a work to the standard where you’re going to play it live before a paying (and critical) audience, and perhaps also in front of tv cameras or radio mics, you’ll be putting in a level and quantity of practice such that it’s virtually inevitable that you will have memorised every detail of the solo part, and a good chunk of the orchestral accompaniment as well.

If the soloist is giving a great performance they’ll be on a knife-edge, and even something as simple as an orchestral woodwind player briefly fumbling an entry could be a trigger for a memory lapse (this particular event actually happened a few months ago in a performance of Tchaikovsky’s piano concerto #1 in which I was playing).

Having said that, even some of the greatest players are not above having the music on the stand in front of them when performing, not to read from, but as a backup in case the memory plays an unexpected trick. Itzhak Perlman, in a Promenade concert the other week in the Albert Hall, London, had the solo part of the Elgar violin concerto in front of him, even though that is a concerto he has been playing from memory for most of his long career.
Orchestral and choral conductors, too, are in the same position as a concerto soloist; they are, in effect, “playing” their chosen instrument which is the orchestra or choir, and the really good ones will have memorised the entire programme, even though they’ll usually have the score in front of them for the performance.

Choirs often sing from memory; and a while ago I was at a 2-hour concert by a chamber orchestra which they played entirely from memory - and standing up in a semi-circle facing the audience, except for the 2 cellists.

I think there is a level of retention much deeper than that of memory, where the music (or poetry, or whatever) is effectively permanently hard-wired into the brain. When that happens it is virtually impossible to forget that piece of music, and it has become, in a very real sense, a part of you. Most of us, who have been playing tunes for a while, will have a number of tunes in that category. Many more are also in memory, but not perhaps at such a deep level. The problem then for many players is not whether they “remember” a tune (although it is in fact in memory) but whether they can recall it without a cue. The problem of recall is too often confused with not being able to “remember” tunes.

Trevor

Re: playing by memory

When I learn things by ear they evolve. Sometimes immediately. I hardly ever play *exactly* what I hear, because I might decide a particular ornament or lack thereof doesn’t suit me. I get all the notes, then every time I play the tune, I add something new. I think classical musicians need music in front of them as a gentle reminder not to get so carried away with the music that they change something - like crescendo when they ought to decrescendo, or trill when they ought to vibrato. If you are obligated to play the music exactly as written every single time, I don’t see the point of trying to carry it around in your head. I memorized a whole heap of stuff when I played classical piano and I always caught hell because I didn’t memorize the dynamics too. In the end I had to have the music in front of me, even when I knew every note of a piece, just to remind me of the niggly details that might have slipped my mind.

I can carry hundreds of Irish tunes around in my head because I’m not trying to carry a bucketload of dynamic particulars around with them. I have a file for dynamics and a file for tunes, and when I play a tune, I put a little of each in a shaker, mix it up and see what comes out. If an orchestra tried that it would be appalling.

Re: playing by memory

In Irish tunes the dynamic is more or less at the same level throughout a tune, basically because the tunes were originally intended to be danced to and the musician had to make himself heard for the benefit of the dancers.

Detailed dynamics in musical scores is relatively modern; in the time of Bach, Handel Vivaldi all you’d see would be the occasional “f” for loud and “p” for soft, and precious little in the way of phrasing or ornamentation. The detailed interpretation was left very much to the player himself or the director of the orchestra - usually a very small affair by today’s standards. Modern editions of these old works will have quite detailed dynamics and phrasing which are the modern editor’s personal interpretation of how it may have been played. I haven’t always found that approach entirely convincing, and in the case of the Bach cello suites I prefer to work from the Urtext edition, which is pretty well as Bach wrote it.

Trevor

Re: playing by memory

Memorizing classical music, unless you are a soloist, makes absolutely no sense for two reasons. One is that there is no point or advantage to it. Two is that each individual part seldom carries a melodic line, but is part of a unified whole. Therefore, in the process of memorization, there is not the kind of frame of reference there is in playing a tune. ITM doesn’t even have rests, let alone separate parts.

Another aspect of classical music is the dynamic markings that indicate how the music is to be played. Written ITM has, at most, suggested spots for rolls, but classical music is far, far more demanding and precise when it comes to dynamic markings. Now, a conductor, during rehearsals, will put his or her own stamp on how a piece is to be played, and these things are also noted on the printed music.

Conversely, it is counterproductive to play ITM from written music, because it isn’t until the tune is thoroughly learned that one can decide where the ornaments will come in, and what they will be. Hopefully, they will not be the same every time. The nature of ITM is very different than the nature of classical music, and the approach that best works with each is different as a result.

bottom line, our preconceptions hold us back more than real possibilities.
