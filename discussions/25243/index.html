Bow Tilt in Fiddling

As a violinist I was taught to tilt my bow toward the fingerboard, so that part of the hair isn’t in contact with the string. It’s not a static tilt, it varies depending on the bow stroke, but generally a forward tilt.

For a while after starting to fiddle my tilt became more extreme, so much so that I would often actually bounce the stick off the strings when doing string crossings! Interesting percussive effect but not really intended, and not so good for the bow.

Lately I’ve been experimenting with keeping the bow vertical with all the hair on the string. I’m not sure whether I like it better or not, but I’ve noticed that it seems to help my bowed triplets (god knows they could use it). And it seems to suit the sort of short rapid bow strokes I used in fiddling.

How do you guys do it? Tilt, or all hair on deck? Any thoughts on advantages/disadvantages to each approach?

What seems to make more of a tone difference for me is allowing the weight of the arm to transfer through my wrist and hand by not bending the wrist too much one way or the other (the “swan’s head” when playing up near the frog, for example). Caitlin Warbelow recently persuaded me to give this less-bent wrist thing a try and I have to say it really has made a difference. Re: Bow Tilt in Fiddling

According to C.H.Hohmann:- “To get a full fine tone the hairs of the bow must be so set on the string that they touch it with their whole breadth. The stick inclines slightly towards the fingerboard.”
He neglects to say why the bow should be at an angle. This seems to be one of those things that get passed down from teacher to student without any reason given as to why. For myself, I can’t see any logical reason why the hairs should be at an angle, mainly because the contact with the string would vary by degrees from very good to non-existent across the width of the bow.
I would do whatever seems best to you, and ignore all advice, including this. :]

Interesting observation from there in regards to bow tension. A tighter bow is a bit more bouncy, therefore the extra control you’d get from tilting the bow is helpful.

Re: Bow Tilt in Fiddling

A couple of weeks ago my teacher started to explore with me the concept of knowing exactly where the bow is on the string at all times, without looking - in effect “seeing” by developing the body’s awareness of arm/hand position in space and what the muscles are doing. She was working on this with me a while ago specifically in relation to bowing parallel to the bridge with any part of the bow length, and I’ve now got that pretty well sorted (most of the time!).

Now it’s the turn of knowing where the bow is between the fingerboard and the bridge. This involves being aware of the sound and of the bow pressure on the string, which varies according to whether you’re bowing at the end of the fingerboard or almost over the bridge.

She notionally divided the region between the bridge and the finger board into five “lanes”, each lane conveniently being the width of the bow hairs. This of course assumes that the finger board is standard-length (not Baroque) and that the bridge isn’t in a very strange position.

Lane 1 is the lane immediately adjacent the bridge, and lane 5 is up by the finger board. Lane 3 is more or less over the top end of the f-holes, and is the lane that has the “sweet spot” when you’re playing open strings or low in the first position.

Lane 2 is the one you might want to use when playing the higher notes in the first position and certainly if you’re shifting into higher positions, otherwise the tone will lose its power and sound muffled. You’d also use this lane generally if you want to really project your tone in a session.

Lane 1 - you play Paganini and Locatelli in the attic? You don’t? Good heavens, where have you been! Then forget about it. This lane would be used by orchestral violinists for really high position playing or for special sound effects. As I said in my response to RichardB, if you’re playing that close to the bridge you’ll have to tilt the bow a little.

Lane 4 is a quieter version of lane 3, quite useful but with not quite so much projection.

Lane 5, over the end of the finger board, is very quiet. Try playing strongly in that region and the tone might well break (not the string!). Again, this is a lane used by the classical people for special effects - it’s not just quiet, it has a different tone colour. I’d avoid it for general session playing unless you playing on your own and have the experience to control the tone in that region. However, it is common to see less experienced players using this lane in sessions as a way of keeping a low profile (very understandable) and if they’re not quite certain of the tune.

When training me to be muscularly and spatially aware of which lane the bow is in she would tell me to shut my eyes, or turn my face away from the fiddle, and tell me to play a long down bow (or up) in lane 3 on the D string, listening carefully to the sound and feeling how the bow hairs distort and the string responds to the bow pressure. Then I’m allowed to look to see what I’ve done wrong! The process continues until it starts to come together, and then it’s repeated on another string, which will have a different feel to it. A later stage is to do it when playing quietly, but if you’ve got the first stage of playing loud then playing softly in the desired lane shouldn’t be a problem.

Eventually, you will able to know instinctively which lane the bow is in without ever needing to look. It’s a similar process to learning how to play a note in a high position without looking at your fingers (looking is difficult on the violin because of the fore-shortened perspective), but by listening and the left arm’s spatial awareness.
