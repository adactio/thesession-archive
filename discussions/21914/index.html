Key of C Whistles for American Learners

I learned/am learning on a key of D whistle. I’ve translated that to a key of D flute. There were times when I wondered if, for more general application, particularly for children in the U.S. not in a good position to decide at such a young age that Irish trad is “it” for them, putting them on to a key of D whistle might not be the right thing to do.

A key of C whistle might be better for a beginner. They pay a price in terms of Irish music and the D major and G major scales, but in the U.S., there aren’t that many “kiddie sessions” for beginners to visit, so speed isn’t such an issue in terms of half-holing.

Probably lots of key of C tunes in U.S. traditional music, and Irish pieces in whistle tablature that can be played directly on the C major whistle with D major whistle fingering. The “trad” system isn’t being questioned, but would I be doing a child beginner in the U.S. a dis-service by encouraging them to learn on a key of C whistle in accordance with that scale and note fingering positions, presumably more easily translated to a Boehm system flute, should they later so desire? (I just ordered a few key of C, “Meg” whistles from Clarke to give away to children.)

(I note that I’ve been learning from a book of bagpipe music based on Irish traditional works, all in the key of C. They sound good to me.)

Thanks in advance for your thoughts.

Re: Key of C Whistles for American Learners

Actually, the fingerings on a D whistle are far more similar to a Boehm flute than a C whistle. Pretty much the only difference is that the fingering for F# on the D whistle is the fingering for Fnat. on the Boehm flute. (To play F# on the Boehm flute you would use the ring finer of your right hand rather than the index finger as on the whistle.) The reason a Boehm flute is classified as being in the key of C is because it’s got the low C key on the foot joint, making C its lowest note, thus defining its key. If you’re concerned about transfering the learned fingerings over to the Boehm style flute, best stick with a D whistle.
But if the kid is only going to be playing whistle, not switching to silver flute, and playing mostly American tunes in the key of C, then sure, teach them the C whistle.

Re: Key of C Whistles for American Learners

Given the price of whistles give them both: Teach them to play a D whistle (most useful for trad and compatible with flutes etc.) and then give them a C whistle to play the other stuff and tell them just to play “same as on the D whistle, just play one note higher than the music is written and ignore the key signature”.

Being able to play a D, C or indeed any other whistle from “the wrong sheet music” is a very useful skill. e.g. something written in A, played in G on a D whistle. Or written in G and play in D. Indeed, any whistle or flute player that likes sheet music should set themselves a target of being able to play sheet music written in any of these key signatures (one, two or three sharps) in any of the others on a D whistle. Doesn’t need to be fast and anyway, after a couple of times through, if you know the tune at all in any of the other keys then you should be able to play it without the notes anyway.

Next target is, of course, transposing from notes in one key to the same key, but played on a non-D whistle. Low F whistle or Bflat for example. After that, if you have mastered the first exercise above then being able to transpose to other related keys on the target whistle should also be easy.

Why do this? Well, if you don’t play Boehm flute and you want to play with other people who aren’t necessarily playing ITM then you will be confronted with all sorts of strange keys. And singers will always have strong preferences for what key they sing in which may not be related to what fits best on a D whistle or flute. We don’t have Capos, nor is the whistle a chromatic instrument, so multiple whistles is what we have to do. Generally, I would always play a whistle which has the approriate key signature as either its’ main one or one of the two fifths, i.e. D, G or A on a D whistle.

To be honest, when I’m trying to find a key by ear I try the D Flute, then if that doesn’t work out comes the C whistle and if that doesn’t work I use the Boehm flute. Once I have worked it out then I might switch to one of the other whistles in my collection. Or if I know that they are playing tons of stuff in G Minor (Swedishish session near here…) then out might come the F flute or Bflat whistle.

And for the kiddies, it is always an option to write out the tunes transposed to D, learn them on the D Whsitle and then just tell them to use the C Whistle when playing along with anyone else. A similar approach works well when trying to play along with Bflat instruments - pick a whistle one tone lower than you think you should have for the music in question.

Re: Key of C Whistles for American Learners

The whole point is that it sounds complicated to explain, but isn’t when you try to do it. And I teach Judo, not music.

Another way to do it is to look at the signature, e.g. 3 flats, think Eflat major - pick up Eflat whistle, then try to find out which is the first note of the tune (or any other particularly obvious note somewhere near the start of the tune). The rest is usually pretty easy from there on. Don’t worry about whether the tune is FDorian, C Minor or whatever - all you are trying to do is find which whistle has the right notes available on it. (By the way, this is why you shouldn’t listen to the Guitar player who shouts “its’ in G”…)

I guess my original intent was to say - teach them right from the start, that all whistles are the same to play - you just need a different one depending on who you’re playing with. Then move every tune you want to learn so that you can play it on a D Whistle - or any other…

Re: Key of C Whistles for American Learners

aruthur--yes, it is easier to learn the D whistle and transfer this to the boehm flute. this can be confusing, but there is a simple reason for this. originally, orchestral flutes were in D. then, they started adding keys onto them, and extending the foot, so you could play down to C natural, while the rest of the flute would finger the key of D the same. then, when the boehm flute was invented, theobald boehm kept the D scale mostly intact. on some whistles, you can get a C natural thumb hole, making it so the only note fingering-wise that is different between the boehm flute and the whistle being F#.

i have a flute from 1864 in the old system. even though it is in the key of C (same make and model as this: http://www.tinyurl.com/vueer ), the D scale is exactly same as on D whistle. so, in other words, on old orchestral flutes, the D scale on a C flute was the same as a D scale on a D whistle.
