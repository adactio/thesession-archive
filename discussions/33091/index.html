Re: Chords by Ear

“Is there a pattern of chords for each key?”

There is a *set* of chords for each key. The *pattern* of chords (i.e. the order they go in) depends on each individual tune.

Since a lot of traditional tunes follow broadly similar melodic patterns, it follows that they can be accompanied with similar chord progressions. But it is not safe to assume that exactly the same chords will work for two similar tunes. To accompany tunes properly, you need to know the tunes or, at least, know enough tunes to be able to anticipate where an unfamiliar tune is going. You also need to be familiar with the relationships between the chords in each of the keys and modes.

So, in short, it is “down to experience and knowing the chords by ear.” Spot on.

Re: Chords by Ear

Yes there is a pattern, but like others have said above it doesn’t mean it will be the same chords for every tune in D major for example. So if you label each scale degree in a certain key with a roman numeral. We will use capitalized roman numerals for Major chords and lowercase roman numerals for minor chords. The chords for a major key for example would be I ii iii IV V vi vii(diminished). The last chord is diminished because it consists of two minor thirds, where a normal minor chord is a minor third with a major third on top.

So if we are to find diatonic chords in the Key of D we would have D Major, E minor, F# minor, G Major, A Major, B minor, and C# diminished. So you can see it is a diminished chord because C# to E is a minor third while E to G is also a minor third. This chord isn’t really used much in practice. Any chord progression in the key of D is likely to include a mix of these chords.

The most commonly used will be the I IV and V in a major key, some other chords like the ii and vi might be thrown in for flavor. For minor keys it can be a little more complicated to explain. Especially considering the fact that minor tunes in Irish trad aren’t really minor at all but they are in the dorian mode. This is a scale that is based off the second scale degree. So the dorian mode of D Major is E dorian. While the relative minor to D major is actually B minor. Both relationships are found in trad music.

In the dorian mode the most commonly used chords are the ii (which is actually the tonic, so an E minor chord in Edorian) and the I (D Major) chord below it. You might occassionally find a modified vi which has it’s middle note raised a half step to act as a V chord, because it is a fifth away from ii, which is our tonic in the dorian mode.

Re: Chords by Ear

So what he is doing is listening to the melody and then playing whichever chord he thinks fits with whatever is going on with the melody. In any given tune there will be certain passages that sound best with a given diatonic chord for that key, because that passage consists of many of the same notes that are found in that chord. So the first measure of Cooley’s Reel has a lot of E’s and B’s in it. Those are the first and fifth scale degrees of an E minor chord. So a reasonably experience backer can tell we are in the mode of E dorian and are starting with a ii chord. The next chord is likely to be a I (D major). So sure enough in the second half of the second measure there is a D and an A and in the following measure there are all the notes (D, F and A) for a D major chord. So the backer can hear that and transition to the D major chord.

Re: Chords by Ear

A couple of points from a fiddler who also plays guitar:

1) Think of the guitar as a rhythm machine first and a chord machine second. You could, for instance, just find the key of the tune and damp the strings and use your right hand to help keep the rhythm.

2) But also, the advice here to sit out one or two times around is good. If you can’t get comfortable with the tune, don’t play at all.

3) The first thing I would listen for is the five chord, or V chord, talked about here. This is typically the chord that resolves the music, so pretty much every tune will at some point hit the V chord on its way back to the I, which is the Key you’re in. So if you’re in G, you’ll be listening for the D chord.

4) The next thing I’d listen for would be a flat 7th, or VII. These show up a lot, so you should be able to hear them . This is when the tune goes from A to G, for instance, or D to C -- that kind of thing. If it’s in the tune, then you should hear it and figure out a way to cover it -- either that or don’t play.

5) Probably the next thing you should know -- and excuse me if you already know this -- is about relative minors. Every major key has one -- for G it’s Em; for C it’s Am, etc. So if the tune starts and is in a minor key, like say Em, then you can expect the V chord and the flat VII to be the same as the relative major. If the tune is in Em, for instance, you can treat the main chords as if it were in G, the relative major. So the V chord would be D, and the flat VII would be F.

6) Now if the tune hasn’t ended (kidding -- you can do this in a flash once you get used to it), you listen for the IV chord, which is the other big chord in a typical progression. So if a tune is in G, you’ll expect to resolve on the D, but you may also hear some C in there -- or, any of the relative minors of those chords.

7) Back to the main idea -- rhythm. Keeping the rythym is the most important thing you can do -- even if it means droning on the key of the tune (which I’ve heard). And keeping rhythm means being very sensitive to the melody players -- follow them, don’t try to lead them.

Tony O’Rourke: “I’m still bewildered as to how you can work out the chords to a tune that you can’t work out what the key is. Amazing!”

It’s just useful to have some warning of where to go next. If you know a tune well, you can often recognise it from the first 3 notes or so (although, logically, this should not be possible), so there’s no problem. But for a less familiar tune, the key of a tune is sometimes not obvious (to me, at least) until the 2nd or 3rd bar. Of course, there’s nothing to say that a guitarist can’t drop out for a few bars and sneak back in.

I would also say that, for someone who is still developing their backing skills (i.e. most backers), having a chord to start from gives the tune a harmonic context, which makes all the other chords obvious. If you start on a wrong chord, it can take a while to get back on track.

The weak link is where the person calling out the key has no idea about keys.

Re: Chords by Ear

Sarah, When you see someone calling a key out to an accompanist, most times what happens is that the accompanist is not working the rest out on the fly. Instead, they have been given a start that gets them to a point, a measure or two into the tune, where they say, “Oh, I know that one.” They accompany the tune well because they are familiar with it, maybe not note for note, but at least the basic shape of it. What looks to the outsider like an ability to do things impromptu is really the product of a lot of hard work and familiarity with the tunes and the music. There is some stuff being done on the fly, but not as much as people think.
It is like a jazz musician improvising a solo. While it is different every time, they are not just throwing things out willy nilly. They have practiced this solo many times, and are very familiar with the changes, or chord pattern, that they are playing over. Accompanying Irish music is kind of that in reverse. Where in jazz the changes are the fixed point the melody circles around, in Irish music is the melody that is the fixed point that the chord structure circles around.
The advice to focus on your fiddle is not negative, it is actually good advice. While accompanying this music is fun, in my opinion, the melody is even more fun. And you are far more likely to be welcome walking into a session with a melody instrument than with a rhythm instrument.
If you do decide to pursue accompaniment, I would recommend taping yourself playing a tune a number of times over and over at a good steady but modest pace. Then sit down with your guitar. Find single notes that fit the melody, then find chords. Before long, you will start recognizing common turns to the melody that point you into the right chord at the right time. You can learn something from reading chords in tune books, but far more by using your ear.
Best wishes, and enjoy the music in every form you choose to pursue it!

While the practice of assigning keys and modes to tunes is a useful approximation, it only hints at the shape of the tunes, and there is a glorious variety of shapes they can take, hinting at one mode or another here and there, marching through arpeggios and runs that take you to places both expected and unexpected. The basics of melody and harmony can be grasped over the course of months, but the subtle aspects of the music can take a lifetime to master, and a lifetime to enjoy.
And, while many can accompany the tunes adequately (and that is where I count myself), there are only a few who make their accompaniment a thing of beauty. As Mark Twain once said, “The difference between the right word and the almost right word is the difference between lightning and a lightning bug.”

Re: Chords by Ear

In my experience, in some traditions it is common practice to announce the initial tonal centre (as opposed to key) of a set of tunes and then any change of tonal centre, regardless of the skill level of the accompanist(s). This is particularly true in Cape Breton where you might get half a dozen tunes in F and then a change to G (or C or Bb). Since the lead player is always a fiddler he is able to announce this change (not so easy for a flute player).
In Scotland some players that I know will always pre warn of changes (again, regardless of the skill level of the accompanist(s). I think it is done out of courtesy and respect rather than a lack of confidence in the accompanist(s). But, I should mention, the accompanist would be expected to hear the mode.

The rule of not accompanying when you don’t know the tune is often hammered home on this site. But in my experience that is not what the melody players I accompany expect. They expect me to play along even when I don’t know the tune (and how else are you going to find out what does or doesn’t work). As I was told recently, you always get a second run through the tune to make things right. Most of the time I get by with only a few bloopers here and there. It’s all about being able to hear the melody and hear what you are playing.

Re: Chords by Ear

Originally, I learned and played by ear, it wasn’t until a few years later ( this was 40+ years ago), that I began to study and learn music theory. Some guitarists (and teachers) learn or pick up on the concept of figured bass. This was a pattern dating to the Baroque period when bass lines were seldom written out. A series of Roman Numerals were written above the melody line with the understanding that the accompaniment would consist of an ad liberato bass line. Done right, it could also be used for the other voicing by it’s designation ( I - VII for major chords, i - vii for the relative minors). Once I learned and practiced this, it became easier to add a chord structure to a melodic line. Since most of the Irish music we have consists only of the melody with no chords structure written. After some experimentation, you learn to begin leaving out chords (or adding in) to compliment the melody. The goal is not to detract from that main line, but support it.

Sorry, Chuck Cochrane but that’s not at all how Baroque figured bass worked.(works) The bass is written out, not “ad liberato.” The numbers, Arabic, not Roman, designate the intervals above the bass, which in turn defines the chord structure-i.e. 6/4 figured below a C bass note would indicated a 6th and a 4th above the C- an A and F respectively which is an F chord in 2nd inversion.
