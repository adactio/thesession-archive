Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

A wee nod to The Specials there, for those of you not “Too Young” to remember.
;-)

Basically, this is a wee discussion about whether it is a good idea to attempt to expand your repertoire too quickly and try to learn an excessive amount of tunes (and songs, if you prefer) in a very short time. Worse still, wanting to “try out” even more new material when you haven’t fully mastered what has gone before.

Of course, I think we’ve all gone through this phase up to a point, especially those of us who started playing as adults, and then we “level out” a little. Nowadays, I’m much more selective although, even now, I always have a few tunes on the “back burner” so to speak.

For those of us who attend or have attended classes and workshops, there always seems to be a “hunger” for more and more. However, the better tutors will tend to concentrate on just one or two new tunes etc in a session rather than “over load”.
In my opinion, this is a better arrangement. I remember the late Angus Grant Junior advising us that if we wanted to play all the common session tunes, we should just learn them ourselves. Wise words.

Thankfully, in traditional music, we are usually taught to walk before we can run. Not so with some other genres. e.g. ukulele groups, community choirs, even some fiddle societies etc where the participants are presented with countless tunes and songs with no focus on any one particular item. My preferred approach would be to focus on one or two at a time until we got things mostly right and then move on after that. However, everyone seems to be so impatient these days or they get bored very easily.

Anyway, I’m sure there will be a variety of views out there. What would your preference or “happy medium” be?

Re: Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

I think a certain critical mass of tunes is probably necessary (beneficial, at least) to get a solid grasp of Irish Traditional Music in general. Exactly how many tunes that ‘critical mass’ comprises, I could not say – somewhere from 50 to 300, perhaps?

I went through the phase that Johnny Jay mentions in my early-mid 20s, acquiring c. 700-800 tunes in the space of about 4 years (a fellow musician I met in Cork once described me as a ‘tunaholic’ – nothing to do with oily fish). Knowing myself, I can say that there would have been nothing to gain from curbing my enthusiasm for tune acquisition – I would just have felt deprived of what I most desired in life at that time. Of course, to claim that I ‘learned’ all those tunes would be delusional – I spent the next 25 years learning to play them (a process which is still ongoing – along with the much more gradual and selective acquistion of more tunes); but I could say that I was ‘primed’ in them, to the point where my fingers can find the notes fairly easily when called upon to do so.

But had I not had that urge (which not everyone does), that would not necessarily make me a lesser musician, and quite possibly a better one.

Re: Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

Good call Jonny Jay, interesting point.

I agree with you on most of that. I have found that much trad ‘teaching’ (and it’s of very variable quality, even from the top guys) seems to revolve around ‘rote’ learning tunes – sometimes to the point of literal distraction.

I assume that the idea is that if you learn enough tunes, you pick up the technique along the way. Indeed, there seems to be something in it – there is nothing like tackling a difficult tune for making your fingers face up to things they can’t do. But as a teacher of 40 years, I have always been unable entirely to set aside my educator’s eye on what is going on, and in pedagogical terms, such a random approach is, one might - ahem! - say – “traditional”. The necessary learning is a happy accident. Probably entirely congruent with the origins of traditional music, from a place and time when the process of learning was less understood?

I’ve become a little irritated with workshops that take this approach, authentic though it may be. In this day and age, it ought to be possible to do the meta-cognition and concentrate on the skills for their own sake. Indeed, I’ve tried to push some workshops in the direction of taking a familiar tune and just concentrating on the skills of rendering it well. It has received some sympathetic hearings – but also gets shot in the foot when you find that everyone in the room has a different version of the tune you’re supposed to be working on!

As for quantity of tunes, sure that pushes against learning them properly. But perhaps there is an in-built imperative in that respect – because I guess it’s fairly common to go to a session and find you don’t know “enough” tunes. So always the pressure to learn more. These days, I am more selective – I reckon I have about 300 decent tunes – not many at all by some standards, but mostly playable reasonably well. And why learn tunes you not keen on?

Re: Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

irational: “sure you can learn a lot of tunes, but just play the main notes and no lift, but why?”

I don’t think there is necessarily a causal link between learning a lot of tunes and playing without lift (or any other desirable musical quality). As Ian Stock says, learning a lot of tunes (however many ‘a lot’ may be) can help develop technique – and why not also those other qualities? There is, of course, the danger that one might lose sight of the real purpose of playing music and focus *solely* on increasing one’s repertoire, neglecting those other aspects. But for me, the almost insatiable hunger for new tunes was part (not all) of what fuelled my enthusiasm for this music; had I spent those years trying to perfect 10 or 20 tunes, I suspect I would quickly have got bored and ended up playing a handful of tunes poorly and without much joy, rather than playing a few hundred to a moderately competent level and deriving great pleasure from the ability to play with others.

But horses for courses… We are not all driven by the same things.

Re: Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

It’s not an either/or situation. It’s yes/and.

To become a decent trad session player, you’ll want enough technique to play the tunes with pulse, lift, proper intonation, and reasonable tone. AND you’ll need a quiver brimming with tunes, several hundred at the minimum.

Of course it takes time to develop the skills to play with pulse, lift, etc. And time to learn a raft of tunes. There are few shortcuts, but persistence in both areas makes further gains easier.

To be a musician is to live in bliss in the moment of playing music and a constant state of mild angst over the desire to improve and expand expressive technique AND one’s store of tunes.

A never-ending stream of tunes. So deep a dive into bowing, fingering, breathing, etc. Only so many hours in a day….

So what does “learning a tune” really mean, anyway?

The whole idea of “learning a tune” then “applying the stuff” seems to imply that for some players, they have frozen instances of tunes in their memory they learned earlier in their musical adventure, then thaw those instances, apply new “stuff”, and then re-freeze them as a new version of a tune.

This is not my experience with learning this music.

Whether now or 30 years ago, I learn the framework of tunes and then play them with appropriate style, swing, and ornamentation created in the moment.

Over the years my distinctions in style, swing, and ornamentation have changed and gotten more detailed increasing the tools at my disposal when playing, but the fundamentals of how I learn the tunes hasn’t.

Re: Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

This is a potentially thorny subject, because people are different. There are compelling examples of people on both ends of the spectrum… There are certainly examples of people that play a lot of tunes, but don’t play them well. It’s harder to find an example of someone who doesn’t play many tunes, but play the ones they know masterfully, and that’s because part of our knowledge about how to express our tunes eloquently comes from being exposed to a wide variety of tunes, keys, and rhythms, as well as experience from playing them. But I have met some newer players that play beautifully, so this is not a black and white situation…

What seems to have worked well for me over the last 25 years is all the pieces put together. Learn a lot of tunes, listen to a ton of the music, play it regularly with people that are better than me, rinse and repeat. And a lot of my progress as a player was driven by my “hunger for more”… It certainly helps that I dove in hard and immersed myself in the tradition. I don’t actively listen to any music other than Irish trad. I also spent a lot of time learning techniques at camps and workshops, but for me, what ties it all together is really playing the music with other people regularly. So there are certainly people that don’t dive into the tradition as hard as I did, or don’t live in a community that allows them to play as much as I do, etc.

On the topic of workshops, I tend to think of classes/lessons and workshops as being different things and serving somewhat different purposes. A lesson or a class is usually instrument-specific, helping guide the students into specific techniques for playing the music on their particular instrument. But most of the workshops I have been to, unless specified otherwise, have been more general. I don’t mind a little tune overload from a workshop like that, especially if it’s from a well respected player. I then tend to associate those tunes with that teacher. And we all tend to have recording devices in our pockets these days, so there’s no real need to feel overwhelmed, because you can always go back to parts of it later.

So in my view, the “happy medium” is a balancing act for each player to decide for themselves, hopefully with some gentle guidance from other, more experienced, players.

Re: Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

“What would your preference or “happy medium” be?” (FWIW: This question applies differently for someone that’s learning their instrument compared to someone who is already adept at their instrument.)

Listening through all of the music you intend to learn, but only actively working on a few tunes at a time. For example, at one point I had a playlist of several tracks with about a dozen tunes that I was ready to learn, but I would only work on a few at a time.

The thing about trying to learn a lot of music at once is diminishing returns. During any given practice session, you’re only going to retain and reinforce so much information, while over time, your brain is constantly recycling “outdated” information for “relevant”(repeated) information. What this means for learning music in bulk is, by the time you get to that last tune in the list, you’ve already lost something from the first tune you were working on. Now, when you get to your next practice session, you have to relearn things you should know already because what you learned before never set in for the long term.

Every tune needs it’s own learning space. Whether that space is just simple exposure and visitation(hearing it and trying it), or dedicated effort and deep study(learning new techniques and skills), every tune requires something. When you finally have that “something” figured out, now it’s time to move on to something new; Or else you end up with a repertoire full of partially learned tunes.

Not to mention, you never really have to learn tunes in bulk. If you’re learning 3 tunes a week, which I would consider plenty, I think you would be more hard pressed trying to find new learning material, at least at a certain point?

Over the years I’ve seen a kind of pattern to learning just about any new activity. I’ll use one analogy. Let’s say you decide to take up fishing (fly or otherwise, they’re just about the same thing). Your first goal is to catch “a” fish. By the way, here is where you start to spend money on a constant turnover of new equipment … or instruments. Next you want to catch a lot of fish. @Johnny Jay. I notice no-one has responded to the point tucked away in your first post and to the fore in your second - gatherings where people are presented with a batch of tunes or songs to go through. Probably not surprising considering the population here.

I can see that some people who know their instrument/voice well and who can sight read/sing correctly might like that as a way of trying out new things. Not fitting into that category (and being self-conscious about major blunders) I am content to leave that, as a regular thing, to others.

However, workshops I have found worth an hour of my time at festivals are where someone does a ‘taster’ workshop for trad tunes from a particular region or country with dots available at the start or in advance. The skilled dot readers get to hear the leader play with the stylistic features that dots don’t show. Those of us more used to learning by ear and being able to muddle through better the first time with the help of dots get to hear the flavour without the ‘classical types’ blasting through with the wrong rhythm.

I have been to workshops, given by trad musicians people here will all have heard of, that were organised by and for local non-trad players as an introduction to traditional music. They were under-subscribed so the word went out a bit wider than first intended. That gave an interesting perspective. Seeing Irish jigs transcribed with loads of slurs written in (and fiddle bowing marked) for non trad players gave me mental overload until I realised it was more or less what I was used to hearing and doing. There was also a lot of ‘oh I didn’t know you played an instrument’ between people who knew one another in other contexts.

Re: Too Much Too Young(Or soon)

I used the term “back burner” a few times in posts here.

In some ways, it is a little bit like a gas/electric cooker. There’s only so many tunes that you can keep cooking perfectly on the main hob at any one time. The number varies from chef to chef or player to player.

Others may still be on the back burner and not quite ready to come to the fore. Some may have already been cooked or prepared and will require to be “reheated”.

So, there is certainly a distinction between the number of tunes which one can play well at any one time and those that they may have “learned” over the years.
Some haven’t been cooked properly in the first place, others may have gone cold but can easily be reheated. Some may require quite a bit of defrosting while a small few may have gone “off” altogether and you have to learn them again from scratch.
