I switched from English to Anglo concertina when I was over fifty. I now play only the Anglo and I love it. I have never regretted making the switch. The Anglo is a never-ending source of delight and frustration.

Money spent on a cheap Stagi is money wasted, IMHO.

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

A lot depends on what kind of music you want to play.

Speaking as an English concertina player:

It was (and still is) important to me to be able to play in any key. Loads of good tunes are in keys outside the range of many Anglos. (as has been commented before, there should be a special punishment for those who write great tunes but set them in keys outside the range of many diatonic instruments).

I have a Jackie treble which I use for outdoor events since it is worth far less than any reasonably playable 40 or 48 key English.

I was fortunate that the music shop I bought it from allowed me to try their entire stock to find the one with the best action. It is not as good as a well-kept (or restored) vintage box, but the price difference makes a compelling argument.

Another concertina player I know, who now has a vintage box, started on a Jackie and when they had got to the moving on stage they were able to sell the Jackie for what they had paid.

On a different aspect: is there anyone local to you who would be prepared to lend you an English to see how you get on?

Hope this helps

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

It’s a question of rhythm and phrasing. A lot depends on the player, but in general:
English concertina plays like a piano accordion. Legato, fluid, rather than bouncy.
Anglo concertina plays like a button accordion. Punchy, dynamic rhythm.
If you want pure drop ITM than the Anglo is for you. For other music the English might be more suitable.

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

If you want to play tunes on a concertina yourself, I think the Anglo would be the way to go. I have a Morse 30 button anglo in C/G and really like it. I started out with a single row (10 button) melodeon in D, but wanted to expand my range and went for the Anglo and enjoy it immensely. The in and out aspect of the bellows isn’t too difficult really. Technique isn’t difficult, it’s mostly just a matter of knowing which button to push and whether to push or pull. That’s pretty easy! Haha- being a little facetious but basically the techniques are simple it’s just learning the tunes on the thing. Probably because I started on a melodeon, I do think of the concertina as linear-- I imagine a two row melodeon (plus a row of accidentals) that has been turned horizontally, broken in half, and then bent around so the lower register buttons are upside down, and the higher register buttons are rightside up… That probably sounds dumb and is probably not helpful at all, but it’s how I think of it, rather than thinking of left vs right hand. Anyways, I’d say if the concertina is for you to play tunes on, and Anglo is a better fit. The 30 button Anglo concertina in C/G (that is a middle row in C and the inner row in G, with an outer row in accidentals and other notes {Bb, G#, C#, etc}) is basically chromatic. Really any key a traditional tune might be in is probably going to be playable on it. E major might be a struggle, but everything else isn’t bad. Very different to play than a violin, but I think learning won’t be particularly difficult.

On the other hand, if the concertina is going to be more for your husband who plays piano, and it’s going to be something he accompanies you on, then maybe an English concertina would work better for him. I don’t play English, but they might make sense to a piano player.

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

Personally I can’t understand the Irish love of C/G anglo concertinas, seeing as how half their tunes are in D, but I guess it’s because C/G anglos were what was available and cheap 100+ years ago, so it’s tradition. The English concertina would have been much more expensive and less available then.
The English does have the advantage, to a trained musician, of being nominally “logical”, with the two outer rows of buttons, the black ones in economy models, playing either the sharp or flat of the white button inboard of it, making the first four sharp and flat keys “easily” available. On the other hand, like piano accordions, it is the skill of the players’ dexterity that will give the bounce and lilt needed in the music, not the in/out pumping on the anglo.

I don’t play either anglo or english concertina, but I do play accordion where the same choice between bisonoric and unisoric exsists.

Bisonoric instruments are lighter and have an automatic bounce to them due to the change of bellows directions. Think of it in a similar way as changing directions with your bow on the fiddle, but then forced. If you play piano accordion or a continental one it’s much heavier to do these bellows reversals as the instrument is heavier. As such, you need to get the bounce fom another place.

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

“One thing I’m wondering - as the Anglo is not as ‘logical’ as the English, but yet it still remains very popular, why is that?”
Lots of people have talked already about the rhythm or bounce of an Anglo. A push-pull instrument can be a dancing partner! You’re partly using larger muscle groups and the push pull system does seem to help players achieve better phrasing almost unconsciously than do the “unisonorics” - English concertina and pianobox.
In Irish music it seems that the availability of cheap German push-pull instruments helped establish that as THE system on which The Music was played.
In the English tradition it’s much easier to add chords and get a really loud punchy sound suitable for outdoor Morris dancing etc on an Anglo than on an English.

BUT if your husband is a pianist and wants to play a mix of music, find an English.

It’s interesting that new Anglos of traditional construction are being made, although they cost £3000 plus.
At the moment the supply of decent vintage Englishes is such that it’s not worth making new ones.

When my partner bought her English from Chris Algar he said that if and when she traded up, he’d allow what she’d paid for this one against the next. She’s still happy with it.

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

You can learn to use the bellows in a more effective way with an English concertina, and some players do just that. It is nice that you don’t have to think about that with an anglo or a melodeon, and it does add extra interest, but with thought and practice you can also do that on an English. It seems, my experience, that those with any classical training, or keyboard ~ piano, piano accordion, etc. ~ tend to favour and prefer the English system, the whole interval and chord correlation of the keys also adding to their preference, thirds and fifths in line, while those who work more on inspiration, including players of the harmonica and related diatonic instruments, prefer getting familiar with an anglo. That’s ‘in general’. Also, if you want the same choices and variety of keys an English provides, that too attracts some. I love both, in the right hands. I don’t care much for the general legato style of playing of piano accordions and how some English concertina players tend to take things, which has had me carrying some bias against them ~ but I have learned that isn’t the fault of the instrument. I think it is best to get some experience with both, give them some time, and see what speaks to you, let you heart tell you, but only after you can manage a tune or more on each. Give it time. A decent instrument isn’t cheap…

Best of luck!

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

The standard advice over on concertina.net is that you can’t make this choice purely intellectually. The potential player really needs to get their hands on each type and try them. Some folks are OK on either (they work for me); others find one crazy and love the other (take your pick). Something about how your brain is wired up, and it varies. At C.net most folks don’t try to convert others anymore; we all know it is so individual and the right answer is different for everyone.
Shops or festivals are good places to try different systems of concertina (what part of the world you are in dictates how easy this is).
A symphony trumpet in the US has a home key of Bb. The last standard metal Boehm flute I tried appeared to be in D (or a diatonic scale in some such key; I didn’t check) if you just played up the main holes. These instruments play in all keys. I don’t find D on C/G anglo at all challenging. But then I’ve never progressed on any instrument without practicing scales, a lot - teaches you the fingerings (I know not everyone here agrees with that either, I guess it is individual again!). Piano players know this very well, it seems to me.
If you want to talk concertinas, drop by concertina.net, the folks there will talk (or type) your ears off.

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

Anglos are logical (although this doesn’t apply to the accidentals row), it’s just a different logic to the English.

You struggled with the push-pull aspect of the anglo, but don’t seem to have given yourself enough time to get to grips with it if you took it back after only one day. For my part, I find the English difficult because the scale alternates from one hand to the other. However all instruments are difficult and unnatural to begin with, it takes time to get used to them and then it feels natural. I’m an anglo player, but I wouldn’t try to push you in that direction if it doesn’t suit you.

You say you love the sound, but which players most attract you to the instrument? If you want to be able to reproduce their sound, you should choose the same system they play.

The other consideration is the type of music you want it for, although this isn’t necessarily the clincher - despite what some say, both Irish and morris can be played on English. However if you want to play fully chromatic music or in a wide range of keys then English or perhaps duet might be the better choice. A 30-key anglo is nominally chromatic but can only be easily played in a few keys, although with practice most things are possible.

As KenC suggested, the place to go for advice is concertina.net where you will be able to contact players of all systems and who probably play a wider range of music and styles then you’ll find here. In Irish music there is a strong bias towards the anglo, played in a particular way.

Re: Tell me about concertinas…

Lots of helpful advice here but you’d get a good overview at concertina.info followed by concertina.com not to mention concertina.net (which someone has already suggested).
If your husband is a pianist, he might even prefer one of the duet systems (a Crane or McCann, not a Jeffries): these are chromatic but allow you to play a tune on the right hand and accompaniment on the left. One of the confusing things about the English system is that, if you try to play more than just a melody, the tune and accompaniment both keep switching hands.
Otherwise, I would tend to recommend English for sight-readers and anglo for ear-players (gross generalisation, I know). Mind you, check out what’s possible on a big anglo by listening to Dapper’s Delight on youtube.
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F4M1hrueZ1g is a nice clip.
