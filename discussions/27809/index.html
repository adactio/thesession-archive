Re: Strathspey vs. Reel?

Yes and no. It’s quite common in scotland but normally the change from strathspey into a reel would feature a different tune.

That said, there are many strathspeys that work as reels once you lose the dots and even out the timing, so this type of change isn’t that uncommon. Works the other way round as well. Reel started off as a strathspey changing into reel time.

A common trick is to start your strathspeys off slowly, building up speed as you go. Once you get the final strathspey going as fast as manageable the change into a reel gives the impression of an additional boost in pace as you set off into some rip roaring reels. The change into a reel rhythm of the same tune can make for a lovely lovely change and adds a certain dynamic.

Re: Strathspey vs. Reel?

The strathspey-then-reel combination was used for certain dances - much of Kerr’s “Merry Melodies” (from the 1880s) is laid out that way. Not very common now.

“The King’s Reel” was written at the same time as the strathspey “King George IV”, as a companion piece to it.

The march-strathspey-reel thing is a solo display idea, not for dancing - it came along a bit later. Sometimes there’s a slow air at the start. It’s popular in Scottish sessions.

Re: Strathspey vs. Reel?

March Strathspey Reel (MSR for short) is the primary form of music used for piping competitions -- particularly bands.

As far as I’ve been able to tell, MSR originated in solo piping competitions. There is a reference to winning “March, Strathspey, and Reel” by piper Archie MacNeill in 1897. It’s not clear to me if he’s speaking of an MSR or if he played them as three events.

Re: Strathspey vs. Reel?

Highland clearances=emigration of Scots to North America=settlements in Cape Breton. They took the music, culture and the language (Scots Gaelic) with them and it exists there to this day.

There has been a recent “rediscovery” of this music in Scotland, although some players visited these shores and Ireland in the past (e.g. Dan R Macdonald).

Some have commented that Cape Breton music is what Scottish music used to sound like, but for me that‘’s simplistic. It seems unlikely there would have been no evolution in Cape Breton too. Also there are other influences, such as Irish music, alive in Cape Breton too.

n the context of Scottish Country Dance, Strathspeys and Reels can be played together as a set - in what is called a Medley. Listening to it played in this way can point out the distinctions between the two types of tunes.

It’s been fairly uncommon that a Scottish Country Dance group would ask us to play a Strathspey/Reel Medley, but we’ve done it. The set is usually played this way:

Strathspey #1/Reel#1, Strathspey #1/Reel#1
Strathspey #2/Reel#2, Strathspey #2/Reel#2
Strathspey #3/Reel#3, Strathspey #3/Reel#3
Strathspey #1/Reel#1, Strathspey #1/Reel#1

Or David Johnson’s point, once “Strathspey reels”. Ta for that quote.

The point I’d make, more of a caveat really, is that one must take into account the wider diversity that existed and the looser use of terminology used to describe, tune/dance types, as they existed before the late 1800’s and early 1900’s; when these things started to become of interest to academics and other interested parties (royal and various other interest societies, think; the attitudes of gaelic revivalists versus that of the peasantry who’s use of the language was unbroken, Flan O’Brian’s “Am Beul Bocht” captures this well, all be it through the medium of satire). It must be acknowledged that there were one or two more locally astute collectors at work during this period but even these collectors exhibit, in some instances, considerable variation in treatment.

In short there is the very real possibility that the terminology meant different things to different people from the period when we had a wide spread peasantry, unenclosed or uncleared, who played and danced to strathspey-reels. Unfortunately none of these people are around to ask, or would have been around to ask by those interested when the study of this medium became fashionable. Some ancestors remained of course but the process of gentrification and generic standardization would have been well under way by then, presenting a significant gap.

Cape Breton isn’t a good snapshot in time to use either and not just because of their own musical evolution in the time since arrival; rather the fact that those scots who did settle there (and other parts of the americas) were not a representative selection of the peoples who would have shared in the use of the terminology. The settlement of that area featured emigrants from specific areas of the west & western islands, in several waves over an extended period of time. So not representative of scots music from the period, rather “their” music from their “area” from the time.

So here we are with our generic terms for tune type in the “modern” traditional scots cannon, left wondering how these tune types evolved too those we have now.

Personally, I think we’ll never really know any exact answers simply because, as I suspect, things were far more fluid than they are today. Simply looking at the specifics of regional styles, I mean that in the micro sense rather than in the more commonly understood wider regional sense; North East, West Coast, Perthshire, Strathspey etc. Simply put there is ample evidence still in existence of distinct variation in style from within these established style regions. It’s on it’s way out, but that seems to be going largely unnoticed to all but, academics and individual music enthusiasts (1800’s revisited).

Again, gaelic is a good example, it’s generic “BBC” gaelic thats making a comeback. Much of the unique subtle regional variety lost. Not as a result of the revival, rather as a result of the very need for a revival in the first place. The various scots trad music initiatives are to be applauded of course, as is the reemergence of gaelic, but there is a generic element to style that seems to be passing all but the very interested, or the locally patronage’d, by. Does it matter? Not really, in the greater scheme of things, but it’s something to be aware of.

I’m no expert, for from it. I’m not even a particularly good player or gaelic speaker. But I’ve traveled the north west highland & some islands getting tunes with people enough to know that tunes receive different treatment from area to area, even glen to glen. I can only imagine that these glens and the areas now empty in between would be different again had the last 250 years not happened.

I’d be looking to Puirt a Beul and piping for the clues (rather than fiddling traditions) to catch the odd chink of light in the shadows.

Re: Strathspey vs. Reel?

In highland piping, reels are 2/4. Strathspeys are written in 4, and it causes great difficulty for learners, because they’re really played in 2. Wm. Marshall in the late 1700s published some reels in 2 and some in 4.

It’s been a long time since I read Johnson’s book, Scottish Fiddle Music in the 18th Century, but the gist of it is that Strathspeys and Reels were developed into their modern forms in the middle of the 18th c. in an urban environment by professional musicians. It’s a well documented history. The dances traveled from the cities to the country. It was sophisticated culture. Most of the best fiddlers of the day were also orchestral musicians.

Despite what has been implied above (>> recent “rediscovery” <<), highland piping is an unbroken tradition. Fiddle probably is too, for that matter. It might be true that the music has gotten more popular in wider circles, but it was never lost to the people who play it.

Re: Strathspey vs. Reel?

Unfortunately, pipers were not musically literate, in general, until well into the 1800s. Fiddlers, on the other hand, were writing things in manuscript for at least a hundred years prior.

There’s definitely something about the student/teacher relationships in piping that seem to be different. I know my teacher’s teacher learned from the legendary “Bob’s of Balmoral”, Bob Nicol and Bob Brown.

And what my teacher teaches me is the same as what her teacher taught, which is what the Bob’s taught -- how to win competitions. Piping, especially in light music (everything but piobaireachd) has been changing. The rate of change is fast enough that the individual sees it. We don’t, by and large, sit around and talk about the past. No one really cares how the great Willie Ross played tunes. What we care about is what we’re going to play for our next competition, and how we’re going to change things to try to do better. Especially in bands.

The point is, looking at today’s piping and trying to extrapolate backwards in time is pointless.

Going back to those 18th c. (mid to late 1700s) urban fiddlers, I think it has been demonstrated, as well as such things can be, they they were, by and large, the source of the music. They didn’t go out and collect stuff, they wrote it. There were book publishers who went out and collected stuff and published it. It’s easy to compare the stuff written by the known composers and the rest; by and large, it’s the first group who’s music is still played by fiddlers today.

Edinburgh, at the time, was the center of enlightenment. There was a lot f culture and a lot of education, and it’s what these guys did. You can see it in their letters -- they were trying to one-up each other, for bragging rights. “say, did you hear the new tune William McGibbon wrote.” That sort of thing. They were pushing the envelope, using their professional training as musicians, to create the new popular music of the day.

In examining the written record, Mr. Johnson has found that at some point in time, the term “Strathspey reel” was first used, and it’s not early. I’m going from memory, but I’m thinking mid 1700s for Strathspey reel. The written record for reels goes back to around 1700. Prior to that there were reels, but they weren’t very interesting; by and large all that old material was supplanted by newer, better tunes. Anyway, at some point, they drop the word “reel” from the end of Strathspey reel. The form of the strathspey can be observed evolving. In the first collected examples there wasn’t much to differentiate a Strathspey from a reel, but over time that chagnes -- particularly when William Marshall started writing them. He was brilliant.

One must remember that the period of time in question is known as The Enlightenment. People of the time valued reason and learning. It wasn’t until the Romantic period that people rejected those principals for the ideal of the noble savage.

Re: Strathspey vs. Reel?

“ The form of the strathspey can be observed evolving. In the first collected examples there wasn’t much to differentiate a Strathspey from a reel, but over time that chagnes -- particularly when William Marshall started writing them. He was brilliant.”

According to both Jack Campin (the one and only) and Fintan Vallely, the first printed examples of ‘strathspey reels’ (two) appearsin Oswald’s Pocket Companion (vol 3) in the 1740s, and they are both titled ‘A New Strathspey Reel’. Both these examples have the familiar semiquavers followed by dotted quavers, and are quite distinct from other familiar tunes in reel time in the book (The Flowers of Edinburgh, for example, appears a few pages before these strathspey reels, and is much like the version commonly played today). The tunes are reckoned to have been composed by Oswald. The first part of one of them is notated thus:

X:1
T:A New Strathspey
S: Oswald’s Pocket Companion Vol 3
M:4/4
L:1/8
R:Strathspey
K:G
g3/2a/2b3/2a/2 e/2b3/2 b3/2g/2|a3/2g/2b3/2g/2 a3/2g/2 e2|d3/2b/2g3/2b/2 e/2g3/2 B2|d3/2A/2 B3/2G/2 E/2e3/2 e2:|]

I would say that this is quite distinct from a reel - like the aforementioned Flowers of Edinburgh as it appears in the volume. These tunes appeared around the time William Marshall was born.

I’d agree with that, but like to point out that; while the edinburgh urban fiddlers (and contemporaries) you refer to undoubtedly had the whip hand in shaping the tunes we play today, they drew their inspiration from the music already around them, they didn’t invent it but rather they wrote original material based on a familiar body of existing material. As scotland at that time was mainly rural, thats where the influences were largely drawn. Of course much music was on a professional footing at that time, paid for by rich aristocratic patrons and vulgar host alike. People simply didn’t have the leisure time unless wealthy or were paid. The enlightenment did inspire a vigorous acceleration in all aspects of civil society in scotland at the time, history remembers us for it. We took inspiration from outside that shaped the arts and science at home.
