Is it right that the Performing Rights Society should bully pubs into paying them for a licence for hosting sessions?

I jumped onto a 15 year old thread that had raised this question and had some informative replies. But I think it deserves a new thread of its own, as one contributor commented.

I’ve been playing in sessions around the Welsh Borders for about 30 years. Sessions come and go as pub managers change etc. An accepted fact of life.

But recently a pub we’d been playing in for many years has been targeted by the PRS who are threatening to take them to court unless they get an expensive PRS license. The pub isn’t owned by a big brewery, it’s run by a local collective who saved this very old countryside pub from being sold to a commercial developer. Their margins are tight.

Even though we’ve had a monthly session there for many years they’ve had to cancel the sessions under pressure from the PRS. The pub doesn’t have a jukebox, doesn’t play radio or streamed music. The outcome of the PRS hasslement is that we can’t play there any more. One less outlet for playing traditional music. Or for someone to hear it.

The thing is that this is a folk tradition pre-dating the commercialisation of music and a music industry. The PRS attitude is helping to destroy grassroots folk culture by trying to make money from supportive venues without giving anything back.

Re: Is it right that the Performing Rights Society should bully pubs into paying them for a licence for hosting sessions?

Just to get this clear, I’ll reiterate something that was alluded to in the other thread: no performing rights organization that I know of divides a pub’s license fees up based on the music played in that particular pub on some particular night. Generally, there is a formula for “popularity” of a work based on completely unrelated factors such as number of radio plays, streams, and album sales, and a work’s score on that formula determines its share of the total pub and cafe license revenues. (yes, at least in the USA the PROs (Performance Rights Organizations) come after coffee shops - I fought this fight back in the ’90s)

Now, there are nuances. For example, I believe that if you put on a classical music concert and submit the works that are to be performed, the fee for that concert will be allocated directly to the holders of the rights on the works in question. I would imagine that musical theater works on a similar system. But for pubs, the license is just an annual fee which the PROs divvy up on the assumption that the pub’s musical offerings broadly reflect the popular taste. Which is a stupid assumption, of course, since what’s played in pub gigs usually is not the stuff you’re hearing on the radio, but it makes it easy for the PRO.

Re: Is it right that the Performing Rights Society should bully pubs into paying them for a licence for hosting sessions?

PRS is there to enforce the copyright of composers, and this is backed by law. Surely it is right that composers whose works we enjoy playing and listening to should receive some recompense? The system of licensing venues is fairly clunky and imprecise, but it is better than the alternative, which would be to seek permission and negotiate a royalty (or waiver) direct with the composer themselves. That would be unworkable. Plenty of folk musicians are PRS members (full disclosure: including myself) and although I am not a prolific composer the royalties more than justify membership, and certainly for a professional musician for whom they can comprise a significant part of their income.

Unless a session is a bona fide private event which cannot be overheard then it is public performance. The default position is that a licence is required unless only non-copyright (or music by a composer who is not a PRS member) is to be performed. So a performance by a singer-songwriter who is not a PRS member and who will play only their own material will not need a licence. However if they are a PRS member then it will need a licence and they can then claim royalties.

“Traditional” does not mean the composer is unknown. Often tunes which are assumed to be in the public domain are in fact in copyright. At a session, which is by its nature informal and spontaneous, it would be impossible to prevent the playing of a copyright tune, and that would make it very difficult to persuade PRS that no music they control will be played and no licence is required.

Of course the pub should not be bullied by the PRS representative. Unfortunately this seems to happen all too often, and I have personal experience in similar circumstances to the OP, although that was many years ago. If the session is the only music at the pub then Tariff P 3.1.1 would seem to be appropriate, which is £13.66 per session. This does not seem unduly expensive, but if it even that is too much for the pub then surely the musicians should be willing to contribute a small sum each? I wonder whether PRS reps are paid on commission and try to persuade venues to take out more expensive licences than they need to? I suggest the pub applies directly to PRS via its website.

I wonder whether the pub’s cancellation of the session is less about the money than the attitude of the PRS rep? That was the situation in my own case, the landlord wasn’t bothered about the cost but was annoyed by the rep’s attitude and preferred to lose the music rather than give him the satisfaction of winning. It took a great deal of negotiation and diplomacy to save the folk club.

Re: Is it right that the Performing Rights Society should bully pubs into paying them for a licence for hosting sessions?

I see two basic questions here which seem to be getting mixed up with each other in an unhelpful sort of way.

First, there’s the concern about whether a session that performs a repertoire of entirely traditional tunes ought to be the basis for charging a performance license. Notice that I’m stripping away a lot of the noise here, that’s intentional. We can add complications back in, but this is I think the key question that the original post was attempting to address.

Then there’s a concern, which seems to me a bit of a straw man, about whether performance rights organizations should exist at all and should be allowed to levy license fees at all.

I think most people’s intuitive answers are “no” and “yes”, respectively. The trouble comes in managing the complexities, but it’s important to recognize that a “yes” to the second question does not imply a “yes” to the first. Since the justification for organizations like the PRS is about getting composers and songwriters paid for the use of their work, it’s hard to argue that they should be charging for the use of music in the public domain. You can get there, but it takes some around-the-corner sort of arguing, based on the idea that it’s just too complicated to get it right, and that the wrong outcome of charging a venue unjustly is a better wrong outcome than you’d get by not charging them. Again, you can make this argument and you can make it convincing, but it takes a bit more work than we’ve seen so far.

It seems to me that it doesn’t make sense to worry about the corner cases - what if a session plays a copyrighted tune? what if they play a Bothy Band arrangement? what if someone sings a traditional song that has the same tune as a copyrighted song? Does Paddy Fahy’s estate get a check? etc, yadda, and so forth - until you’ve got a clear position on the basic issues, and you understand why you have that position.

From what I’ve seen so far there are a lot of good arguments underneath what’s been said here, but there’s a lot more certainty on all sides than is warranted. Ethical questions are never as easy as we want them to be, but a bit of humility makes them a lot easier to talk about.

Re: Is it right that the Performing Rights Society should bully pubs into paying them for a licence for hosting sessions?

If all the tunes played are public domain, or by composers who aren’t PRS members, then PRS has no jurisdiction. If you can demonstrate that is the case, then if PRS demand a licence fee you can refuse. However, in practice, it is likely that most sessions will include at least one tune which PRS administers.

When a composer joins PRS they assign their rights to collect royalties to the organisation. Whatever their personal views about sessions, they cannot then waive royalties on a one-off basis or for particular types of performance such as sessions. Their FAQs say “Members of PRS can apply to PRS for Music to have royalties waived for a piece across all areas. But they cannot waive rights on a one off basis.” However PRS covers only performing rights and has no jurisdiction over the publication of tunes on The Session.

Yes, the process for distributing licence fees to composers is inefficient, especially for minority music. The best way is to submit a return of the music played to PRS, but that can only be done by the licence holder ie the venue, or by a PRS member. That may work for the music industry, it doesn’t work for pubs where the licence holder has no involvement with the music, and where it is unlikely that any of the musicians are members.

I have suggested that the “small gigs” scheme should be open to all (currently it only allows members to submit their own setlists) but they haven’t understood the question. Nevertheless royalties can and do find their way to the right place. I regularly submit setlists for sessions, and as well as receiving royalties for my own tunes some also goes to composers whose music I play. I can’t see why someone who is not a member should not be able to do this, so that composers can get rewarded. However I have struggled to get PRS to understand what I am talking about.

The issue here is not whether composers should be rewarded for their efforts - I hope we would all agree that they should. Neither is it the cost of the licence, which for these sorts of events is trivial and could easily be covered by the musicians themselves if the venue really cannot afford it. The issue is that some PRS reps are unnecessarily aggressive and confrontational in their approach, and fail to understand that the easiest option for the venue may be to stop hosting music rather than pay for a licence (which probably doesn’t apply to most of the venues they deal with who are in the music business). That sort of approach is not in the interests of PRS or its members if it results in the loss of venues for live music. Again, this is something I’ve struggled to get PRS to understand.

And whether or not you like the way it is applied, this is the law. It is right that pubs should pay PRS for a licence to play music. It is not right that they are bullied into doing so or made to pay more than necessary. The answer is for musicians to educate themselves about copyright and PRS so they can assist the venues where they play, and encourage and help them to engage with PRS rather than stopping hosting music.

In reply to meself, the presumption is that a licence is required. If you think otherwise the onus is on you to demonstrate that no music administered by PRS has been played, so it is in your interests to cooperate with a PRS rep, should they make themselves known.

Gimpy. the system works fine for the vast majority of sessions. Occasionally there is a problem where a particular pub refuses to pay the very modest licence fee and instead stops hosting music. That is regrettable, especially for the session affected by it, but it does not seem to me to be symptomatic of a wider problem, and you can’t expect PRS to find a different way of doing things just for a small niche area which anyway generates low royalties.

There is an issue with the way royalties are distributed. They can only distribute royalties for performances they hear about, but they cannot monitor every performance so rely on samples from selected venues, which disadvantages minority genres. If that is a concern to you, speak to the licence holder of your session and persuade them to submit returns of the music yoy have played to PRS. You’ll have to provide them with all the details of the tunes played, and their composers where this applies, but most sessions have a core of standard tunes. You’ll probably have to offer to handle the paperwork on behalf of the licence holder, as they probably won’t be interested. But that’s the only way PRS can know what you have played and send royalties to the right people. Otherwise the licence fee for your session goes to Ed Sheeran and other millionaires.

It has been many years since I owned any establishments that required music licensing, but I always remember that it was a shock when you got the bills every year. And that may be part of the problem. Paying £710.32 for a year worth of weekly sessions isn’t that bad, but it’s in a lump sum. If it came in at £60 per month, it might not be as much of a shock for the establishment.

In the US, there are multiple licensing entities (ASCAP, BMI, Sesac), and they each will collect fees. The fees are generally based upon what kind of music (recorded, live), how many nights a week, the occupancy of the establishment (whether the establishment ever hits capacity or not), and whether there is a cover charge. So let’s say you have an establishment that can hold 150 people, and you have one session a week (no cover charge), and play recorded music the other nights. That turns out to be $5.37 per occupancy person per year for the live music ($805.50) and $2.49 per occupancy for the recorded music ($373.50), for a total of $1179 per year. But that’s only one of the three licensing authorities, so if you’re talking $3000 a year, and it comes all at the same time, it can be jarring, especially for a small business.

FWIW, I am an ASCAP member, and I know my music has been played on the radio and my compositions have been played in sessions, and I don’t believe I have ever collected a dime from ASCAP. Those royalties may be lumped in with my publishing deal, which distributes to the streaming services as well. But I just looked, and I am receiving less than $5 per year for streaming of my two albums, but there aren’t any line items for licensing royalties, and I’ve never received a direct payment from ASCAP…

Re: Is it right that the Performing Rights Society should bully pubs into paying them for a licence for hosting sessions?

wild.irish, in all likelihood, the pubs you asked to host a session already had a license to cover the music they run over their PA system (radio, streaming) and/or open mics, live music gigs, etc.

Even undertaker premises (“funeral homes” in the US) need a license to play music. And churches too, for music performed other than during the worship service (e.g., weddings).

The goal of compensating composers and arrangers for use of their intellectual property is fine and well. But the system is skewed to favor the chart toppers, not composers of small-market or niche music (who could use the dosh). And license fees are hard to justify for venues that host only casual, informal music like non-gig sessions. So the system inadvertently constrains our opportunities for session spots.

Re: Is it right that the Performing Rights Society should bully pubs into paying them for a licence for hosting sessions?

wild.irish, to pick up on your analogy, if I want to enjoy a cabbage I am expected to pay for it. If I didn’t, I could expect to be harassed by the greengrocer. If I want to enjoy a good tune why should I not be expected to pay the composer to use it?

As musicians we are especially privileged, because we don’t have to pay for it ourselves, even if we are being paid to perform it. It would be unworkable for every individual musician to have to approach a composer for permission to play their tune. Instead the charge is levied on the venues. If we play only non-copyright music then no fee is payable, but most sessions will play some copyright tunes.

No composer is obliged to join PRS, but if they wish to get paid for their work it would cost them far more than the one-off joining fee to agree rights privately and pursue those who didn’t pay. PRS is very good value for anyone who writes and performs their own material.

The tariffs are required by law to be reasonable having regard to the value of the rights, and PRS is overseen by the government through the Intellectual Property Office. I don’t think £13 is particularly unreasonable for an evening’s music for up to 100 people.

It’s true the way royalties are distributed is flawed, but nevertheless even casual composers like myself do get paid. If you want to ensure that the composers of your favourite tunes receive their royalties, make sure that returns are submitted to PRS.

Most premises already have a licence, and PRS is only rarely the reason for a venue not hosting a session, although of course it is regrettable when that happens. We should be far more concerned that more than 400 pubs a year are closing down.
