Re: How does “Old time”, “Bluegrass” and traditional American music relate to the Irish Tradition

My advice if you want to play these genres and not just treat it as an academic study, would be to keep them compartmentalized in different categories even if the notes on paper are the same.

For example, I learned the tune “Merry Blacksmith” in local Irish sessions. A friend of mine learned the tune in local OldTime jams. I have never been able to enjoy the two of us playing the tune together, because he plays it with OldTime dotted note swing feel, and I play it as a straight ahead reel. Bluegrass players will take a hornpipe like “Fisherman’s” and flatten out the rhythm feel so it can be played blazing fast.

The historical associations can be interesting, but when it comes down to actually playing the music, I don’t think the historical links can help inform your playing. You have to dive deep into one or the other.

Re: How does “Old time”, “Bluegrass” and traditional American music relate to the Irish Tradition

In terms of the swing and feel of the rhythms, and how they differ between the two genres, it might be worth checking out The Raineys if you can as an interesting case study.

The Raineys were Irish traveller musicians, I think their only recording is from the 50s. If you listen to their playing the rhythm of some of their tunes can be really unusual to a contemporary ITM ear, much more equivalent to Old Time type playing (to what extent this suggests the existence of a more varied style of play on the island pre recorded music eg Coleman etc and a broader range of traditional sounds…I’ll leave up to the individual).

But seriously if you can find it listen to The Raineys play The Sally Gardens. The rhythm is wildly different to what we’ve come to know (sounds bloody brilliant too).

Re: How does “Old time”, “Bluegrass” and traditional American music relate to the Irish Tradition

There is very little specifically Irish music influence upon Old Time music. Irish immigration was very largely confined to urban areas - “Gangs of New York” gives a Hollywoodized but not unduly so picture of this. The Irish presence in the Appalachian area where Old Time originated was minimal. Linguistic and sociomusicological studies all point to an overwhelmingly English heritage in that region.

Bluegrass is a variant of OT originating in the 1930s Depression, when many people from Appalachia moved to the industrial north in search of work. Guys like Bill Monroe were exposed to Basie and Ellington etc. on the radio and adopted the ensemble/improvised break format and a sense of swing to the music they brought with them.

Re: How does “Old time”, “Bluegrass” and traditional American music relate to the Irish Tradition

In the Canadian portion of the ‘New World’, there are a number of fiddling traditions that overlap to greater or lesser degrees. The most prominent and dominant is ‘Canadian Old Time’ (or ‘Tyme’), which is for all intents and purposes the same as that/those sometimes called ‘Downeast’ or ‘Maritime’ or ‘Messer style’, as in Don Messer. Think Graham Townsend. ‘Canadian Old Time’ is omnivorous and gluttonous in its repertoire, but all the older well-known Irish tunes are basic to its repertoire, along with all the older well-known Scottish and American tunes.

When you go back to the pre-radio and recording times, you find the same tendency in Canadian fiddling generally, with some exception in some ethnically-distinct communities, Cape Breton being the best-known example, with its Scottish Gaelic tradition, although many Irish tunes found their way into that stronghold as well, particularly jigs, which were required for some fashionable forms of square dancing in the early 1900s, if I have that right. Other than that, Irish repertoire was fundamental in the Ottawa Valley area, while in Quebec - well, Jean Carignan (in)famously grumbled that Quebec fiddling consisted of nothing but bastardized Irish tunes. And, of course, anywhere enough Irish settled, their music culture settled with them; the south shore of Newfoundland is a prominent example.

Stylistically, pre-‘revival’ Irish instrumental music in Canada tends to sound more Donegal than Sligo. I don’t believe Coleman et al had much influence in Canada, Carignan notwithstanding, pre-revival.
