Early Music Tunes in your session?

When I was young & attended folk festivals, more years ago than would be politic to admit, early music was often included - Strawhead with Gregg Butler playing his ‘chair leg’ (shawm) was a great favourite at Durham.

Our group recently played a ‘gig’ of early music tunes during the interval of a play based on a medieval theme. It was superb fun - especially as we were able to find some harmony parts to go with Bear Dance & Horse’s Bransle to give it extra pzazz.

These tunes are regularly included in our Sessions, along with Morisco, Jamaica, and other Playford tunes such as Maiden Lane, Newcastle and Portsmouth.

If your Session doesn’t specialise in ITM, do you sometimes play early music tunes? If so, which ones are popular?

Or do you think Sessions should really stick to traditional tunes from the eighteenth/19th/20th/21st centuries, or they become too diffuse or rarefied?

(I tried to find other threads about this on The Session but the earliest one was plugging a website no longer available, alas. https://thesession.org/discussions/22529)

Re: Early Music Tunes in your session?

The trouble with much early music is the challenge of the rhythms. Many modern tunes have complex rhythms but at least we often have You Tube vidoes of the composers demonstrating how they should be played.

The early music that works is usually tunes with a solid rhythm and these are mostly dance tunes, hence the preponderance of Playford. Some of these tunes, unfortunately, are in flat keys and not (generally) suitable for sessions.

Re: Early Music Tunes in your session?

Slight digression: The history of Staines Morris: I’ve been looking further into the history of this piece. I’ve known it for a very long time and there re a lot of different aspects to its use over time.
I first heard the tune as a Christmas Carol called Blessed Be That Maid, Marie on an LP called Make We Merry with the Toronto Mendelsson Choir and the Elmer Isler Singers in the early 1970s. It was there as an instrumental piece by the highly respected harpist Judy Loman.
A lot of people are under the mistaken impression that its first appearance in written form was in Playford in 1651, where it appears as a country dance (and not as a morris). However, the first written notation is in the William Ballet Lute MS of 1590. The words for Blessed Be are from the Sloane MS and date even earlier from the 15th C.
The lyrics: “Come Ye Young Men”, etc. to which is most commonly sung and associated with today are from a Restoration era play called Actaeon and Diana by Robert Cox (1656) and were used for a ”Maypole Song” in that play. It is not known what melody Cox set these words to. William Chappell put the tune of Staines Morris to those words in his incredible book, “Popular Music of the Olden Time” (1850’s) and they fit, so it is likely that this was the tune that Cox had in mind. Personally, I think those lyrics are quite twee, but probably suitable for a play of that era.
Martin Carthy popularized the tune with these lyrics on his album Prince Heathen with Dave Swarbrick. Further recordings of the song were made by The Albion Band and Shirley Collins and it subsequently entered into the modern tradition.
However, the tune has existed for much longer and it appears that it was possibly known as a Christmas Carol long before it was a dance tune or song in a play. This Youtube recording is much like Loman’s version. The William Ballet melody is slightly different from the one in Playford. It is more modal.
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bWh9ZznD8xA
Here is a vocal rendition of Blessed Be that Maid Marie
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JOEqxLTjbM4

Re: Early Music Tunes in your session?

Traditional music has always been adapted and altered consciously and unconsciously. Sometimes a tune is changed to fit a dance (particularly in morris) or to fit a ballad, which may create a variant that passes on into the canon. Sometimes a tune can be altered unconsciously because it is mis-remembered (Of COURSE I have NEVER done that! :). Most versions of Mad Moll are minor, but there is one in the Joseph Kershaw MS (19th C Yorkshire fiddler, which can also be found in John Offord’s excellent book John of the Green, the Cheshire Way) is major. One explanation that was given to me for this is that local smallpipe players may not have been able to play in the minor mode that the tune is normally played in and this local fiddler learned it from those pipers. It became a localized variant. I don’t know if that is true, but that’s what I was told. There are literally hundreds of versions of Greensleeves, some of which bear little resemblance (if any) to the version that most people know. Traditional music is very elastic and adaptable. That is not a bad thing at all.

Talking about Early Music within a Session, another issue strikes me.

When I’m practising, or if I want to introduce a new tune to the group, I look for videos on YouTube. For Early Music, these are scarcer in any case.

But there’s another problem. Many Early Music or Baroque Ensembles are HIP, Historically Informed Performance. If they produce a video, I can’t play along. Modern orchestras tend to tune to A=440 Hz (or a little higher) and period-instrument ensembles often tune to either side of A=420 Hz.2

I can use the videos to learn the tune, though, and if I can hum it, I can play it. But some people need sheet music to first learn a tune, so the whole situation is a bit more complicated.
