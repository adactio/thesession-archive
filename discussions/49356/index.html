Haha! “Jiggie Smalls”, love it! Welcome to the forums! (We’ll have to try to avoid saying your name 3 times in any given post, I guess!) ;-)

I think evolving your opinions is a natural process in any activity, especially as you get better at it. My opinions of many things related to the music have changed over the years. Off the top of my head:

* I used to think big sessions were lots of fun
* I used to try to stay on the straight and narrow of “pure drop”, but then realized that most of the players that I really like have their own styles. And while they blend well with others, they’re often unique players, so that allowed me to loosen the boundaries I was putting on my own playing.
* I have grown to not like some of the bands and recordings that I used to think were the cat’s meow.
* I used to feel that my mandolin allowed me more expressiveness than my banjo, and I now feel the opposite is true.

I like this topic, because it has made me realize how much I’ve grown in the 25 years since I first played Irish traditional music!

Re: Things you’ve changed your mind about

Excellent question to ponder!

I can certainly echo how my musical tastes have evolved and I ebb and flow on who’s the cat’s meow (for me) and I can also say that I have learned to let up a bit on the “pure drop” aspect too. Following that would be to still listen to other types of music. For a while, I would hardly allow myself to enjoy other kinds and genres.

My biggest change has been with the approach to instruments, in
a) not all instrument fit one well
b) it’s okay to learn how to be better on other instruments too
c) if you are struggling with an instrument, never 1) assume it’s a bad instrument and 2) assume you just will never get it. Especially with #2- I struggled with a flute that did not fit me well at all, and assumed it was *all* me, when in fact, I got a different flute and it was **night and day** and I can now actually play pretty well for intermediate playing level.

Finally, learn to enjoy the prospect of playing for your own joy, or for the person walking down the street when you are on the porch or park bench or “being heard” by others that you are not intentionally playing for. The extreme vast majority of my playing is alone, and I’ve had to come to grips with that it will be like this unless I move somewhere else where there’s a bigger trad scene. I’m already driving 2 hours to a session and go often, but learning to enjoy playing *for myself* has been something that I’ve had to learn to enjoy and I’m still learning how to do that well.

A handful of us continued to play gigs. Although we did not announce this during subsequent sessions and definitely held any “rehearsals” for upcoming gigs separate from the regular (weekly) sessions.

Re: Things you’ve changed your mind about

When I was a wee music beginner I used to presume that different traditional music styles like Irish, African and American hillbilly and all the old world traditional music styles and instruments from around the whole world were all mostly unique, and only in the more recent times mixing because of traveling people and instruments.

But now, oh, now I’m far more enlightened and quite sure that in the roots of most authentic or true-to-origin music there is often striking silmarillionarities in what the music is like, shared across many cultures, locations and times, and maybe in ways we’ve lost track of.

Another good one: like most music listeners, I once believed that “Blues” music had a very simple feel and structure, and by default, if you heard the blues, then “the blues was the blues”. Then I read a book called “Escaping The Delta”, and learned all about the historical development of the blues and what the blues musicians thought, did and experienced. I used to believe a blues musician would go into some little joint in Mississippi on a weekend and play nothing but the “blues”, because that musician was a “blues” musician in a “blues” music style area. What nonsense. With good reason, those musicians played a variety of music and it was the record companies that pressured a lot of musicians to homogenize their music into one narrowly defined style that they could market for record sales, to mostly white people.

Another good one: Before I knew what the heck the “Blues” meant, I, like millions of others who are naïve, thought you played blues music to get the blues and enjoy it. What nonsense. Once you understand what the “blues” are, you absolutely don’t want the blues and don’t want others to have the blues either. If you play “blues” music, certainly for the public, you can certainly identify with anyone with the blues and express having the blues, but if you do so, it’s done not to get the blues or enhance or sustain the blues, but to get OUT and BEYOND and HEAL and stay away from the blues, personally and socially. It’s done to help people, not to make them wretch their lunch. It seems the record companies and a lot of musicians who superficially dabble in the blues, figure that playing the blues is to give people the blues and it all boils down to playing the same 3-chord structure over and over, so highly predictably.

I have to think that Irish traditional music suffers from this same clinical malady to some degree, due to the influence of industry marketing, presumption and ignorance. Fortunately, musicians who CREATE music often rediscover where things come from, by going back to original realizations and experiences.

Re: Things you’ve changed your mind about

Over the years there have been some changes in what I have wanted to play as an instrument. I came into the music backing my fiddler Significant Other on guitar, then picked up mandolin to play melody, and later learned flute as a different window into the music.

For a while I thought I could just play flute, avoiding the opportunity cost of splitting up my practice time between very different instruments.

But it turns out there are tunes I prefer on mandolin, like the ones with important notes below the flute’s low D note (those darned fiddler tune composers!) that don’t sound right to my ear with the low notes “folded” up an octave, or with a close harmonizing substitution. I also like throwing in double-stops or partial chords sometimes. Then again, the flute is so perfect on other tunes, with a wider range of ornamentation and (arguably) expression.

So I seem doomed to be a multi-instrumentalist rather than the best I can achieve focusing on only one instrument. At least I don’t foresee picking up yet another instrument at this point. I sometimes see a great box player or piper and think…. hmmmm… maybe? But there aren’t enough years left in me. I have more than enough with mandolin and flute to enjoy this music.

“You don’t improve your technique by doing difficult things wrong. You improve your technique by doing things correctly.”

Yes indeed. I’ve seen people fumble through ornaments up to speed, because they haven’t spent the time to practice them slowly and correctly. Practice doesn’t make perfect; perfect practice makes perfect.

“I used to think that music shouldn’t be simplified for the sake of accessibility.”

I’m not sure exactly what is meant here. If you mean ornaments, I’ve heard good trad players who hardly do any.

When I used to teach flute workshops at festivals I’d find a reel that stood on its own perfectly well, a tune that didn’t require rolls to “sound right”, for newbies to Irish trad. We could focus on playing that reel with good timing and flow without getting bogged down with ornaments.

Next I’d present a reel that had a G roll sort of built-in to it, which I’d use to introduce the roll. (Flute newbies always seemed to find the G roll easiest to do.)

Re: Changing the melody to make it easier to play.

Sometimes we do that so the tune lands better on our instrument.

Sometimes the change is a “placeholder” that allows us to play the tune at a session while we practice the tricky bit at home.

Sometimes the change ends up being a good variation that we keep. We might even prefer it over the original melody.

The only caution is that some tricky bits are there to teach us a new skill, and if we don’t work on them, then our playing can too easily plateau.

If a phrase is giving me fits, I’m happy to suss out a work-around so I can play the tune. But I keep working on the tricky bit too, woodshedding until I can do it. It’s a great way to keep improving.

All the while, refining the next skill that comes along….

Re: Things you’ve changed your mind about

P.S. Jerone, I realize I’m not telling you anything you don’t already know. Sorry if I came across as pedantic. Just wanting to clarify for others who might read this thread.

The concept of progression isn’t something I’ve changed my mind on, but it’s worth mentioning in this thread.

Progression is when you work on “easier” skills as steps to eventually tackling “more difficult” skills. You can use this approach on many levels—learning first-finger rolls on fiddle, say, before trying them on middle finger or ring finger. And also learning simpler tunes before wading into more complex ones.

Progression also applies to a lot of the topics brought up here—moving from big sessions to small ones, perhaps, because you can play with more nuance and hear what others are doing. Or learning to listen more closely, in finer detail, and letting that shift your taste in which artists you prefer. Or how you focus your woodshedding time to make more efficient, more effective use of it.

You can take almost any activity and see it as a line of stepping stones or stair steps that lead to improvements.

That said, sometimes it’s also helpful to take a breather from relentless optimization—it’s okay to be present with the skills and musicality you have right now and enjoy playing for the sake of simply making music.
