Let’s talk about money!

Warning: This discussion is about money. Some personal finances are discussed. You have been notified.

In two more months, I will be at my 1st complete year as a full-time musician. Since over 95% of my work has come from busking, it’s time that I begin to think about a 2nd or 3rd method of income, so that I can migrate from busking into something more consistent, but equally sustaining. It is projected that I will have to busk for some time next year, but the goal is to gradually reduce it over time, hopefully to 50% by the end of the year. Busking does have its own value as a marketing tool, but that’s not what I want to talk about. I want to talk about this concertina investment.

I’ve reached a point in my life where it’s time for me to stop thinking like a hopeful idealist, and start thinking like a businessman. Me being so early into the workings of making a career out of music, I have to be mindful of ways that I can generate more income, rather than focusing on collecting “recreational” instruments. Now, I love the concertina, I really do. And I will not be satisfied without it, and Irish music, in my life. But the question is, would it be wise to spend $2,500 on a concertina when I have other investments to consider? Or should I put it off until later?

The upgrade will cost a solid month’s pay. For the price of an intermediate concertina, I could buy a nice sounding piano, and all of my required studio equipment, including a DAW(Digital Audio Workstation). I could pay my car insurance for a year, or my auto loan payments for a year. Or, I could pay six months of rent. The only thing about all of these investments that made me question them is that they are going way ahead into the future. If I already have sustainable income, is it necessary to think so much further ahead? I don’t even need a piano, for recording, and my next music project only requires a digital music writer, and some studio time. That’s only going to cost a few hundred bucks, and the program that I’m looking to work with is Finale PrintMusic. Even my busking set-up is easily replaceable if anything were to happen to it, and it pays for itself every couple of days.

So I’m not really sure what direction to take. Buy the concertina and be happy, or make other investments and be safe? Should I make it wait? Or should I reward myself for a successful first year on the music grind?

I think you’ve answered your own question. No, it would definitely NOT be wise to spend $2,500 on a concertina.

You are a professional musician now. That means you give everything cost/benefit analysis. Buying a lovely instrument is what you do to reward yourself for having sold over 1000 copies of your first album (or something); it’s not an essential purchase. But right now, you don’t even have the tools to record an album. And if you don’t even have those, nobody’s going to be able to hear (or care) whether you’re playing a good concertina or a second-hand beginner one.

If you want to diversify beyond busking, then you need decent recordings, you need a calling-card. That’s your priority. The musicians we all know and love in Irish traditional music played on whatever they could get their hands on to start off with, they weren’t playing on expensive instruments. I would suggest to you that you diversify into: teaching students; playing paid gigs such as functions, weddings, festive occasions, St Patrick’s day etc etc; and maybe some session musician type stuff (and maybe online stuff like fiverr).

Re: Let’s talk about money!

Cheers to you, Jerone, for taking the plunge into music as a profession and keeping a level head about it!

This kind of decision is really the kind of thing that we have to deal with as adults. There needs to be a life/work balance that keeps you happy, without having severe consequences to your future. So it is good to think about these things before letting your inner child make hasty decisions that cost you money that could be used more effectively elsewhere.

For many of us, having a life/work balance means working to make enough money to support our music habit. But the lines blur a bit when your work and enjoyment include the same things. So it’s probably not a bad idea to treat the concertina as part of your hobby (and not your profession, for now, at least). But you should also think about the idea that busking on concertina might make you more money than busking on fiddle, because its a more curious instrument that many people have never seen - so you might be able to justify the purchase without the downside.

You mention that the biggest complaint you get from audiences is that you don’t have any recordings. And my suggestion would be to capitalize on that the best you can. Build yourself a website. As a professional musician, you should have one, if you don’t already. That way, you can have business cards with your website, and encourage your audiences to sign up on a mailing list, etc. Or a less expensive option is to build a facebook group, and invite your audiences to join the group for news about where you’re going to be playing, etc. You could even set it up so that people could select whether they’re interested more in your piano and composition, or whether they’re more interested in your trad. Then you have a more persistent connection to your fans, and that will grow over time, and you can use that to stay engaged with the people who care about your music.

Then you have the ability to do things like specifically target people who like you with a Kickstarter or GoFundMe type campaign when it comes time to record. As much as I dislike a constant barrage of requests for funding to make albums, it can seriously help offset the cost of producing one, if not pay for it outright. Typically what people do is set up different levels of “sponsorship” - the base level being something like $20 for a signed copy of your album. So that is basically just “pre-selling” your album to people, which increases your exposure and engagement with your fans. (Then there are usually other higher levels of sponsorship that might include things like a private performance, or being thanked in the liner notes, etc…)

So that could also be a consideration with regards to whether you can (or should) afford the concertina.

I will say that I feel lucky to have a career which affords me the ability to play music semi-professionally, and not worry about things like whether my first album will ever actually pay for itself… Thinking about trying to make music my main source of income is a pretty stressful thought for me. So again, more power to you for following your dreams and making them a reality!

↳ Posted by Reverend on September 25th, 2017.

What money?

Talk about money? Most professional musicians I know make a paltry living at best. When I was a professional musician, in the 70s and 80s, I was thrilled to make $1,000 on a weekend, playing for dances, weddings, and the odd concert. But it wasn’t every weekend. When I was singing and playing the fiddle in a ballad band I made $100 a night. That would be $300/night today. Sounds good, right?
Wrong. I had small kids to bring up. I hated being on the road, sleeping in a strange bed, eating shit food. I never made much more than $15,000 in one year (today that would be about $45,000). Tax free, sure, and enough to live on, but it meant constant driving and doing repairs myself on my own crappy car. I had to keep awful hours. Life in a bar sounds romantic but it really sucks when you do it night after night. It gets boring and ruins the music. It it’s a good gig, at a concert or festival, you get cranked and have to stay up half the night, leveling out. It’s bad for your health, physically and mentally.
I made some money on a Rounder recording (http://www.ebay.com/itm/Sealed-PAT-SCANLON-BLACK-WATER-STRING-BAND-Songs-For-Future-Generations-ROUNDER-/272359193537?hash=item3f69df7bc1:g:8vkAAOSwMgdXxp3O -- that’s me on the left), and some money on an album of trad music (Creagan). A song I wrote got picked up by a radio station as a morning theme song and I made something like $117 on that. If the song had been picked up nationally I could have made some good money, but how likely is that?
A friend, a professional piano player, told me that he wished that he’d gotten a good day job and played for the fun of it. I took his advice and that’s what I did. I still get the odd gig but I am home most every night. Playing music now is a lot more fun. I don’t get quite the ego kick that I got out of audience adulation -- not to mention groupies -- but that isn’t so good for my head, either.
So my advice would be to get the new concertina and have fun. Get a good, money-making job and play the music you love. I don’t want to thwart your noble ambition. This is just my take on the pro scene. If you do go full bore as a pro, and you don’t make enough money, well… you have to sacrifice for your art. As Bromberg says, “You got to suffer if you want to sing the blues.”

Meself has a point. I don’t have kids (and hopefully never will), but my partner would certainly balk if she knew I was spending $2,500 on an instrument, even given that I make pretty good money as a musician. Honestly, I’d balk myself, since like you I can see that sum as many times my rent, but I also know that sometimes you have to pay for a good instrument, and concertinas especially so. The nicest instrument I own is a Martin guitar I bought when I was 15, because I had a job but nowhere to spend the money. If you want a concertina and can swing it, it might be worth poking around to see what’s available at a good price.

And that’s part of the key. You don’t need a new one, you want one, so you can afford to wait until a great deal comes by. I did the same when I felt like upgrading my flute; I watched eBay, Craigslist, and various forums, and jumped when I saw a really good deal. If I had immediately ordered one new from the maker, or jumped on the first used one I saw for sale, I would have spent a few hundred dollars more, and I got exactly what I wanted in the end anyway.

Finally, I’ll say that I’ve just sold off a few of my instruments, and I managed to make back a good bit of what I spent on them. If the concertina you get ever does lose its shine in your eye, it will probably still fetch a good price if you keep it in good nick, and you’ll have spent a few hundred dollars at most on what will amount to a long-term rental. Not bad.

Re: Let’s talk about money!

During our own period of performance, from 1993 -2005, I played excusively on lower end instruments, an Applause Guitar, a Harmony Tenor Banjo, a GKWMI Bouzoucki, Clarke and Generation pennywhistles. The only high end instrument I had were my GHB’s, they were Naill’s and a fine mid set. We made money enough, that for several years during that time period, I had to claim income tax on the earnings. Eventually toward the middle of our hay day, I replaced my guitar and bouzoucki with better instruments, but I still refrained from buying higher end instruments to play in public. I just couldn’t justify the expense to myself. Instead, I invested in a PA system with enough inputs to mike everything, and to get away from the poor PA’s that were present in most of the pubs and bars. It paid for itself withina year. I regret now, not investing in recording, especially since a couple of our members have passed on. Even if we had only sold a few CD’s, it would have increased our marketability beyond the Front Range and the 3 state area we performed in. So choose wisely.
