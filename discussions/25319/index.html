↳ Posted by Guernsey Pete on August 16th, 2010.

Re: Any pointers?

Just because there are six strings on a guitar doesn’t mean you have to play them all, all the time, by strumming right across them. It might be great fun for you, but a little restraint will work wonders. There’s nothing wrong with hitting the bottom two strings or so on the beat, damped, throughout an entire section, instead of playing a different chord every nano-second,
It’s better not to change a chord when you should, than to change when you shouldn’t. In other words, don’t anticipate a chord change unless you are absolutely sure that it is right. If you hang on, the tune will come back into the chord. If you change, you will get two wrong chords instead of one. And don’t forget that it is quieter behind the guitar than in front -- watch where you are pointing it.

If you really want to push the envelope, so to speak, take everyone else’s advice regarding learngin the tunes. Then if you wnat to throw your own creative edge to it, learn and understand as many scales and modes as you can. Doing this allows you to create ANY chord for any occasion or feeling. You play these chords in a trillion different ways, each lending a different feeling to the music. For example, tri- finger plucking, single picking, strumming,and arpegios will all give their own little twist to any chord. While some of this may not be the “traditional” way to do it, it will be your way to do it. And nothing says your way won’t sound great and keep you and your audience enthused.

Re: Any pointers?

Great advice from the other guitarists on this thread, but Id like to point out that knowing how to play the tunes will help very little in knowing how to back them . Just as knowing the chord patterns wont mean you can play the tune!
What you need to do is straight forward IMO, find a tune player who also backs and can write down the chord patterns for you. or find a good source of chord patterns such as provided my mixo’lydian on this site or the fiddlers fakebook.

Knowing how to play the tunes is pretty much irrelevant to backing them. Being familiar with the tunes in your head is however essential, so listen to a lot of trad. Playing along with recordings of solo players could be of big help but firstly you need to gain an understanding of the typical structures of traditional tunes. Music for a found ‘delirium’ :-) is not a typical trad tune and to accompany it you definitely need the correct chord patterns as it is a bugger to back, very unintuitive .

Basically each tune has a fundamental chord structure as defined strictly by the melody. Now of course its quite possible to substitute chords if you so choose but IMO this is to be avoided.
Learn the chord patterns and build up your repertoire one tune by one . Good luck.

here’s something I didn’t see earlier…learn about substitute chords to mix things up. so you have your 3 chord trick in the key of D going, so that’s D, G and A. The A you prolly want to leave in its place, but B minor and F# minor both substitute for the D and E minor is a substitute for the G. Throw in a substitute here and there in your 3 chord trick.

you do that, maybe have 2 or 3 voicings for your common chords, and you got that falling scale in your pocket already…that and some rhythm variation should take you a long way

Other substitutions to consider would be to replace a Bm chord with an Em in D tunes, Em with Am in G tunes and so on. This can be particularly effective going into the 2nd part when lots of tunes would start on the relative minor (Bm in the case of D tunes).

I am not sure I would play a full F#m in place of a D chord but a D chord with an F# in the bass often works well.

If you are going to accompany tunes, you may not need to know them well enough to play them yourself, but you have to know them at least enough to recognize them, so you know what tune you are dealing with, and what accompaniment works. No amount of chords written down on paper can make up for that.
And until you can leave your pieces of paper behind, you are not ready to show up at a session.
Nothing wrong with starting with paper. When my wife was learning fiddle, she used the book, The Fifer’s Delight, a collection of fife and contra dance tunes collected by Ralph Sweet. The collection had the advantage of having good, simple guitar chords on the tunes, which I used to accompany her. Made it more fun for her, and I learned as much as she did. As we progressed, we started closing the book as we played, especially on the tunes we heard at the local pub, preparing ourselves to go out in public. I have to admit that those days with just the two of us, playing whatever we liked, however we liked, with no one judging us, no pressure, were some of the best times I ever had playing music.

“I understand fully what substitution is NCFA ,I neither like it or do it myself within the genre. ”

Count me among those confused by spellbreaker’s arguments. When is a chord a substitute? If, leaving aside all the modal tunes for the moment, every tune just uses the 3 “standard chords” there would never be problems with the chords played as sessions. But clearly some folks always substitute chord X for chord Y at a certain place in a particular tune. If chord X is not in the “standard three” is it then wrong? Or a substitute? I think not.

Quite aside from that issue I don’t think you can properly back without knowing a good number of tunes in whatever genera you happen to play in, Irish trad included. It is probably the case that you can begin working from written out chord sheets, but to be competent in a session you need to be able, as others have said, to anticipate what is coming and to understand “what works” with a given tune. Without a good sized repertoire of tunes that just isn’t going to happen. So, no you don’t need to know every tune, but yes you need to know many many to be successful.
