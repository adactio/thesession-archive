Re: Newbie questions

Indeed, there are many previous replies.
I feel there are two or three answers to this problem;
one is being so wrapped up in the music that the audience doesn’t matter, that the important thing is the flow of the music and the joy it brings;
another is to know the music intimately, that the true notes will always flow without thinking from your fingers, and you can step back mentally and concentrate on the detail and the accent because the notes will come out right to start with;
the third thing is not to be bothered about mistakes, to enjoy the moment and put your heart into it, knowing that any true lover of the music will forgive your mistakes knowing that you are truly giving it all you have got.
I have known experienced musicians fighting the “end of the bed” syndrome, where you can play a new instrument perfectly at home, but you take it out to the public, however small and friendly an audience, and go to pieces. The only real answer is to get back on the horse, to try it again, in front of an audience, and give it your best shot. And then do it again. I always feel that this audience fear is a bit like shyness - it’s you yourself that are doing this to you, and you have to conquer it yourself.
Good luck.

Re: Newbie questions

It is natural to experience “performance” nerves. The art in playing in front of other people is being able to harness those nerves and use them to enhance your playing. That’s what makes the difference between a live “performance” and a recorded “performance”. (How many times do you buy a CD after a gig and be disappointed.)
Everybody makes mistakes. More experienced players are better at playing through those mistakes so that few people notice.
And remember that anyone listening to you playing is not doing so in anticipation of you messing up. They’re there to have a positive experience. If you enjoy yourself (and don’t get flustered when you make mistakes) then they will enjoy themselves.

Re: Newbie questions

In saying “Don’t perform, just play”, all I am saying to Knithryn (and others) is: Don’t get filled with the tensions and apprehensions that come (to all of us) when you are performing in circumstances which oblige you to play perfectly - such as special performances, or playing for payment - just play the tune same as you do in your own kitchen. If you are playing among friends then no one will be bothered (if they even notice) that you have played a bum note, and if you are not playing among friends then you should go somewhere that you can.

Another way of saying this is “Don’t regard what you are doing as a performance which is being judged, but just as playing a tune like you do at home.”

It’s not about whether a session can or can’t be a performance, it is about what you consider yourself to be doing as you play. And if you consider yourself to be “on show” then you create tensions and fears that need not be there.

I continue to be surprised that some people make such a fuss over this simple and clear distinction.

DonaldK is really on to something there. It’s pretty natural to get rocked a bit whenever you are in a situation that’s somehow different than the circumstances that you normally play in. Sometimes you can’t hear yourself, sometimes the lighting is weird and you can’t see properly. Or, you find yourself playing with musicians who have rock steady rhythm and you realize then that your own rhythm isn’t. or it’s quite cold and your muscles won’t respond properly etc.
The key is to practise playing in as many different circumstances as you can. I think it is a good idea to get a set of tunes worked up that you know so well that you can play them no matter what is going on. Shoot for 120% so you can afford to lose a little when the unforeseen happens. ‘Blankie tunes’
And then get yourself playing in new situations. Play for one or two other persons, play with one or two or persons, play for a few more, and do it as often as you can.

Re: Newbie questions

Evolution has inflicted upon us a mechanism that mitigates
egregious behaviour, making us blush, sweat, stammer, pass
out/wind/water, empty the bowels, shake, avoid eye contact… etc. etc. in any combination and to varying degree. You are not unique in this regard, and it helps if you tell yourself so, and believe it. If there is anyone who has not felt any of the above symptoms, he is not human.
Playing with other people, mercifully, is not the same as playing in front of other people. If you think of playing as sitting spotlit on a stage before a critical, knowing audience, you would be exceptional in the extreme if you felt calm. There is a tipping point, an emotional switch, whose approach we should watch out for and avoid, and there are several tactics that can be brought into play to avoid it. One of the most important is not to think of yourself as sitting on a stage. After all, I don’t suppose you feel nervous at the thought of talking to your friends. What if they were all playing instead? Would you feel nervous at joining in if someone offered you a seat? If you can keep in mind that you are really just joining in, not performing, you are halfway there.
Also: what do YOU think of someone when he or she is playing? The chances are that everyone in the room thinks the same, and would think the same when it is you who is playing. Keep in mind that you are not that special (of course you are to everyone who knows you). What feels like the approach of doom when it is your turn to play, is no big deal for the rest of the assembly.
Some other things: do not dress specially -- wear
clothes you would usually choose, and feel comfortable in.
Don’t get drunk before you start playing.
Choose tunes that you know you can play well, and offer them rather than yourself or your playing. In other words, ‘Here’s a good tune, what do you think?’ rather than, ‘Listen to me play this.’
Play for yourself, not the people in the room. If they want to listen, then great; but if they don’t, it doesn’t matter.
Try to think of picking up your instrument as just a normal action in the course of the evening, like taking off your coat if it is too warm, or getting out your phone to show your long-suffering friend your latest crappy pictures. It is not the start of a performance, but part of a natural progression in a musical evening, like going to the bar and buying everyone a drink. Hah.
If everything fails and you feel nature winning, there are still some things to bear in mind, if you can think straight. Most important: don’t stop! If you make a mistake, chances are that nobody noticed, or was even listening. Keep the rhythm going, get the tune back, soldier on. If anyone _was_ listening, he would think it _his_ fault that he lost the thread for a moment. It is not a recording to be listened to over and over, and the human ear is very forgiving.
If you feel your confidence slipping: PLAY LOUDER. And remember that confidence here is not some vague psychological attribute; but confidence in your ability, that is, ‘I know I can play this tune well, and do it justice.’ If you can’t play it well, choose something else.
It is easy for me to sit here and make a list. The hard bit is yours: the mental adroitness needed to avoid that emotional switch.
Deep breaths, inner calm, what the heck.

Re: Newbie questions

I agree muchly with Gam, although I recommend getting completely drunk beforehand.

Actually, not getting drunk is a good start, and beginning with something simple is a good idea. Also, keep in mind that if you’re performing for an audience, then your job is to put the audience at ease. You don’t have to be perfect, or a virtuoso -- so don’t think of it as an ego thing. Keep it simple, smile and be friendly, and ignore any mistakes you make.

Which gets to a point that seems to be central to performing. That is, do you play to produce nice music, or to avoid making mistakes? It’s easy to get caught up in the “avoiding mistakes” thing, but that can be a nasty trap. The minute you hear something even slightly out, you recoil, and it’s downhill from there. Instead, listen for the nice stuff, like you do at home, and try to enjoy the parts that are sounding good. It’s true, a live audience won’t hear, or will forget, any mistakes, if you’re feeling comfortable with your own playing. It’s only when it affects you that they will care. And the part about it live music comes with mistakes -- that’s true, and it’s sometimes the best part.

Jim

“self-conscious”

An interesting expression that I’ve never really thought about before. I think being conscious is very important when playing music - either performance or non performance.

A lot of people talk about getting to the point where your tunes are on auto pilot. I think this is a bad thing. It’s very uncreative … it’s the equivalent of being unconscious. I think you should be aware and proactive and reactive at all times.

But this is a different kettle of fish to being self-conscious. I know it’s a kind of contradiction, but you have to be conscious of yourself and others’ playing of the music, but not self-conscious. The only way I can think to describe it sounds a bit corny … it’s like the music takes over the self. The music becomes more important than the self. The best music is utterly selfless. “The self” is the pin that bursts the bubble.

Re: Newbie questions

I was seriously hampered for years by dry lips when playing the harmonica in front of people, something that never happened to me playing in private at home. I tried all sorts of things, including KY Jelly, to try to overcome it (that was a very bad idea. The harmonica slips marvellously through the lips for about 45 seconds, then suddenly seizes up, taking half your mouth with it). What I eventually found that worked was a combination of things. First, be as confident as you can that you are fine with remembering the tunes you want to play (sounds obvious, but just because you can get through a tune at home doesn’t mean you won’t get memory lapses under duress). Second, don’t try to play tunes that contain bits that are right at the limit of your ability until you’ve really nailed ’em. Third, play as much as you can with other people. Not because they will cover up your mistakes (though they will, a little bit), but because you can then spend your time listening and interacting instead of fretting about your playing. This is the one thing that will raise your game more than anything else. As Ms Lonelyhearts said (sexist? Moi? :-D), you have to get away from thinking about the next notes you’re going to play. Of course, there are skills to be honed… Fourth, as has been said, what to you may sound like a massive gaffe will usually pass unnoticed by anyone listening, and in any case they can’t do what you’re doing so who cares anyway? After a year or three you learn to laugh off mistakes. Anyway, they’re not mistakes. They are part and parcel of live music The last thing I ever want to hear is a piece of music played note-perfect. Can’t think of anything worse.

[WARNING: Don’t bother reading this if you already understand why not all sessions are performances (i.e., concerts, shows, presentations to an audience.]

Okay. I ask only because many of us here have no problem with the words “perform” and “performance.” We understand that the word “perform” means “to carry out, to do, to execute,” ***and it also*** means “to present entertainment to an audience.” Since the word has at least these two distinct meanings, depending on the context within which it’s used, good communicators are wise to be aware of the connotations when using the word so as not to create confusion.

Let’s look at the etymology of the words “perform” and “performance” (from the online etymological dictionary, which is based in large part on the Oxford English Dictionary, the gold standard for word usage and etymology in the English language):

performance 1530s, “carrying out of a promise, duty, etc.,” from perform + -ance. Meaning “a thing performed” is from 1590s; that of “action of performing a play, etc.” is from 1610s; that of “a public entertainment” is from 1709. Performance art is attested from 1971.
perform
c.1300, “carry into effect, fulfill, discharge,” via Anglo-Fr. performir, altered (by infl. of O.Fr. forme “form”) from O.Fr. parfornir “to do, carry out, finish, accomplish,” from par- “completely” + fornir “to provide” (see furnish). Theatrical/musical sense is from 1610.

For both words, then, the original meaning expanded from “to do, carry out,” etc., to an additional sense and usage of perform and performance as “public entertainment,” specifically a presentation of theater and music. This sense of these words dates back about 400 years. Clearly, these meanings weren’t “redefined” or “invented” on thesession.org.

Many people on theSESSION.org (Jack included if he still stands by his quote above from Random_notes) also understand that at least some Irish traditional sessions are different from formal concerts or shows presented to an audience. (Certainly some sessions ***are*** more like concerts to an audience, and that’s fine if that’s what the participants want them to be.)

But other sessions (I’ve been to many such) are soooo unlike a concert to an audience that to describe them as a “performance” only creates confusion--it clarifies absolutely nothing.

So what’s the big deal? Well, there’s a not uncommon risk that some people will mistake the session for a performance in the sense of a band giving a concert, and that in turn significantly alters the way musicians choose and play tune, and how the musicians go about their recreation (pausing to chat, going to the bar for a drink, stepping outside, etc.), and the way the other people in the pub respond to the music and interact with the musicians. So if someone prefers that their session doesn’t turn into a concert or show, then it helps--given the 400-year old common usage of the words--to find words other than “perform” and “performance” to describe what that session is.

As it happens, the word “session” is short, sweet, and to the point. Calling such a musical gathering a “session” also opens the door for someone to ask how a session is different from a concert, show, or performance, and we can readily explain that distinction.

I’ve been lurking in these debates for some while, so it’s time to stick my head above the parapet.

Like a lot of people here my practice sessions tend to be fairly unstructured. I just play tunes and when I stumble over a section then probably I will stop and play that section more slowly or maybe try and play it a bit differently. Sometimes my wife will feel the need for a bit of company and will bring a book and join me in the room. That somehow changes the nature of it for me and I will mostly try and play the tunes straight through and cover up the gaffes as best I can. An element of performance has crept in, just with that one person in the room who isn’t really listening.

There’s more. My practice room has a wardrobe with full-length mirror doors and sometimes I will play standing in front of these. My wife thinks this is narcissism. No, I explain, the mandolin projects all its sound forward and the mirror is good at reflecting the sound back so I can get a better impression of what I sound like to others. But also when playing like this I tend to really go for it and give it my best shot. In essence I am performing for myself.

Now think of the scenario where you are in a session and someone strikes up a tune which you used to know but you haven’t played for many years. Chances are you will try and play quietly to yourself, dropping in and out of the ensemble. Effectively you are practising. However by the third repeat the tune has come back to you and you are playing it loudly and confidently. Rather than being focussed on remembering the notes you are now concentrating on playing the tune to the best of your ability, fitting in with the groove, adding the twiddly bits, making a contribution to the lift and drive of the piece. That’s no different to what a concert performer does, so to me it’s not unreasonable to use the verb ‘perform’ to describe this activity.

So, I choose to pigeon-hole my playing as either ‘practising’ or ‘performing’. As such, performing is playing but playing is not necessarily performing. If there is a third category of ‘just playing’ then to me that has negative connotations of going through the motions, playing the tune on auto-pilot without too much regard for what is going on around.

Using my definitions, the participants in a session are predominantly performing, so I am going to side with Jack and say that for me a session is a performance, albeit a different kind of performance from a concert. The primary audience for a session is the session itself, just like me playing to my reflection. However the audience will also be supplemented those denizens of the pub/bar who are paying attention from time to time.

Re: Newbie questions

Practising, rehearsing, making music in a session, performing (in a concert). They’re all playing, but they are four very different things. The trouble is that we don’t have a verb for the third one. I now accept that it’s not another mode of performing, as for many people here this has the association of ‘playing in a manner designed to please or impress an audience’. Words come to mean what people think they mean, irrespective of any dictionary definition.

In other genres where musicians get together to play for their own edification they would mostly call it a jam session or just a jam. ‘To jam’ also works as a verb, but unfortunately ‘to session’ doesn’t.

Re: Newbie questions

The verb I most often heat associated with playing in a session is “sit in,” as in: “Mind if I sit in?” Jazz and studio musicians commonly use session as a verb, but again, that may have shades of meaning slightly different from what most of us do when playing in a pub session.

“Play” is such a good word for making music, regardless of whether you mean noodling up a new tune, or having sets (wink, wink, nudge, nudge) with some friends, or wearing a tux and being paid to impress an audience as part of an orchestra. It’s still all “playing” at some level, or it isn’t music.

Words take on meaning through common usage. Dictionaries merely document that usage, and they always lag behind reality.
