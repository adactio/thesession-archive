Music and pain

For a few weeks now I’ve been playing in a particular session where the seating arrangement (the usual cushioned low bench found in pubs) appears to be giving rise to post-session pain. I’ve been playing tenor banjo in the session. It’s not my preferred instrument. I favour the mandolin but my crappy little mass-produced mando doesnt have sufficient volume to be heard above other instruments.

The combination of the poor seating and, I suspect, tension caused by my comparative lack of ease with the banjo (which causes me to grip too tightly, etc.) and the fact that the session environment means that we’re all playing way too fast for our own (and the tunes’!) good seems to be causing some musuculoskeletal wear and tear. The upshot is that this time last week I had a nagging pain in my ribs(!), which was exacerbated if I tilted my head in a particular direction or raised my arm above a certain height or at a certain angle.

I hadn’t suspected the session as being the cause of my discomfort and by the weekend the pain was so pronounced that I suspected I might have some heart trouble and had myself checked over at the local hospital. I received a clean bill of health in the heart and lung department. The doctor suggested that it might be some minor muscular issue and told me not to worry. The pain gradually receded and I put it to the back of my mind.

Now the pain has re-emerged and I’ve put two and two together.

Has anyone else had similar problems? I’m reluctant to give up on this particular session and I will make an effort to arrive early next week and base myself on a stool as opposed to the bench. However I suspect that the seating is a contributor to the problem, but not the sole cause; the tensions caused by nervous gripping and playing at a speed beyond my natural capability for a three- or four-hour stretch also have a part to play. If the pain resurfaces, then I may have to reconsider playing in sessions at all!

Advice from fellow musicians greatly appreciated.

Re: Music and pain

The wooden flute is a notorious pain inducer. Small improvements on the way you hold the instrument may make big differences on the amount of pain. For example, if i align the embochure with the finger holes, it’s pain country in a few minutes, but if i have the embochure turned towards me, much more bearable.

Maybe you should experiment with your strap, the angle you hold the instrument, even the kind of pick.

Have a Guiness right before you start. It’s a good muscle relaxant, and will help ease the tension.

Re: Music and pain

Aidan, A few thoughts off the top of my head.

3-4 hours playing in a session at a stretch? Bearing in mind you’ve come into trad playing later in life (like I have), I think you’re doing far, far too much. You’re under no obligation to be playing all that time, so I suggest you cut down your playing in a session to about a quarter of what you’ve been doing, and spread it out over the 3 hours so that you can rest and relax for several minutes between your plays. Get up and stand or walk around during these rest periods. Also the rest gives you a chance to listen to the other players. And, dare I say it, the players of quieter instruments might appreciate the occasional cessation of the tenor banjo!

Before you start, try warming up with some body and limb stretching exercises (no straining, mind!) for a few minutes. A couple of years ago I went on a baroque orchestra workshop held by the Orchestra of the Age of Enlightenment, and the tutor placed great emphasis on the importance of this warmup. Also, the basic, simple and inexpensive exercise of vigorous walking for 20-30 minutes a day will work wonders for body posture and torso strength.

I don’t know how long you’ve been playing, or your standard, but may I suggest you have your playing posture and technique checked out by an experienced teacher who should be able to give you remedial exercises. A physiotherapist who specialises in musicians’ ailments would be very useful. I believe the big London orchestras use them. The music colleges in London would probably also have some names. Don’t forget that even the best and most experienced musicians can lapse into sloppy postures and let technique slide if they don’t watch it, with the aches and pains that will inevitably ensue. It even happened to one of the world’s greatest classical guitarists once.

I’m inclined to think that a session like yours that is apparently playing at full throttle all the time is sooner or later going to be bad news for all concerned, both musically and physically (as you’re now finding out!) Are there other players who are uneasy about the relentless speed? Is there an identifiable person or persons driving the session? If so, then perhaps a discrete and friendly approach (outside of the session) by one or two of you explaining the problems and suggesting the musical and physical benefits of slower playing on occasion, may work. Otherwise, as a last resort, a change of session may be indicated, even though it means you may be leaving friends behind.

I agree that a flat-topped firm stool is usually far better for a musician than the average pub bench seat. It should go to ensuring that you maintain an upright posture. Would it be inconvenient for you to bring along your own favorite stool? In the orchestras in which I am a cellist there are one or two of the older string players who prefer to use their own chairs, and the double-bass players always bring their own stools.

Re: Music and pain

Aidan, I’m sorry to hear of your pain. I have scoliosis and have to take great care to avoid my back “going out”. I can only tell you from my experiences that my pain eventually localizes in my ribs, but the cause is in my back.

I was told as a teen to remember that the muscles and ribs wrap from the spine to the front and that having strengthened stomach muscles will go a long way toward providing support to the back. I was taught the proper posture for standing, sitting, leaning, lifting and carrying weighted objects (there are “proper” ways that avoid back injury). That combined with lumbar support and using proper posture has helped so much.

Some types of chairs will aggravate my difficulties…I’ve learned to identify and avoid those. Even wearing waistbands that pull back on me when I am seated will put enough stress to put things out of whack. Leaning forward and bending just slightly to reach for the faucet (if I bend from the waist with a straight back and legs) has been enough to do it if I don’t remember to bend my knees first and bend in a more hunched way.

I agree with those who told you to see a specialist. Specialists can evaluate your body movement and level of strength, and tell you specifically what your needs are. Besides my orthopedist, I also saw a physical therapist who specialized in back-related sports injuries.

From what you’ve said, I think your problem may be turn out to be as simple as how you bear the weight of your playing arm, your posture, and possibly some muscles that need strengthening. Your body compensates to provide support, often with muscles that aren’t used to the load. When I feel pain, that’s the cue to evaluate how I am moving, carrying weight, my center of gravity, and if I have developed the muscles to bear that task.

On the question of posture, one thing I learned from singing lessons is that you need to regard your back as a column, much like a stack of toddler’s building bricks. You need to keep each block nicely balanced on the one underneath, or you build up points of stress and that’s where it goes when it goes. Not to say that you need to sit bolt upright, you don’t want to go into a siezure - but just to be aware of the natural stacking of your vertebrae and muscles and make sure that you are supporting them from the midriff. The keyword for singers: support. From the pelvis, upwards…

Re: Music and pain

Henk (and others), I can personally vouch that repetitive stress/strain injuries can be overcome, but it does take a lot of patience, trial and error, and persistence. In my case, I also found that I couldn’t play pain free until I eliminated all the contributing factors from other areas of my life--so I rigged up an ergonomically correct mouse pad for my computer (it’s now on a slant to prevent my wrist from cokcing back on itself--no more carpal tunnel symptoms!), quit playing ice hockey (a tough sport on the hands and shoulders), get even more religious about daily exercise and gentle stretching, drinking even more water, etc. I’m now at the point where I use scissors to open all plastic bags (like in cereal boxes, bags of chips, etc.) rather than risk straining my hands trying to pull them open, and I wear work gloves for any little chore around the house, even just tightening a loose doorknob, etc., for the added cushion and grip the gloves provide.

As a fiddler, I’ve found I have to do special shoulder exercises to keep my rotator cuffs happy, and I absolutely refuse to play when my hands are cold. Cold hands equals low circulation equals muscle and tendon injuries if you try to play at speed. I use warm water, rice bags heated in a microwave, and winter gloves to get my hands warmed up before playing, and I tend to overdress if there’s a chill in the air. It’s always easier to take layers off if you start to overheat than it is to warm up once your chilled, and trying to play with cold muscles is just asking for injuries.

The biggest factor though is learning to relax when you play. For years, I played with a death grip on the fiddle, on the strings, on the chinrest, on the bow. It took a real concerted effort over many months to finally loosen up and realize how little effort is actually needed to hold a string down or move the bow. And remember--you don’t have to hold the fiddle. Just stand (or sit) under it. Losing all that tension and excess muscle activity goes a long way to avoiding aches and pains and also improves your playing tremendously--you’ll sound more relaxed and fluid, and you be able to play faster, cleaner, more smoothly.

I also keep a mandolin handy to learn new tunes and practice on. This allows me to keep the tunes fresh with fewer hours on the fiddle, and subsequently fewer opportunities for repetitive stress problems, especially with my bowing arm. Playing whistle and flute also help in this regard, but mandolin and fiddle share the same fingering, so the tunes more directly transfer onto fiddle when it’s time.

Good luck…don’t give up playing, but find a way to give up the pain.

Aidan, you’ve had great advice here, so I won’t repeat it, but wanted to suggest something else to you.

I play a number of instruments, and I also suffer from fibromyalgia. Repetitive stress can drive me to new summits of pain. I’ve found, along with finding different posture/holds on the instrument/taking breaks, etc, that taking a daily supplement of magnesium can do a LOT to stop muscular pain and spasm. Muscles need magnesium to work properly, and without it, they will cramp and spasm, and can cause you horrific pain, headaches, etc. It takes a while for supplements to kick in, but I found that muscular spasm reduced after only a week of adding magnesium supplements to my life -- and now I can play longer and enjoy playing without so much pain. I have a certain amount of chronic pain in my life, but this has made a great deal of difference!

Let us know how you go.

Re: Music and pain

Will, I did some checking on side effects of taking magnesium supplements, but didn’t find any specific reference to nosebleeds, only to a laxative action when magnesium is taken in amounts too large for the body to handle. Nosebleeds were mentioned as symptoms of conditions that magnesium might be taken as a treatment for, such as high blood pressure.

However, I wouldn’t discount the possibility that you might have had a reaction of some kind to the supplements -- maybe not to the magnesium itself, but to other substances in the supplement. Magnesium is frequently made up with other minerals and nutrients, which help in the absorption of the magnesium, including some of the B vitamins. You might have developed nosebleeds from a reaction to those nutrients, or to one of the other ingredients in the supplement you took. Some people have nosebleeds as part of allergic reactions, when the lining of the nasal passages swells and becomes irritated.

Wish I could be of more help. You might want to do some checking on the other ingredients that were in the supplement that you tried.

All the best,

Tove
