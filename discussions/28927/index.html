Re: What is a reel?

Reels are in cut time (2/2). It is vital to the rhythm to play two beats to the measure. If you play four, it will sound very choppy. Often, reels are written with a 4/4 time signature, but those accustomed to playing ITM will automatically translate that to cut time.

Many players favor a very rythmic style of playing (sort of a boom-chuca-boom) which I love, but don’t always use. I tend toward a more fluid style that sounds more even and less bouncy. The tme signature is the same, but the lilt is different.

Sometimes players play reels more like a hornpipe, which is in 2/4. It is more of a walking rhythm and is usually slower. Actually, when playing alone, I often play a reel as a hornpipe on the first time through, change to a slow reel on the second, and play two more times through in a fairly uptempo reel time. It’s a nice way to settle into the tune and create an interesting build.

Re: What is a reel?

Reels are played in 2/2 not 4/4. The difference with the time signatures is very subtle but the difference is important. 2/2 indicates that the notes are grouped(therefore played) as 2 groups of 4 quavers whereas 4/4 would be 4 groups of 2. The same applies to hornpipes which normally are played somewhat slower. There are many examples of reels being notated in 4/4, I’ve even seen polkas in 4/4 but it is not correct. Jigs can be written in 4/4 using 4 groups of triplets but the convention in notating Irish music is to write them in 6/8 which is 6 quavers, but more importantly it is 2 GROUPS of 3 quavers. In relation to Llig’s point about classically trained musicians being unfamiliar with the genre, part of the problem is a unfamiliarity with this subtle difference in time signature. And unfamiliarity with ornamentation also.

Re: What is a reel?

what i didn’t understand when i got into irish music, even having had a few years of piano as a kid and thinking i knew about rhythm and time signatures, is this---

a lot of beginner teachers or sessioners will tell you that, “reels are in 4/4 time” or something like that. also, folk reels written out are often written in 4/4 time in eighth notes. sometimes, but not always, the transcription marks the time signature with a big “C” with a line through it that indicates “cut time.” But quite often in folk music transcriptions, they don’t bother with the big C.

So what’s there, is, the 4/4 time signature, and eight eighth notes in the measure, which the uninitiated quite understandably try to play in a four-pulse-per-measure grouping--a litttle pulse per pair of eight notes, four times in the measure, with the downbeats a hair more emphasized than the offbeats.

how it really should be written but only occasionally is, would be,--in groups of sixteenth notes. cut time is not just 4/4 time sped up, it is 4/4 time with the notes grouped differently---one faint pulse per four little sixteenth notes, twice per measure, not one pulse per pair of eighth notes four times per measure.

the other thing that may be confusing the OP is that irish reels are not syncopated the same in every region, every county, every community, or even from player to player.

dance-based folk music is syncopated. that means, some notes get more emphasis (usually by holding them a hair longer than the others), and others less (so they are sometimes sped up a tiny fraction to make up for the ones that are held a little longer). african-american blues and jazz musicians called this “swing,” and now many genres of music, including irish, use this word to denote that syncopated jiggering of the notes to give the music lift or movement for dancing.

syncopation or swing is like points on a spectrum in geometry, or beads on an abacus---there are an infinite number of hairsbreadth differences in how you can syncopate notes. there is a lot of 4/4-time dance music around the world. there are even a lot of reels in different genres around the world. but each tradition has its own way of distributing the swing.


my point is, not only are ireland’s reels swung different from the 4/4-or-cut-time dance music of other world genres, they are swung differently from region to region of ireland itself. some irish reels actually do sound a little less like running sixteenth notes (cut time), and more like true 4/4 time with dotting, more like a hornpipe. east galway and east clare reels sometimes sound like this, because they are playing at a more relaxed speed, so the pulse starts to sound more like true 4/4 time, but with dotting for lots of swing. but other times, you can hear the reel being played as cut-time--the fast, running-sixteenth-note sound. i think that can be confusing to someone trying to make sense of how to articulate, phrase, or syncopate irish reels. it was for me….

Re: What is a reel?

“And for the record, there are no ”types of reels“ - a reel is a reel. They do not break down into categories or types.”

I disagree. Irish reels span a range of types based on rhythmic and phrasing differences. There are flowing reels and boxy reels, reels where one-bar phrases predominate (e.g., Man of the House) and reels where multiple-bar phrases predominate (e.g., The Morning Star), reels that are heavily arpeggiated and reels that follow a much more linear melodic line. Finally, there are single reels and double reels, a distinction based on the number of bars and repeats in each part of the tune (which is partly based on differences in phrase length).

Most anyone who plays this music for long has had the experience of segueing from one reel into the next and lurching through an awkward change in pulse or “feel.” If you ignore how reels fall into various types of feel, you run the risk of lurching a lot. Not a pretty thing.

Of course, some reels are built in such a way that a good player can impart different feels to the same tune. To do this, it helps to think of reels as being in 4/4 because that gives you 4 beats in each measure, each of which can take an emphasis as you choose. The conventional notions of 2/2 being strong/weak and 4/4 being strong-weak-medium-weak simply do not apply to Irish traditional music played well. The emphasis moves around among the 1st and 3rd eighth note of each group of four in a reel, shifting as often as it’s consistent, within the feel of the given tune.

All of which is why it’s so important to listen to and feel the music, rather than being hidebound to the conventions of written notation and formal theory.

Ailin, this will sound like I’m dodging your question, but I’m not meaning to do that. I just don’t think the notions of cut and common time apply across the board to how reels are played in this music. The beat is more fluid than that. The better players move the accent, if you will, around. If you think of a reel having only one strong beat per measure (2/2), you limit what you can do to vary the pulse. If you thinking of it having a strong and medium beat per measure (4/4) you’ve instantly doubled your options. But Irish traditional musicians go further--they hear four beats per measure, and any one of those can be a strong beat. So you can play all of the following feels for a reel (each word represents an eighth note):
The first two are quite common in sessions, where the group wants to pulse in synch:
ONE and two and THREE and four and
one and TWO and three and FOUR and

But all of the following come into play when a good musician plays around with a tune:
ONE and two and three and four and
one and TWO and three and four and
one and two and THREE and four and
one and two and three and FOUR and
ONE and TWO and three and four and
ONE and two and three and FOUR and
one and two and THREE and FOUR and
ONE and TWO and THREE and FOUR and
one and TWO and THREE and four and
…and so on.

In the “nyahshops” I teach (e.g. at ZoukFest, Montana Fiddle Camp, and Portal Irish Music Week), I demonstrate some of the most common options by playing the C Part of Morning Dew and moving the emphasis around.

BAGF EFGA|BAFB ADFA|BAGF EFGA|BcdB ADFA|
1234 1234 1234 1234 1234 1234 1234 1234

Thinking of this bit of melody in 2/2 would lead you to always emphasize the first note of each measure, and give slightly less emphasis to the 1st note in the second group of 4 eighth notes in each measure.

Thinking in 4/4 opens up more possibilities, but good players go beyond and emphasize any of the 1st and 3rd notes in each group of four. It’s not random, but in service to the melody, with some variability allowed--welcome, really--so the pulse though steady is fluid, not static, not predictable.

Re: What is a reel?

“I reiterate, conventions are not necessarily established by agreement.”

This is a disagreement over terminology. In linguistics and social sciences, convention is associated with the meaning of a sign, with arbitrary associations between the word “apple” and the fruit which it names.
This sort of convention is necessarily a product of mind, of “agreement” speaking very loosely, although it is practically never the case that we consciously agree on the meaning of a word or a sign from scratch.
This is absolutely not the sort of signal that a flower uses to attract a pollinating insect or that a poisonous insect uses to alert a predator. In both cases, we notice, “deception” is possible, so we might think that this is a little like language. However, it is not the case that the insect or the flower can choose whether to deceive; this choice has been made for them. You can choose whether to say “apple” or “waxwork designed to look like an apple”, but the flower can only say “sweet stuff in here”, regardless of whether it’s true or not.

You might want to use “conventional” to refer to associations in the natural kingdom, but this is not at all what the linguist means by the word, and it’s not by any means a standard usage, so we might want to use some sort of typographical convention, such as capitalizing the word when used in your sense, to ensure that we know we’re talking about two very different things.

Knowledge is passed down directly from generation to generation in the animal kingdom as parents teach their children the things they will need to survive. But a new study has found that, even when the chain is broken, nature sometimes finds a way.

Zebra finches, which normally learn their complex courtship songs from their fathers, spontaneously developed the same songs all on their own after only a few generations.

“We found that in this case, the culture was pretty much encoded in the genome,” said Partha Mitra of Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory in New York, co-author of a study in Nature on Sunday.

Birds transmit their songs through social interactions, as humans do for languages, dances, cuisine and other cultural elements. Though birds and humans have clearly followed different evolutionary paths, birdsong culture can still inform theories of human culture.

Normally, male finches learn their complex courtship songs from their uncles and fathers. But if there are no vocal role models around, the song will deviate from the traditional song and be harsh to female finch ears. Each bird, then, must learn from his father or uncles, as they learned from their fathers, and so on — but this can only take us so far down the lineage.

“It’s the classic ‘chicken and the egg’ puzzle,” Mitra said. “Learning may explain how the son copies its father’s song, but it doesn’t explain the origin of the father’s song.”

Mitra’s team wanted to find out what would happen if an isolated bird raised his own colony. As expected, birds raised in soundproof boxes grew up to sing cacophonous songs.

But then scientists let the isolated birds give voice lessons to a new round of hatchlings. They found that the young males imitated the songs — but they tweaked them slightly, bringing the structure closer to that of songs sung in the wild. When these birds grew up and became tutors, their pupils’ song continue to conform, with tweaks.

After three to four generations, the teachers were producing strapping young finches that belted out normal-sounding songs.
