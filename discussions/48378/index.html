Style tips for long sets of Strathspeys & Reels

I know the heart of The Session is *Irish* Trad - but I usually get a pretty friendly response to questions involving related music. My question is based on my fondness for long sets of Strathspeys & Reels the inhabitants of Cape Breton share via YouTube, either in pubs, at festivals, or in someone’s kitchen. However - input from purists as well as fans of CB, Scottish and other subcategories more than welcome.

1) What is the upper end of how many tunes *you* *personally* like/tolerate in a set?
I get it - there are no laws - I could put 38 tunes in a set and no one can stop me - but if you were either in a pub or at a party and someone started playing - assuming they were good - how long would you actually be enjoying it before you’d think ‘ok we get it - take a break’.

2) What’s a good ratio of reels to strathspeys?
I’m very partial to strathspeys and reels. I know airs and/or marches often precede the strathspeys, and I don’t object to tacking one on - but none of them grab me the way strathspeys and reels do. Their inclusion would be to appease the M-S-R gods and it would be easy for me to use restraint. It’s the strathspeys & reels where I have little self control. I see some close to 50/50 but there are usually more (or many more) reels.
Again - I know there are no laws - but I would be interested in hearing the gut feelings of those with longer personal history in ITM (in the umbrella sense or in the very specific Irish sense - the flow of wisdom in this community is net from you to me - so I’ll take it all in for consideration).

Thanks.
-Heady

Re: Style tips for long sets of Strathspeys & Reels

In Cape Breton you would rarely get more than three of four strathspeys before transitioning to reels.

Strathspeys don’t play any part in square dances, but are danced to by solo stepdancers who use them to demonstrate their dancing chops. Starting slowly, the tempo should increase until the tension is released by the relaxed half time feel of the reels. Given limits of stamina, two or three strathspeys might be danced to, leaving enough energy left for a few reels.

So, to sound authentic, three or four strathspeys of increasing tempo, then any number of reels.

Re: Style tips for long sets of Strathspeys & Reels

I’d say that the limit for *any* kind of tunes in a single set at a session is 3, or maybe 4 at a push if the last tune is one that literally everyone there will know and enjoy playing.

Sessions, or *good* sessions anyway, are shared communal participatory events; and within the group there’ll be someone who’s considerably less fond, and/or less knowledgeable, of strathspeys than you are.

Of course if it’s your gig, or your album, put as many together as you like, but do watch out for people getting bored as number 6 or 7 clicks in.

Or if you’ve got a local equivalent of the wonderful Sheffield Sessions Festival, put yourself forward to run a Strathspeys Hour!

Re: Style tips for long sets of Strathspeys & Reels

It really depends on the situation, I’m afraid. The CB tradition is still closely tied to dance, and a dance fiddler will keep on with the jigs or reels till the dance is through; this has the fiddlers and other ‘insiders’ accustomed to sets of great length. At house parties or in bars, the music is often not the sustained focus of the entire event; for many revellers, it becomes part of the background, and thus it makes little difference to them how long a particular set goes on. In more of a ‘performance’ context, you might approach it differently if you’re playing in Mabou than if you’re playing in Poughkeepsie. In a session, again it would probably depend on who the other participants are, and on what the musical focus of the session is purported to be, and what its particular unwritten etiquette consists of. Rule of thumb: if you find yourself playing solo at a session, don’t stretch it out; wrap it up. Other than that, there’s no rules, really.

Re: Style tips for long sets of Strathspeys & Reels

Not just in Cape Breton, but most places where sessions are tied to dancing, longer sets aren’t at all unusual.

Any one player may string together 10 or 15 minutes of reels or jigs, and it’s also common for the set leader to give a look or a shoulder shrug to invite someone else to keep the tunes going. In this way, a set of tunes might run for 20 minutes or more, and I know of one bygone Irish session where 45 minute sets weren’t out of the norm.

There’s a traditional music organization in my area that sponsors a band for contra dances. It’s very open - once you attend two rehearsals you’re allowed to play for the dances and they’re very welcoming - you can play down beats if you can’t keep up - they just want people to get involved and grow and enjoy it.

So I’ve played a couple of dances with them. We usually do sets of 3 jigs or 3 reels with a waltz in the middle of the dance before a short break. We usually play each of the first 2 tunes in a set about 5 times and then the 3rd tune as many times as it takes to get to the end. The dance is an educational experience so they give the participants lots of instruction and time to get into a groove.

I decided for now I’ll just basically copy the set Andrea Beaton dedicated to Marc Boudreau at Celtic Colours in 2016.

I think I accurately tracked down the tunes from the names listed in the YouTube video and picking through the aka’s here. I went through the various tune settings and if one of the settings was posted by someone who seemed particularly knowledgeable about CB music I started with their setting.

The settings I liked best for Marry Me Now had a penultimate measure that I didn’t like so I replaced it with the penultimate from a different setting.

I did not necessarily look for settings that most matched Andrea’s performance - I just recognize some of usernames as people I’ve seen give a lot of good answers regarding CB tunes.

I have gotten *slightly* better at learning by ear after attending an online workshop where Caitlin Warbelow taught a way to use symbols as mnemonics to chunk the tune and work it into your head - but because I play a viola I wanted to play around with some music editing software to see if I could just jump octaves rather than drop a 5th - this way if I ever find myself in a session and one of these pops up I might know it in the key the fiddlers will most likely be playing.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BYQMncWCbj0&list=RDMMBYQMncWCbj0&start_radio=1

Originally I used the new thing I learned from your answers (tunes with 8 measure sections get played twice, with 4 measures, thrice in STM) but I would either have to play it too fast to sound good (or at least too fast for *me* to sound good), or it would be closer to 6 minutes, so I put everything in twice.

When I play Irish music I find most of the A parts can be played as-is on a viola, but most of the B parts have to be dropped an octave. With these tunes MOST of it need to be dropped an octave. Sometimes that makes it less playable because what were odd fingers become even fingers and the string crossings are different - but it came out more or less playable. A few parts are in the original octaves and I played around with the software until the transitions tune to tune weren’t awkward.

Thinking of dancing rather than sessions, the classic multi-reel set in Scottish dancing is The Eightsome Reel: you would usually start and finish with the same tune, which is played AABBB to fit the dance, then you need 8 more tunes in between, usually played either AABBAB or ABABAB, again to fit the dance figures. Have fun with that!

Re: Style tips for long sets of Strathspeys & Reels

Has anyone mentioned the March(es) which sometimes start off these epic sets? Or sometimes airs? Or sometimes both?

Air > Marches > Strathspeys > Reels.

The other thing is the keys.

With ITM players usually jump around between various keys, while in CB music oftentimes some, most, or all of the set will be in the same key, for example “A”.

However there will be shifts in modes, say, starting in A Major then A Mixolydian then A Dorian. Sometimes the final tune returns to Major.

Also after a Strathspey or two (or three) that dwell around the A and E strings they might go down to a Strathspey (or two) that dwells around the G and D strings. Ditto with the Reel portion of the set.
