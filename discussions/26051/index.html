Re: noob wants to know what a “set” is

A set is two or more tunes played one after the other. Note: say ‘tunes’ not songs, unless you were singing. Typically in Irish music you stay with type, a jig with a jig, a hornpipe with a hornpipe. Typically you do a key change between tunes but not always. There are some famous sets by great players that have become standards, and your friends may have been discussing some aspect of one of these.

Re: noob wants to know what a “set” is

I think different players have different ideas of what makes up a good set and how important it is to put together a good set. I play with some people who seem to play just whatever comes to mind, and others spend a lot of time composing their sets. Then, as you mention, many people just play the sets that they learned from this or that or the other record, and expect you to know, for example, everything the Bothy Band ever recorded. To me, that gets a little old, but if they’re enjoying it, who am I to complain?

Anyway, if you’re just learning tunes, it’s best to just learn tunes. Once you have three jigs, put them together in all the logical combinations and call the one that you like best a “set”. Nobody will complain - if they do, they’re a yutz, and you can tell them I said so. :) (Okay, they might complain if you pick three tunes that someone just played, some people are fussy about that).

After you have six jigs down, then you’re going to have to start thinking about putting them in sets, but by then you’ll probably have some opinions about which way they go, and you know what? Those opinions will be correct.

Re: noob wants to know what a “set” is

Anybody who laughs at these questions is an idiot. Everyone learned some time, and you’re doing it now. Good for you.

For key changes, there are people who will cite rules like “change up a fifth for excitement” (ie, sets should go from G to D to A), but as long as the tunes flow nicely from one to the next, you’re okay in my book. You’re likely to be staying in the “whistle modes” - the modes of D and G that are well suited to a D whistle. There are tunes in D minor, and some of them are good ones (learn Julia Delaney, that’s a great reel and not too hard to play) and there are some great reels in A major, and then there are the flat-key tunes (which seem mostly to be in the Scottish tradition, and its offshoots in Cape Breton music), but for now you’re best sticking with D and G and the related modes. You’ll know which are the good ones to learn, though, because they’ll be the ones you hear at the sessions where you are.

Oh, here’s a tip for getting the rhythm. Just think of the great double dactylically-named composer of jigs, Paddy O‘Furniture. If his name fits the tune (“paddy o’furniture, paddy o’furniture”) then it’ll be a jig. If it goes “applebutter applebutter” it’s a reel. If it goes “dup dee ai-deedle-up-di-dee deedle-up dee ai deedle up dee ay” it’s probably a polka. And if it goes by itself, it’s probably Leonard Cohen’s keyboard.

Re: noob wants to know what a “set” is

I don’t wish to sound heretical, but there’s no law that says you can’t mix up tune types if you want. They do the strathspey-reel combination a lot in Scottish music. A lot of recordings have some nice mixtures on them. The Bothies did that jig-plus-six-reels one. There’s a cracking set with a jig going into reels on Irish Times by Patrick Street. The Chieftains did it all the time. In Cork ‘n’ Kerry they mix up polkas and slides.
Typically, “slower” tune types will lead into a reel or two at the end. You can even start with a slow air (though if it’s a really poignant tune you don’t want to be erasing its effect too abruptly). Hornpipes into reels can work really (reely?) well. A lot of people think that this is wrong, but then a lot of people believe in flying saucers. There are no rules reely.

Re: noob wants to know what a “set” is

Sets are just one way for a group of people to decide what they are going to play. It’s easier to say ‘such and such a set’ than this tune, that, tune, followed by another tune. General consensus is usually the deciding factor, and as newcomers join in, they also learn a whole set, or whichever tunes they don’t already play.
You’ll know if two tunes don’t go together because the transition sounds awful / weird / contrived / wrong. Other times you know they do go together because your fingers / brain are being led along that path. Sometimes combinations arise by happenstance and the pleasant surprise stays with you forever. As Steve Shaw says, there are no rules. You decide.

Re: noob wants to know what a “set” is

Lrichtn - Of course the word ‘Set’ doesn’t just apply to Irish Trad. It applies to ‘most’ types of music where a selection, or set of tunes are played. However, in Irish Traditional it can apply to certain Solo Dances. These Set Dances are danced by an individual dancer to a particular tune. ‘The set dance called the Blackbird for instance is danced to the tune ’The Blackbird. There are many types of these particular solo dances. Then there are the figure dances which are danced by a group of dancers. Some of these figure dances can also have the word Set included in the name of the dance such as ‘The Cavan Set’ etc etc. The tunes played for these type of ‘Sets’ can consist of Reels, Jigs, or Hornpipes. Mixing tune types

My 2cents on this issue - mixing tune types is all very well if you are playing solo, or if you and your friends have settled on a specific set. But for improvised sets or sets that you bring to a session, sticking to a tune type is less likely to confuse your session mates. And, after all, this is still the traditional thing in Ireland - certainly if you play to dancers you need to stick to a tune type! (well there are exceptions to this too, but only on the stage as far as I can tell.)
