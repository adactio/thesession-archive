Well, on the one hand, it’s good to encourage people to make music together.

But on the other hand, it’s also good to have a bit of filter so that sessions can happen among *musicians* and not just anyone with two rocks to bang together.

Not that there aren’t times and places for banging rocks together and generally just unleashing quasi-musical noise on the universe. But I would hope that we can also have times and places for something a skooch more focused than a free-for-all.

So the way I’ve read the comments here over the years is that, yes, if you play this music and are willing to use your ears, then you’ll be welcome at most sessions. And if you desperately want to show off your repertoire of Bill Monroe songs or play boom-chuck accompaniment all night or test drive your new Highland pipes chanter, then no, you’ll be better off not crashing the party.

As for posting sound clips, I suspect a trice of things tend to stifle that.

One is that most average (and even good) session players don’t want some nitwit on a web site to compare them to the professional (i.e., mega-rehearsed, studio-glossy) recordings so many people listen to. When I throw a sound clip together, it takes all of 10 minutes to turn on the pocket recorder, play the tune, cuss at the wobbly bits, load it to the laptop, save it as a compressed (lower sound quality) mp3 file, and upload it to some web page. So of course it’s not going to sound like the latest RCA-produced year-long project by name-your-favorite-player. I think it was Ambrose Bierce’s definition of an amateur: someone who keeps doing what they love even though the results are mostly disappointing. :-) Frankly, I’d rather you follow yhallhouse’s advice and visit my local session, or send me an invite to yours, so we can hear one another live, face to face. At least the beer will be good.

Second, we’ve had a long but narrow history of a few folks whose sound clips revealed that their own playing didn’t support the dogmatic vehemence with which they proclaimed their advice on how to play well. So of course this forum called them on it and cried “pants!” It would be wrong, however, to mistake this response for the real timbre of this forum. Overall, people have been complimentary and supportive of most efforts posted by other members.

The other stopper may simply be that, for many of us, recording ourselves and broadcasting it to the world runs against the grain of why we play this music at all. It’s not about becoming a rock star or concert virtuoso. It’s not about trying to impress anyone or preserving perfect ripples of music. And it’s not about using the latest technology to reach a wide but possibly shallow audience. Instead, it’s about hanging out with friends over pints and tunes and having a good time making our own music, for ourselves.

Sure there are upsides of posting a sound clip, but my hunch is that the potential pitfalls outweigh those for most folks.

Well Jerone, I teach people to play who’ve never before touched a fiddle, mandolin, guitar, banjo, or flute, and I get them started on tunes right away. And in decades of teaching, I’ve never had a student who needed scales and arpeggios to sort out where the notes are. I would guess that you’re opinions on this are influenced no small amount by how you learned piano.

In fact, it’s usually easier in the beginning to play a tune that only uses certain fingers, rather than having to note every finger as in a scale. For instance, I may start a new fiddler with Amazing Grace in G. The whole tune is played without using a middle finger. On fiddle, this is advantageous because the middle finger moves between two positions--high, near the ring finger (for F# on the D string) and low, near the index finger (for G on the E string). Instead of dealing with that right out of the box, I get people playing a real piece of music using just open strings and their index and ring fingers, which always land in the same places, on any string.

I’ve also found that, for most people, it’s easier to more accurately hear a melody they’ve already sung and know by heart, than to accurately hear the whole and half steps of a scale. This is particularly important on a fretless instrument such as fiddle.

After a new fiddler is comfortable with where their index and ring fingers go in G and D major, I explain the two placements of the middle finger and we play music that reinforces those placements.

I introduce scales and arpeggios only of someone asks and wants to play them, or if they want to play music that improvises over chord progressions (such as bluegrass, swing, etc.).

Since Irish traditional music doesn’t typically improvise over chord progressions, honing your scales doesn’t help much *for playing this music.*

Jerone, as a music teacher for many years, I’ve found that people do better finding the notes and understanding the layout of notes on their instrument by playing simple tunes that they already have in their heads. A point worth considering--for the rest of the folks here who actually *do* consider other’s comments and ideas--is that the last 60 years or so have brought a significant change to how this music is taught. Hiring celebrity teachers also raises the question of priorities. Do we all aim to be recording artists and performing stars? If not, then why look solely to recording artists and performing artists for instruction? “Renown” shouldn’t be the primary criterion for choosing a teacher.

Understanding keys and modes can be useful in this music, but that’s not a reason to practice scales. In fact, you can understand keys and modes without playing any scales at all. That said, understanding keys and modes is more useful for chording accompanists than for melody players.

Exactly. I think the point we keep coming back to is that in this music, by definition, everything you need is in the tunes.

A bebop player or a post-bop player needs to shed arpeggios because they’re going to be assembling solos at times out of those arpeggios, and the chords they’ll be arpeggiating won’t be just “the chords of the tune”, they’ll be running a bunch of extensions and such off of the ii-V-I that they started with, so they need to have those patterns right there, on the tip of their tongue, or at their fingertips, depending what they play.

Playing trad, you have a tune. If you want to play a tune, you learn it, and then you play it. And if you want to learn to play the tune better, you play it with lots of people and you listen and you get ideas. After you’ve been playing for some amount of time, you eventually can listen to your own head for ideas, and they’ll be there.

If you’re interested in learning theory, that’s great, not a problem, but it’s a metalanguage, not the thing itself. If you want to speak Spanish, it’s no good to get a linguistics degree. I know, I’ve done both :)

FWISW (@ fiddlelearner: “for what it’s worth” :p) someone very close to me is an accomplished player and teacher.

This playing teacher teaches kids by ear until they reach a certain age at which point the kids start reading and, learning basic scales and arpeggios, the emphasis is on enjoyment which may hopefully lead to enthusiasm in both traditional music and the instrument, generally.

The teaching of scales and arpeggios, whilst useful from a theoretical point of view, and all that jazz, is a method sometimes employed by this teacher to have the pupils sharpen up on position fluency accuracy and intonation.

This has proven to be a very successful method for this individuals pupils. The idea of using a scale or arpeggio is that it’s a simple way of approaching accuracy/intonation etc without interfering with ear learning and lesson time, as the scale or arpeggio playing exercises mostly happen at home whilst the tune learning remains the bulk activity at the lessons (also these short exercises are more likely to be practiced at home by the lazier kids).

Gradually the kids sharpen up, and by this stage, the kids usually have a fair wee repertoire under their belt, are able to play by ear, read music to varying degrees of ability and understand, to some extent from a technical point view, whats happening under the fingers. It must be stressed the emphasis remains on playing and enjoyment throughout.

Adult beginners and adults coming to the fiddle with previous experience may or may not be given scale arpeggio lessons as part of their home work, it seems to vary, depending on the individual and the expectations of the individual.

Some of the fiddling kids are also put through the formal mainstream qualification system in tandem with learning trad, thats down to the parents wishes and, expectations. Scales positions and arpeggios are a big part of that.

I don’t see what all the fuss is about myself, music is music after all. Whether or not tunes have an absolute tonal centre is fairly irrelevant IMO, it’s all music and can all be expressed using the standard western music system.

I occasionally find myself listening from an adjacent room whilst the home lessons, rather than the formal schools lessons, are under way and I can assert that learning the odd scale or arpeggio, doesn’t seem to have had a negative affect. Jerone, there’s a principle in programming which a lot of people subscribe to now. It’s called “YAGNI”, or “You Ain’t Gonna Need It”. The idea is that instead of spending a lot of time writing code in advance of need, you write the code that you need now, to do the thing you need to do now.
This ends up saving a lot of time and making for cleaner code, because you end up writing only the code that you actually need.

I think the same idea could apply here. If you’re actually going to play tunes that go “D E F# G A B c# d”, then you’ll learn to do that when you play those tunes. On the other hand, if you’re not going to play that sequence of notes, time spent getting them down is time that you don’t spend learning tunes. And time spent honing them and getting them perfect is more of the same. When you need the notes on the low string is a good time to learn them. Before then, you don’t need them.

Think about this: would you tell a beginning fiddler that it’s essential to woodshed the A flat scale? Probably not. If they ever need it, they’ll learn it, and if they don’t ever need it, it’s not getting them what they really do need. Would you tell a novice to woodshed the sequence of notes “Gb a F b Eb c# d” just in case they might need it? Well, of course not. It might be cool to be able to play Escher-esque runs in two keys simultaneously in different octaves, but you’re never going to need that in a fiddle tune, so why bother? Now, would you tell a fiddler to practice the sequence “F#AA2 BAF#A dfed BcdB” just in case they’ll ever need it?
No, you wouldn’t. When they come to learn the Glasgow Piper, they’ll get that bit down. Until then, they don’t need it.

Yes, the first time you come across a new thing, it’s tough to learn, but if you learn it first when you first need it, you learn just what you need to, and therefore you’re always learning stuff you need. That means that everything you do is aimed at your goal, which to me sounds like it can be summed up as “learn more tunes better, now”.

And that’s the logic of turning your attention to learning tunes. Part of the logic, anyway. There’s more, but I think I’ll let others make that case if they want, or I’ll come back to it another time if you want.

Now, am I against learning scales? No, of course not. I think a fiddler who’s interested in looking at the ideas of Western classical harmony needs to have a solid understanding of scales and chords and whatnot, because that’s the language in which those ideas are best understood, and I don’t think there’s anything wrong with having or wanting to have that understanding. What I am saying is that if you want to learn this music, you don’t need to get that stuff. What you do need is tunes.

I’ve always encouraged people to learn some basics of scales and chords, and I still think that’s a good idea, but when you talk about what’s needed to play tunes, I have to admit that the only thing you need is simply “tunes.” Everything else, interesting as it might be, is optional.

"Soloing- the scary bit!

The interesting part and, to the rule-bound classical player, the really terrifying part, is when you have to leave the melody behind you and, like a hang glider pilot jumping off a precipice, take off into the unknown. This is the part where the little kids in the audience stop chattering, the hard-of-hearing turn up their hearing aids and every single person stops to focus-- on you. No pressure!
Actually this part is not half as difficult as as some people would have you believe. Most tunes have a tonal centre which runs all the way through, and to which you can constantly refer. In practice, this means that with a tune like Dinah , Lady Be Good or Autumn Leaves you can play the same major or minor scale all the way through the chord sequence. The notes will sound different in the context of different chords, but basically you’ll find that you aren’t playing any wrong notes at all. It’s a very useful exercise to try alternating one or two bars of melody with one or two bars of the major scale. It’s a first step towards improvising, and gets you used to the idea of moving a step away from the melody, whilst remaining aware of where you are in the sequence. At this stage you will need to have some chordal and rhythmic backing whilst you’re playing; I would strongly recommend the software Band in a Box (see below).

The Pentatonic Scale

Try the same thing using not a major scale, but a pentatonic scale. ( a five-note scale with no semitone (half-step) intervals. You’ll find that this is not only even easier (once your fingers get used to the patterns), but is also far more melodic; a simple pattern of any two or three adjacent notes in the scale will create their own melody.

If you’ve ever been to any of my lessons or workshops, you’ll know I bang on for ages about these scales. If you don’t know what one is, the G major scale would be G,A,B,D,E,G. You can transpose this to any key using the same intervals of tone, tone, tone and a half, tone, tone. It’s the same as the black notes on the piano. Practice these scales in first position up and down and round and round ; on two strings, three strings and four strings so that your fingers know them without any effort on your part. Then even more useful, paractice them in what I call a closed position- with your fingers locked in maybe second or third position, using no open strings. The great advantage of this is that, once you’ve learned the patterns out of first position, soloing in the key of C sharp or F sharp is literally as easy as C or F, so long as you stay in position. When I want to show off to other fiddlers at a gig I occasionally play a blues which ascends by a semitone every twelve bars, so I go through all the keys. People who don’t know how hard it is to play in those weird keys are not impressed. People who do are Very Impressed, whilst people who’ve figured out how I do it just yawn and look nonchalent.

Then try the blues scale by adding a flattened third to the major pentatonic. Again this will fit easily anywhere over your simple chord sequence, and now it is starting to sound really jazzy.

This approach to the melodic side of improvisation I laughingly call the Snowplough Method, since it shoulders aside any chord in its path with careless abandon. It’s a great starting point and will give a jazz violin beginner a lot of confidence to keep going. It’s the natural starting point for a violinist, who is used to thinking only about melodies, not chords. However, beware..there may be trouble ahead! Not all those plain white virgin snowfields are as safe as they look, and believe me, there are plenty of rocks hiding just under the surface!"
