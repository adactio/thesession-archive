The Laws of Motion: Fiddle Bowing Laws

Disclaimer: I’m not giving any advice, or trying to make any rules. I’m just sharing the physical laws that I’ve found.

When I heard that proper bowed triplets had to always start on a down bow, I gave up on them until I knew for sure that that was the case. A few months ago it was confirmed by a workshop instructor that indeed, proper bowed triplets are required to start on a down bow to come out cleanly.

Still frustrated, and a little intimidated, but having no other choice but to get over it, I decided to look deep into what the bow was doing; which direction it would end up in after certain phrases and articulations.

(Since proper bowed triplets require a down bow, and the 1st and 3rd beats are the beats we emphasis,
we’ll assume *for this discussion* that all reels start on a down bow, unless the first note of the reel is a quarter note* Also, every line of bow notation is it’s own line of notation, and is separate from the one above or below it. I also wrote this assuming that we all understand note values and how they relate to each other.)

^3=Bowed Triplet
~ =Roll

This is what I found over the last couple of months of practicing Bowed Triplets.

Bowed Triplets change your starting bow direction:
D-U-^3 U-D-U-D…
^3-U-D U-D-U-D…
So, for every one Bowed Triplet; one Quarter Note, one 2 Note Slur, or one Single Cut is required to set up for the next Bowed Triplet.
D-U-^3 U-U-^3
^3-U-U D-U-^3

3 Note Slurs, Dotted Quarter Notes, and Rolls don’t change starting bow direction:
D-D-D-U D-U-D-U…
~-U D-U-D-U…
So 3 Note Slurs, Dotted Quarter Notes, and Rolls won’t upset your bow direction for an upcoming Bowed Triplet,
D-D-D-U ^3-U-U D…
but neither will they correct your bow direction.
^3-U-D U-U-U-D U…

2 Note Slurs and Single Cuts correct bowing:
U-D-U-U D-U-D-U D…
D-D-U-D U-U-D-U D…
but they also upset bowing
D-U-D-D U-D-U-D U…
See the paradox?

I took these notes because I noticed a lot of patterns. I figured that it wasn’t as complicated as my brain perceived, so I decided to write everything down in an effort to make things easier and more tangible.

So that was it. Nothing to be afraid of or intimidated by. It’s all very simple. Now my brain can take a rest.
Since everything is written down and confirmed, I won’t have to second guess my bowing anymore if i’m consistent with practicing. It will become second nature much sooner, now that I know for sure what’s going on with the bow.

Look at it in purely physical terms -- the bow moves across a string, stops and moves back. The only difference between an up- bow and down-bow is gravity. Since you are supposed to be in control of the bow, it is no great hardship to counter the effects of gravity when you are moving the bow at an angle less than perpendicular through a distance of a few millimetres. This ‘start the triplet on a down-bow’ nonsense should be put to rest once and for all.

Re: The Laws of Motion: Fiddle Bowing Laws

I didn’t realise I was famous eb?!

As for this “inherent bounce”, I’ll do my best to explain.

Do I start on a down or an up bow? I certainly never actively think about it, nor do I find myself falling apart if the bow was moving the wrong way at the beginning. If I were to film it and slow it down, my bow does of course move across the strings (it has to, otherwise it wouldn’t really make a sound!), so it isn’t *just* bouncing.

Jim wrote: “I don’t understand that. Surely the bow is in contact with the string all the way through the triplet, often digging in quite hard?” - the “gaps” in a triplet played in the conventional sense occur because the bow changes direction. The gaps in my interpretation occur when the applied pressure changes. I’ve found that rapidly changing the pressure myself at speed isn’t really possible to get the full triplet. What is possible though is altering the pressure at the start and allowing the bow to spring back on its own and oscillate - this makes it “skip” across the strings as I move my bow and create the gaps in the ornament. This all happens within about 1cm of bow, as otherwise I’d lose control.

Is it a true bowed triplet? I’ll leave that up to others, like I said it makes the sound I want, the effect is really to introduce breaks into an otherwise long note. It could split it into 2, 3, 4, 5, etc. Having said that, I use this ornament in a range of cases:

1. On a long note, first bar of the Flooded Road to Glenties (on the B): A | F~B3 cfec |
https://thesession.org/tunes/3440

2. On a shorter note, first bar of the B part of the Banshee (on the A): | ea2g efgf |
https://thesession.org/tunes/8

3. On a single quaver in a jig, e.g. Atholl Highlanders first bar (on the C): | e3 ecA |
https://thesession.org/tunes/107

Introduce a very percussive cut on the note et voilà, you have a cran :)

Regarding the little finger, it isn’t always on the bow…

Finally, regarding U-D-U or D-U-D, look at a triplet run in a hornpipe, that may often have consecutive sets of triplets. Would you play this D-U-D D-U-D? Highly unlikely methinks. D-U-D U-D-U is the way I’d do it. Now, why not play those triplets faster and faster, perhaps on the same note rather than a series of different ones. Surely you can still start with an up-bow?

I think it is perhaps explained best using the inspiration for the way I play: check this clip of Tommy Peoples http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jHqFlS3gNMs - it’s a good clip in that there’s a pretty decent close-up.

In the first tune, watch his trebles closely (yes they’re quick!), and the way the stick moves. The way I understand it, and how I get that sound, is that on applying pressure to the bow, it pushes back, springs off the string and then falls back under gravity. So really what I’m doing is applying pressure before the ornament, such that it leaves the string at the start of the ornament and then the “bounce” is the rest of the ornament.

It’s a stutter, most certainly, and arguably the real skill is not in producing the stutter but in controlling it so that it doesn’t dominate the phrase. Tommy does it perfectly.

I think some of the confusion might come from people having different idea as to what a bowed triplet is.

If you take the view that it is three notes in quick succession, then it is very possible, and quite normal to do it on both the up and down bow.

Here is a simple looped 4-bar riff in 6/8 that my teacher gave me to help with independence of up and down bows, showing in particular how accents change according to the direction of the bow:

|: A)Bc dcB | ABc (dcB) | AB(c d)cB | ABc dc(B :|

I don’t know whether it is possible in ABC to indicate up and down bows, so I’ll have to describe what’s happening:

Bar 1 - starts with a down bow;
Bar 2 - the last three notes are slurred with an up bow;
Bar 3 - notes 3 and 4 are slurred on a down bow;
Bar 4 - starts with an up bow, and the last note is a down bow slurred with the first note of bar 1 when looping back to the beginning.

The exercise can be varied by transposing or rewriting the riff so as to use string crossings.

A triplet is merely 3 notes, nothing more than that really. They can be played either way DUD or UDU.
Try simply bowing a slip jig rhythm on an open string; DUD, UDU, DUD, then UDU DUD UDU, while accentuate and emphasising the 1st beat of each bar and to a lesser extent the first of each 3 triplets.so the underlying 3/4 rhythm is intact.

The jig ; Dud, Udu | Dud Udu |

Re: The Laws of Motion: Fiddle Bowing Laws

Thinking more, maybe he is actually using his wrist: as your forearm is moving the bow one way, there are two different ways your wrist can drive it the opposite way momentarily:

The first is a lateral movement (hold your forearm above and parallel to the desk with your palm facing down, then bend your wrist right and left, with the palm still parallel to the desk). That is the movement I use - I also play hurdy gurdy, and that is exactly the wrist movement that is used to accelerate the handle and fire the trompette while your arm keeps turning at a constant speed. Doing it this way your fingers are then used to keep the bow parallel to the bridge.

The second way is to rotate your wrist (hold your arm parallel to the desk again, but this time rotate your hand so that the thumb comes up). If you used this wrist movement with a rigid grip on the bow, you would either lift the bow off the strings, or ‘dig in’, depending on bow direction. So as your wrist rotates one way, your fingers need to rotate the the bow the other way to keep it in proper contact with the strings. This, I think, is what we are seeing in TPs hand.
