As has often been mentioned here, it’s unlikely you’ll find a session with all these popular tunes played in one night. There are more than 50, of course, and more like a few hundred.
Also, depending on where you are, it may be “popular” Scottish tunes or something else.

However, when you eventually get acquire a fairly large repertoire of tunes(popular or otherwise), it means that you can go to more sessions anywhere/any time and be able to play for at least part of the night.

Re: Where do so many people learn Julie Delaney/Drowsie Maggie/Road to Lisdoonvarna etc etc etc etc etc etc

I think the bottom line is that if you want to go to sessions and play tunes jointly with other people (as opposed to rotating solo performances, for which anybody can choose to play anything) then you need to assess which are the tunes that everybody else is likely to play.

Things never stand still, but after a while a picture of core sets will emerge and then you need to pick which ones to make an effort to learn. Make the wrong decisions and you will find little to join in with. Make the right decisions and you will be able to play along with many sets. There may need to be a compromise between picking tunes you really really like and tunes that everybody is likely to know.

Some tunes may be a little over-played but they do give you a ‘passport’ into other sessions

Even the well-played tunes can be injected with interest by the right players in the right moment.

There’s also a perverse tendency amongst some, I think, to pick tunes that people can’t easily join in with - like ‘bad’ keys for others to play along with or notes dropping below D to trip up whistles - or conversely picking up a weird key whistle - then there’s the capo 3 brigade…

My view of some of the tunes mentioned above:

Julia Delaney - brilliant tune with a lot of scope for variation
Drowsie Maggie - lots of variations possible (and different versions) but it can be a bit wooden - some people do a speed challenge with it - generally playing it fast but badly
Road to Lisdoonvarna - both the slide and the reel are really good tunes - I don’t find them overplayed in my area
Out on the Ocean - it’s ok - at least nearly everybody can play it
Silver Spear - ditto
Maid Behind the Bar - there is more to this tune than just in the familiar rendition so I’m still finding depth in it
Jimmy Ward’s - I usually get half way through this before I remember what it is
Old Favourite - not fussed
Shaskeen - never played it - can’t even think how it goes
Kitty’s Gone a Milking - ditto
Harvest Home - there are hornpipes I like less (The Belfast for example)
Coleraine - never played in my presence as far as I know
Kesh Jig - I’m going to see the Bothy Band in January and really hoping they will play their classic set…..
Swallowtail Jig - it’s ok if not played too fast - some people round here have got hold of a strange middle 8 type bridge for it which I don’t really like that much
Flowers of Edinburgh - used to play it - hasn’t come up much recently
Soldier’s Joy - has become a comedy tune round here (deliberate wrong notes) : I don’t like comedy tunes - but this tune works well for clog dancing and there are different version of it
Off to California - it’s ok - I prefer the Led Zeppelin version
Red Haired Boy - I actually like this one as you can flip between Irish and bluegrass styles with quite a lot of variation
Shebeg Shemore - I find it a bit tedious but people like it so I usually try to join in
Free Bird - I saw Lynyrd Skynyrd play live in 1977 and they were sensational - not heard it at any sessions yet but De Dannan have probably covered it
Jumping Jack Flash - deceptively hard tune to play well I think
Stairway to Heaven - good tune, less keen on the words - De Dannan again? I’m sure it would translate to an Irish set very well. When I was young it was the staple of young males encamped in music shops - being perverse I therefore never bothered to learn it. Rick Wakeman has an interesting piano version so I might try it on keyboards

I also find that some very familiar tunes like Star of Munster (which probably comes into some peoples‘ hate lists) have absolutely brilliant ’other’ versions like in G minor - but expect to be playing solo if you are not careful

I remember the early days of “The ALP” which was the forerunner of The Scots Music Group “Here in Scotland”.

One of my favourite early tutors was the late Angus Grant Junior(Sadly missed from Shooglenifty). His policy was to to teach good, interesting tunes regardless of whether they were popular or not. So, we got quite a variety.

↳ Posted by Wodanaz on November 17th, 2023.

Re: Where do so many people learn Julie Delaney/Drowsie Maggie/Road to Lisdoonvarna etc etc etc etc etc etc

Many of these sets came from albums by seminal bands of the 70s and 80s, the Chieftains, Planxty, Bothy Band etc, who were new and exciting and whose interpretations attracted people. It was harder to discover new music back then, there were fewer albums being produced and the most influential ones had a huge impact.

These sets then became self-perpetuating, because they became sessions standards which everyone knew, so much so that it becomes almost impossible to change to a different tune because everyone is expecting follow on with the usual one. They have become the core repertoire for many session players, not just as individual tunes but as entire sets. Individual sessions also have their own standards, which may comprise lesser-known tunes, but if you want to be able to drop into a session anywhere then you need to know these.

This doesn’t just apply to Irish music. I play English music, and there are still many standard session sets which can be traced straight back to albums by 1980s bands such as Old Swan and New Victory Band.

It is easy for more experienced players to become jaded and to seek out more unusual tunes. However these tunes became standards in the first place because they are damn good tunes, and when put together by some superb musicians they make damn good sets.

Look at a few of the tunes you’ve listed on the “Recordings” section of this website and see what albums they are on…they’ve all been recorded by the most popular Irish Trad Musicians/Bands ever. The Chieftains, The Bothy Band, Matt Molloy, Kevin Burke, Noel Hill, Altan, Michael Coleman, John Whelan, Frankie Gavin/DeDannan, etc.

As others have said it comes down to the fact that they are simple tunes with strong melodies that are ubiquitous and people have them in common. It’s great to hear and learn uncommon tunes, but it generally is more difficult to find people to play them with and they are much harder to learn since there aren’t as many sources and their melodies tend to be more complex and difficult to get under your fingers.

Re: Where do so many people learn Julie Delaney/Drowsie Maggie/Road to Lisdoonvarna etc etc etc etc etc etc

I envy those of you who came across this music in your youth. Growing up in the USA’s Deep South, I never encountered it in my younger days. I was closing in on 60 when I visited Ireland for the first time and fell in love with the music.

I’ve been a singer and musician in other genres since childhood (with a big gap when Life had other plans for me), and I’ve always enjoyed making music with other people, whether in orchestra and band, choir and chorus, or folk jams and sing-alongs. The social aspect of ITM is part of its appeal for me, so from the start I was interested in learning tunes that I might encounter in a session. The massive daily slow sessions at The Swannanoa Gathering’s Celtic Week gave me a good idea of what tunes were commonly played by beginning players from all over, so that informed my initial “tunes to learn” list. (At the time, I wasn’t able to attend a session regularly.). The city where I live now has a strong session scene, so most of the tunes I’m learning these days are those played in sessions locally. That’s why I learn them - so I can play music with others.
