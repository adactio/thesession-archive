Keeping practicing interesting 7 days a week?

Since being inside more I made it my goal to practice at least 1 hour per day.

This may be small fry for some people but I think it is a fair amount without being too daunting for a daily schedule. Oftentimes I would end up practicing an hour per day cumulatively but not in one sitting, but now I am doing one sitting and do find it much more productive generally than little pecks through the whole day - although I also do the pecks too.

What I find challenging is staying motivated when cycling the same few tunes. I will, say, learn 5 tunes by ear and say “I will not switch until I master these 5!” but after a few days that becomes tedious as my willpower wains.

I actually have been learning more tunes and different tunes types (as you may have noticed my slow airs thread). That is the best way to keep things fresh and I suppose no more is needed now I think about it as that has been working well for me; it is just that I have in the past felt guilty for leaving the last set o tunes I learned before I had learned to play them well but likewise I was having to force myself to practice them by that point.

My previous rational was that if you keep learning new tunes then you will not master any but I don’t think I hold that view any more as you are always honing your craft on the instrument aren’t you even if you only have a superficial understanding of a given tune before moving to the next - sure you want to settle on at least a handful to learn deeply but another thing I find is often I don’t really like the tune I may have learned.

What I mean is I might have given one a quick listen on youtube and just learned it maybe because it is a popular session tune rather than it having really spoken to me, but it is harder to find the latter type of tunes, so good to learn both/many and I guess you will naturally settle into practicing more the ones you really like and on the way it is still aiding your progress by learning ones you aren’t that fond of.

The more I learn is the more I will hone in on the characteristics of what I like about a tune.

Re: Keeping practicing interesting 7 days a week?

Practice should enjoyable. If you’re not enjoying practising/playing then you won’t learn well.
You are right not to keep playing through the same few tunes. You are not going to see much noticable day to day improvement to satisfy your lust for playing. It takes time for skills to be absorbed so having a variety of different things that you enjoy to stretch out your practice sessions will help avoid tedium and frustration.

The current lockdown has allowed me more practice time than I’ve had in ages. I’ve been getting to grips with some pieces that I’ve been tinkering with on and off for years. But I’ll maybe only run through a particular piece twice in one day, maybe focusing on one or two particular sections that needs work and playing through those slowly. But then I’ll give my brain a rest and switch to something totally different that’s not as challenging.

If I’m not enjoying a piece or tune then I’ll ditch it (unless I’m getting paid to play it of course). Playing should be a pleasure.

Re: Keeping practicing interesting 7 days a week?

The things that have helped me:
Record the tune and post it/share with a small group of others. Record yourself playing one of your most troublesome tunes, make lots of mistakes then check it later to prove how much you’ve advanced. (You’ll be surprised).
Work on lots of easy ones rather than a few difficult ones.
Learn tunes with the names of ex girlfriends -it works, the tunes you will NEVER forget!
Try not to do alternative things that are more interesting.
Big mix of different types of study, reading, scales, writing tab/notation from tunes in films, songs etc.
Good instrument, enjoy the tone, ALWAYS play musically, get your whole body going.
Try to remember tunes by visualising the TAB, notation, by ear, by in-the-air finger movement.

Don’t let anyone try to convince you that you should do this or that.

Re: Keeping practicing interesting 7 days a week?

During our “shelter at home” time, I’m reading through all 800 tunes in the two volumes of “Smoke in Your Eyes” on my 5-row C-system chromatic button accordion.

My goal is to play every tune twice, both melody and left-hand chords (CBA has a Stradella-style bass) perfectly.

It’s a very useful exercise that has very little to do with making music, and everything to do with putting my hands through pretty much every pattern that would come up in trad tunes on an instrument that before the pandemic, I never used for traditional Irish music.

It’s just a lot of raw meat for my brain to chew on to solve the problems that come up for fingering patterns, which for CBA, on the treble side, are unlike any other instrument I can think of.

7 weeks into this, I’ve completed 350 tunes, with 450 to go… If nothing else, it’s a relaxing sort of meditation for me.

I’ve done this before in the past on Anglo concertina and other instruments, it’s always been a useful exercise.

Now of these 800 tunes, I probably know 600 by memory well enough to play them in a session, that’s not the point here. I do not recommend using this as an exercise to learn tunes, but primarily as way to train your brain and muscles to effortlessly solve the fingering problems one encounters frequently on free-reed instruments in various tunes and to increase overall fluidity.

Re: Keeping practicing interesting 7 days a week?

I recommend that my students break up their practice time into three parts:

1. The first 15 or 20 minutes should be scales, arpeggios, and chords. I recommend students practice a different scale and the seven modes around the circle of fifths each day. So for example, C today, plus the arpeggios and chords for 145 pattern, G tomorrow, etc.

2. The second chunk of time, about 20 minutes or so, should be any new tunes that you are learning.

3. The third chunk of time, about 20 minutes or more if you have it, is playing through tunes you already know, jamming online with friends one-on-one, or going outside with your instrument and playing on your porch or lawn. This is a really important part of everyday’s practice and something to look forward to.

Re: Keeping practicing interesting 7 days a week?

I do think it’s important to learn the primary scales used for the vast majority music on the instrument, but that’s a tiny subset of the full circle of fifths.

Pretty much one just needs to be fluent in the scales of G-Major, D-Major, and A-Major (which also gives you their Dorian equivalents of A-Dorian, E-Dorian, and B-Dorian), then expand to include C-Major and F-Major, which give you D-Dorian and G-Dorian as well.

These also give you the scale patterns for the other common key/modes of D-Mixolydian (based on G-Major), A-Mixolydian (based on D-Major).

With just those 5 scales, you get the vast majority of the keys and modes for the tunes.

Of course there are others, like true minor tunes vs. Dorian mode that requires a one note variance here or there, and there is that occasional odd tune in E Major, but my point is that fluency in scales for the entire circle of fifths isn’t strictly required for this music.

> if that’s not how the instrument was taught historically then what exactly is one doing as far as playing “traditional” music.

I think it’s dangerous ground to generalise about traditional pedagogy. For one thing, historically it was mostly kids that learnt to play, and kids can be incredibly badly taught and still turn out well. There are also plenty of examples of traditional musicians teaching with a lot of attention to detail in technique.

Lastly, as traditional music changes in nature and style over the years, the demands made on the musician have increased and lots of instruments have seen ideas brought in from classical music pedagogy, without doing any particular harm to the music that I can see.

Clearly, Will has a strong position that traditional players can benefit from scale/interval work whether or not it’s how the music was traditionally taught. That’s fine and works for some people, go that route if it appeals to you.

Others disagree and just achieve fluency through playing tunes. That’s fine, go that route if it works for you.

Not everyone learns the same way. I find that perfect practice of the tunes with variation and working on precision in my technique over time in the context of playing tunes themselves is what works for me.

I think we can agree that as long as the end result, no matter how you got there, is the production of music that would be identified as well-played traditional Irish music, both technically and emotionally, then mission accomplished.

Now, one could argue there are circumstance, perhaps in a more academic setting, where playing with the specifics nuances and style of a particular player is the goal. That’s a completely different goal from general high-level competence in for what is, I think for most of us, primarily social music, and would require a different style of practice focused more on analysis and re-creation. That’s its own thing, but in itself I think requires a level of fluency in both playing and more importantly, listening, beyond social playing.

One of the reasons I completely switched from classical playing to traditional Irish music about 30 years ago was that I saw in it a true virtuoso music with an accessible social dynamic and community I never experienced in my classical playing.

I could see that this music was something that would take a lifetime to master and specifically didn’t require the horrible tedium of the kind of practice that was required to play at a high level on classical flute. I learn the tunes by playing the tunes. I don’t much care if anyone agrees with me or not, that’s what works for me. I encourage other players to figure out how they learn and then do that and don’t let others tell you that the way you learn is incorrect.

Whatever level of fluency I have, on the several instruments I play, is the result of thousands of hours in the chair, but essentially none of it resembles what I used to do in my classical days.

I got out of classical because I hated the tedium and rigor of that kind of practice. That would not work for me now. Probably it would make me a better player, but it would make me hate the music.

I am still curious about how this music was taught historically, and the idea that perhaps one should learn a tradition the way it was taught, both in the home as well as in formalized training systems like CCE. It would be great to see more actual facts posted about that rather than opinions.

Re: Keeping practicing interesting 7 days a week?

Certainly, the benefits of frequent practice are many, while a lack of practice is visibly/aurally detrimental. The frequency of practice though, is entirely subjective on the individuals part. The goals of practice should be three-fold. Maintaining the skills you have, challenging the development of more and/or greater/ better skills, and maintaining/increasing your repertoire of music.
Whether those goals can be met will depend entirely upon the person and their mental approach to practice. I’m a firm believer that anything less than at least weekly practice, those three goals can’t be met. Like anything we do of value, we get out of it, what we bring to it, and put in to it. So if daily practice is working for you, by all means keep at it.
