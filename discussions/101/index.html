Re: How to play Irish music

Great questions. I’ll try to be brief in my answers because I’m sure others will want to respond in depth.

1) First Position -- Yes, mostly. Open strings add to the sound.

2) Add Slurring -- Yes, but not everywhere. You get a sense for it as you listen to other masters play.

3) Adding Ornaments -- 5-note rolls, bowed triplets, crans & strikes are almost always usable if sheet music says BAB or DCD or any other NOTE-NOTE BELOW-NOTE combination. When do you do one? Once again, you get a feel for it.

4) Vibrato -- Traditionally, a very light (nearly non-existant) vibrato is used.

5) When to join in -- You join if you know the tune, and you can play. There is some role for improvisation, but it is far more rigid as compared to other music forms. Irish musicians do not ‘jam together’ as Bluegrass or Jazz musicians would.

6) Sessions -- Know the tunes by heart (no sight-reading at sessions). For tunes to learn, check out ‘the virtual session’ by the BBC (check the Links on this site). That site alone should answer almost all of your questions.

Have fun!

Re: How to play Irish music

But before you worry about ANY of that…put your fiddle in its case, latch the lid, and leave it under your bed.

Then go find a session or two or three and LISTEN. Listen to the music, the talk, the body language, the clink of pints, the beat of feet on the floor. The more of this you can do BEFORE you bring your fiddle, the better. Some session regulars are a bit put off by talented classical players who show up with little to no understanding of traditional styles, skills, and tunes. Rightfully so, if the newcomers can’t shed classical pretenses. So if you want to fit in, take your time and learn as much as you can. A real, live teacher experienced in the tradition is invaluable.

You’ll notice that extremely few tunes go above the little finger A on the first string, so root yourself in first position. Getting the notes in first position will help you find a more authentic bowing, fingering, and sound. Remember that trad tunes are “composed” on the instruments used to play them--many “fiddle” tunes are made to fit the instrument. Don’t go out of your way to make them more difficult or fancier than they need to be.

Bowing is highly inidividual. I was once criticized by a classical player because my bow directions weren’t matched to other fiddlers in the group (all of us took different approaches to bowing the same tunes). That’s just how it works. It may help in the beginning to learn a tune with separate bow strokes for each note, then add slurring as you develop a feel for the music. In the long run, it’s best if you don’t stick to any one approach or set of bowing patterns anyway.

Beg someone to show you how to do the rolls (similar to but also completely different than mordants) and triplets. It takes even the best classical players (and also the rest of us) months or years to play Irish ornaments with authority, so get started now.

Learn all the tunes you can, especially jigs and reels if your main purpose is to play in sessions. Find a local session and ask if you can bring a tape recorder. Then learn all the tunes they play. Picking up tunes by ear this way is essential for a good session player, so don’t give up on recordings even if you’re not accustomed to doing it this way. Use sheet music if you need it, or to help remember tunes once you’ve worked them out by ear, but push yourself to learn by ear as much as possible.

Oh yeah, have FUN, relax, respect the music but forget about taking yourself seriously (at least until you can play 600 tunes by heart while dodging the dancers and seducing the button box player across the cicrcle without missing a beat).

Hope this helps!
Will

I would echo what all the above replies recommend,especially Will’s advice to maybe listen first.
There are indeed plenty of sessions and classes in the UK and you are bound to be within striking distance of a fair few if you live in any major city. On my touring travels I’ve been to a couple in Manchester and Liverpool but suspect you might live in London if you’re freelance.If so,well London is your lobster,aha!
Historically, north London is where you’ll find a lot of sessions but the whole capital has plenty to offer,south,west and even east.
Pete Cooper runs classes on Wednesday nights at Cecil Sharp House (also Scottish and English tunes I believe) and there’s a bar open afterwards and there’s a class run by Brendan Mulkere at the Swan pub in Stockwell(straight opposite the tube station,Northern Line).You could also check out the Irish Centre in Hammersmith or just ask around a few Irish pubs.I will leave you my email if you want any further ideas.
Speaking of Pete Cooper,his tutor:‘Mel Bay’s Complete Irish Fiddler’ is one of the books wich I found helpful as well as Matt Cranitch’s ‘The Irish Fiddle Book’ - BUT(!): a word of warning in that you will no more be able to really play any type of fiddle style just from reading a book than you could learn to ride a bike/drive a car from the same means.
But in conjunction with the human contact and using your luggoles a lot more than I’m afraid I do in concerts, they have their uses.
Oh,and get used to the luxury and sonority of those open strings as well!
As for tunes, I think you could do a lot worse than by beginning with the jigs but see what your other session players or teachers say. I think they ’re useful for trying to get the rhythm and for having a go at the ornaments.
Forget (or try to) the straight jacketed approach to bowing in big orchestral sections and indeed a lot of other classical traits which is n’t easy unless you’ve maybe done both traditions at the same time in a mainly trad music household when you were a kid.
Get yourself a few albums as well- Kevin Burke is a player I would say has had the greatest influence on my playing,such as it is, and try to listen to as many players as you can. I think you probably have to imitate at first before you can get anywhere near your ‘own’ style,whatever that may be.
If you want to listen to the difference between the written note and the tune you actually hear,put on Tommy People’s setting of ‘Farewell to Ireland’ (from ‘The High Part of the Road’ ,Shanachie 34007) and you’ll get an idea!
It’s a Hard Road to Travel (sorry 4 you Andy McGann fans out there!) for us classical types but it’s a great experience with a lot of fun too,not to mention a lot of drink,if you like that sort of thing which I DO!

Oh yes, I would especially recommend Ciaran Carson’s book:‘Last Night’s Fun’ although you might want a dictionary to hand unless you once did a degree in unusual words…
So,all the best ,sweetlily ,and good luck!

Re: How to play Irish music

I guess I should put my two cents in…
1. Listen to some good old music
2. Learn how to play it.
No easy task.
As a general rule relax your bowing wrist, let it sway counter to your bowing direction. Also, don’t use any vibrato, try to replace it with deadly accurate intonation. Rolls are similar to turns in classical music, but have rhythmic differences. Try to arrange lessons with a good Irish fiddler to go over the finer points. As a last note (I will be crucified for this) stay away from Eileen Ivers, Liz Carroll, Tommy Peoples & Martin Hayes(Sorry Will). While they are monsters of technique & very fresh sounding, these players are more worried about their purses & wallets than they are about playing pure drop music.
