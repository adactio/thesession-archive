Re: music and language

I dunno, you’d *think* that people with a talent for music would also have a talent for languages. Maybe they are more *likely* to be good at languages, than people who are not musical. But it’s not uniform. I know people who are brilliant at music and can pick up tunes very fast by ear - but don’t exhibit any talent for foreign languages.
I think it shows that there are many aspects to each skill, and one can be strong or weak in so many varying areas; the musical and linguistic strengths may or may not overlap. Also, I guess it’s a matter of early exposure, both in music and in languages. If you come to learning new languages too late, perhaps that particular application of your ‘musical brain’ just doesn’t develop very richly. So you might be able to learn tunes by ear on the spot, but not have developed any mental techniques for storing vocab or recognising and breaking up the elements of foreign speech.
We should teach both from pre-school age, in my view!

Re: music and language

Here is just a theory. I am *very* good at math (I have a PhD in it), and pretty good at music (but not *very* good), and I hear the music/math thing all the time from other people.

From my experiences with both, it seems to me that there are different ways to use our brains.

Some things we work through very deductively, like figuring out scales and rhythms (if we read music), working out what key whistle to use to be able to play with others while minimizing half-holing, etc. We also do that when we find mnemonics for learning vocabulary and grammar, and we use it when we solve math problems by following the steps.

Some things are very intuitive -- like when we are improvising really fast, and our fingers just “know” what to do. Or when we are working geometry problems, and “see” what needs to be done next. Or when we hear the rhythm and inflection in a sentence in a foreign language, and remember that way.

Maybe we see connections between these disciplines because people who are very good at using their brains in different ways can extend those skills across topics? So if we are good at thinking in different ways, it might not matter what we are doing -- we are just pretty good at it. All of this within reason, of course. I don’t think there are any absolutes in life. (but I could be wrong!)

Of course, that makes all of the musicians in this crowd extra intelligent! So that should be enough proof, right? :-)

Re: music and language

Patterns. Definitely patterns.
Math is all about patterns. So is music. So is language. (If you want to be reductionist, anyway, and for the purpose of this post I do.)

I used to spend idle hours playing a math game my dad brought to my attention, given my childhood interest in patterns. (If you’ve ever read Alfred Bester’s story “The Pi Man”, well, I *really* identified with Abraham Storm.) The game goes like this:
Pick a number, 2 through 9. Multiply the number by 1, by 2, by 3, by 4, and as much farther than that as you care to go. (Some of them, I took up to the tens of thousands.) If your answer has more than one digit, add the digits together until you’re down to one digit. (So 324 would be 3+2+4 = 9.)
Now look at the patterns that have emerged. For instance, say you chose 6. The multiples of 6, added reductively as described, form a nice regular rhythm: 6 3 9 6 3 9 6 3 9. If you chose 9, you’ll rapidly discover that any multiple of 9 reduces back to 9. 5 does a nice little dance: 5 1 6 2 7 3 8 4 9 5. And so on.

My thought is that musos are good at patterns. Some of them are good at the right kind of patterns to make them good at math and/or languages also; others aren’t. I’m good at the kinds of math that require pattern recognition --- I can solve quadratic equations at a glance --- but lousy at any kind of math where pattern recognition is of no use. Likewise with languages, if I can see patterns I can get fluent, and if not, not. I loved studying Japanese because it’s such a highly patterned language, as well as being really beautiful. Likewise Latin, although some of the patterns there are harder to see. And if I click into the pattern in a piece of music I’m home free; if not, I can’t learn it worth a darn.

Of course, I’m an aspie, so this may just be my own fixation. ;)

Re: music and language

Pythagoras held that all was number. He also began the path of our theoretical basis for music, based on ratio: i.e. number, not language. His observations still largely hold as valid today, even after discovering the physical reasons for ratio being the basis of our percetion of interval and scale.

I’m with Bobhimself, the good Reverand and Mr. McDonnell, there is certainly a pyschological companant to music, without which it would not even exist, but it is at heart, physics, and our perceptions of music are the aural analog of being able to see whether two boards are the same length or not, and our sense of consonance in music the analog of the sort of sensation we get when we see that one board is exactly half the length of another.

Geometry, not language, because the “language” of an instrument is its physics. Do not confuse metaphorical terms with the reality. You stop the string at half its length to produce a vibration that is twice its unstopped rate, in turn causing your eardrum to vibrate at twice the rate, which produces a “sensation” of the tone being “half as long.”

It is a raw sense, like touch or smell (in fact, physically, it is touch), as opposed to language which is entirely about abstract meaning. Sensual.

It’s interesting to note that physicists and mathematicians were at the forefront of the renewed appreciation of Baroque music, before the “romantics” came along and ruined it all. The Baroque composers appreciated the significance of number to music.

Note that there is a *difference* in the way musicians and non muscians react to music in the brain. The nonmusician percieves it directly through the “pleasure center.” Musicians have been *trained* to percive music in differenet ways, which is the core to why classical musicians have such a difficult time with Irish music, because their “language” is different.

But the capacity to understand and appreciate something that can be called “music” exists outside of that training.

The idea that language developed as a means of gossiping I have found interesting, and even compelling, but. . .I find the idea that it developed as a means of shouting “Watch out for the effing lion, you moron!” more compelling. To the extent that *higher* language developed as a means of gossiping I find the idea that its function was to let you know your mate was sleeping around while you were hunting to be more compelling than the idea that it is some sort of “grooming replacement” or enhancement (and we still practice grooming).

Sex is the root of everything in those organisms that practice it (“Oooooo, look at the pretty colors on that flower!” and music is analogous to color, a sensual stimulous, like sex.)

I think it’s clear that language has a genetic basis, but that basis *only* comes to the fore with training in the abstract meaning of various sounds. Without that training language produces no particular response in the brain at all.

Of course all of this has only to do with tone, melody. There is also duration, rhythm, but plants have that, and there are few who would claim they have langaguge skills (a plant begins to react to the coming day and the coming seasons *before* any direct stimulus, by timing the duration of the day and knowing that the sun is *about* to rise/set and the weather is *about* to get warmer/colder).

This, again, is direct perception of ratio. Geometry.

Mathematics.
