Re: Speaking

The old joke is a language is a dialect with an army, and there’s some truth in that. It’s similar to the situation with, say, Danish and Swedish.

Irish and Gaelic (the usual terms in English) really only became distinct languages in relatively recent times, and they retain a fair degree of mutual intelligibility when speakers are trying to be understood. To some extent it was historically a spectrum, and geographically close speakers of Irish and Gaelic would have spoken very much like one another. Centralised education in both languages has made this less obvious, as has the collapse in the number of speakers in both languages.

Re: Speaking

“In reading liner notes, or other sources, I have encountered the terms, ”Irish Gaelic“ and ”Scots Gaelic.“ Are these two distinct languages?”

It might be well to understand that there are two main branches of the Celtic languages, all of which descended from a presumed Proto-Celtic, which forms a subgroup with Latin in the Indo-European language family.

There’s “P Celtic” or Brythonic, which was the group of languages spoken in Britain at the time of the Roman invasion. Currently spoken are Welsh and Breton. Cornish died out as community speech a couple hundred years ago.

Then there’s “Q Celtic” or Goidelic, Irish and its descendants Scottish Gaelic and Manx. (It was spoken in Continental Europe before the Romans messed it all up.)

The Brythonic/Goidelic division can be seen pairs like map/mac “son” and penn/cenn “head” etc.

“My gal Sal” doesn’t really give clarity, since the vowels could be long or short depending on where you come from. “Gaelic” as pronounced by Scots has a long a in the first syllable. And in Gaelic itself the language is called “Gaidhlig”, which is pronounced pretty much the same.

Unlike Irish and Gaelic, Manx spelling was devised by English speakers, and the sounds were written according to the way English was spelled. This was probably the worst possible orthographic solution to writing the language, given that the English spellings we now use were first determined by Normans according to the principles of written French, and largely remained in force although there were major changes to vowel sounds in the 15th and 16th centuries.

Re: Speaking

Welsh took on the Latin alphabet, while giving some letters or combinations new values for sounds that didn’t exist in Latin (e.g. ch, gh, ll and others) but it retained a pretty clear correspondence between letters and sounds. Pronunciation didn’t change much over the years, and the language didn’t have many dialects, basically only North and South Welsh. Irish and Gaelic are also phonetic (as long as you understand the underlying system), but had to accommodate more diphthongs than Welsh. They also had far more dialect variation.

Re: Speaking

Old Irish had many more sounds needing represented than Irish does now, or English. The orthography of Irish was invented, presumable by early Christian monks, but we don’t know who; the earliest manuscripts are estimated to be a century or two after the system’s invention. Although it’s jarring to native English speakers, it’s a beautifully conceived system, and although it’s evolved it’s the same fundamental orthography those 6th century monks invented, and it is consistent enough that I can confidently read and pronounce a passage in Scottish Gaelic, even if I’ve no idea what the words mean.

> I wonder whether Manx orthography captures all the phonetic nuances of the language

No, it doesn’t, and while it’s a perfectly good written form for any speaker of the language, it has drawbacks compared to the Gaelic and Irish orthographies. Of course Manx has plenty history of its own and the existence of theoretical criticisms is not a reason to change, any more than the state of written English is.

In theory, no, because once you know what the sounds are and what the spelling of those sounds is, you can then read and write anything you can say. As far dialects, they do indeed end up represented in variant spellings, usually widely understood - but also until recent times there was a widespread formal register that varied less than vernacular dialects did.

> Was literary Irish the language of the monks or did everyone speak it?

My understanding - and I’d welcome correction on this - is that there were several different registers of speech according to social class, and much of the early writings we have are in relatively high-register language (and thus conservative). I believe there were times when the highest registers of speech spoken by medieval bards and aristocracy were very different from vernacular language. How different they were at the time of the earliest evidence we have is difficult to determine, though.

Re: Speaking

> I’m curious whether you meant there may been a greater range/distinction of registers, at one point, or perhaps a more formalized distinction between registers

Well, I’m definitely on ground here that I’m no expert on, so take it with a bit of salt! Almost every language will have registers of speech; you’ll always talk differently to a vicar than to your younger sibling in front of the TV. In some languages those registers will have wholesale grammar changes and vocab changes. In Scottish Gaelic Biblical language is often recognised as its own register because scriptural texts are often written in the Gaelic of centuries past, and forms like the conditional imperative of the substantive ‘bitheamaid’, for example, are now formed indirectly in the modern language, ‘leig dhuinn’.

Going further back, my understanding is that the language used by the bardic tradition consisted of its own register, not used for any other purpose, and it might well be that uneducated people wouldn’t even really know that register of speech at all.
