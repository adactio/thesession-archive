You’re playing in a session and a tune you don’t know is called?

What do “you” do?
I realise this can be a loaded question. But I don’t want to trip anyone up with the OP. I only want to hear what each of you might do when it happens.

When I know I do not know the tune I try not to rush in. If I think I know it and begin playing along, but it quickly becomes obvious I don’t know the tune, I stop playing. At this point I listen. My entire focus is on “listening” to the tune and I don’t play along if that (my playing) either disrupts the session players who know the tune or even if it disrupts my ability to listen to the tune as they are playing it.

Re: You’re playing in a session and a tune you don’t know is called?

There are stages (s) between not knowing a tune (U) and knowing it (K). There are also variable degrees (d) of loudness of your instrument between very small mandolin (P) and pipes (F). Whether or not you play along (N) when knowledge is less than K depends on how much U + s is short of K and how much S + d is short of F and who might be there who would be very annoyed (A) and how big they are (B). This gives a likelihood for N 0f ((U + s) * (P + d))/BA.

Re: You’re playing in a session and a tune you don’t know is called?

Whenever the topic of ‘noodling’ comes up on this site, I’m put in mind of those meetings on ‘The Detectorists’. Hearing a tune I wrote played in sessions in towns I’ve never visited before is, on the one hand, very gratifying and a treat I never thought music would give me. On the other hand, usually, the tune has been altered a bit, maybe even a lot. Someone had passed the original on to someone who misheard parts of the tune, these changes becoming entrenched in other players‘ versions and round and round it goes. It’s simply the folk process. Variations in tunes, whether regional or player to player, are an essential part of our tradition. So I have to disagree somewhat with those amongst us who look down on ’noodling.‘ How better to learn a tune than stumbling along (quietly, I agree) with those who know it better than I do? I have to say in all my years of sessions, mostly in Québec and Ireland, I’ve never encountered a scowl, admonishment or wagged finger aimed at my, or anybody else’s ’noodling.’ Now, if I’m in the company of Kevin Burke, Martin Faye or Seamus McGuire, fair enough, I’ll listen more than play. (Who wouldn’t?). Otherwise, the more there is, the more there is. I believe we need to encourage one another rather than impose restrictions. It’s just a session after all, not the Albert Hall.

Re: You’re playing in a session and a tune you don’t know is called?

I tend to eschew formulas such as “a likelihood for N 0f ((U + s) * (P + d))/BA” as I can never remember whether the units are metric or English.

Instead I prefer to respond to a new session tune with which I am unfamiliar with one of several options.
1) Dont’t play, but look bored, annoyed and impatient for the duration. Drum my fingers.
2) Play a tune (simultaneously) which has at least some of the same chords, and hope for the best.
3) Take the opportunity to tune the instrument while others are playing the new tune. No sense wasting time.
4) Ask players who know it to call out all the chords during the tune.
5) Noodle loudly. Maybe practice some other tune.
6) Ask the players to stop and repeat any section that is a little tricky.
But then, I’m a guitar player, so this might not work for everyone.

Re: You’re playing in a session and a tune you don’t know is called?

I truly get what Ebor is trying to say. My own perspective on playing tunes and the nature of friendship is my own. Others may disagree, but that’s the beauty of friendship. There are many reason’s for us to gather. Playing music is one. There are many ways to express friendship. One way, and I think it’s a most important way to express it, is through respect. If I put those two notions together I can only conclude that I would not disrespect my friend by fowling up a tune that he/she has chosen to play. I’ll respect them by listening, learning the tune later, or just holding out each time it comes up. Just as I wouldn’t join a curling team because I can’t play it, I’m way to old, and I don’t even know what curling is! It’s out of respect for those who can play.

Of course there’s another equally important way to show friendship; by forgiveness. We can learn to overlook the mistakes, the lapses, in a tune by our friends. Perfection, as Ebor nicely points out, isn’t the goal, community is. Somewhere there has to a common ground. On the left side of the bell-shaped curve (of possible behaviors) is the insistence on rock-star perfection for every note in every tune. On the right side, there is mindlessly dabbling at every tune that comes along. Both ends are expressions of boorish disrespect. Friends meet somewhere in a middle that suits the nature of the session and only the players get to decide where that middle is.

For me, I say if I don’t know your tune, I won’t play it. If I like it, or at least see that you really like, I’ll respect you and learn it, and play it, another time, hoping you’ll forgive me my faults. In my own beginning I sat in for, not days, weeks, or months, but a couple of years before I felt I knew a couple of tunes well enough to attempt to play a tune in a session with a rather high level of musicality. I believe that showing them respect is what has made many of us friends now.

I’m not sure if this has been mentioned already (Llig once told me off for saying that. He said I should have read the thread properly :-) ) , but customs and practices are liable to change over the years and this applies to sessions too.
A lot of musicians here advocate the use of recording devices, phones etc rather than trying to pick up tunes “on the fly”. However, in the old days, not all musicians were comfortable with being recorded in an informal situation. These days, most don’t mind as long as people are polite enough to ask and are part of the session.

On the other hand, while you still had to respect older and more experienced musicians, I seem to remember that there was much more encouragement given to newbies in sessions back then. Also, sessions weren’t quite as fast and furious, sometimes more spontaneous and informal. There wasn’t the remit laid down to start at 9PM and finish at Midnight on the dot or the requirement to “entertain” the punters etc. So, the regular players had more time to “go over a tune again” so that other musicians could pick it up and so on.

Also, many players now take the view, even more so these days, that you should do all your learning outside the session environment. There are far more opportunities to do this these days with various courses, technologies and so on. So, “no excuse” not to be prepared seems to be the general attitude.
Of course, there are also beginners’ and training sessions, slow sessions etc but these are actually a fairly recent phenomenon. Many of these are offshoots of courses and other projects. This is fine but, in some ways, this encourages and perpetuates a two tier system where it becomes harder to play in the same sessions as “the big boys/girls”.

The comments above are more observations rather than complaints. I’m sure many of us are happy with the way things are but I’m sure there will be different views on the matter.

Re: You’re playing in a session and a tune you don’t know is called?

Further to the above, most newcomers to sessions in the old days had probably thought long and hard before making the first step and were already really interested in the music.
I, myself, had already been an avid listener for some time and had been a frequenter of session pubs even getting to know many of the musicians. As such, they realised that I was very interested and respected the music. Therefore, it’s likely that this made them more accepting and prepared to put up with some of my shortcomings.

These days, with taster workshops and courses, it’s tempting for many aspiring musicians to attempt to enter the environment before they are quite ready or even having much listening experience to the music …. i.e. they’ve learned the bodhran in a day. Even attending classes doesn’t necessarily prepare you as the repertoire you learn might not be transferable(Usually isn’t) to many sessions. So, naturally regular session players can be a bit wary of newcomers these days.

Re: You’re playing in a session and a tune you don’t know is called?

I just try and play quietly or pluck to myself, if I seem to be getting somewhere I ease into the tune on the second or third time around, and play along, at least with the phrases I’ve definitely got.
If I’m drunk with good friends I might jump in a bit quicker and take risks, if it’s some dreadfully serious session with dreadfully serious musicians I’ll be much more hesitant. If it’s a fantastic session with really fantastic musicians I’ll be very wary of crashing in with a half realised version, and anyway maybe I’ll just want to enjoy listening to what they’re doing.
Often in my quiet playing or plucking to myself I realise that I wasn’t nearly as familiar with the tune as I thought or it’s just very twisty and complicated so I stop altogether. Sometimes I stop altogether and the tune just goes round and round and by the fourth or fifth iteration I am actually familiar with it after all so I have another go at very gently easing myself into it.
Generally I only end up joining in with tunes that are very simple or that I’ve heard plenty of times before even if I never actually sat down and worked them out. If it’s working I’ll typically only play bits of it the first time round. The second time I’ll have it but with a couple of mistakes and the third time I’ll be playing the tune reasonably solidly. Probably a slightly stripped down version but without clashing with the others. I’ll still be playing quietly mind you, not trying to jump into the forefront of the sound.
If it doesn’t feel like that’s realistically going to happen by the third time round I’ll just stop. Or play the simple a part and let the others deal with the complicated b part or whatever.
