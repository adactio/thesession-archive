my experience learning by ear and improving

I wanted to share some success I’ve had with getting much better at learning tunes by ear. I’ve learned almost all my tunes by ear, but for my first few years of playing mandolin and banjo, I learned roughly one tune a month. Part of the reason (other than simple laziness) for my slow rate of learning was that learning tunes was mentally exhausting.

For my learning, I used transcribe (https://www.seventhstring.com/xscribe/download_mac.html), and did almost all of my learning in tiny chunks (say, a measure or half a measure at a time), usually at 50% or even 25% of the original speed. It would often take me many hours to get a single tune figured out.

Back in October of this year, I decided I wanted to start working hard on getting “session ready”. Among other things, this meant learning a lot more tunes, so I set myself the challenge of learning a new tune every day until the end of 2016. I didn’t make it the whole way, but I did about a tune a day for 30 days, and then slowed down to 2 or 3 per week.

The difference this has made for my ear learning is huge. By the end of the first week it was already getting much easier, and now I’m at the point where I’m able to learn simpler tunes a whole phrase at a time without slowing them down at all. I thought I’d share my experience in case it inspires anyone else who has previously felt that learning by ear is super difficult. Try doing a tune a day for a month!

Here’s some other miscellaneous notes:

- If you’re learning on banjo, check out https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCac70x-gvPHEcI_Tv6djatg, https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCeg50sbXK0JxS3ksL8u5Qsg, https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCezqqXjtVNfKfmKs0wU4m8w, https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCTaPjbQzcsD4bOogV4TShIQ, and https://www.youtube.com/user/ShaskeenReel

- I found it easiest to learn from banjo recordings, but I also learned a few tunes from other instruments. I found learning from accordion to be fairly easy, fiddle fairly difficult (owing to double-stops and interpretively-tuned notes), and flute or whistle completely impossible.

- People have pointed this out many times in this forum, but you really start to recognize similar chunks and patterns in between tunes, which I think is part of what makes this such a fun learning curve to climb. The first time you hear the first two measures of a tune, hit pause, and then play the passage note-for-note on your first try is a really fun feeling.

- One big difficulty I’ve had with learning so many tunes so quickly is that they started to bleed into each other a bit. Tunes that I could previously play rock-solid would suddenly acquire random out-of-key notes from other tunes that had similar but not identical patterns. This problems seems to be going away on its own slowly.

- I think this exercise helped my technique/mechanics quite a bit as well. When I’m learning a tune, I’m not stressing about keeping a fast tempo or hitting triplets or whether I’m going to flub the high B. As a result, I’ve been spending a lot more time playing relaxed and easy, and some of my problem areas have improved significantly without actually putting much direct effort into them.

That’s it! I’d love to hear about other people’s experiences learning by ear.

Re: my experience learning by ear and improving

The vast majority of us are kinetic learners. We learn by doing tactile things. Or rather, tactile things *plus* visual or auditory things. Some of us are really good at the auditory or visual thing (not me), and can have a tune down within a few minutes. Some are ridiculously good kinetic learners and remember things better by playing than by any of the other two. Good teachers and institutions (of all academia) are starting to realize that combining all three elements is the best way for students to learn. It’s no different for musicians.

IMO, figure out which type of learner you are and try to utilize those strengths. Or, conversely, figure out where your weaknesses are and try to shore them up. It all comes down to our brains forming more connections between its music center and other parts. More connections in our brain will only enhance our overall understanding of the music, so it’s all good! Learning and progressing is incredibly important for me as an individual, perhaps even moreso than the experience of playing music itself.

Re: my experience learning by ear and improving

In response to the last two posts, I don’t find that tunes stick any better for me with either ear or dots, but reading those posts brought up a concept that I’d like to share. When I was in school, I majored in theater and did well. Most of my fellow students would highlight their lines in a play with a yellow marker. I never did that, preferring to memorize everything in the scenes I was in. I found that my onstage timing and reactions benefitted from knowing the flow of everything that was going on around me. Sure, I had to listen on stage, but there is a craft as well as an art to acting, and during rehearsals, your fellow actors have not solidified what they are going to do, so you should have everything going for you that you can. You also should have options if someone goes up on their lines (happened to me more than once).

In music, the correlation is not direct, but I find that when I practice or play by myself, I hear in my head what would be going on around me if I were playing in a band or a session. This brings out the flow and lift of how I play the tune. I’m guessing many who learn by ear do the same thing, but don’t necessarily do so when they learn from dots, hence the conclusion that ear-learning sticks better or sounds better for them. My conclusion is, the more you experience of the tune in your head of how the tune should sound to you in a group setting, the better player you will be and the better ear-musician you will become.

Re: my experience learning by ear and improving

Learning by ear: I’ve had no problem doing it, and I’m told I’m very good at it. Where does it come from?

I think, for me, the big thing was admiring my father’s whistling - the pucker up and blow thing, not the pennywhistle. He whistled around the house all the time, and at about age 6, I worked and worked to be able to do it too. I took to it so much that kids made fun of my whistling all the time.

And for me, the connection is one can’t whistle except by ear. There’s no “when I see that note, I press this button or that fret.” It’s all in your head. Same for lilting, of course. And once it’s in your head, getting it to come out on an instrument is relatively easy. (Somewhere I found this quote: ﻿“You should never learn a tune you don’t know!” - Tom Anderson, Shetland fiddler. I think that’s what he was talking about.)

This matters to me now because I have young grandkids. I plan to do all I can to teach them to whistle, and to sing of course. I think that gives a mental foundation; the instruments can come later.

One more thing: When I’ve got a tune in my head and am trying to get it on fiddle or whistle, I notice that I tend to remember key parts by knowing what finger gets a phrase going. That is, I don’t think “the B part starts on an F#.” Instead, something in my brain sends the reminder that the index finger needs to be down.

That’s how my brain seems to work, anyway.

Coming late to this discussion - thanks Mark and others for the insights and tips on learning by ear. My experience is that of a long-time backup guitarist/singer who has belatedly resolved to ‘learn the tunes’ (thanks largely to the cajolers on the Mustard website!). I’ve always used my ear to figure out how to back tunes, but now that I’m actually getting the notes themselves under my fingers, over the past couple months, I’ve found that:
1) Actually knowing a tune helps my backup playing significantly (I can hear the chorus of ‘well duh!’)
2) My ear training for chordal accompaniment, although probably helpful, needs to be sharpened considerably. The strategies discussed on this thread will no doubt prove useful.
3) Some tunes that I’ve backed for years at contradances, etc., come relatively easily, presumably because they are already wired in my brain circuits at some level. However, certain passages need to be listened to and worked out to because I never had to sweat the details-- or in some cases my brain seems to have carried out the folk process of altering a passage so I need to re-learn it correctly.
4) Dots can be helpful for complicated passages, but I much prefer to sharpen my imitative skills and aspire to learn 90% of my repertoire that way.
In case anyone is interested, my lead instrument of choice is bouzouki/cittern (I love the sound, I can go back and forth between lead and backup, and besides noone else in my local session plays one). My dream is to add fiddle or (more realistically) whistle.
