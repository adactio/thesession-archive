Workshops - learning by ear.

I am not too bad at figuring out what a tune is doing, even sometimes playing along on the fly by the second or third time round during a moderately paced session on unknown tunes with common patterns in them. However! When in a workshop, I often find myself getting the first two bars ok, moving on to the next two and then by the time the third pair is there, having forgotten the first two, or at least forgotten enough, that I can’t play them at the speed the teachers seem to want there. That leads to stress which drives the second two bars out of my head. And so on…
If there is a break and I have a recording, then I NEED to write down the tune and then I can play it fine and the written down version is not really needed - it is the ACT of writing it down that I need. If notes have been provided then I will find myself not looking at them after a couple of repetitions anyway.
Can anyone give me a reasoned argument for any advantage in the first situation that I am missing? Or any useful advice other than “do it more often…”.
Also; breaking the tune down into bars is so much less useful than breaking it down into phrases, but almost every tutor I have experienced uses bars which makes the musical context of the small section being learnt much harder to place musically, and therefore harder to remember it as a tune.
Also, a note on learning by ear (for me) with a recording which is a completely different cognitive process and usually starts with being able to hum the tune after a number of repetitions and then playing it automatically and easily. This does not map well to the workshop scenario, but is a much more generally useful skill I think.

I find the important thing when learning by ear is to learn the tune rather than think about the fingering. Once I have the tune in my head the fingering will follow.

In a session that happens naturally because you are presented with the entire tune, whereas in a workshop there is perhaps a tendency to think more about the instrument, especially if the tutor is showing specific fingerings. If you are usually good at picking up tunes then I suspect this may be the reason you have difficulty. Ask the tutor to play the tune several times before breaking it down to teach, so that you have a sense of the whole tune.

Re: Workshops - learning by ear.

Crackpot, sounds to me that your problem isn’t you or your learning style but the teaching approach at some workshops you’ve attended.

I once sat in on a tune-learning class at a festival. It was set up for a well-regarded fiddler to teach one or two of his own compositions. He jumped right in to the first tune, teaching it bar by bar, which was too short for any sense of phrasing. After the second bar, I interrupted him. Here’s a paraphrase of how that went:

Me: “Say, before we learn the tune, could you perhaps play it a few times through to refresh all our memories?”

Famous fiddler: “Oh. I assumed you were all familiar with it already.”

Me: “Sure, it’s a brilliant tune, but just to get the shape of it in our heads before we dissect it?”

Ff: “Right, of course.”

Me: “And maybe give the key, or at least the home note—what you hear as the tonal center. Bit of a legend to the map.”

Ff: (long pause; you could see the mental gears grinding as he tried to sort this out). “All right then, this is a reel in G, but it’s minor. Or sounds minor, not major. But it lands on G at the ends. I’ll play it a few times through, not too fast.”

And so he did, three times at an easy lope. Lovely playing, wonderful tune.

Then he proceeded to teach the tune not bar by bar but phrase by phrase. I don’t know if playing the tune had simply put him in a more musical frame of mind, or if he had a small epiphany and realized that bar by bar was not the way to go. But something clicked, and the rest of the lesson went smoothly.

Sometimes our musical heroes need a little nudge when they shift into teaching mode.

Re: Workshops - learning by ear.

It may be also worth noting that it’s not usually the main purpose of a workshop to learn the tune (a happy bonus) but to use the tune as a vehicle for learning the tutor’s approach to playing, phrasing, ornament, technique etc. If you like the tune then the tutor will usually give everyone the chance to record it and/or provide a written copy in one form or another. Sometimes people get over anxious about their ability to remember all of the tune and miss the most interesting parts of the workshop. I teach by ear and, in a large mixed workshop, on a very rough rule of thumb, I’d be trying to pitch it so that most (but probably not all) of the group can experience playing the tune fairly comfortably all together at the end. But I’d be much more concerned to ensure everyone learned something they could apply to improving their playing or their approach to learning other tunes.

Re: Workshops - learning by ear.

Here’s my "works for me approach, with the neuroscience behind it.

First I think of a tune as a destination. Reaching the destination well is more satisfying than starting well and falling apart. Think of, let’s say, the A part of any tune. It has an ending, a resolution of the story. I learn that figure first. The resolving figure has a figure that leads to it. Look and listen to any tune and you see/hear the tension and resolution all the way.through. Be sure to listen to the tune all the way through, at tempo, and often enough to hear where it’s going. Hear the phrasing in the tune, ignore the bars, and never think of them as a series of notes. Maybe the simplest example is “It’s fleece was white as snow”. Of course you may discover that each figure , call them phrases if you prefer, has its own resolution. It even solves the problem of when to breathe. The tune provides plenty of opportunity. I rather think that learning a tune front to back too easily leads to a lifeless presentation, at least for me it does. When I’m taught a tune that I’ll go back and relearn it “backwards”. Like I said, it works for me.

Re: Workshops - learning by ear.

Most tunes (trad ones) have a “question/response” or whatever you want to call it phrase structure.

Off the top of my head the first part of The Boys of the Lough

Opening phrase > continuing on the path >
opening phrase > wandering from path (dominant chord) >
opening phrase > continuing on the path >
opening phrase > return to home (tonic chord) (Often set up by brief return of dominant chord)

(In a “classical” score where letters are assigned to each section it would be ABAC ABAD.)

That’s how I would introduce that tune, using the phrase structure.

About sheet music, when I was doing flute workshops I felt that I had a duty to address the various learning modalities that would be present. There were always people from the “classical” world, sightreaders, either with their trusty Boehm flute or an Irish flute they had just bought, dipping their toes in the water of “Irish flute”.

It would be a disservice to them to not have sheet music. Furthermore, it would be a disservice to them to have the music notated in a misleading way, so I would write tunes out to capture how they’re played as best I could.

But there were also always ear-learners in attendance and I made sure to emphasise that IMHO ear-learning was preferable. I urged the sightreaders who also were good ear-learners (most are) to turn their sheet music upside down and focus on listening.

Years ago, after a late night session petered out, myself on fiddle and a well-regarded fluter were the last to pack up. As I was about to loosen my bow, he nodded at me and launched into The Killavil Fancy, easy, at nearly half tempo. We played it three times through to get in synch, and then he started changing the phrasing, at first just with his breathing—where he paused for a breath, how he used that silence, creating more percussive emphasis or less, running two or three short phrases into one long breath, etc. Then came rolls, long rolls, short rolls, rolls on top of rolls. And then a subtle replacement of a note, then a cluster of notes, then inverting the melody of a whole phrase, and so on.

We played Killavil Fancy at least 40 times. I couldn’t help but latch onto his pulse and phrasing, but otherwise kept my fiddling of the melody straightforward, the better to pay attention to everything he was doing. It came in layers, each built on an overall musical idea, and so the concepts, the ways of thinking about interpreting a tune, were clear and easy to grasp. They stayed with me. Those 40 times through Killavil Fancy forever enriched how I play this music.

Some form of that experience, possibly slowed down broken into chunks, would make for an excellent workshop.

The problem with workshops at festivals is that it’s not always clear what the purpose is. Perhaps the organisers haven’t thought about it that deeply, or perhaps the person running it isn’t sure what they are expected to provide (I’ve led a few, and have been given no guidance what the organisers expected from me).
And of course they can’t communicate with participants in advance.

I agree that mixed-instrument workshops should be about ensemble playing (and I have been to some very good ones) but often they are just an opportunity for people to play together (who perhaps don’t have the confidence to join sessions) and become tune-learning exercises.

Instrument-specific workshops should focus on technique and interpretation and the tune should just be a vehicle for this. Although I am not a reader, and although I am pretty quick at picking up tunes, I do find it helpful when the leader sends dots and soundfiles in advance. However I did attend one where the leader sent dots in a key which isn’t playable on most standard instruments (although he taught it in a more accessible key), and a lot of what he was talking about was only possible on his own highly-customised instrument, so whilst interesting it was not very helpful.

The biggest advantage from a workshop should be the opportunity to listen to and discuss ideas with an experienced musician, perhaps one of your heroes. For me, that is even more important than simply playing, because these opportunities don’t come along very often, and certainly more important than coming away with another tune.

Re: Workshops - learning by ear.

I know of three sorts of ensemble workshops. One type is for organised band parts, and these usually have proper arrangements and dots to go with them. The other type is about playing in informal small groups without actual arrangements but using the qualities of different instruments together with harmony and rhythm to support the melody, rather like a rehearsed band will do but in a session context (although it really only works with small-ish groups of similarly-minded musicians). Neither are perhaps particularly appropriate to ITM which tends to favour a strongly melodic approach.

The third type is effectively a led session and consists of either playing familiar tunes or perhaps learning new ones, They tend to be aimed at less experienced or less confident players, are often “slow sessions” and often there is heavy reliance on the dots. However sometimes they are an opportunity to play together with famous musicians.

David, yes, I quoted DonaldK’s bit, but only to contrast my experiences with workshops, which had nothing to do with arranged ensemble playing.

I too am bewildered at some comments on this thread about multi-mixed instrument workshops necessarily being about ensemble arranging. A trad session is “ensemble” playing, in a sense, yet far different and more freewheeling than playing as a band or fiddle orchestra or any other “arranged” ensemble. Which is why they’re called sessions and not ensembles or bands or orchestras.

And why wouldn’t at least some workshops welcome the usual mix of session instruments? And why wouldn’t they teach session tunes, and coach about how to interpret and vary tunes (within the customary parameters of session playing)? Surely there are far more aspiring session players than trad band members, and they would be your more likely workshop customers.

Maybe it’s a cultural thing—do some trad scenes focus on ensemble arrangements (e.g., Scottish fiddle orchestras)? That’s not something I have much experience of.

I’ve long thought that there’s a dodge in having performing/recording artists teach workshops to people who are primarily or exclusively session players. Too often, the artist comes at the music from far different motivations and expectations than a session player would—the artist needs to distinguish him/herself from the competition, sell albums, fill concert halls, flawlessly execute complex arrangements, feature new (sometimes exotic) material, etc. A session player, on the other hand, should want the ability to blend in, to build a personal repertoire of widely played tunes, to play by the seat of the pants (not knowing what tunes are coming next), and have solid enough musicality to lead sets when needed.

Yes, people want to take workshops from “famous” names. Sometimes those artists are wonderful sources of musical insight and inspiration. But too often the participants are there mostly so they can later boast of having learned from so-and-so, even if all they “learned” was an obscure tune that’s soon forgotten.

There is a big difference between a one-off one-two hour mixed instrument workshop and a week-long festival ensemble set-up with many hours of playing together.
For the former, there is just about time to learn the tune(s) - maybe add in a harmony line, or have certain instruments play certain sections in a very simple arrangement.
For the latter, the possibilities are endless. I also went to 3 of Rick Taylor’s ensemble workshop weeks, and found them challenging in a very enjoyable way - pushed the boundaries of my playing and thinking outside of the box (good idea for a box player!), playing jazz standards, some of Rick’s own compositions, as well as Scottish and Irish tunes. The first year, there were rather too many tunes in flat keys (see Howard’s comment above!) but a bit more G and D in the second year and beyond!
Then I have been to the very big workshops at Sidmouth - John Kirkpatrick’s Big Band, Nick and Mary Barber’s Ceilidh Band - and at Whitby - Festival Orchestra. These involve daily workshops and an end of week performance, and typically have 50 or so participants playing all sorts of instruments including brass. The music usually is sent out beforehand and often in 4 or more parts, and is best suited to intermediate + players and quick sight-readers.
Ok, I’m straying away from Crackpot’s question, but answering those who didn’t see the point of mixed instrument workshops. I just LOVE them in all the shapes and forms that I have described: the joy of playing together, hearing a piece of music evolve, creating a new arrangement, the sound of the other instruments……
