Irish Musics ability to sound “full” with a single instrument

I’ve noticed many things about music over these past few months, and tonight I decided to take a break from listening to Classical to listen to some good old Irish Traditional music. Anyway, in my listening session I noticed something, a single fiddle sounding like a whole orchestra, then it got me thinking, was this baked into the style out of necessity? It would make sense that the poorer farmers of Ireland would want to listen to something more than just a melody. A single melody by itself is great but can quickly wear thin on its own if a) it’s not supported by something and b) If it’s all you listen to ever, then I listened to Conal O Grada and that’s when it really clicked. There’s implied polyphony. The quick switches of register create the illusion of multiple voices. Imagine one person trying to sing all of the parts in a song, the bass, the tenor, the alto and the soprano. The perpetual motion of the melody gives the illusion of someone trying to sing all of it by themself. It’s an interesting little thing that I thought I’d share.

There’s nothing uniquely Irish about the solo use of essentially monophonic instruments. The ability to outline harmonic structures is implicit in the mere notion of melody. Re: Irish Musics ability to sound “full” with a single instrument

There are many examples of a single instrument maximising its sound and impact.

It’s by no means confined to ITM.

My grandfather was a locally respected Appalachian fiddler. I have his fiddle; the neck has been filed flatter, and the nut and bridge carved to match this more shallow arc. It’s practically impossible to play the D or A strings alone, and quite easy to play three strings at once. I never heard him play- he died when I was a toddler- but he clearly employed double-stops (and possibly triple-stops) and/or droning as a matter of course.

And, there’s a rattlesnake rattle inside to give a faint buzzing sound.

Highland pipers are well aware that though their chanter only has 9 notes and the drones play a single note (in octaves) each note of the chanter has a unique colour against the drones, creating a rich and changing palette of tone as a tune is played. Ditto with Bulgarian pipes, which have a single drone.

Obviously the Pipe Organ, harp, uilleann pipe, accordion etc are ways for a single player to employ polyphony.

Re: Irish Musics ability to sound “full” with a single instrument

I haven’t been left with this impression at all. I single violin has never sounded like a “whole-orchestra” to me. I’ve heard some impressive playing from melody instrument soloists, but I can’t say that this is due to some perceived polyphony.

The notable thing about Irish music, in my opinion, is the percussive and rhythmic expressions you find can be gotten out of legato and wind instruments. Flutters, chirps, clicks, snaps, etc. The percussive nature of the music adds a dynamic that makes the melodies much more interesting; Engaging to the point of being danceable. This is why the music is so satisfying while being melody dominated.

Also, even though Irish music isn’t necessarily unique when it comes to it’s capacity to be interesting enough with melody alone; It is unique in that those melodies are exceptionally dynamic and full of character. Unlike some styles of music where the melody lacks in movement, most Irish tunes are full conversations; some being short arguments/exclamations, and others being complete narratives. You can’t say this about a lot of music. Even other music that has narrative quality doesn’t offer the same depth of movement as Irish Traditional Music.

The music sounds complete because it is complete. There are no illusions.

Re: Irish Musics ability to sound “full” with a single instrument

“The Baroque dance suites often sound like they are meant to be alone and empty in that way when it’s a solo instrument”

By definition, the word solo designates something done by one person alone. If that activity involves the use of a musical instrument, the tool will impose constraints of its own on what the performer can do with it — again pretty much by definition.

“Why doesn’t Irish music make use of extended techniques?”

Musical genres tend to be characterized by a set of expressive techniques culled from those that are otherwise practicable on the instruments it employs, which is itself a selective process. A basic technique in one context can easily be an extended technique (whatever that even means) in another.

There is nothing exceptional about ITM here. The stylistic earmarks of the idiomatic performance of traditional dance tunes reflect the tastes and perception of the community in which they developed. A lot of the tunes taken to be Irish can be traced back to published compilations where they appear alongside tunes by named Baroque composers — jig rubbing shoulders with gigue.

“The real thing about Irish traditional Jigs and Reels is they change registers so fast you barely even notice. Like every time the low D or high E is used on a fiddle it’s a new register for the instrument”

This straddles the gap between overstatement and meaninglessness. What on earth is a barely noticeable rapid change of registers, or a low D being in any other register than the one it belongs to? How about links to a performance or two of an Irish dance tune on a fiddle that illustrates the point you’re trying to make.

Re: Irish Musics ability to sound “full” with a single instrument

“That’s not in the complexity of playing, but the complexity of the music itself.”

Nah, I don’t agree with the premise that ITM is somehow special when it comes to how possible it is to sound “full” with a single instrument. Setting aside Classical music, there are other folk genres where a single instrument can fill up enough space to capture an audience with a solo, unaccompanied performance.

I don’t believe it’s some special quality of register shifts, implied polyphony or “movement” inherent in ITM. I think it’s that when we learn to play this music reasonably well, we gain enough familiarity to hear the subtleties in a very good solo instrumental performance. The things a newcomer to ITM would probably miss. Those same subtleties and potential to sound “full” exist in other folk music when an instrument is played solo. Our ears just aren’t trained to hear it, unless we live within that other culture and play that other music.

Hi Everyone,
I am new to this site and to ITM. I discovered ‘The Session’ because I was searching the Net for Music that had Melody as its primary focus. I picked up a Musical Instrument (a Mandolin) for the first time in my life at age 54 (9 years ago) and taught myself to play it and to read Music. I have only just, in the last 6 months, begun to play it publicly. During the prior 8 1/2 years, I quietly learnt and practiced a number of Folk, Medieval and Baroque Melodies, either at home or on my bicycle travels. I can now add to that list two ITM pieces, ‘Carolan’s Farewell to Music’ and ‘Inisheer’, both of which, to me, need no harmonic addition whatsoever, they are simply stunning Melodies rich with emotion and depth of feeling.

On my extensive bicycle travels, I would often be practicing quietly in Public spaces and it never ceased to amaze me how frequently I would be confronted (whether I wished it or not) by a Muso (usually a Guitar player) who proceeded to inform me that I was going about things incorrectly, and that I should learn how to strum Chords and read TAB, so that I could expediently reach the Public performance level they were at. This was usually followed by them fetching their Guitar and assaulting me with a wall of sound in the form of G, C & D Chords. I was seeking nuance and subtlety, the perfect, considered placement of my finger on a Fret, the perfect considered understanding of my finger leaving that Fret and moving to the next. To me, Melody encourages us to strive to achieve that perfection (however elusive it may be), whilst Harmony can sometimes be used as a means of covering up our desired expediency.

I have recently started adding Harmony (Arpeggiated Chords) as well as a Walking Baseline on a Liuto Cantabile, and Percussion on a Frame Drum, to some of the Melodies I play, using a Loop Station.

But to me, the beauty of the two ITM pieces I quoted above, need nothing more than the Melody played on my Mandolin.

Regards
Andy
