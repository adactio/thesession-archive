Temperament systems in Irish music

Apparently old Irish music like the old recordings used somewhere in between C Natural and B natural instead of C natural, so the result was an oddly out of tune sounding intonation. This is what people call the Irish temperament. On the fiddle the C naturals are slightly flat. Does anyone know anything more about this? It seems consistent enough to call it a feature rather than a flaw. It’s definitely intentional. Seems that standard equal temperament killed this and eventually C natural was used. I hate to start a discussion about the old pure drop, but it seems Irish music has been sort of changed inorganically. Was It ever really meant to have Harmony? Does Harmony really suit the music? These are questions I don’t have a definitive answer to, but I would love to hear other people’s thoughts. Here are a few of my thoughts on the subject. I think Irish music was always monophonic. That is one melodic line potentially with a drone. I don’t think standard Western Harmony really suits the music. It’s like the difference between true flamenco and flamenco as popular culture thinks of it. If you look at tunes from O‘Neill’s book, the first thing you’ll notice is the rhythms are very different and not standardized for each dance type. The meter is, but The rhythms are not. It’s not like today where each reel is 8 eighth notes per measure. There’s dotted rhythms, triplets all of that mixed together. People would consider this impromptu ornamentation of the tune, which it very well could be, but That’s not the only possibility. The other possibility is that these are integral parts of old tunes before a standardized version of the dances were set. Seems kind of sad that history has forgotten what the music was like before session playing became a standard way of playing the music. These dotted rhythms in #1,300 in O’Neill’s “woman of the house” you wouldn’t see that in a modern-day reel. At least I didn’t when I was learning this music. Seems like the old Irish music was much more diverse and experimental when it came to playing with what dance music is. Just a thought. Everything became standardized and homogenized after the revival in a way that it never was before then. We should look at the past in order to innovate in Irish music on its own terms. Screw classical Western Harmony. The pure drop had it’s own intonation, interesting rhythms, and overall language that has become extinct in modern practice it seems. I don’t know. Just some thoughts I had. What does everyone else think? Am I just a crazy nut job with rose tinted glasses? Or is there something to what I’m saying?

Re: Temperament systems in Irish music

In ITM, say 100 years ago, there was much, much less regular contact between musicians, and there was no real radio or TV programming with music. This meant that individual and local styles thrived and grew independently of each other. Like dialects. It didn’t matter that you weren’t tuned to A = 440 because chances are you played with other ppl locally and it didn’t matter as long as you agreed. And because you played locally your local crew would know loads of crooked tunes because “that’s how we play it here.”

When RTE and touring ceildh bands and records and TV showed up in the early 1950s, musicians started hearing way more of how other ppl played. When the ITM revival happened in the 1960s and sessions started-- as a byproduct of ppl at various fleadhs “having tunes” outside of formal performances-- there was “pressure” to play in more standardised tunings and rhythms because that is the only way a diverse bunch of ppl can not sound horrible together.

It’s a kind of iron law of music that as time goes along thins get standardised. If you want to see this, listen to Led Zeppelin’s “Black Dog” with its tempo and time-sig changes. A song like that could *never* debut on the radio now, because everyting has to be 3:40 long and in 96 bpm time, because of having to be mixable with commercials and other songs.

Re: Temperament systems in Irish music

As I understand it, O’Neill transcribed what he heard in Chicago, no different than the many local session tunebooks being produced today, it just happens that this one survived the ages.

All transcribed tunebooks will have their local differences and the settings may vary from session to session and are always influenced by the interpretation of the transcriber.

I don’t know how accurate useful it is to extrapolate from O‘Neill’s to the whole of traditional Irish music at the time outside of Chicago other than to say “the players in Chicago played the tunes this way when O’Neill transcribed them”.

As far as intonation, some of the shading of pitches you refer to actually improve the consonance of harmonies. If anything the move towards equal temperament on many common session instruments has made the harmonies more dissonant, which is pretty apparent when you have Uilleann pipes tuned in D just intonation using the regulators playing against, for example, a box or concertina in equal temperament. The more experienced fiddlers I play with understand this and will shade their intonation when playing with pipes to be more consonant. Taking the thirds out of the chords on the box and limiting them in backup on guitar and bouzouki can go a long way to reduce the dissonance when playing with pipes.

Re: Temperament systems in Irish music

“It’s definitely intentional. Seems that standard equal temperament killed this and eventually C natural was used.”

I would not call it intentional, and it’s also not the case that eventually C natural was used. What you’re probably talking about here is what is sometimes referred to as the Piper’s C, or C Supernatural. It’s a result of how certain diatonic instruments like pipes, whistles, and flutes produce the note when cross-fingered.

I get that slightly sharp Cnat when I cross-finger the note on my flute, and I actually prefer that sound to the Equal Tempered Cnat when I use the C key. Other flute players might prefer the C key, or blow the note flatter with embouchure. Not an option on pipes and whistles, you’re going to get that sharp Cnat with cross-fingering.

In one local session where a piper is one of the hosts and leads many of the tunes, I’ve noticed the fiddlers will instinctively match his sharp Cnat. In another session that rarely includes pipes, whistles, or flutes, the fiddlers stick closer to a 12TET Cnat. So as far as the “C Supernatural” goes, it depends on who is playing and what instruments they’re using.

Re: Temperament systems in Irish music

gilmo789, yes, plenty of single reels in particular feature a first half that essentially repeats four bars, and a second half of eight bars where the final two (or sometimes four) bars feature a different melodic phrase, basically an extended ending.

Re: intonation, as others have said, on fiddle it is routine to shade intonation to match the just or tempered tuning of other instruments. This is especially true when playing with uilleann pipes, not simply because of a given chanter’s intonation but also to match the ways C and other notes are articulated on pipes. Many trad fiddlers do intentionally retain a “piper’s” intonation when playing solo because they prefer it. To ears trained on formal Western music, it may sound off, but once you get accustomed to it, there’s a richness that’s missing from modern temperaments.

Finger placement on fiddle also varies depending on key—for example, the first finger B natural note on the A string is slightly sharper in, say, E major than it is in G major. Fiddlers who don’t make these adjustments sound “off.”

i happen to be reading the trevor wye practice book for the flute, book 4, intonation and vibrato, where he explains “on the fingers” the discrepancy between harmonics (multiple-of frequencies), harmonies (3rds, 5ths, the 2/3, 3/4 frequency ratios) and the 12tet equal temperament scale (pianos, guitars, mandolins). to appear to be in-tune against other harmonics-rich instruments, the flautist (and the fluter and the piper) must “sweeten” most intervals. in irish music this effect/result is called the piper’s C and piper’s F. it is all pure math relating frequency ratios, has nothing to do with shamrocks, esthetics, A440 or number of repeats and length of A and B parts on tunes.

Re: Temperament systems in Irish music

If we’re talking uilleann pipes, when every note of the scale is heard against a fixed note the musician can choose, for each note, from multiple options which have varying degrees of consonance with the drone note.

Here it’s all laid out with the numbers https://www.kylegann.com/Octave.html

It’s all about The Harmonic Series, the series of pitches present in all musical sound, and very much present in the sound of the drones.

Generally from tradition to tradition (Scotland, Ireland, Galicia, Bulgaria, etc etc) pipers make the same choices, because their ears are hearing the same phenomena.

The lower the note is in the Harmonic Series the stronger is its presence in the tone of the drones.

If we’re talking D pipes, the Harmonic Series goes

Fundamental D
1st Harmonic D
2nd " D
3rd " A
4th " D
5th " F#
6th " A
7th " C (natural)
8th " D
9th " E
10th " F#
11th " G

So all the chanter notes are present between the 1st and 11th Harmonic. (C# is the 15th Harmonic.)

Pipers have ears, they can hear the Harmonics, and they can hear how a chanter note will beat against the Harmonic note if it’s too flat or too sharp compared to the note present in the Harmonics. This is what leads pipers of all traditions towards the same chanter tuning scheme.

You can look up where all those Harmonics are pitched https://www.kylegann.com/Octave.html but here’s how they vary from Equal Temperament

D +/- 0
E +4
F# -14
G -2
A +2
B -16
C -31 (this is the Harmonic minor 7th, the 7th Harmonic, the strongest blend with the drones)
C# -12 (this is the 15th Harmonic)

Specifically about C natural, there’s another C present in the Harmonic series

C +18

Highland pipers preferred the +18 minor 7th up until around the 1980s, when the taste changed to the -31 (7th Harmonic) minor 7th which does ring better with the drones.

On the uilleann pipes many pipers keep their finger in contact with the edge of the top fingerhole when fingering C natural so the pitch can be controlled to the taste of the piper. It’s stuff like that, and how each reed has a slightly different built-in scale, and how pipers can alter pitch with various fingerings, and rushes, and tape, which foils attempts at determining chanter scale by measuring the holes, used by musicologists such as Podnos.

Well, your man is wrong of course about a rigid system of 8 notes existing with a violinist.

For one thing, the “Western” scale (by which I suppose he means Equal Temperament) has 12 tones.

For another thing, violinists use that scale when it’s called for, but use Just Intonation when that is called for.

As I’ve pointed out before, you can sometimes see (if you look closely and quickly) a violinist hitting a double-stop and one finger quickly moving from the muscle-memory ET spot to the in-tune JI spot, typically to bring the sharp ET Major 3rd down to where it’s in tune. (That is, if they don’t hit the JI spot from the get-go.)

The pitches present in the Harmonic Series are physics, not the creation of any human culture West or East.

Re: Temperament systems in Irish music

In the actual playing of these tunes, at least by adept musicians well immersed in the tradition, it’s not uncommon for the “accent” or emphasis to move around. Trad musicians tend to think in terms of strong beats and weak beats, the placement of which can be shifted around each time through a tune.

In a reel, say, the strong beats can land on what other genres call the downbeat (ONE two three four) or on the back beat (one two THREE four), as suits the tune and the player’s interpretation of it, which itself is variable. And it’s possible or even probably that a player will use most or all of the potential permutations of this in a single rendition of the tune (each of the following representing a single reel measure):

ONE two three four ONE two three four
one two THREE four one two THREE four
ONE two THREE four ONE two THREE four
one two three four one two three four
ONE two three four one two THREE four
one two THREE four ONE two three four
ONE two three four ONE two THREE four
and so on….
