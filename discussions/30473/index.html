Re: Deriving Song Melodies from Dance Tunes.

I guess lyrics would have a certain internal beat just like the tune, so part of if would be matching the number of syllables to the beats per measure (and number of measures). You wouldn’t necessarily have to be locked into AABB structure; you could write a song using just the A part of the the tune (or B). Or, you could modify a tune to fit a particular lyric. Every tune has that particular phrase, or hook, which sets it apart. I suppose one would want to incorporate that in the song.

You could study how others have done it. Cherish the Ladies have a song to the tune of the Foxhunter’s Slip Jig. And, there is a song to the Rocky Road to Dublin on a Chieftan’s CD. Also, isn’t Andy Stewart’s Take Her in Your Arms sung to the tune of the Otter’s Holt? I’m sure there are many more examples. Maybe the process for airs would be just to dig up the long-forgotten lyrics in the first place, since a lot of them started out as songs.

If you are after singing a song and have people be thinking to themselves, “that sounds like Mist Covered Mountains,” you would need to incorporate enough of the tune to be recognizable. Supposedly, the Yellow Rose of Texas is based on Patsy Geary’s, but not the entire tune.

Tune titles would suggest a story line for the song.

Burns adapted tradtional tunes to his lyrics. I don’t know his process, i.e., if he had a tune in mind when he composed a poem, etc. There have been tunes written to some of Yeats’ poems (and others).

Probably doesn’t help much, but all I could think of at the time.

Re: Deriving Song Melodies from Dance Tunes.

If you look up the history of dance tunes, you will find that a large proportion of them started out as song tunes. The older the tune is, the more likely it is to have been a song.

Burns used several different procedures. Usually he pastiched an existing older song, sometimes he started over with unrecognizably different words, sometimes he wrote words to newly composed tunes.

“Rocky Road to Dublin” was written as a song tune, on commission for a British music hall performer. I think the composer based it on an older Scottish song tune, “Aye Waukin O”, which was very well known across the British Isles at the time.

“The Foxhunter’s Jig” is a variant of a Scottish or northern English song tune from about 1700, “Follow her over the border”.

So, there’s a good chance you’ll simply be reversing a process that went the other way a century or three ago, with several sets of original lyrics easily discoverable.

Re: Deriving Song Melodies from Dance Tunes.

About taking existing jig tunes, reel tunes, etc and using them as the melodies for original songs, the man who comes to mind is the Scottish singer/songwriter Andy Stewart (not to be confused with the other Andy Stewart, who sung twee Scottish songs back in the 1960s).

Some of his most successful songs were written in that way.

For example he took the tune of a very common Irish reel (I can’t think of the name at the moment; the Bothy Band recorded it), a reel where the first part is in a minor key but the second part is in a Major key, and used the minor 1st part for the verses and the Major 2nd part for the chorus of his original song.

This has a very long history in Irish music and a large number of the oldest Irish melodies exist in both song and dance-tune versions. Which was the original, the dance-tune or the song? No one can say, and they may have always coexisted.

Re: Deriving Song Melodies from Dance Tunes.

If what passes for my voice is anything to go by, the average bloke who is not a trained or assiduous singer can only sing really comfortably (or at least, without any effort to speak of…) within a span of nine notes of a major scale, including any accidentals present - i.e., little over one octave. Canny hymnal composers have realised this - a whole lot of traditional hymns fit within these limits, at any rate in the Anglican hymnal ‘Hymns Ancient And Modern’.

Nearer to trad, quite a lot of jigs, slip jigs and 3/2s from older Scottish and Northumbrian music come within the one octave / nine note range, and sure enough, quite a few are associated with songs or at least surviving snatches of these. Examples include Elsie Marley, The Peacock Followed The Hen, Felton Lonning and Shew Us The Way To Wallington, and there might be tune versions within this range that fit Johnny Cope and Came Ye O’er Frae France, though the versions I’m sure of run just over this range.

Some of these songs seem to be tongue-twisters, at any rate when sung at dance-music speed. Perhaps the singers sought prowess in this field, and hitting the octave jumps etc.of this particular music with some precision.

Re: Deriving Song Melodies from Dance Tunes.

Yes indeed many if not most traditional hymn tunes have a limited range. Probably the most common hymn tune of all, HYFRYDOL, has only the range of a sixth. (I’ve come across over twenty different hymns which use HYFRYDOL.)

But some old hymn tunes have a quite large range such as SLANE (Be Thou My Vision; Bridegroom and Bride) which has the range of an 11th.

Loads of traditional jigs and reels have only the range of an octave or an octave plus a note- O Neills Music Of Ireland has dozens and dozens of them.

Re: Deriving Song Melodies from Dance Tunes.

“I was thinking more in the line of simplifing the tunes that made them more like airs which could be sung easily.”

In the tradition it’s the opposite: the air version of the tune is more melodically complex, the dance-tune version is simplified.

Tomas O Canainn in Traditional Music in Ireland has some examples, side-by-side, of a traditional dance-tune and its related traditional sean nos song, such as

Tuirne Mhaire = An Brianach Og
The Black Bird (the one in G) = A Spailpin a Ruin

and the sean nos air in each case is far more complex melodically.
