Stripping out Ornamentation for practice

Hi all,

I see fairly common reference in these forums and in other places to ‘stripping away the ornamentation’ when learning a tune. The idea makes sense (although in some cases it does require alternate fingerings, but that’s not a complaint) - but some tunes seem to lend themselves to that more naturally than others. Are there rules to get into if you’re trying to strip the ornamentation away from a particular setting of a tune? Obviously removing rolls/cuts/whatever you call them but also flattening out triplets, turning them into one long or two short notes rather than three - but is there a

Just as an example, take the first measure of Rolling in the Barrel - the first setting on this site (https://thesession.org/tunes/500) implies something quite different from the second to me. If you were to take the ornaments away from the first, you’d be left with
|:EE E2 BE E2 which feels kind of boring, if you take the Turn and make it a single long note. Turning it into:
|:EE EE BE EE feels maybe a bit jackhammerish
but both of these assume that the ornamented note in the first measure is a single note triplet.

I’ve heard recordings where the first measure is more like:
|: EE G/F/E BE E/E/E
and so there the second setting makes sense - reducing it to something like
|: EE FE BE FE
or
|: EE GE BE GE
etc

But enough out of me - do you have a general approach to simplifying for practice when either learning from a written setting or a recording?

Re: Stripping out Ornamentation for practice

My opinion (and personal practice) on this likely isn’t a popular one, at least on this forum. But even as a beginner (half a century ago), I learned tunes with ornaments as part and parcel of the tunes. I mostly learned tunes on the fly directly from other musicians who were not playing stripped down settings.

Though challenging at first, this had several advantages:
- I learned the various ornaments in their natural habitat, in the context which they are used. So I immediately understood their role in enhancing the pulse of the tunes.
- Conversely, I learned the tunes with their pulse intact and robust, with the cuts, rolls, and triplets where they were wanted to help generate that pulse.
- I understood that ornaments were not static, they could be varied—substituting a triplet for a roll, say—and quickly learned the many ways they could fit into a tune.

Nowadays, if someone teaches a stripped down setting, as I learn the tune I tend to ‘fill in’ where I hear the ornaments belonging. Some stripped down settings are so bare they lose the pulse, especially the subtle, internal pulse of a 3 or 4 note phrase.

That said, having a feel for the tune’s bare minimum does come in handy. At a gig (and for my own pleasure at home), I might start a tune out with a very bare bones setting and then fill in as I repeat the tune. But the bare bones version often features very different phrasing and the barest of melody compared to what most people would hear as the basic tune. I hold unexpected notes, smooth the pulse over several measures, and play at the softer end of the dynamic spectrum, with few accented notes.

Not everyone’s cuppa, I know. But it’s how my mind’s ear hears this music.

Re: Stripping out Ornamentation for practice

Adam, a common and effective option for creating pulse (without using a roll or triplet) on long notes, such as in your example of Rolling in the Barrel, is to use a cut to break the note where the accent wants to fall.

So instead of DE3 BE3 you might play DE{G}E2 BE{G}E2 or DE{G}EE BE{G}EE.

(I don’t play piano accordion, so forgive me if my choice of G for the cut note is cumbersome on your instrument. Find a cut that works for you.)

Another option is to play a melodic phrase instead of the long note. Tunes that feature something like G~B3 d~B3 can readily be played GBAB dBAB, and the filled in notes let you vary the note length (long-short long-short, say), slurring (if any), and emphasis to suit your feel for the phrase.

Once you have the pulse as you want it in a stream of distinct notes, then you can explore how to create that pulse on one long note in GB3 dB3 without using ornaments. On fiddle, I might give the bow a little push—a slight extra pressure and speed—in the middle of that B3. I’m guessing on accordion it’s a matter of doing the same with the bellows?

All of which reveals that (on some instruments) pulse can come from one hand or the other, and often comes from both hands working in tandem. Simply experiencing this can be an epiphany! Rinse and repeat a million times (using ornaments, using melodic phrases, and just pulsing the driving hand) and you’ll find it does become automatic to the point where you cannot hear or play the music without pulse and nyah.

Re: Stripping out Ornamentation for practice

Years ago I commented somewhere here that I personally felt that it was a cop-out to simplify a tune because it was too difficult. I have since changed my mind on that opinion, because over time I’ve learned that it’s more effective to learn a simplified version and just develop more advanced technique over time. If playing simplified variations helps you maintain your rhythm and flow, it’s better than forcing yourself into more advanced technique that’s going to trip you up.

So my stance on this where ornamentation and articulations are concerned: Develop your ornamentations in slow practice, but if you can’t play them at speed then leave them out at speed. As a movement becomes 2nd-nature, it takes less and less effort to execute it, which gives you mental space to add to the movement. This is a healthy way to build up your articulations over time, and avoiding bad habits and compromised technique.

Re: Stripping out Ornamentation for practice

I agree that focusing on good timing and phrasing is key to playing music well, and those elements are especially important in this music because it hinges on playing in unison with others.

That said, I still think it’s six of one, half dozen of another over the long haul. I’ve heard people who learned a batch of tunes without any ornaments, while woodshedding on ornaments on the side, and when they finally went to incorporate the ornaments, everything went haywire—the tunes lost their rhythm and timing, the ornaments turned to mush, and they struggled to get through the tunes at all. In short, they regressed significantly on their learning curve, and it took months (or longer) to put it all together.

So I’m not convinced that practicing ornaments in isolation (and tunes without them) is somehow better or more efficient than learning the music whole, as it’s actually played in the wild.

It’s also not true that learning a tune with ornaments locks you into always putting the birly bits in the same place. As I said earlier, if you learn one tune from a variety of sources (from different instruments), you get a variety of interpretations, including a wide range of types of ornaments and when and where they’re played. (You also learn from the outset that this is how the tunes are played, no one “right” way but rather many possibilities.)

Sorry, I sincerely don’t mean this to be an argument; I’m simply presenting another perspective. I’m all in for following a “learning progression,” from basic skills and concepts to more intermediate to advanced. But in my experience, it’s best to balance this with the understanding that the sooner you start learning cuts, triplets, rolls, cranns, etc., the sooner you’ll be able to do them. And the sooner you play tunes with ornaments and variations (and of course good timing and phrasing), the sooner you’ll feel comfortable doing just that. If you put off learning something for 8 months then it will just be another 8 months down the road before you gain competence in it. Reminds me of the adage that the best time to plant a tree is 20 years ago.

Of course, some people really struggle with rhythm, timing, intonation, and other fundamentals. Or they’re learning this music in relative isolation, not able to immerse in tunes played in person by good musicians. Such folks will likely benefit from woodshedding a while on basic skills separate from the tunes. But plenty of other people find a more holistic approach suits them better—the distinct elements make more sense and are easier to execute when they come together as *music,* matching what you hear your mentors and musical heroes playing.

Re: Stripping out Ornamentation for practice

“Its a fine line between ornament and playing technique”

In this music, cuts, taps, rolls, triplets, smears, bends, etc., aren’t really ornaments. They’re how we “speak” the notes and pulse. So while we can vary which ones we use and when we use them, their purpose is more integral than mere “embellishment” of the melody. There’s no fine line—they are techniques, tools for how the notes are spoken and how timing is articulated.

Yes, you can play the tunes without them. But if you want to sound like an Irish trad musician, you’ll want to use some of these bits.

Re: Stripping out Ornamentation for practice

The birly bits don’t make this “art music,” whatever that is (except a red herring).

David, yes, you move the ornaments around as part of varying a tune, and different instruments use ornaments in somewhat different ways, places, etc. But that doesn’t necessarily mean your default setting of the tune is stripped of all the birly bits.

Nothing is more tedious than sitting at a session next to someone playing the Humours of Tulla and literally plays the B part: |af f2 df f2|af f2 g2 fg|….

Now there are any number of ways to play that phrase, many of which would use some birly bits:
cuts
af{a}fe df{a}e|af{a}fe….
short rolls
af~f2 df~f2|af~f2….
long rolls
a~f3 d~f3|a~f3….
trebles
af f/f/f df f/f/f|af f/a/f….
and so on.
Or you could do a melodic variation
afef dfef|afef….

So which one of those is “the tune”?

I’d say they *all* are because any one of them works in a traditional style and sensibility. I’ve even heard good trad musicians occasionally play afff dfff|afff… in such situations, though rarely, and not twice around. You could even turn it inside out and play f3e d3f|f3a…. But you won’t likely hear anyone who knows what they doing play af f2 df f2|af f2… because it so poorly lends itself to creating proper timing and pulse.

When you learn a tune, ideally you’re also learning it within the larger context of the music.

In my experience, it’s not about melody OR pulse OR ornaments but about melody AND pulse AND ornaments.

Will, I agree.

I find in real world experience that some players learn tunes with specific ornamentation and are incapable of playing the sparse version of the tune.

I see this often in sessions, where as the speed increases, these players inevitably crash and burn because they’re trying to cram every little thing that they practiced into the music no matter what speed the tunes are being played at.

To me this is the heart of the matter.

When I teach whistle or concertina tunes, I generally teach fairly sparse versions, and then talk about opportunities for variation in those places where there are choices and how to separate notes. After that we might talk about melodic variation or fills. I always emphasize being able to scale and adapt your playing of tunes to the speed being played.

What I would do as far as ornamentation, particularly on concertina when the execution of multi-note cuts and rolls is more physically complex, playing reels in a session at a relaxed 90 bpm is going to be very different than that same set of reels played at 120 bpm for dancers. As the speed increases, I’ll move towards sparser and sparser versions of the tune, reduce multi note rolls on repeated notes to single note cuts or no cut at all, drop any discretionary single note triples, etc. all in the interest of maintaining solid rhythm over everything else.

This is a skill one has to practice and master as much as any other aspect of the music.

Adam, my “expendable” comment wasn’t aimed at your “not required to achieve lift” but at the many comments that encourage getting rid of the birly bits. Notably, Will suggests doing so in sessions. In my world, that advice should come with a warning that such an approach may not be welcome at all sessions.

I started playing this music 50 years ago, among players of a much older generation, and so I likely have a different perspective from anyone who’s comparatively new to this music.

Frankly, “ornaments” is such a misleading term. The birly bits are not decorations or embellishments added to a note but how the note is sounded. Each note has a beginning, middle, and end. We articulate those parts with cuts, taps, rolls, cranns, triplets, smears, and such. Not on every note, of course, and not the same way each time around. But they are integral to the dialect that makes Irish trad music Irish trad. Think of them like the use of glottal stops in Hawaiian—a native speaker says “Hawai’i,” with the stop between the two i’s. Non-native speakers often say “Huhwhyee” all in one slur. To a Hawaiian, that sounds wrong.

In music, lift and pulse are mostly generated through precise timing and emphasis, the latter of which is created by stretching and cutting short certain notes (really, the beats those notes land on), and dynamics (playing strong beats a bit louder). Cuts, taps, rolls, etc., are all ways to articulate timing and emphasis. Without them, the music sound less than Irish. Yes, we also articulate timing and emphasis with bellows, breath, bows, etc. No one is saying that the birly bits are the “only” way.

My point is simply that to be a musician, you have to learn all the essential bits. It’s important to understand how they all work together, in context not in isolation. In the beginning, accept that you’re going to feel overwhelmed, that your playing is a messy work in progress. You *will* spend decades fine tuning your timing and phrasing (and more). But if that’s all you’re woodshedding on, then you won’t be making music. Back when I started learning this music, you played tunes—music!—from day one, and you learned all the “truckly how” in the context of tunes. I’m forever grateful to my mentors for giving me the music that way, rather than dissecting it into disparate elements. I don’t expect my approach to be popular, and it doesn’t surprise me to read Will and others advocating a more step-by-step progression. ]]]]But enough out of me - do you have a general approach to simplifying for practice when either learning from a written setting or a recording?]]]

There’s good news: On Irish accordion, what some might call “simplifying” or “stripping out” ornaments, is totally acceptable and traditional in place of a complex many-noted ornament. For those long notes in, say, “Rolling in the Barrel,” or any of the longer pauses in a run during a tune where a five-note ornament might go, it is perfectly OK to just play a triplet on the same note, or with a cut in the middle. You won’t win the All-Ireland, because the Comhaltas gatekeepers want to hear box players imitate five-note fiddle rolls. But for my money the most beautiful and old-school-sounding Irish box playing keeps the ornaments clean and simple.

The trick is to get the rhythm of the ornament right. If you do a simple triplet, you want to jigger it so it syncopates rhythmically like a five-note ornament would in that spot. There is a little teensy bit of dotting in the ornamen,t whether it is a simple triplet or a five-note barrage of gatlin’ gun staccatissimo. The teensy bit of dotting distributes differently in jigs than in reels. You can get the gist of it by listening a lot--you already seem to have the idea.

You can also do what is sometimes called “half-rolls,” which is a triplet with one cut inserted after the first time the main note plays. You could also reverse that, and insert your cut right before the last note in the triplet. Lots of very fine traditional box players and fiddlers as well, just do this and don’t do five-noters.

I personally hate five-note fiddle rolls on the accordion. What sounds like a cool little ripple on a fiddle sounds like a car alarm to me on an accordion, and I hate the “major-seventh” sound of the lower tone in a classic five-note fiddle roll when played on an accordion. On PA or CBA I either play simple triplets, “half-rolls” like I just described, same-note staccato ornaments akin to what fiddlers call “bowed triplets,” or piper-ish cranns where the roll is made up of a triplet with two cuts inserted, one above the note, and the second still above the note, but lower than the first cut. You choose what notes to use as cuts by trial and error. If the note sounds weird, don’t use it.

But honestly, on a piano accordion, you do not have to do that much to get a great traditional sound ornament-wise. You’ll hear the hotshots doing the gatlin’ gun thing, but it’s not needed. The most important thing is no matter how simple your triplets or ornaments, is to get the rhythm of it down.

It comes to mind that I might pass on one of the most useful things I was taught in a workshop. I’ve found them almost useless for actual teaching, the use has been getting to be up close to great players and watch and hear them play. But every workshop I have heard maybe one useful thing in terms of actual input.

So, this input was many years ago, from the late great Joe Burke, in what he called the master class at Willie Clancy week. It doesn’t just apply to accordion, it applies to any Irish ornamenting. But he is the person who articulated this to me early on. In traditional Irish ornamentation, a “cut” is rhythmically NOT a “grace note.” In classical music a grace note is shorter, a little filip that slams onto the note it’s ornamenting. In Irish ornaments, a cut gets the same value as the other notes in the ornament. So, if you’re inserting a cut into a same-note triplet to make a “half-roll,” the inserted note is the same value as the three notes in the triplet. The same goes if you are doing a five-note roll or crann--the two cuts you insert into that long ornament, have the same note value as the triplet note you are ornamenting. It’s tricky because when I started playing Irish accordion for some reason I had a Willie Clancy book. And the cuts were written like “grace notes.” But they are not played like grace notes.

RIP Joe Burke.

Another thing you can do that didn’t come to mind in my last posts, is to induce the accordion to do the triplet or roll for you, or give you the sound effect of a triplet or roll. The sound effect would be that of a same-note triplet or longer same-note roll. There is a fine free-reed player and character who posts or did post here who dubbed this effect, “The Phantom Button,” since it’s kind of an “aural illusion” (as opposed to an optical illusion) that the ear takes for a roll with more notes in it than your fingers actually played.

It’s more well-known as something concertina players do--you can give a light tap or whack to the side of the concertina with the hand that isn’t actually playing--you time it with the note you want to be a triplet or roll, and once you get the timing it will make the note sound more times. Some Irish players and teachers call it a “slap roll” or “slap triplet.” Other concertina players get the effect with a well-timed jiggle of the knee or thigh.

You can do the same on the accordion, so long as your accordion chassis and reeds are responsive enough. I do it all the time on concertina and on small PAs and CBAs. Not playing much bisonoric aka “diatonic” box lately, but those boxes do it too.

It’s easy, it’s fun, and as one knowledgeable West Coast musician who sometimes posts here has remarked, it gives the impression of a slur, that affords some relief from the “typewriter” sound that free-reed instruments can have.
