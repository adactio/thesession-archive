Quote of the day

“The beginner, and strangers coming to this music, should not, initially, bother with the forms of ornamentation. It will be time enough to begin decorating the music when one has impressed the rhythm on the ear and by practice acquired a certain agility with the fingers. It is his rhythm which distinguishes the performer who plays as to the manner born. When beginning to learn this music one should aim to play in that manner. There is no difficulty in doing this, in becoming a native, provided one listens only to genuine players and one has chosen an instrument on which no other form of training had been received.”

– from Breandán Breathnach’s “Introduction,” CRE 2, xiv.

Well Ive been saying this since I got here regarding ornaments, and taken a lot of grief for it on these pages , but the second point makes me think…..
What are your opinions ? Agree, disagree? comments?

Re: Quote of the day

“It is his rhythm which distinguishes the performer who plays as to the manner born.”

I agree with this sentiment. I’d far prefer to listen to a musician with great rhythm and minimal ornamentation over a musician who played loads of rolls and triplets and crans but let their rhythm fall all over the place.

However, I’m not sure ornamentation should be totally ignored until one can play the tunes inside out with good rhythm. To me, learning the ornamentation is an essential aspect to learning your instrument. Learning how and where and when to use the ornamention effectively is a whole other kettle of ball games, but I feel the mechanics of it should be taught early on as a basic part of learning to play the instrument. Taking the example of the whistle, there’s nothing particulary complicated or mysterious about playing cuts, taps, rolls or crans. It’s just a matter of getting your fingers to do what your brain wants by repeated practice. I don’t see why that couldn’t be taught alongside good rhythm. When you have your rhythm, ornamentation and articulation sorted out, you have your building blocks to develop style.

Re: Quote of the day

To me it comes back to something I’ve mentioned in other contexts - deliberately separating learning how to play your instrument and learning tunes into two separate activities.

If I hear an ornament I haven’t come across before I might isolate it and learn how to play it so that I can add it to my toolkit, but if I’m learning a tune I will always start by stripping it back to the bare melody and learning that. By the time you know the tune well enough that you can play it without thinking you find that the ornaments it needs have magically put themselves in place without you noticing. Learning just the bare melody means that as your playing improves and your toolkit of ornaments grows (or if you want to play the tune on a different instrument with a different repertoire of ornaments) you can change the ornamentation without having to relearn the tune.

Re: Quote of the day

“one has chosen an instrument on which no other form of training had been received.”

I can quite see where Breathnach was coming from here, but I strongly disagree. The issue here is one of idiom - playing not just the right notes in the right order at the right time, but playing each one with an appropriate length, dynamic, tone, articulation, and (perhaps) ornamentation such that the resulting style is that of the generally accepted style of music which you are trying to play.

The “traditional” approach to teaching idiom is to not really teach it at all, except perhaps by demonstration. Some learners manage to pick it up, through luck, judgement, or a flash of insight. These learners we call “talented”, and we try not to think too hard about the rest. Children, in particular, are good at internalising idiom and therefore often don’t need to be taught it, in the long run - they often just pick it up as they go.

As a GHB teacher, I spend a *massive* amount of time dismantling a single simple tune and teaching pupils the fundamentals of idiom: how similar rhythmic passages must be weighted, how ornamentation must be tightened or loosened at a specific place, how a small number of successive notes relate to each other. I listen to the pupil play a section of a tune, and if it isn’t “right”, it’s *my* job to figure out what they are leaving out and what I need to explain in order for them to understand what they need to change to make it correct. My aim is to have them playing a simple tune as well as anybody else could play it, with no hint that the player is a novice.

This is a little bit of a diversion from the question of prior experience, but I hope it makes sense: someone with prior experience is simply coming with a different idiom. They need to be *taught* that difference and usually they aren’t, then they get the blame for somehow being rubbish. That’s wrong and unfair.

Re: Quote of the day

I agree in general with the original quote that ornamentation should come on top of the melody & rhythm, and/or after the feel of a tune has been learned. There are a couple of obvious exceptions where ornamentation needs to be part of the early learning…

(1) On the whistle (or flute or pipes) you can articulate adjacent similar notes only by tonguing or by cuts/taps. I’ve agreed with the idea that a beginning whistler should drop tonguing to get the melodic flow and learn clear fingering, which means that you need to learn cuts/taps pretty much at the beginning… well, maybe a little after you’ve succeeded with “Mary had a Little Lamb”.

(2) Dynamics of volume is important to the feel of a song, but the whistle has less volume dynamics than fiddle, for example, so expression and emphasis will require learning rolls (or re-introducing tonguing).

So, at least with the whistle, you can’t put off learning at least some decorations. Certainly the fiddle requires greater technical skill just to “scratch” out a tune, so maybe the “Mary had a Little Lamb” phase lasts a little longer.

Re: Quote of the day

I do agree with the general thrust of the second part of the quote, certainly for fiddling and I suspect it applies to other instruments too. Certainly crossing over from violining to fiddling is possible - I did it, but I do think it is harder than starting from scratch. Thinking back to when I made the transition, there were others who started from scratch at the same time. Sure, I had a huge advantage in terms of tone production and technique. But in terms of pulse, lift, nyah, as soon as they had mastered the basics of the instrument they streaked ahead of me. People with just a couple of years experience were making better music than me, with my umpteen years of violining.

It’s difficult to explain, but I think it comes down to how you approach the music - when you play violin your hands work in coordination to produce each note, and if you get the note lengths right then a rhythm emerges. But in fiddling your right hand lays down the rhythm first, and the left hand superimposes the notes upon it, almost as an afterthought. You can see what I’m talking about more clearly in Cajun music or American shuffle bowing, but I think the same thing is happening is a slightly more refined way in well played jigs and reels.

A better way to explain it, that applies to other instruments besides fiddle is to look at how a classical and a trad musician approach a new tune. The classical musician would play it through and think “OK, those are the notes, now where do I put the accents?” Whereas the trad musician would think“OK, its a jig, so it goes Wah ha ha wah ha ha wah ha ha wah ha ha. Now, what are the notes?”

Please take my response w/a grain of salt. I went through the original quotation, considering it phrase by phrase. These are just my thoughts & I don’t intend to give them any more importance than they might deserve; or not.

Thanks, in advance, for anyone who chooses to read them.

“The beginner, and strangers coming to this music,”
This is a very broad, inclusive cross-section of musicians. The one thing they have in common is their initial introduction to “this music”.

“should not, initially,”
reasonable enough, given the 1st bit.

“bother with the forms of ornamentation.”
‘forms’ ~ plural, indicating an array of ornaments & the various instruments on which they are played.

“It will be time enough to begin decorating the music”
again, reasonable given the first bit.

“… when one has impressed the rhythm on the ear and by practice acquired a certain agility with the fingers. It is his rhythm”
Breathnach is pointing out the importance of ‘the rhythm’, it’s need to be impressed (on the ear) & practice.

“which distinguishes the performer who plays as to the manner born. When beginning to learn this music one should aim to play in that manner.”
No comment, at this time.

“There is no difficulty in doing this, in becoming a native,”
He’s simply returning to his premise, which I assume is to lay out a basic, focused approach to learning “this music”."

“provided one listens only to genuine players”
It’s hard to ignore the possible connotations of the phrase ‘genuine players’. For the sake of argument I’ll assume they are players on who’s ears the rhythm has been impressed, the agility of the fingers is there & they have bothered with particular forms of ornamentation.

“ . . . and one has chosen an instrument on which no other form of training had been received.”
This is where I would like to read Breandán Breathnach’s full introduction (as I requested twice in the above discussion) before commenting or attempting to interpret his meaning. Lest anyone think Breandán Breathnach parsed the entirety of Irish traditional music (not to mention other aspects of Irish arts) down to a single, minimalist style I see no good reason to come to that conclusion.

Re: Quote of the day

Breathnach knew exactly what he was talking about, said it very well, and is IMO absolutely correct.

You simply cannot play the music in the true traditional manner if you do not have the rhythm right. Timing, phrasing, rhythm are where you start when learning this music. Ornamentation is important, but is absolutely useless of you don’t have the right rhythm. On the other hand, the music can work with little or no ornamentation if the rhythm and phrasing are correct.

There is no substitute for listening to good players, who play the music properly. Hearing the music when very young, can help instill the proper sense of rhythm. If you start with other forms, it requires a lot of work to internalize the proper way to do it. Even really good players, who have listened to the music all their lives, usually cannot explain precisely how the rhythm works. I think Breathnach has it right, you need to listen carefully to people who do it right, those he calls ‘genuine players’. If you immerse yourself in their playing, it can be done ‘without difficulty’.

Thanks to Dave I have Breathnach’s Introduction. Here is the last part of it; including the quote Mr. Evans posted:

CEOL RINCE NA hÉIREANN II ~ Introduction

Teachers and others who hanker after uniformity and standard versions are asked to
note that the transcriptions in this volume are not prescribed as the correct settings, as
definitive versions which must be rigidly adhered to. They are, it is hoped, correct settings,
settings perhaps better than most other correct settings because of the musical taste of the
performers from whose playing they were transcribed. They should be regarded, however,
chiefly as an aid to memory, as facilitating the learning of a tune, some part of which,
perhaps, had hitherto defied the ear. Players must avoid tying themselves to a text. If they
hear a turn or twist in another setting which appeals to them they should make their own
of it. All have equal rights in this field: they are subject only to the norms observed by
players who are accepted as good performers by other bearers of the tradition.

Some tunes, particularly the set tunes in ²₄ time have been selected with the beginner
in mind. They are catchy, simple in structure and strongly accented and so are easy to
learn, but none of these has been simplified for that purpose. They, also, have been trans-
cribed exactly as rendered. The beginner, and strangers coming to this music, should not,
initially, bother with the forms of ornamentation. It will be time enough to begin decorating
the music when one has impressed the rhythm on the ear and by practice acquired a
certain agility with the fingers. It is his rhythm which distinguishes the performer who plays
as to the manner born. When beginning to learn this music one should aim to play in that
manner. There is no difficulty in doing this, in becoming a native, provided one listens
only to genuine players and one has chosen an instrument on which no other form, of
training had been received.

In a real sense this collection belongs to the musicians of Ireland. It is a testimony to the
richness of the tradition they have gathered from past generations and to their generosity in
passing it on for future generations. The idea of compiling a complete collection of the
music immediately appealed to those instincts which had led them to preserve their store
of it and their appreciation of the merit of the work was evidenced by their desire to
participate in it. All gave ungrudgingly of their time and music. For this and for their
friendship I express my sincerest thanks and gratitude. Go gcúití Dia a saothar is a soilíos
leo.
Breandán Breathnach
