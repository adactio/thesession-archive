Bouzouki’s fine. Amps, not so much. If you’ve ever played in a rock band, you know what happens: the guitarist wants a little amp too, just to hear himself. Re: Personal session applification

There are lots of little micro amps out there, some with speakers but a few like the Vox amplug are just for driving headphones:

http://www.sweetwiter.com/store/detail/amPlugAcous

If your zouk doesn’t already have a pickup installed (which would be the most convenient way to use something like this), then you’d need a contact pickup and a plug adapter. That should work. There is also the slightly hilarious “Mighty Moe Amp Strap”, which does have a speaker but it mutes when you plug in headphones or ear buds:

http://www.musiciansfriend.com/guitars/mighty-moe-ampstrap

Note: it’s not recommended in pro audio circles to use just one earbud with something like in in-ear monitoring system. It has to do with the psychoacoustics of how your brain processes sound. You’ll always have a tendency to run the volume higher with just one earbud in, and the other ear open. So be careful with your volume level.

Or, you could just get a louder instrument. Maybe a carved archtop model? :) Those are louder in sessions than most flattops I’ve heard.

Re: Personal session applification

Whilst I wouldn’t dream of doing it in a million years in anybody else’s session, I use an amp for my harmonicas in our noisy pub settings. It’s a battery-powered Roland Mobile Cube and I use a Microvox harmonica mic and power pack. The other fellers wouldn’t have it any other way because, believe it or not, they want to hear me (and so do I). The only aim is to play with really clean settings at very modest volume. Don’t say you don’t approve until you’ve heard. I’ve been doing this for about fifteen years and no-one complains. When it goes quiet in the pub towards the end of the evening I put all that gear away and just play, which I enjoy far more if I’m honest. I could use Hohner XB40 harps, which are very loud compared to ordinary ones, and I do quite often, but they are a long way from being my favourite harmonicas.

It’s true, mixing audio is where I first noticed this - I found there was a marked deterioration in the quality of my work after about an hour, and my ears had a peculiar “boxy” feeling after the same time. After a while, the penny dropped, and I started taking breaks.
I think it’s likely that you notice it more there because you have to actively engage with the sound, and what you do with the headphones on persists, so you can notice the effects later. If your hearing starts to go funny after two hours of practicing, there’s nothing later to remind you of it. If my hearing goes funny when I’m mixing a piece for radio, I hear it when it goes over the air, and I think “what was I thinking?”.
