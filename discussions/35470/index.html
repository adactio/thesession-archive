Slow Death of the Album

My favorite collections of tunes are on audio digital archives, utube and digital clouds. My albums and CDs have retired and taken their much deserved rest and relaxation after countless spins in a circle.

I feel the slow death of the album is a great thing for traditional music. I have saved thousands of dollars and the music from the underground is better, more traditional and full of life and drive. I find professionally mastered CDs boring, sonically missing something and devoid of life. I enjoy them the first time but then the thrill is gone. Some unprofessional recordings have me going back over and over thirsty for something I can’t explain, a sonic super buzz? What’s going on? Am I de-evolving?

Re: Slow Death of the Album

Boyen,
“artists willingly put their music on Spotify and get money every time you listen to it.” Although technically true, that is a major distortion of the real situation. In most cases the artist has no say in the matter and a great many are extremely unhappy about having their music on Spotify, because what they get paid for it is negligible.

The first problem is Spotify itself. The way their payments work is that that Spotify generates a certain amount of income each month. They take all their costs plus a percentage for profit, and whatever happens to be left over (which generally isn’t a lot) is put in a pot to be divided between the rights holders. In theory if 1/1000 of the plays that month were your song, you get 1/1000 of the pot. Except that in order to fill the catalogue and get the service started they had to do deals with some major labels, giving them a much bigger percentage, and leaving even less for the smaller labels. Currently labels are getting less than 0.001p per play on Spotify, compared with about 0.6p per play on iTunes, if you assume that a track bought on iTunes will get played 100 times.

The second problem comes from the labels. When you buy a CD the band typically gets about 35% of the profit. When you listen on Spotify they typically get between 1 and 5% of the pittance that is paid to the label. Why do they get so little? Because legally a play on Spotify doesn’t count as a sale, it is ‘derivative use’, and when most musicians signed their contracts Spotify didn’t exist and income from derivative use was negligible, so a low percentage made little difference. All that mattered to the artists was a high percentage on sales.

So why do musicians put their music on Spotify? They don’t. Their labels put it there, the musicians have absolutely no say in the matter. The record label holds the rights to the recording and they control how it can or can’t be used. For a label with 10,000 albums on their catalogue all those 0.001ps add up and it becomes worthwhile, but for the musicians, no.

Youtube is another can of worms. Apart from all the above problems of ridiculously low returns, which don’t get passed on to the artists, Youtube has the additional problem of bootlegging. At least Spotify only accept material from the registered rights holder, but anyone can upload to Youtube. For every legitimate play on Youtube, where the rights holder gets paid, there are 20-30 bootleg plays, where the rights holder gets nothing. I know Youtube are making a token gesture towards copyright enforcement, but unless they are made directly responsible for their content, so that rights holders can sue Youtube for infringement rather than having to try to chase the individuals concerned, Youtube will never work as a commercial means of distributing music.

Re: Slow Death of the Album

The idea that anyone with a Mac has more studio capabilities than the Beatles is an illusion, created by people trying to sell Macs. It is true that the Mac gives you more recording and processing power than they had, but that is only a very small part of what makes up a studio. The choice of halls, engineered for different acoustic characteristics, the cupboards full of microphones (each of which on its own may cost several times the price of a Mac) the selection of instruments and amps, the monitoring systems and, most importantly, the staff of very skilled and experienced engineers to operate it all. The reality is that going digital has made very little difference to the cost of building or using a professional recording studio. True, the Mac gives everyone the opportunity to ‘have a bash’, and produce home recordings, but producing music of commercial quality, that people are likely to buy in reasonable quantites, still costs just as much as it ever did. If the new media doesn’t find a way of paying the artists properly it is those high quality commercial recordings we will lose, and your listening pleasure will be reduced to an endless stream of home-recorded Youtube wannabes.
