Issues with drummers in sessions…

Heya folks ! I joined this forum quite recently and after reading a couple of threads I came to see an interesting situation. It seems to me that drummers don’t seem to be the most appreciated kind of musicians in ITM : constant jokes about bodhran players, constant complains about drummers not able to keep rhythm, too loud etc…
After about 15 years playing percussion instruments (Drum Kit, cajon, bodhran, congas mostly), I came to have a immense interest
in Trad music, and started playing cajon in sessions. It actually fits really well, and really enjoyed by the others members of most sessions i go to. Now my questions: why is that ? too many bad experiences with bad drummers or simply do you think percussion is not necessary/ appropriate to ITM ?
Also I would like to open some form of debate around the fact that some people do not consider drummers as proper musicians. I wish to make a point there: well I believe that actual drummers have a better grasp on rhythm than most musicians. Rhythm I believe is a key stone to be able to play music (especially with other people) and be able to understand it and play with it is a real skill.

Here’s a longer answer, although I might be wasting my time.

You are kidding, right? About only drummers “having a better grasp on rhythm”?

Do you play another instrument? Most people with an “immense interest” in Irish trad learn to play the tunes. And they learn that the melody already has the rhythm built in--a note for every beat, pretty much, there are not a lot of rests in Irish music. So a drum really is not necessary to punctuate things further. But most modern popular music has drums pounding away throughout, so a lot of people miss them if they aren’t there.

As for cajon, I suppose it depends on the session. I don’t see it getting a warm welcome at sessions where they celebrate the unique qualities of Irish trad music, rather than piling on a lot of other influences.

Re: Issues with drummers in sessions…

I’ve had more bad experiences that good with bodhran players in sessions. You must be aware that it’s common for people with no knowledge of Irish music (or music at all) to buy an inexpensive bodhran and inflict themselves on the local session. In fairness, whistle and guitar are equally problematic.

Percussion is absolutely part of ITM, with the understanding that it’s accompaniment and should never detract from the melody players. Spoons, bones, frame drums, and percussive dance have been in the tradition for a very long time.

As for general musicianship, on the community/folk scene, I find drummers generally have a lower level of musicianship. I can’t tell you how many times I’ve heard community orchestra/band drummers bungle entrances, fail to follow the conductor, or play with their drums out of tune in rehearsals without noticing, while the rest of the ensemble was doing fine.

Obviously there are truly great percussionists - John Bonham, Buddy Rich, and Neil Peart jump to mind - and one can argue that it takes a better grasp of rhythm than melody players tend to need in order to play polyrhythms on a drum set. However, that’s not relevant to Irish music. You won’t make any friends claiming that you have a “better grasp” of rhythm in your local session where folks are playing music that has rhythm and rhythmic variation as a central element. If you think you have a better grasp of Irish rhythm than most melody players, you aren’t really listening to them.

Re: Issues with drummers in sessions…

“Nah, there’s no percussion in Irish music or dance.” (Examples of percussion follow.)

Which brings up another aspect to the “how much percussion is too much?” question--the session tradition, versus the ceili band and/or step-dancing traditions.

Someone who would certainly know (talking about accompaniment in general, at sessions in Ireland in the 1950s) said something like: “There was never a guitar at those old house sessions, and a piano? Those houses were small, and the people were poor. There was no room for a piano, and no money to buy one with, in the first place.”

I think that Irish trad music--at least, as played at sessions that try to emulate that original kitchen-session sound--is a kind of chamber music, really. Small instruments, and a relatively small sound, is part of the charm. Too much drumming will destroy that charm, if that’s the goal of the session.

On the other hand, if the goal is to drink beer and blow off steam, then that’s different.

Re: Issues with drummers in sessions…

@pandaren_928: “I now play flute, whistle, and uilleann pipes, and through my years of drumming, I find that playing with the rhythm of a tune and do variations has been made easier. What I really meant then was that having some experience in playing with rhythm in its naked form will help to have a better grasp on it.”

That may have been true for you, but I disagree with the idea that this is some universal truth about the merits of percussion.

As a melody player, there are things one must learn about rhythm that have nothing to do with “percussion in its naked form.” For example, learning how to not speed up during the parts of a tune you have well-memorized, and then slowing down on the tough bits. Or learning how to listen for the pulse of the tune within the sound of all the other melody players, so you can lock in with the group. In other words -- following the rhythm, and not trying to lead the rhythm, which is the curse of all bad percussion players (and not a few bad guitar backers).

And then of course, there are the idiosyncrasies of how rhythm is expressed in all the different traditional melody instruments -- the motion of the bow, or the squeeze of a bellows, the tonguing of a whistle, the breath pulse of a flute.

The bang of a tipper on a drum head is dead simple, compared to what one has to do to express rhythm with most melody instruments. One learns to work through the technicalities. Banging on a drum doesn’t help at all with that.

BTW, I say that as someone who used to play kit drums in rock bands as a kid, who now occasionally backs ITM on guitar and plays melody on mandolin and flute by preference. I know what it’s like to be a drummer, and I don’t hate drums in the abstract. I enjoy the sound of a well-played Bodhran in a session… as long as it isn’t on every tune, and as long as I’m not sitting next to it (unless it’s a fairly quiet and sensitive player).

I just think that ITM is a case where there is so much rhythm intrinsic in the notes themselves, and the dance forms, that learning percussion “outside” the melody isn’t of any special benefit.

Re: Issues with drummers in sessions…

I know this thread ended 2 years ago but I wanted to add something to it.

I believe a lot of the dislike to drummers in sessions is more to do with how usual it is and a fact that most (not all) are used to 4/4 ‘rock’ style beats as that is the first thing many drummers including myself learn, in fact I spent years only playing straight 4/4 2/4 and 8/8 beats and the very odd time a 6/8.

Now my background is mainly hard rock and metal but I’ve always had a deep love for the traditional music. I recently started performing with a Irish Folk / Country group who often add a couple of sets of tunes into the performance - at first I just sat out of these until I returned home one evening and began some research into how I could add to the performance.

I now join in and play along after having done my homework I knew it it wasn’t all about playing backing beats from memory and that I really need to listen to everyone around me and play close attention to the dynamics of other players and match them.

Where I differ from most of the people I have seen before and people commenting about drummers in this thread is that I leave the snare drum alone (Unless i throw the snares off and use it as a tom) but I will mainly play using the bass drum and toms to give some pitched accents to my rhythms - I play a small kit with only 2 toms some times 3 so I also make use of a double bass drum peddle that I had.

Now I’m not playing blast beats like you would in metal but rather gentle accents and double taps to go with the rhythm of other players and to give that deeper sound I cant reach with my toms. My bass drum is smaller than the average bass drum allowing me to get more tone than a thud which I think adds to this style.

I believe I add another level to the traditional style and have been given complements by some hard core trad musicians. So I believe that kit drummers should not be completely discounted from this style but more educated in how they can perform this music. Its not as simple as rock drumming and gosh I found learning Jazz easier but it can be done an when its done right it can sound amazing.

Don’t be so quick to discount them from the fun and love of this music.

As for the comments about cymbals - I am yet to find a real use for them in this style apart from using a hi hat with foot peddle to keep timing the odd time. But that said I am more than happy to leave the cymbals alone and leave that space for the others around me.
