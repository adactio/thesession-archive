Re: Back pressure

Back pressure occurs when the whistle has a conical bore, meaning it is shaped like a cone rather than a cylinder. Because the tube is narrower at the bottom than the top, the air pressure backs up a bit. This does not occur on whistles shaped like a cylinder. Clark whistles are conical, as are Copeland whistles. Most are cylindrical.

I think you’re right, Mark M. Because the flute family (which includes the whistle) are flow-driven, resonance occurs at impedance-minima, not impedance maxima as in the case of the reeds, eg Uillean Pipes. We had him set up with a capillary tube running out of the corner of his mouth into a manometer.* The late Prof Neville Fletcher (who pioneered that technique and had used it in his own flute research) reported that Grey did use a slightly higher pressure than Boehm players, but not dramatically higher.

My understanding (and subject to correction, I’m no expert) is that we’re not actually pushing air down the bore of a flute, conical or not. Instead, we’re creating a jet of air across the far edge of the embouchure hole that splits, and sets up a vibration in the air column inside the bore.

Re: Back pressure

As I understand it, cross fingering, and venting don’t open holes to “let the sound out” as if you’re pushing air down the pipe. What they do is change the location of the pressure node in the vibrating standing wave that’s inside the bore. Here’s some info on that, liked from Terry McGee’s web site:

http://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/fluteacoustics.html

In the section on Opening tone holes:

“If you open the tone holes, starting from the far end, you make the pressure node move closer up the pipe - it’s rather like making the pipe shorter.”

In the section on cross-fingering:

“Closing the downstream hole extends the standing wave even further and so increases the effective length of the instrument for that fingering, which makes the resonant frequencies lower and the pitch flatter.”

And that’s about as far as I can take it because I’m not an acoustician, just reading stuff online and trying to make sense of it. Maybe someone else can chime in here on the details.

↳ Posted by duffyb on September 6th, 2018.

Re: Back pressure

I’ll have a go.

A reed claps shut under the pressure from your breath. An impulse caused by the closing blades shoots down the reed, out the staple, and into the bore of your instrument. It travels down the bore, and (all the fingers are on just now) it reaches the end.

The bore up till now has been narrow. But at the end, the pressure wave exits into the big wide world, and there’s too much air for this little impulse to move. So it bounces off, and returns up the bore. It reaches the reed, and pops the lips open again, and the cycle begins anew[1].

When that wave hit the end, and reflected, a small portion was lost to the outside air, which then travelled through the air, becoming sound.

Now, when we lift a finger, the same thing happens. The pressure wave travels down the bore, but now it meets an open hole. So it reflects here, and because this happens closer to the reed, it takes less time for the wave to travel and return, so the wavelength is shorter, frequency higher, and the note sharper. So far, so straightforward.

What happens when the wave meets the open hole depends on the hole. If that hole is “big”, the air inside it is heavy and difficult to move. If it’s small, it’s less so.

Let’s say we have a couple of “big” holes one after another (big means some combination of depth and diameter compared to the bore at that point). If the air in the hole is difficult to vibrate, the wave will tend to shoot past the hole, and the reflection will occur somewhere towards the second hole.

If we now close that second hole (cross finger), the wave now travels further down before it reflects. On average, it travels half a hole spacing further than before - so the pitch drops by around a semitone.

And that is crossfingering.

Venting is rather different. Now, instead of one wave travelling down and back, there are two: one from the reed, and one from the bottom end of the bore. They meet halfway up, and at this point no vibration occurs. Because the wavelength is now halved, the frequency is doubled, so we get a note an octave higher.

Lifting a finger at this halfway point forces the node to occur exactly at that point, and eliminates other second order effects. Notice that if you crack open the top hole of whistle, you can play right up the scale in the second octave with an unusually clear, pure sound.

You can also vent other holes to get higher harmonics (subject to how much you care for your neighbours). Try the third octave with the A hole.

[1] This isn’t really a physically correct description of reed oscillation, but as a mental model it will do.

Re: Back pressure

Calum, that’s a good analysis of how the pipes or other reed instruments work.

In the case of the flute (or whistle), there is no physical reed, but a similar switching action takes place. You blow a thin jet of air just below the “edge” and it sends a pressure wave down the tube, just as your reed slamming shut does. This reflects from the first opening in the same way, and when it returns, it grabs the air-jet and lifts it out of the embouchure hole (or window, in the case of the whistle). So the jet acts rather like a reed - it does the switching needed to convert Direct Current (the unidirectional flow from your mouth) into Alternating Current (the oscillating air column up and down the tube). The magic is that it does all this with no moving parts! Solid State technology!

And yes, Ailin, of course there is air in the tube (with the far end open, it would be quite tricky to keep it out!). But the DC airflow down the tube is not that great, and pretty insignificant energy-wise compared to the AC airflow. It’s the AC (the vibrating air column) that makes the sound and it’s the AC that sneaks in and out through leakage in pads, diminishing the energy of the vibrating air column.

Re: Back pressure

Back-pressure - IT’S ALL IN THE HEAD!

I think Murnie Jessle’s answer is a good indication of what the “feeling” of back-pressure is. Back-pressure is more correctly described in relation to the MINIMUM AIRFLOW required to sustain a clean note and that flow rate depends on design factors governed mostly in the head of the instrument.

The cross section of the windway in the fipple is between 8-12% of the cross section area of the body/tube. The windway is where the principal constraining of the airflow takes place. Next up in terms of how the airflow is affected is the distance across the mouth to the blade and the angle, shape and placement of the blade relative to the mid-point of the windway. The latter elements significantly affect the voicing and how the air is split (roughly 50/50) between the stream which exits at the mouthpiece (and produces the sound you hear) and the air which travels down the tube to induce the resonance frequency. Higher notes (frequencies) require faster air and therefore more flow – basically blowing harder. Think how long you could hold a D note versus D’ versus D’’.

The instrument maker determines all the above measurements and there is a noticeable difference between various models and designs - contrast say a Clarke or Killarney versus a Chieftain/KWL session model. My subjective guess is that it isn’t by a small margin either.

Re: Back pressure

There is no back pressure on a flute, it has an open hole at both ends, so that the ends of the pipe are always at atmospheric pressure. The key to understanding it without going into the physics might be to think about how you play louder and softer. With a reed instrument (which is closed at one end and open at the other) it is energized by pressure - the harder you blow (apply more pressure), the louder it plays, because the closed end of the pipe is a velocity node and a pressure anti-node. With both a whistle and flute the pipe is open at both ends (the whistle is open at the fipple). The end of the pipe is a pressure node and velocity anti-node, and to make it play louder you have to increase the velocity, not the pressure. With the whistle this means applying more pressure on the mouthpiece to make air flow faster through the airway, so you do have a small amount of ‘back-pressure’. But with a flute you can’t pressurize anything, because the flute isn’t sealed to your lips - you are blowing into the atmosphere, which is at atmospheric pressure. You are moving air - giving it energy in the form of velocity, which is what energizes the flute, but you are not pressurizing it. There is no ‘back pressure’. However, with a wider bore you have to move a larger volume of air per second to achieve the same velocity and sound volume, and I think it is the need for less volume of air for a smaller bore that you are confusing with ‘back-pressure’.

What we are talking about with a resonant pipe isn’t constant flow and velocity, it pressure and velocity waves. Someone compared it to AC and DC electricity earlier, the maths and physics is exactly the same and pressure and flow are linked together in the same way as voltage and current.
