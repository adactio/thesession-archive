It all rests on the fact that a pitch interval of an octave corresponds to a doubling of frequency (hence a doubling of playback speed) and there are 12 equal semitones (or 1200 equal cents) in an octave.

So if p = pitch shift in cents, then the corresponding percentage increase in speed = ((1200th root of 2 )^p - 1) * 100 or (2^(p/1200) - 1) * 100

So for an upward shift of a semitone (p = 100), the speed must increase by 5.95%

For a downward shift of a semitone (P = -100), the speed must decrease by 5.61%

…not that I’m a physics geek or a stud.

For a an upward shift of a whole tone (p=200), the speed must increase by 12.24%

For a downward shift of a tone (P = -200), the speed must decrease by 10.91%

“I don’t exactly understand exactly what cents represent in this context.”

A cent is simply 1/100 of a semitone (as a semitone is 1/12 of an octave). So a semitone is equal to 100 cents, a whole tone is 200 cents, a quarter tone is 50 cents, an octave is 1200 cents etc.

If you are not dealing with intervals of less than a semitone, then you can do your calculations in semitones if you prefer. If p = pitch shift in semitones, then percentage change in speed = (((12th root of 2)^p) - 1) * 100
or ((2^(p/12)) - 1) * 100

Yes. The change in cents per change in frequency is proportional to the frequency. But the math is still remarkably easy.
dc/df = kf, where k is a constant (dk/df = 0). This produces a first order, linear homogenous differential equation which has the general solution:
c = C_1 * e^(kf), where k is a constant in units of seconds and C_1 is a constant in units of Hz. This is the general form of the differential equation, and it can be solved as an IVP based on the fact that 1 cent = 100th of a semitone and that going up one semitone is a 5.9% increase in frequency. You solve this.

You are all going to loose if this is a contest. Lets start by going in depth into wave properties and boundary conditions. Perhaps a delightful lesson of Fourier series approximation of periodic functions is in order.
As you all know, the cosine and sine functions all have a period of 2pi. Therefore, the functions cos(phi) and sin(phi), where phi = 2pi/L, will have period L. Now, consider a piecewise function defined such that f:[a,b]->R, and a and b are such that the domain of this function are a subspace of R. We can extend the function as periodic over all of R, and the period will be b-a.
Now, any infinite series combination of a_n * cos(n * phi) and b_n * sin(n * phi) + a_0 / 2 will match the piecewise function on the domain you specified it, as all the waves in the infinite series meet at both boundaries. You compute a_n, b_n, and a_0 using a definite integral.
This can be use to model sawtooth waves, for example. It’s also very useful when predicting how a string will vibrate when it’s deformed. Moreover, we can judge how prevalent overtones are based on looking at the wave function. Note that a_n * cos(n * phi) and b_n * sin(n * phi) represent the general form of an overtone of the fundamental frequency, and their sum produces the wave function. Therefore, a function with more overtones will look less like a pure sinusoidal curve and more like a sawtooth wave. I know this because a_n * cos(n * phi) + b_n * sin(n * phi) is an orthogonal function to a_n-1 * cos((n-1) * phi) and b_n-1 * sin((n-1) * phi).

Confused yet?

Re: For the acooustics physics geeks (studs)

>>>:“they would have to have infinitesimally small diameters”
This is the equation for the frequency of the note’s fundamental based on the tension, length, and linear mass density of the string:

f = sqrt(T/µ)/(2L), where T is the tension in the string, L is the length of the string, and µ = m/L, where m is the mass of the string.

An infinitely thin string is what is called an “ideal string” in physics, and it is massless. Therefore, µ = 0. So, regardless of the length or tension of this string, you are going to produce a frequency of infinity. Because of this, you can’t produce all possible frequencies even if you could bend the strings.
