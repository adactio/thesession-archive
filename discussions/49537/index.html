Variations: How did you learn?

There are a lot of great threads on variations already, but I’m currently enjoying the fun of bringing more adventurous variations into my solo playing, and interested to hear more about how others have approached that path. How did you start tackling variations? Do you sit down and work them out and memorise them for later? Or are you doing it on the fly? Did you have a teacher? When does it become Jazz and not ITM? ;).

Re: Variations: How did you learn?

I’ve always enjoyed variations as integral to playing this music. My earliest mentors would include variations as they taught me a tune, so that’s how I play the music. And how I listen to the music—always paying attention to how other musicians interpret the tunes, how their basic setting may differ from mine, and what variations they include.

While it’s fine to pick up specific variations this way, the real fun comes from grasping the core concept of the variation and then applying it in other places, other ways, other tunes. Maybe even varying the variation in some way to make it your own or to better suit a different tune.

As long as the bones of the tune are still recognizable and danceable, and your session mates aren’t kicking you out the door, it’s not jazz. :-D

Re: Variations: How did you learn?

I’d suggest there are many degrees of “variations”.

Before experimenting with melodic variation, where you significantly change the notes of the tune but not so much to have it be unrecognizable, I think it’s easiest to start with looking at variations in ornamentation, from none at all to more complex options.

For example, I taught a Zoom workshop called “34 Variations on the First Six Notes of Jimmy Ward’s Jig”.

The handout for it is available here:
https://michaeleskin.com/handouts/jimmy_wards_variations.pdf

The first 32 variations are primarily ideas for ornamentation (or lack thereof).

If you want to play with melodic variations, it’s useful (but not required) to have some idea of the key and mode structure of the tune and the flow of chord changes. That will help dictate which notes will sound consonant within a specific passage.

There are many good resources on learning backup theory for traditional Irish music. My favorite is:

“Celtic Backup for All Instrumentalists” by Dr. Chris Smith
https://www.melbay.com/Products/97205M/celtic-backup-for-all-instrumentalists.aspx

also on Amazon:
https://www.amazon.com/Celtic-Back-Up-Instrumentalists-Chris-Smith/dp/0786640650

Beyond that, I suggest finding a player of the instrument(s) you play who plays the kinds of variations you enjoy and either arrange for some one-on-one instruction.

If that’s not possible, import their recordings into a slow-downer program like “Transcribe” and reverse engineer what they are doing phrase-by-phrase, ideally with tunes you already play basic versions of with little variation.

Re: Variations: How did you learn?

Most beginners start learning variations as soon as they pick up their first few tunes.

Wind players need to insert breath spaces, and all novices come up against bars with awkward transitions where they can’t fit all the notes in without losing the beat. They start the journey out of necessity, by tweaking the bits they can’t quite do fast enough.

Then they realise some of their little shortcuts are the same as better players’ deliberate phrasing. Others aren’t, but a few of them sound good enough to keep.

Then they start putting little extra bits into tunes that have the same note repeated several times, or held as a dotted quaver at the start of several lines, because it’s getting boring by the third repetition.

And they’re off.

I really like Michael’s approach. I think of variation and ornamentation from the mindset of renaissance dance music: I have a bunch of stock patterns (e.g., rolls, upper/lower neighbor notes, passing notes, acciacciaturas, trills, tuplets, etc.) in my stable of ornaments. I try these out when playing through a tune to see which of them best fits the tune’s character.

Re: Variations: How did you learn?

My first teacher/mentor, in the 1970s, impressed on me the plasticity of Irish traditional music from the very beginning.

Reading between the lines which some have written above one might get the impression that there’s a one-and-only “real tune” which at some later stage in your development you jazz up with variations, any meanderings from the “real tune” being daring and potentially eyebrow-raising.

I wasn’t taught like that at all. For me each tune I learned was a process of exploration from the get-go.

“Better to learn 20 ways to play one tune than to learn 20 tunes” he once said.

Because what he was teaching was the structure, the traditional motifs or building-blocks and how to constantly be choosing between the blocks and how to move them around to assemble, on the fly, an infinite number of ways to play a tune, all of them as much “the real tune” as any other.

Re: Variations: How did you learn?

Here are some ideas that helped me start to incorporate variation into my playing:

- Start small. As has been mentioned, start with ornamentation. The first thing to do is try removing ornaments that you learned as part of a tune. If you learned a roll here or a triplet there, try taking it out. And then try finding other places to put ornamentation that helps propel the tune, or give a phrase a different feel. This is actually something that newer players may find more difficult than they might think. We tend to build “muscle memory” for playing tunes, and doing anything that breaks your pattern can derail your playing. But this is the first step in putting your conscious brain more in control of the music coming out of your instrument, which is integral to playing with any variation!

- Expand your variations a bit, by trying things like leaving out notes, swapping the order of notes, playing double stops, or even doing single note substitutions, where you figure out what chord is being implied, and try substituting another note from the chord for a note. (BTW, one easy way to try note substitution is if you play a double stop on a note, try just playing the other note from the double stop instead of the original note). Doing this kind of stuff helps you build a little toolbox of things that you can try in tunes other than the ones you were originally working on.

- Don’t forget about pickup notes and connecting phrases. These are the things that we use to keep a tune flowing, and often times these notes are not given to you specifically by a teacher or transcription of a tune. And changing up your connecting phrases can make the following part sound different. For instance as a simple example, take maybe the beginning of Maid Behind The Bar. The first note is an F#, but it’s itching for pickup notes. People will often play DE|FAAB, but try switching it to AG|FAAB instead, and see how that changes the feel. (These are by no means the only way to pick up or connect the beginning of that tune). And then incorporate some of these ideas into your toolbox.

- Look for inspiration from other players and recordings. If you have a favorite artist, take one of their recordings and break it down. See if you can learn every variation they do in a tune that you already play. Or even better, try notating every variation as you’re learning it. (Keep in mind that solo playing in recordings will often do things that wouldn’t be appropriate for a session, but there’s still a ton to learn from it).

- Start to think smaller about individual notes. If you listen to just about any experienced player, you’ll find that they give shape and meaning to pretty much every single note. Changing the attack, sustain, timing, emphasis, dynamics, and resolution of a note can make it feel very different in the context of a phrase. Sliding into a note or attacking it hard, letting it ring or cutting it off, playing it boldly or playing is shyly, playing it slightly early or delaying it, etc. And then start to think about these note expressions in the context of the phrase and how each of the notes can fit together eloquently to convey a sense of emotion or poignancy to a phrase.

- Start to think bigger about tunes as a whole. If you think about a tune as a story, what is the story, and how can you use variation to tell the story differently (while not stepping on the toes of the musicians around you).

So these are all things that I have given thought to. But being able to do it on the fly is something that takes time and experience. Don’t be afraid to try things when you’re playing. If you’re in a session and try something that really doesn’t work, laugh it off, and file it away in the drawer of things not to do!

If you want to take an even deeper dive, Eliot Grasso, the wonderful piper did his PHD thesis on variation in Irish traditional music, and you can download a PDF of his dissertation here: https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/xmlui/handle/1794/11557

Re: Variations: How did you learn?

Both cancion and ross make good points as well!

If you’re getting bored of always playing tunes the same way, that’s a sign that your playing is maturing. I had a conversation with Caoimhín Ó Raghallaigh once about variations, and one of his suggestions for practice is to play one tune for a long time, like an hour. Play it every way you can think of, and try to change the mood. Do every little variation that you can think of, and then… keep playing it and relax your mind a bit and see what else you come up with. The potential boredom of the exercise can be a catalyst for growth!

And ross brings up the fact that mistakes can be a catalyst as well! It’s pretty common to hear a player say something along the lines of “ooh, that was cool, I wonder what I did!”, and sometimes you’ll hear a player take a “happy little accident” and try to refine it on subsequent times through the tune, so that they can remember it and add it to their toolbox.

The final thing I will say is that as you progress in your journey with variation in this music, you get better at it. In my case, I slowly started to trust myself, which opened the world of possibility up even more. I tend to think more abstractly about my variations now. When I am playing, I am not thinking specifically about what little tools from my toolbox I want to use, I’m thinking more about what I want it to feel like, and I let that desire turn into what comes out of my instrument. So it’s pretty common that I surprise myself a little bit with what I just played! This is a sign of a mental “flow state”. The surprises are usually happy, but I still occasionally end up doing something kind of clunky. However, I’ve learned to just let the past go and keep my focus on how I want the music to feel in the flow.

Re: Variations: How did you learn?

I would agree that digging into a tune and letting it evolve is the most natural way of finding what that tune has to offer. I’ve occasionally sat down and tried to “come up with variations” by synthetic means - pencil and paper, music theory, and so forth - but that rarely produces anything that sticks around, or that could really fit into a session. But by around the ninth or thirteenth time through a tune, ideas start to happen, and some of them stick with you.

It’s also helpful, if you primarily play one particular instrument, to try learning your tunes on something else. Even if you don’t play that instrument in a session, it’ll show you some new angles on the tune which will inform your playing of it.
