Are these rolls?

I’ve been at the flute for a couple of years now, but I’m finally taking lessons on a regular basis. My teacher is cleaning up my understanding of rolls, and I’m having trouble squaring her version with anything else I’ve read or heard in recording from the likes of Matt Molloy or Catherine McAvoy. Perhaps someone here could tell me if I’m way mistaken, or if my teacher has a particularly unique take.

The way I feel I hear them in tunes, rolls are either three evenly spaced eighth notes (ex. Catherine McAvoy at the beginning of The Haunted House jig), split by a cut then a tap, just like “blips” between the notes. Sometimes they aren’t quite so evenly spaced. Matt Molloy seems to have some where the first note is held just the slightest bit longer, but they are still three distinct notes with just “blips” between them (ex. beginning of Pull the Knife and Stick It Again jig).

My teacher wants me to play them where the main note is held as long as possible to then cram the cut-note-tap-note into the last teeniest bit of the last note. If I had to describe in notation, I’d say it’s like the dotted-quarter note becomes a quarter note followed by four 1/32nd notes, or possibly even a longer first note and even shorter last four notes. Like, a long initial note and then just this buzz at the end of it.

With off-beat or short rolls, her method is similarly unique, at least to my ear. Take the B part of Cooley’s, where we have several E-B-B-B bits. She said to play E-B then tongue the second B and squeeze an entire long roll (note-cut-note-tap-note) into the space of those last two 1/8th notes. Again, I haven’t heard that in any recording, nor read anything like it anywhere else.

I don’t personally really like it. It just feels like flailing my fingers around. In fairness, maybe that’s because it’s hard, but I don’t think it’s only that. But I am now able to do it regularly and consistently, so it’s not JUST that they’re hard or that I’m incapable. I’ve worked them long enough to be able to do them enough that I feel I can fairly say that I don’t like them musically. And I just haven’t heard anything like that from any big-name musicians I’ve listened to. And I’ve never seen a description similar to that anywhere I’ve read about them either. But I know I’m not misunderstanding her -- we’ve talked about it several times, so I’m clear on this style.

She’s the teacher, I get that. But these applications just seem really outside of what I’ve read on this forum or heard in recordings. I think I’m going to ask this at our next lesson, but before I do, I was curious if anyone on here would tell me that I’m way off-base, and I just not hearing what I think I am from recorded musicians.

Re: Are these rolls?

It’s all a matter of taste.

I would describe your teacher’s version as “swung” long rolls. That variety of roll works just fine on any long note (three quavers or eighth notes) in any tune where the rhythm is “swung” -- which is the case for most people’s playing of reels, jigs and hornpipes.

Where a swung roll doesn’t work (to my taste) is in “off-beat” rolls, i.e. BE E2 in a reel.

Straight long rolls (even rhythm) on the other hand work just about anywhere. Except in a hornpipe with a pronounced swing, IMO.

So learn both ways and choose what you want to play when.

True short rolls are different and are not compressed long rolls the way most people do them.

Re: Are these rolls?

Well I certainly hesitate to intervene in your teacher’s advice and lessons, but I hear your curiosity for other interpretations.

For me, the Brian McNamara piping sample above is a very musical rendition and more or less how I’d play the notes too. Note, he is not lashing along at great speed so the rolls can in fact be more measured. You can also delay them (I didn’t even realize that this is what I tend to do until a teacher pointed it out…) I like them like that. Now, ask yourself, what happens if we speed up -- inevitably the cuts above/below the note have to be more fleeting and may in fact give a slightly different effect. Nothing wrong with either approach.

If you want a detailed rationale for the way to “think” about cuts and rolls I have to recommend Grey Larson’s book. He has spent more time than many working out exactly what the masters are doing and has a consistent notation and idea and name for all of it -- including long and short rolls. The essential take home message is NOT to think of the notes above and below the basic note as taking the form of classical notation -- rather they are gestures to decorate the basic note. As such, they have to be executed effortlessly and without an attempt to say count all the micro beats. The basic note has to still take the same time, with the gestures. He is well worth a read and I cant do justice to all the nuances here.

The next point is to try to practice and learn these without conscious effort. What the great piper Seamus Ennis might call having the “knack.” If you are there computing deliberate subdivisions of time, you are not going to make it effortless. Great players do tend to draw admiration for their complete and musical completion of the notes in the time and space allotted. (See Shannon Heaton, and the glowing cover notes from Liz Carroll.)

On Cooleys Part II, I would never recommend a hard tonguing action. I would go for E B ’ B A B where the little mark means to put a cut above the second B. I’m sure Grey Larson has ideas on this one too…..

Re: Are these rolls?

Ted I’m not sure what you’re describing, but it sounds like it could be a difference between the way rolls might be played in jigs, and in reels.

In jigs a “long roll” occupies a full beat and can be timed dead-even, or dotted, or anywhere in between.

In reels I think it’s well to distinguish between “long rolls” which occur on the downbeat, that is, the long roll occupying the first three notes of a four-note group

4/4 | E‘E,EF G’G,GA |

or long rolls having the upbeat fall on their second note, that is, the long roll occupying the last three notes of a four-note group

4/4 | BG‘G,G AF’F,F |

For me the former function pretty much like long rolls which occupy a full beat of a jig, the only difference is that they’re followed by a linking note (unless you take a breath right after the roll).

But the latter have a different function, the “cut” falling on the upbeat, functioning more or less like a “short roll” which happens to be preceded by the same note.

If not preceded by the same note

4/4 | BD‘G,G AD’F,F |

I don’t know if I’d necessarily time it any differently than if it were preceded by the same note.

In both cases I’d “snap” it a bit, like a short roll.

“Grey Larson’s book” a +1, he explains the connection to the piping music, to the mechanics of pipes, explains difference between ornamentation and articulation, and teaches rolls and cranns on every note (even the most hard ones) using a very clear and consistent language. “must read” for any fluter and whistler.

“rolls”, (long rolls, short rolls and cranns) going by G.L.’s book, the sequence of finger moves is pretty much standardized, but the timing is not. Re: Are these rolls? WHICH ROLLS? Let’s dive into *how* and *why*!

Hey Ted! I think it’s safe to say that based on this thread and your observations you already know there is no single right way to play a roll. The best way would probably be learning the roll technique from your favourite player(s), closely paying attention to the context the roll is being played in. This may end up being not the way your teacher has it – and that’s okay as long as you’re consistent! However, if you need some analytical base to fall back to – which is not a bad thing in my books – here’s my take on *how* and *why* the rolls are played differently.

Just to define the terms, we’ll be talking about *long rolls* – those that start with a lead-in note and have their base note separated by two cuts – this is what I mean when saying “a roll”. Some people would instead teach *short rolls* as basic rolls, with long rolls formed by adding a lead-in note to the (short) roll – that is also a legitimate POV as long as one is clear with their definition. I prefer calling any of the following compound ornaments “rolls” to avoid having to invent names and playing rules for each individual case: ~E3 (on-the-beat roll) / B(E~E2) (off-beat roll) / (E2~E2) (extended / half-measure roll). There are also *condensed long rolls* which are played within a space of a short roll (a crotchet) and follow the relative timing of a reel triplet (E/E/E) – this is what seems to be happening in the case of your teacher’s preferred off-beat rolls technique – and other individual varieties of rolls built on top of the general types. They sure do exist but are just not as common.

THE *WHY*

The reason the rolls are so varied is that their execution rests on several key variables – and each of these represent a *spectrum* of possibilities. One can talk about A) the timing of the roll and B) the softness / sharpness of the roll; we’ll focus on the former since that seems to be your main issue here.

The timing of the roll depends on a few variables:

1) Position of the roll in a tune (on the beat, on the off-beat, spanning across both)
2) Tempo of the tune (relatively slower or faster for the player)
3) Lift / swing of the tune (determined by the player’s style)
4) Individual preference

To simplify things a bit, consider two abstract “schools” of playing the rolls and ornaments in general (disclaimer: they are not real schools, just two distinct ends of a spectrum of approaches):

Functional <=====> Ornamental

“Functional” approach means fitting the ornaments into the rhythm to generally enhance it and propel the tune forward – here, the rolls are completely subservient to the tune’s rhythm, tempo and lift, making variables 1-3 really important. “Ornamental” approach treats the rolls more loosely as an additional flavour, they may not be strictly rhythmic and may not perfectly fit the points listed above (1-3), however, far from being a random “flailing of fingers”, every time the rolls are played they still need to be consistent with the individual player’s way of doing things (4).

To state my bias, I gravitate towards the “functional” approach – but I also think that’s the dominant one in the current day and age, with a lot of influence coming from the music of the pipers.

*THE HOW*

To expand on the above, let’s talk about variables 1-4 in some detail.

1) Position of the roll in a tune: the more you lean towards the functional approach, the more you have to be aware of the context the roll is being played in as it determines its general rhythm.

= An on-the-beat roll (~E3) has a slightly extended (but not fully dotted) single lead-in note which is also the base note.

= An off-the-beat roll (BE~E2) has two lead-in notes that last a quaver each, the first one usually being different from the base note. It can also be imagined as a combination of two lead-in notes and a short roll. Whether you’re playing winds, reeds or the fiddle, these two parts can be played partly or fully slurred or deliberately separated depending on the player’s preference and further adjustments can be made (including the seldom-heard addition of the condensed roll discussed above).

= A half-measure roll (E2~E2) is basically an off-the-beat roll with an extended lead-in note that lasts a crotchet. It may be less common but some musicians, including my favourite fiddler Denis Murphy, play them a lot so I like to raise the awareness of this “stretched” roll rhythm! (An honorary mention goes to polka rolls played within the space of 6 sixteenth notes but details would be too much for this text :-D )

2) Tempo of the tune: “functional” rolls are timed differently depending on the tune’s pace, they are stretched (played more evenly) for slower tempos and condensed for faster ones. For a noticeable change, this in effect translates to whether the tune is played significantly slower or faster compared to the “normal” tempo the player is used to. Again, there is no commonly accepted way of going about it: some teachers would slow down everything but rolls when showing the tune at half-speed, firing them as “ornamental” placeholders, others would always adjust their rolls to the specific tempo.

3) Lift / swing of the tune. In a perfectly “functional” roll, one of the cuts (depending on the roll’s position) falls *precisely* on the off-beat of the tune, notably lifting the tunes that are played in duple meter. In reality, this is more commonly done for the off-beat rolls while on-the-beat rolls are stretched however the player likes. Still, when the musician’s style includes an abundance of rolls, they do end up accentuating the lift or the absence whereof – which is why “rolling” reels played really slowly and evenly will sound robotic and those played with a prominent lift everywhere but in rolls could sound uneven and “stumbling”. Finding the right “swing balance” for the on-the-beat rolls is really a matter of context and preference. On top of that, one can try highlighting different notes in a roll as an additional rhythmic effect / variation (!>!E{G}E{D}E - E{G}E!>!{D}E or !>!E{G}E!>!{D}E? – that is the question!)

To sum up, this is why listening and adjusting to your sources is so much easier in traditional music than trying to measure your rolls with a ruler! :-D It can be done though… Just remember the points listed above when analysing the ornaments in slowed-down tunes: there’s a high chance the spacing between the notes of a roll is only valid for the original tempo and lift the tune was played in and would have to be adjusted to some other proportions when played slower or faster in the same style.

It’s very simple. There is no right or wrong to performing rolls, provided you understand their function. They are both ornamental and also a means to articulate notes without using your tongue.
