Sounding Scottish is nothing to be ashamed of. But if you really want to sound more Irish, you should:

1. Buy the cheapest strings you can find.
2. Practice bowing up over the end of the fingerboard, with your bow at all sorts of odd angles.
3. Use very short, scratchy bow strokes.
4. Forget about dynamics and expression, just keep battering away at the tune.
5. Play everything way too fast.
6. Never start playing before the fourth pint.

From what I remember of how Jerone plays from clips he posted a while back, he has a very “bowy” style: bowing every note or most notes rather than having long runs of notes on one bow stroke. When people roughly -- and I mean very roughly and not often correctly -- generalize about Irish verses Scottish style, they hone in on the practice of some Scottish players of changing the bow direction on almost every note and gives the tunes more of a “jaggy” feel. While lots of Scottish-style players do in fact play this way (but certainly not all) it’s no more than a loose characterisation.

I’d also say that if you’re doing it *deliberately* to play in a certain style, as Katie was in that Cape Breton tune video, that’s one thing. If you’re doing it because it’s a habit you struggle to break, then it’s something to work on. I’ve met lots of beginnery fiddlers who change bow direction for every note because they haven’t figured out not to.

Re: People are saying my playing sounds Scottish…

As a couple of posters have said (recalling what I have heard of your playing), I think a lot of it comes down to single bowing vs. slurring. As a beginner fiddler of 8 years myself, I’m not qualified to give specific bowing advice, but the best advice I can give is this: Home in on a tune, slow it down enough that you can listen to each note you play, experiment with slurring different groups of notes, changing bow direction in different places, single bowing some notes and slurring others - and listen to the effect it has on the feel of the tune. Then do the same for another tune. The more you do this, the more you will develop flexibility in your bowing - and an intuitive sense of what works and what doesn’t. It’s a big mountain to climb - of which I’ve yet to reach the foothills - but it should pay off in the end.

The other thing, which I don’t think anyone has mentioned on this thread, but should be obvious anyway, is *listening*. Listen to a range of Irish fiddle music - old and new, different regional styles (which some say don’t exist), Irish music from the ‘diaspora’ (USA, England, Scotland, Australia etc.). Also, if you want to know what makes your playing sound Scottish, seek out Scottish (and Cape Breton) fiddle music as well.

Re: People are saying my playing sounds Scottish…

>>“It would be nice if you folks could tell me.”

OK, I’ll stick my neck out, but others will almost certainly tell you something completely different.

The first thing to realise is that there are major regional differences in Scotland, just as there are in Ireland - East coast fiddling has a lot of classical influence, and tends to be more technical, the West coast has a lot of Irish influence and is more like Irish, The Scottish Isles and theborders have different styles again, so any comparison between ‘Scottish’ and ‘Irish’ fiddling is can only be in terms of very, very broad generalisations.

The biggest difference is that Irish playing tends to be much more rhythmic than Scottish - traditionally Irish music was played solo, or with only melody instruments, so the player had to put the dance rhythm into the tune. In Scotland they played in small bands, so originally there was a cello or bass instrument of some sort to provide the rhythm, later a piano or accordion, and possible a snare drum, so the fiddler didn’t need to generate the dance rhythm, he could just play a melody over the top like the lead guitarist in a rock band.

Dancing also affected the tempo - an Irish reel or jig is a step dance, danced on the spot so the steps can be short and fast, Scottish reels and jigs are travelling dances - the dancers are taking longer steps as they dance around the room, so the tempo has to be a bit slower.

Depending on the area and tradition, Scottish fiddlers also tend to use a lot more double stops and drones than the Irish. This might be down to trying to emulate the highland pipes (there is a lot of pipe music in the Scottish fiddle repertoir). But it is interesting that the areas where double stopping and droning are most prevelant - the Northern Isles and the Borders - aren’t the areas that you normally associate with highland pipes.

Re: People are saying my playing sounds Scottish…

I’ve found that a lot of people go about learning to slur in a daft upside down way. Even the phrase “learning to slur” is daft and upside down.

Think of it this way. The notes you have in a tune may well be the notes you have in a tune, but it’s the articulation, particularly the rhythmic articulation, that makes it the music. So your starting point should be your string of notes. play a phrase, a whole phrase with one single bow stroke - it doesn’t matter how slow your bow will have to go. Then think of where you want your little rhythmic accents and put them in, one at a time, either with a flick of a finger lightly touching the string for as small amount of time as you can, of changing direction. Then do the same phrase again and put in some different ones, but don’t put in too many, make them deliberate.

So what you are learning to do is where to articulate, not where not to articulate.

“I know a Scottish sound when I hear it”

Usually, I can tell the difference too although there are several different styles from both countries depending on the area and historical context of the music. Also, there are many good players who are very capable of adapting to whatever style of music they choose while others may not be and the result is often a homogenous form of the music(I probably, unfortunately, fall into the latter category to a certain extent as do many others).

There’s also the question of whether every single tune should necessarily be played as originally intended, i.e should an Irish fiddler always play a Shetland reel or a Scottish tune in the local style or vice versa? Obviously, this doesn’t always happen if you listen to most players.

However, if you are going to be getting into a particular style of music in great depth or keeping the company of good players from a particular genre then, of course, it’s crucial that one should make the effort to adapt to what’s going on and learn the intricacies of the music.

no clue what is afoot in your case, but sometimes when people say that fiddling sounds “scottish,” they are saying, classical-ish. they don’t really have the musical vocabulary to convey the nuances, but they think it is classical-ish. for various historical reasons, scottish fiddling can be very generally seen as closer than irish features of classical violin in terms of articulation, phrasing, bowing, blah, blah. and this may be what somebody thinks they hear in your playing….

Re: People are saying my playing sounds Scottish…

On a slightly different note, if you do have any interest in learning Strathspeys, don’t learn them from recordings of Irish players. I’ve heard some from Irish players who will play Irish tunes in an Irish style extremely well, but they murder Strathspeys.

The origins of the reel and the jig are oft debated, but the Strathspey is distinctly Scottish and IMHO should remain such.
