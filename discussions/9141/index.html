Knocking off the odd bits

The yellow board has been slow lately, so here is something to chew on.
Do any of you notice, when you look at a transcription of a tune that you originally learned by ear, that there are more notes on the paper than you are playing? Maybe some of my session-mates are lazy and knocking out notes as they play them, or perhaps my ear is lazy or slow, because the tunes are being simplified along the way.
Perhaps it is a byproduct of the high speed at which a lot of people approach the tunes. For example, in jigs I tend to find on paper a lot of three eighth note groups where I play a quarter note and an eighth (replacing a double jig pattern with a single jig pattern), or sometimes even play a dotted quarter note.
Your thoughts?

Re: Knocking off the odd bits

Afraid I’m just the opposite, Al. I tend to add notes and articulations. Cuts, slides, slurs, rolls, triplets, etc., and often melodically fleshing out those long, held notes. Of course, the point is to vary it a little each time around, so sometimes I do replace three eighth notes in a jig with a quarter and eighth, or a dotted quarter. But the next time around, the quarter gets a cut note or triplet or short roll, and the dotted quarter gets a roll or a double stop or a melodic motif.

Those nuances are the heart of the music. If you can’t add them in, or find you’re dropping them out, you might be playing too fast.

Re: Knocking off the odd bits

Actually Al, this is one of the potential downsides of playing in sessions. Sometimes the tunes are eroded into lowest common denominator settings. Often the norm in sessions that emphasize speed over lyricism--you end up sacrificing the finer details for the sake of keeping up. It can also happen when people aren’t immersed in the tradition, or come to it from a less nuanced genre, and so may not hear it’s nuances.

I’d much rather play with someone who will go only as fast as they are able and still get the nuances in, than drive the tune too fast for their own abilities (even if it’s in my comfort zone). In short, I’d rather play the tunes with feeling than all punch and nothing else.

Even if your local session blandizes everything, you can still focus on lift, life, and nuances every chance you get at home. And bring that attitude to your session when a lull presents an opportunity. When a tune is played well and rich with ideas, it can be contagious.
