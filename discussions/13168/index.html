Boehm-system flute in Irish trad research

Hello. I am a student doing research for my Masters on the use of the Boehm-system flute in Irish traditional music. As a flute player trained in the classical tradition and an avid learner on the trad flute, I am very interested not only in the technical and stylistic advantages and disadvantages of using a Boehm flute in traditional music, but the way in which this instrument is perceived in the community of traditional musicians both within and outside of Ireland.

What I’m wondering is:

1) Is there anyone here who performs traditional music primarily on a Boehm-system flute? If so, how did you start playing it and what do you like/dislike about it? How do other musicians react when they see/hear you playing a “silver flute” instead of a trad flute? Do you ever feel pressure to switch to a trad flute?

2) This question is for anyone: what is your opinion of the Boehm flute as an instrument for traditional music? Have you ever encountered a player at a session, festival, etc. with such a flute, and what was your reaction (both before and after hearing him/her play)? What would you imagine the background of a musician playing a Boehm flute to be?

Also, if anyone has any anecdotes about Boehm flutes in Irish music--either from your own experience or something you’ve heard from someone else--or knows of any particular player of note who performs on a Boehm flute (I am already familiar with players like Joanie Madden and Paddy O’Donoghue), I would really appreciate hearing them.

Thanks much!

P.S. Don’t worry, I won’t take anything that is said here for use in my dissertation without permission from the individual(s) who said it. If you’re worried about saying something that you are afraid might be a bit touchy on the subject and don’t want to offend anyone, you can feel free to e-mail me at ptacey@yahoo.com to remain annonymous.

Re: Boehm-system flute in Irish trad research

To answer TradLad’s question, a Radcliffe was a kind of hybrid flute that was designed after the Boehm-system as a way to try and combine the tone and intonational quality of the Boehm flute with the old fingering of the simple system (like the flutes favored by trad players). According to Hamilton’s “Irish Flute Player’s Handbook”, it was really more of a design “to give a choice of fingerings on the Boehm flute, rather than a development of the simple system flute.” Carte 1867 is another model which applies this kind of system.

Re: Boehm-system flute in Irish trad research

According to Mick Maloney, most Irish players that went to the Boehm flute did so because of the difficulty of finding a good wooden instrument. This, however, was before all the new makers cropped up, and keyless flutes came into being (roughly around the 1970s). For example, Mike Rafferty used to play a Boehm flute, but I don’t believe he does so now. Noel Rice is another Boehm player. Sarah Baughan used to, but I don’t think she does now. She got a very nice sound.

You can usually tell a Boehm flute when you hear one. How it sounds, though, is not really the issue. There’s no way a Boehm flute will sound like a wooden keyless or eight-key. The isssue is really about playability and the use of ornaments. Depending on the sound you want, the Boehm flute has limitations, whether or not they can be overcome. The key clatter is another drawback, IMO.

As a player of both, I find that the Boehm is not very satisfying for ITM. I’ve tried many times, but have given up because I just don’t feel it is well suited to the style. I think the Boehme flute and the simple-system flute are two different animals, and each has its strengths and weaknesses. I wouldn’t play jazz or pop on my vintage eight-key, let me tell you.

Re: Boehm-system flute in Irish trad research

I have a wooden Boehm, a silver Boehm, a wooden 1867 Boehm and at least 3 unkeyed simple lads. I generally play the wooden Boehm out at sessions and the occasional gig.
Right - here’s your question 1 + answers:

1) Is there anyone here who performs traditional music primarily on a Boehm-system flute?

Yes me.

If so, how did you start playing it and what do you like/dislike about it?

I had played the whistle for a few years, very occasionally venturing out to low-key sessions. But I loved the sound of the flute. One day I walked past a music shop and heard someone practicing a flute. i took a maddy and went in and bought it. It was a silver Boehm. I had no idea how to play it. My first lessons were from a young guy, also called Danny, who played guitar in a rock band but was classically trained on flute. This was done on a barter basis. I used to grow home-grown weed so I would give him a load of quair shtuff and a bottle of home-brew wine for some lessons. That was enough to get me started. So I then just practiced tunes off albums, Bothies, Planxty etc.

I didn’t dislike anything about it then as I soon got as good a sound as some people playing simples. Some people, that is. Then I moved to London and got thrown in at the deep end with the likes of Paul Gallagher. The tone of the metal flute finally got to me so I switched over to simple system. but I was too ingrained in boehm fingering so I finally moved to wooden Boehm, via the 1867 patent system, which I find horrendous. The wooden Boehm I have now is never going to have the response of the simple system, but hey, it’s only a hobby. It’s easily good enough for local sessioning.

How do other musicians react when they see/hear you playing a “silver flute” instead of a trad flute? Do you ever feel pressure to switch to a trad flute?

I haven’t heard any complaints except from one person calling it a typewriter. He used to slag everyone anyway, telling them that’s what people do at sessions in Belfast (ie sit and slag each other off. I’ve since played with many people from The North and found this not to be the case.) So, asimilar to what was said above, I’ve picked up the odd supercilious glance from other simple system players, but it’s been fine when I’ve started playing. In fact one guy in at a session in a city near London was almost embarrassingly fawning, after being somewhat stand-offish when he saw the Boehm weapon coming out.
Yes, I used to feel under pressure to switch to simple, but not any more except if I’m in the company of a really good player, Mick Mulvey or someone. But I’m too old to be faffing about trying new yokes now. I usually always ask if I can have a go on someone’s simple flute if I meet another flute player at a session, so I can still handle it - but I feel the stretch after a couple of tunes….in fact - Oh! I almost forgot - one of the main reasons why I finally made the decision to switch back to Boehm was because I had quite a bad RSI (Repetitive Strain Injury) from excessive computer use at work. I went over to the Fleadh in Enniscorthy (1999 or 2000?) while I was recovering from the RSI, played the simple flute solid for about four days after which my wrist was raw and I had to go on cortisone injections. Even now after one or two tunes I can feel it again. So that’s why I’m stuck with the Boehm.
Hope this answers your question1 - I’ll let everyone else do Q2

Re: Boehm-system flute in Irish trad research

I think sometimes we forget that the ‘Irish Flute’ is really the classical flute of the 19th century and as such was designed and developed to play chromaticaly intensive classical music of that period. When it is played as an Irish folk instrument, the technique is different, and the music itself actually drove some modifications to the instrument (such as enlarging the f# hole to eliminate the need to use the key to bring that note into pitch, as well as simply eliminating the keys altogether). I learned classical flute as a child and only recently (in the last year and a half) began delving into Irish music. The first thing I did was cut out tonguing of the notes altogether (I needed to overcompensate to balance out 20+ years of classical playing). I also switched to a simple-system flute and began to work on my tone (listening to mainly Frankie Kennedy/Altan recordings) and working to immitate his style and sound. I’ve always been an intuitive musician who plays largely by ear (despite being a proficient sight reader of written music on the flute). I also began absorbing tunes and attending local sessions and immitating the sound and style of the fiddles and whistles. The other thing I did was forced myself to learn the piper’s grip fingering style on my right hand, which greatly increased my speed and relaxation while playing. I guess what I’m trying to get at, is the techniques make the music sound ‘Irish’ regardless of the instrument. I suppose if I chose to play tunes on my old studen model Bohem flute, it would still sound true to the traditional sound since I’ve developed the technique for playing that style. I actually play some slow tunes on an Andean quenacho from Bolivia (that is basically like a low whistle with no windway, only a notch to blow on). It sounds superb, but I cannot manage fast tunes on it due to the large bore and large finger holes.

Re: Boehm-system flute in Irish trad research

That was a really pompous response. Not “truely into it” Come on. Perhaps they didn’t have hundreds of £ to spend. I agree with the ornaments bit - they don’t work well on the Boehm - but them there are plenty of good trad players that use ornaments sparingly (and some bad ones that use them to excess).

I used to use a metal flute in sessions and frequently it was borrowed by other flute players to do things for which it was better - but they wouldn’t swop. And I wouldn’t swop back to metal either now. Is that image? I wouldn’t rule that out, but it goes much deeper. Sound, tone and ornamentation are the key.

The problem (if there is one) is more likely to be with the player. Classically trained players use breathing, tonguing and phrasing differently. They sometimes fail to get any swing / lilt of other such expression in to playing and focus too much on tone and dynamics. That is usually a case of not listening carefully enough to trad playing.

By the way, Mozart didn’t write for the Boehm flute - it didn’t exist. He wrote for simple system flutes and if any of think you can play Mozart well on your simple system flutes good luck. Try something in Eb for example.

Instruments I would much rather see left away from sessions include keyboards, piano, dobros (I’ve seen them), saxophones and clarinets. Given the choice of sitting between two banjos or two boehm flutes - I’d take the flutes. Why? Because you can still hear yourself think.

Of course in your thesis you should ask yourself, what is this tradition and when was it invented? To what extent is what we see today a modern invention. What was the variation in playing styles 100-150 years ago. And lets be honest, the bouzouki, metal strung guitar and tenor banjo are about as traditonal to Ireland as the snake.
