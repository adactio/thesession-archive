Re: Bodhran - learning to play.

There is a clue in your comment, I think -- “when I turn the music on”. You seem to be learning fixed rhythms, then trying to apply them to tunes. This is not the way to go -- learn the tunes on the bodhran. Play them slowly, a bit at a time if you like, and one at a time until you can get through without making a mistake.
The very act of playing the bodhran isn’t normal, that’s why it seems difficult. You have to learn how to play it, just like any other instrument. You wouldn’t expect to start tap-dancing just by learning the rhythms, and it’s the same with wielding a little stick with millisecond precision while at the same time remembering to breathe, not to salivate, and to pay attention to the sounds coming in through your ears and reconcile them with the version of the tune that you have internalised. The answer is the usual one -- practise (Or practice).

“How much time would you suggest that I should devote to practice each day?” - oh, you are going to hate the answer…as much as you reasonably can, really. The more the better, but a little bit each day is way better than a whole bunch once in a while. It seems like around 1000 hours and you get comfortable enough to play what you hear. If you practice 3 hours a day, that will take about a year. If it’s one hour a day, around 2 or 3 years, 15 minutes a day…well, you could be 10 years on before you get the knack. Now, none of that is absolute, and thank God you didn’t take up fiddle, but the idea is that the more often you can get in good quality practice, the quicker you get to the level where you can stop thinking about mechanics and start playing music.

Deborah, don’t get disheartened by the thought of 3 hours a day. That ’practice doesn’t have to be spent physically drumming (you could grow to dislike it). Nate Ryan’s advice is good:- it’s still practice if you hum in your head or quietly diddle out the tunes to yourself. The pencil idea is also good practice. Tom B-R also offered good advice. Do lots of listening until you have the tunes in your head, and if you can diddley-dum them you’re on your way. This also is valid practice time. I was listening to a John Doherty (fiddle) recording the other day. I’ve heard it hundreds of times, but I suddenly became aware of what I thought was his fingers moving on and off the strings. I then realised that the noise was some very subtle but good drumming in the background, and the drummer was just very tightly going along hitting note for note. So my advice- allow about two of those hours a day to listening and learning to lilt. Its all valid practice, and it works.

Re: Bodhran - learning to play.

The drawback with learning, memorising or recognising time signatures and/or pre-defined rhythms is that it tends to confine the tune instead of support and enhance it.
For example your ‘pineapple apricot’ mnemonic, when fitted over the idea of ‘jig’ can easily predetermine what you are going to hear. Jigs are not 123 123, 123 123. A few might be, but most are not. On top of that, the rhythm is not just to be found in a single bar or half a bar. For example, a jig might go:-

diddly DADdly diddly dum,
diddly dum diddly dum
diddly DADdly diddly dum
diddly dum diddah
and another, with ostensibly the same structure, might go:
DIDdly dadly DIDdly dum
DIDdly dum DIDdly dum
DIDdly diddly DIDdly diddly
diddly dum diddah
or the last line might even go something like:
diddly dumpty diddly diddly

The rhythms of the various pauses and accented beats weaving through the whole piece are as important as the individual beats in the bar, and they vary with each tune, which is why I said in my first post to listen to the tune -- meaning to seek out the overall rhythms and structures of the whole thing, not each bar.
You can see with a glance in the ‘diddly’ examples above that the DIDs and DADs form a simple underlying pattern that is different in each case, despite the ‘pineapple apricot’ approach fitting both. If you listen for these contours, these foundational rhythms, in a tune, you will find it easier to play along rather than merely trying to keep up, and moreover your enjoyment will increase exponentially as you learn to discern more and more patterns. Good luck, and keep practising. I look forward to hearing your first CD.

Re: Bodhran - learning to play.

Hi Deborah

You will hear lots about phrasing while learning a tune, here on the session. It applies to playing the bodhran as well. There are rhythmic patterns that correspond to the phrases in the tune. In a lot of cases (but not all) you can break a tune into rhythm patterns that last for two bars of the tune

If you take the Kesh for example (1-3456|123456) would be the first phrase (B) then (123456|123456) would be the second (A), you repeat the B phrase then you get (123456|1234--) the C phrase. (The “-” means you skip that beat). So the first part of the Kesh jig is in a BABC phrasing pattern.

The second part of the tune uses two of the phrases - AAAC.

Note the reason that I designated the first phrase as the B phrase is that the A phrase is the most basic jig rhythm -Pinapple-apricot as has been mentioned before, done twice in a row.

David

Re: Bodhran - learning to play.

“could you give me a diddle example for a reel, please?”
Well I can try, but it’s a bit complicated. You’re not alone in having trouble picking out rhythms.
You often hear the emphasis on the first of the four beats, and quite
a bit of swing:--

SKITHery-idle DOOdle-idle DOOdle-idle DAH diddly-
EYE die Doodle-idle DOOdle-idle DUM

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JODnID2iuBE

Or more simply :-

DOOdle-eye BaDOOdle-ahh
DOOdle-eye BaDOOdle-ahh

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f0pedfZckGc

Others place the emphasis more on the third beat, with quite a

straight rhythm --

BID BAD der-bidder BAD der-
bidderBAD der-bidder BAD

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MH6qSHHnTm0
http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3_flqxFuNM4

See the thread on Reel rhythms posted recently -- some interesting comments there.

Bear in mind that you will find different patterns within a single tune depending on the player, the tune and/or the instrument (and quite possibly the weather -- sometimes, as with squid-wrestling, it is hard to pin down).

Number of patterns to discover = (number of tunes x number of players) squared.
I made that last bit up. :) What I mean is, find the groove, but don’t get stuck in a rut.

Re: Bodhran - learning to play.

Going back to how I started on bodhran - books didn’t work for me ( or for another instrument I later took up). Having played orchestral percussion and piano, I thought I had a good sense of rhythm ( and in my head, so I don’t feel the need to stamp my foot as well!)
What did it for me was going to a course of bodhran workshops at a festival: 2 hours a day for 5 days…..practice in between, then did the same course AGAIN, the following year. As advised there, don’t try too much too soon: get the basic 6/8 jig and 4/4 reel sorted first. Triples, rolls and 9/8 can wait a bit! ( in my case, 5 years before I really got triples!)
You mention your arm getting tired: that might be down to technique. I have seen some players who have the whole arm from shoulder downwards going up and down and all over the place. Most of the movement should be forearm/ wrist (technical term is pronation/ supination of forearm) and VERY r-e-l-a-x-e-d, so that you can keep going almost indefinitely (not that anyone would want you to!!) Stop and shake your hand loosely every so often to keep that relaxed feeling.
I did practice with CD tracks, but picked the slower tunes that I thought I could keep up with first.
And when you finally feel ready to join that first session, knowing when NOT to play is the secret of being accepted, as well as your undoubtedly tasteful playing. And in some sessions there’s a sort of etiquette about only one bodhran at a time, so you may have to wait a bit for your chance. Good luck!

go to your local hardware store and get a selection of different diameter and wood type dowel rod . cut them into different lengths between 8“ and 10”. round of the ends and mess around with them and by process of elimination youll find your size. its just like golf clubs , no one size fits all and a fraction of the cost. theres no point spending much on tippers until you learn a bit of the knowledge. with my students they all have different favourites and flavours to their playing. everybody has a different inner bodhranian and you have to kind of go with how you feel and dont become rigid in your thinking. a kind of a zen thing. the lumpy sticks can have certain advantages in the beggining as they help to develope muscle tone but after that theyre should only be used to start a fire. for advice about tippers check out a guy in germany called christian hedwitschak. google “artbodhran” and explore the links about tippers and such. youll learn alot. hes making a custom drum for me now and i can recommend him highly. best of luck and give it time

Re: Bodhran - learning to play.

hi Nate
broken fiddle bows make excellent tippers but more suited for top end style, which they excel at and probably led to its developement. start them at about 11" and study the top end style e.g john joe kelly. if you want older more traditional double ended style these tippers are a bit thin . you need a bit of weight towards the ends hence the proliferation of sticks with lumpy things on the end. i would suggest that no one should need more than a 10% weight difference between end and say the middle or you will have to fight against the centrifugal forces caused by swinging the stick, the faster you go the greater the force and so on. once you get proficient and your hand becomes fit youll grow to hate the lumpy things , because you realise how much they take from the performance .they are purely training aids
