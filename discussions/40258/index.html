Added by Jerone on January 30th, 2017

The Value of The Slow Practice.

During my 25 years on this earth, I have learned that there are indeed some things that live up to the hype. Things that people rant and rave and boast and praise about. Water and Meditation being two of them. One of those things, I feel, happens to be The Slow Practice.

While trying to verbalize how I have come to feel about this method, I realized something. We never speed up to correct mistakes. We go back, slow down, and go back over. When it comes to motor skills and dexterity, progress lies in perfect repetition, and slowing down insures a higher probability of perfect repetition.

I have also realized, in real time, that you don’t need to practice at speed to perform at speed. Because a good performance is determined by how well you know the piece of music and how little you have to think about playing it. How fast you can play it is dependent on how well you know it.

You know, I’m not sure if everyone has this, but for me, there is an intuition, a certain anxiety, that I feel when I don’t “know” a piece of music. If I may not know all the words to a song, or if there is a certain phrase in a certain tune that I can’t play fluently. It’s a strong and apparent anxiety, but it’s only in the moment. When I slow down enough, that anxiety dissipates. But there are other times when I DO know the piece of music mentally, but haven’t quite worked out how to delivery it on the respective instrument.

On the anglo concertina, the push/pull factor has highly impacted how quickly I learn how to finger tunes. It’s more to think about… “It is this button, but is it push or pull”? So it could take hours with a tune before I’m able to deliver. It became apparent that the ability to deliver a tune isn’t always about dexterity, but sometimes it’s about just knowing the instrument. Slowing down gives you a chance to truly get to know the instrument. Focus on what you’re doing and how the instrument is responding. Power and control. Command. After I don’t feel anymore anxiety, and I’m on my several dozenth perfect repetition, the speed is already there.

It doesn’t matter how fast or slow a piece of music is. If I haven’t given it it’s due diligence, then I will make mistakes. Those mistakes have a way of vanishing after slow practices. But even with fast pieces. As of late, I’ve been working on more classical music because for whatever reason I’ve developed a strong taste for it. Slow practice is recommended when dealing with fast pieces so that you don’t develop and instill bad habits. I’ve been working through the 3rd Movement of Beethoven’s Moonlight Sonata on piano, and as fast as it is, the only way to get at speed was to learn the notes through and through, slowly. I didn’t have to practice at speed. When it was there, the speed was there. When it wasn’t there, I ended up tripping over my own fingers. The inability to play at speed always accompanied not truly knowing the piece of music.

So if you are opposed to slow practice, I recommend you give it a chance. There really is no point in rushing the learning process. I can’t think of a time when speeding through problem areas actually worked. I would go as far as to say that it doesn’t work.

Happy playing everyone! Keep the music goin’!

Re: The Value of The Slow Practice.

I totally agree. I’ve been aware of ‘slow practice’ for a year or two but haven’t managed to do it all the time. It’s so much more fun to just play the tune over and over. But I wasn’t making as much progress and I realized I was even wasting most of my time. My teacher kept giving me the same feedback, and I wasn’t able to correct my bad habits.

So lately I started working on a combination of deliberate practice and slow practice and I’ve been making much more progress. After my class I write down all of her comments on a single page. In a practice session I pick one, and come up with some excercises to work on it (if she hasn’t given them to me specifically in our lesson). I try to isolate the thing I’m working on. For example by doing it while playing scales. Then a simple song I know well. And then in the context of the tune I’m working on with the teacher. That’s the deliberate part.

I slow the metronome down very slow and when it’s correct I speed it up slowly, until i make mistakes. Then slow it back down and repeat. This is the slow part.

This combination has helped me make a lot more progress lately. I’m fixing my mistakes and she’s not having to tell me the same thing over and over. At least not as much. :)

Re: The Value of The Slow Practice.

You’re on to something Jerone. Slow is good. If you can’t play something slowly It’s really unlikely that you can play it fast, at least with bomb-proof consistency. That said, and whole-heartedly believed, Fast and Slow are relative terms. I once read an article about a marathon runner who always started his long training runs at a very slow pace of 8 minute miles. Just a slight bit slower than my fastest mile ever! I once worked with a ski-instructor who pointed out that if you always practice a thing one way, that’s the only way you’ll ever be able to do it. Where’s this going?

A wonderful flute instructor at a workshop drummed one idea into my thick, stubborn, head…discipline. My occupation, working with chronically ill patients, gave me the chance to see how those with discipline did better than those without. I should have been able to see that for myself but I didn’t. Just as you don’t build sculpted muscle mass by going to the gym and hurling heavy things toward the ceiling, you don’t build musical skill by knocking out tunes at turtle speed. It has to be done carefully, with a specific objective. The active part of the brain hast to be engaged. So yeah, slow is good. Sometimes that means half speed, sometimes just a bit slower than light-speed. Point is that whatever speed you working at it must be done with discipline before you get anything out of it. Even when you practice playing at speed, do it slowly. Think about it, you’ll get what I mean by that. (Don’t just play fast, play fast with discipline). It’s easy to turn the speed dial up before the tune, tune (or the skill) is really there, but think of it this way. The tune you’re playing has a voice, something to say. Why else would you bother to play it? Don’t you think you owe it to the tune, and yourself, to play it well? Surely anybody who has to (or gets to) listen will appreciate it.

I’ve thought about this lot over the last few years. Speed comes when it will, and yeah, I believe you have to practice playing at speed, but speed is the least important thing. More important, most important, is playing with purpose and that purpose is more than just busting’ up the quiet.

Re: The Value of The Slow Practice.

I agree with the general opinion that slow learning is crucial, as opposed to rushed playthrough, but I’m wondering if practising tunes at speed may not be beneficial, at times. I did learn quite a lot like Kellie describes, when I started - starting slow, speeding up gradually, but then I sometimes went to high speed to try how some things work at full speed.

I think that many things in reels/jigs start making sense only at speed; how to pulse a tune, where to put extra accents, etc.. E.g. the B part of Fermoy Lasses/Mason’s Apron - the nice jumpiness that can be achieved is something I found very hard to add there when I was practising the tunes slowly. Sometimes, a roll in a tune is played as three near-equidistant quavers, and sometimes it’s swinged almost into a crotchet and two semiquavers - playing the variants feels very different at slow and quick speed, and when playing slow, I always had a tendency to play the rolls quite evenly separated, which didn’t always work.

I can recall being very confused by tunes, not knowing how to “feel” them - and I wasn’t good enough at listening to get this out of the recording at all cases - in these, it just helped to first play quickly (and very badly) with a recording, feel my way around the tune, and then slow down and start learning properly.

Also, in some things, I suppose there is a kind of almost discrete jump between slow and quick - e.g., some tight piping triplets feel very different to be performed slowly and quickly - in the quick mode, the whole triplet is felt like a single movement, while slowly, it’s just quite awkward.

What I found really helpful for practise in general, when I could handle a whistle/chanter a bit, was to practise tough bits on a stick - a stick of wood on a walk in nature, a handle in public transport, or handlebars on a bike - just going over triplets, or difficult parts of tunes. It’s not great for learning new things, but it seemed to help a lot with technique, or even rhythm, and when practising “for real”, I could focus more at the music. But maybe this is just a weirdness, I don’t know anybody else doing it like that.

“I’m not sure what we are talking about, here. When learning a tune, one has no choice but to practice slowly. How can you possibly learn a tune you don’t know at tempo?”

This isn’t necessarily true. If you’re learning a tune by ear from a recording, you play along with the recording until all the notes you’re playing match the recording, because you correct the mistakes as you go. I do this all the time and would happily demonstrate for you if requested. I would have to post it tomorrow though because I’m busy this evening.

“My question about slow practice centers more on how anyone can really avoid playing slowly until they at least have the bare bones of the tune down, or why they would want to.”

It’s not so much that you want to. If you’re a skilled performer, you no doubt know how to cover up/ignore/bypass your mistakes, to maintain the rhythm. That same process happens when learning a new tune, if you’re learning it by ear by the means I mentioned above.

Note that I don’t learn all tunes like this, only the ones that are too difficult to “hear”. Sometimes tunes are played too fast, or the recordings aren’t perfectly clear, so it’s difficult to discern the notes and phrases. Using this method helps my mind deduce the notes and phrases being played, “filling in he blanks”. When I do use this method, I only use it to learn the bones of the tune, then I go back over it slowly to correct the mistakes. But you’re tempted to play it at speed because you’ve learned how to bypass your mistakes.

I’ve been reintroduced to slow practice by taking online lessons. Since I’m taught the tune, I’ve never attempted to play it at speed, because it’s taught to me note by note. So I start off slow, which is contrary to the method I mentioned higher where I start off at speed.

Re: The Value of The Slow Practice.

I value clarity and definition more than speed. There is clearly a trade-off between the two - a point of diminishing returns. For me, learning a tune well, slowly and precisely, can often lead (unexpectedly) to the playing of that tune much faster when the time comes. Being clear and distinct and right on the beat begins with achieving those attributes where you start the learning - slowly and carefully getting to know the tune. Once you have that solid, go for it. Speed and clarity with good definition and timing is the key to lift in a tune, but speed isn’t the most important factor IMO.

Re: The Value of The Slow Practice.

For me, a fiddler, “slow practice” means going through the music at half-speed or even slower. It’s the only way to get clarity in both tuning and coordination between bow and fingers when playing fast with a separate bow stroke for each note.

The biggest mistake everyone makes when practicing fast fiddle music slowly is that they naturally use a longer bow stroke. The problem is that a long bow stroke is a quite different physical action (full arm) to the short bow stroke of about 2" (wrist and fingers) that you need when playing at speed, and when you speed up you’ll find that the coordination won’t be there because you haven’t practiced it with a short bow. The answer is to practice playing at half speed (or less) with a short bow stroke to get the coordination right, as well as being able to listen to whether the notes are in tune. I advise using a metronome for slow practice, because otherwise there will be a tendency to speed up without realising. Controlling your speed by metronome is the best way to keep the metre steady in slow practice.

Finally, try to have both left and right hands well relaxed when playing. This is the most fundamental aspect of fiddle playing, and if you have a problem in this area, which everyone has in the earlier stages of fiddle playing and which holds them back, then face-to-face tuition with a good teacher is the solution.
