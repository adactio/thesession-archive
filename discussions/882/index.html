Now that I’m Warmed Up (And You’re All Leaving), I’ll Play a Tune….

Zina’s discussion on talking while playing got me to thinking about another bugaboo most musicians run into at some point in their pursuit of excellence…that raw 20 minute break-in period between picking up your instrument to play (at a session or performance, or even for practice) and the instant when the screeching stops and actual music starts to flow.

In my early years, it used to drive me nuts--by the time I got “warmed up,” my available time to play was gone, or I would be worn out. Now, with way too many years under my fingers, I’m grateful that (most days) the music starts to flow within a few passes of the first tune. Sometimes it takes a little longer because of creaky joints and stone-cold muscles. I know that whistles and flutes usually need a few minutes to warm up to the player’s breath, and something similar happens with rosin between the hair and strings.

But what I’m really getting at isn’t the physical aspects of warming up so much as the mental one--moving from a verbal, visual, or analytical frame of mind (a requirement of my day job) to a musical one as part of preparation to *play* music. This has grown easier for me over the years. Kevin Burke (somewhere, deep in the cold, dim caverns beneath Lithuania, Bloomfield is gnashing his teeth and sticking pins in a voodoo doll in my likeness ;-)…sorry…. Kevin Burke once told me that if you just tap into the music, then you won’t have to warm up. It just comes out “good” right away. Of course, he was also able to demonstrate this with no hesitation. Heh. Zina too has mentioned Burke’s notion (and I’ve heard other music greats say the same thing) that the music is just out/up there, all the time, and all we have to do is plug in our mental/spiritual speaker wire and let the music come out through us.

I guess I agree. For some years now, I’ve noticed that it takes less and less time to get in the zone. Sometimes I know I’m there even before I get the fiddle out. Other times, it comes even when I don’t expect it. Last Tuesday, for example, I sat in on the local session after three weeks without playing at all and found tunes rolling off the strings like I’d been practicing 10 hours a day. (Well, okay, I slaughtered a couple of tunes, but the majority of them just clicked, and I wasn’t stuck on pre-arranged variations or only my core bag of tunes. They came out with lots of lift and feeling. You know what it feels like.) I’ve also found that my state of mind (relaxed, ready for fun) is far more important in deciding how well I play on any given night than how much practicing I’ve recently done. The most important factor in playing well (and doing it with little or no warm up), it seems to me, is being more directly tapped into my emotions rather than rational thoughts. That said, I’m still not sure *how* this works, and am more than happy to just succumb to the mystery of it all.

But I’d still like to hear everyone else’s experiences and anecdotes and advice. It would help to hear different ways of thinking about this, to pass on to students and cohorts in crime. Give it a go…

Re: Now that I’m Warmed Up (And You’re All Leaving), I’ll Play a Tune….

My thought on this is that the brain gets in the way (no pun intended). I have played guitar for 38 years, and a long time ago I discovered that if I tried to think about how a particular song went or remember something I had not played in a long time, the best way to do it was to not think about it and let my fingers just do it. So, I would just stare at the wall or something, and out it would come.

Fiddle is a heck of a lot harder to play than guitar, but I find that the same thing works for me, if I just stop thinking, “don’t miss the roll, or make sure this or make sure that” a tune will come out a lot better. Also, if I can’t remember how a tune goes, blanking out and letting the fingers go, still works. I think this is why some people insist that ITM is an aural tradition, better learned by ear than by reading the music. I would venture a guess that mimicking what you hear, as opposed to analyzing what you see gets into that whole right brain/left brain thing.

Jill, I too enjoy walking before playing. It getes the blood flowing, and it’s easy to forget about the day’s frustrations even in just a 5 minute walk. Then you’re ready to play.

And Pearse, that “blanking out” and letting the tune basically play itself is exactly what I’m talking about. It’s like an alert dream state. Burke says he aims to be physically relaxed and mentally fierce in pursuit of the tune. To me, that suggests hearing the tune unravel ahead of you as you play, and not being aware of much of anything else. For the better players, it looks and sounds like they’re magnetically attached to the tune and it just pulls them along unerringly.

Zina, I agree that this can be difficult for people with less experience on their instruments. That’s why I’m interested in hearing how other player’s experience it--so I can talk about it with students. At the very least, I think it helps to be aware of the phenomenon so you won’t shy away from it when it finds you. At best, maybe it’s something that can be actively learned. I know if I were just starting out, knowing what I know now, I would spend some time playing just one tune over and over again, for 10 or 20 minutes straight, until the tune took over and I was on autopilot. And then just keep letting it play till it’s done. I still do this, when the mood strikes, or when I’m too wound up about something unmusical and need to change my focus. It helps.

Re: Now that I’m Warmed Up (And You’re All Leaving), I’ll Play a Tune….

From my point of view, what you are describing is the point that a person can reach after they have trained the body’s muscles to automatically relax and then go into the placement/actions that you need to make the sound, which is a function of knowing how to relieve tension, warm up, and having learned good “technique” on the instrument. This is true not only of playing an instrument, but also of singing. Getting the muscles warmed up is important to avoid physical injury (muscles or tendon strains). A couple of warming up methods that Radim Zenkle taught in his mandolin workshop are to put your hands in a sink of the hottest water you can stand to get the blood flowing and the muscles warm and/or to take a rubber glove that fits your hand tightly, cut off the fingertips, and wear the glove for a few minutes before you start to play.

You can hopefully convince your students that once the muscles are mastered to make the right sound while relaxed, the real magic begins with the sound flowing seemingly without effort. The biggest revelation to me in studying voice was to learn that it is like athletic or dance training. I see playing the whistle the same way. Even a talented athlete needs a good coach in the beginning to show how to avoid muscle injuries, how to use the body in a way that maximizes speed and strength, exercises that build strength and endurance and lung capacity, and when those techniques are learned, it needs to be constantly practiced in order to keep that muscle tone and endurance going. When the body is trained to it’s potential in moving, breathing, enduring, then the soul has the ability to call the body at its command in expressing the music. When the groundwork is solid in training the muscle placement and action, it becomes automatic and you don’t have to think about technique any more, the body just responds. I think dancers can relate to this, too. When you’ve trained your body to the point that you don’t have to think about moves, the muscles are strong and warmed up and know where to go, and the dance flows. The music flows effortlessly when you have internalized the muscle technique so well and for so long that you don’t have to think about it, because it has become a part of you. All self-consciousness is gone, all tension, because the music has become a part of you.

For beginners, it’s frustrating to go through the necessary training period to get the body under command. It helped me to picture myself, as my teacher told me, like a beginning track athlete who needs to go out on the track every day and train, to get the muscles a little stronger and faster every day. I needed to learn every technique I could to maximize talent I was born with, to get the body to do things it wasn’t used to doing. It’s hard to be a beginner at anything because it takes so much time, training, and patience not to give up when a beginner knows their sound sucks. Keep telling your students to have faith, it will happen, but like the I Ching says, “Perseverance furthers” ;-)

Re: Now that I’m Warmed Up (And You’re All Leaving), I’ll Play a Tune….

Good comments all around. Alice, I agree that warming up physically is important and shouldn’t be skipped. Over the years I’ve found a handful of stretches, exercises, and other activities, including specific tunes, that help me get ready to play. (Often before a long night of playing I’ll put on rubber gloves to wash dishes in the hottest water I can get to warm up my hands and arms--glad to hear I’m not the only one doing this.) That kind of warm up is good for the soul, too, but in this discussion I wanted to focus on the mind/soul side. Lots of people do physical warm ups. Not as many seem to be aware of the mental/emotional preparation to play. It’s more like a state of grace rather than a state of readiness. Some people apparently just live in it most of the time, while others can switch it on at will. Some of us (myself included) had to learn it (I was a rowdy child :-). I borrowed what I’d learned from sports and other physical activities (especially juggling) to try to teach myself how to lose myself when playing music. That and playing for fun with other people finally worked.

When I said “shy away,” that may be too strong a term. But I’ve taught some people who don’t have the patience to play one tune for more than 2 times around, let alone 20 minutes or all day. They resist it. Zina, I agree that the “deep” approach works well. I go through spurts where I play lots of different tunes, but most weeks I concentrate on 2-3 tunes. Lately I’ve been rooted in Beare Island Reel, your Paddy Fahy’s (George’s recording inspired me--there’s *a lot* to explore in that tune), and Dance of the Honeybees. Between those three tunes, it feels like I can work on every ITM skill and musical idea available, except jig rhythms, so all the other tunes I play at a session improve too, even if I’m not playing them until the session night. All the work transfers.

Sos, I went through the same thing of trying so hard to find the zone that I couldn’t feel it when I finally tripped over it and fell in. I kept thinking it was something that required absolute privacy and zero distractions. But what finally clicked for me was playing in sessions. Porter and the company of like-minded friends are a mighty lubricant (though you do have to find your balance point between inspiration and inebriation). The key for me was the chance to lose myself in the overall sound, to listen not just to *my* fiddle but to how it blended in with all the other voices. There is great freedom in all that noise, the opportunity to just swing the bow and twitch the fingers while someone else is “responsible” for never losing the melody, beat, or tone. At first, I’m sure I sounded horrible, but I played softly so it didn’t matter. Eventually you quit being self conscious and just latch onto the music. Going to a session at least once a week is probably the best way I’ve found to “practice” letting go and learning what the zone feels like. Looking back on it, I can understand why it’s so hard to lose yourself when playing by yourself--that’s all you have is your own sound to listen to. But in a crowd all playing the same tune, you can let your own sound be overwhelmed. You’re gone, and there you are.

I remember years ago seeing a video of myself playing and realizing that I actually *looked* like a fiddler, even though I still didn’t feel like one. Seeing how easy I looked with the fiddle and bow, I soon genuinely relaxed and my playing improved. So maybe there are two sides to getting into the zone. One is all the hard work and bazillion hours it takes to really feel at home on your instrument and with the music. The other is letting go of all the hard work and any tension and just enjoying how good it feels to play.
