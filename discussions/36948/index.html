Yet another fiddle strings thread: Question about Obligatos

Hello! It seems that nothing works better on my fiddle than Obligatos, as far as I can tell. However, dang, they are EXPENSIVE strings (~$80 USD). Is there anyone else out there obsessed with string testing who might have any ideas of something cheaper that might have a similar effect?

The things that Obligatos seem to do for my fiddle that other strings don’t: The low end is clear, focused, and responsive with a great deep bite and growl. The A string is responsive, powerful, and has a nice focus too. Everything is really stable, with no false notes etc. They aren’t *easy* to play (ie they don’t forgive sloppy bowing), but now that I’ve figured out how to get the best out of them, it seems I can play more clearly, calmly and with more nuance. And, I know Obligatos are generally regarded as not-so-loud strings, but on my fiddle it seems I can get quite loud and it carries really well. AND, they seem to last a long time (over 3 months, which is more than I’ve gotten out of anything else).

Other strings I’ve tried that are all totally okay but don’t measure up nearly as well include: Chromcor, Prim, Vision (the regular kind; the second best strings I’ve tried), Tonica, Helicores, and a super cheap chinese set called For-Tune (great name!!) which was actually not too shabby, but so very loud that I actually had ringing in my ear for a day after trying them… their wrapping was actually a knock-off of Evahs, so you can *look* like you paid a lot! I know, you probably get what you pay for, and Obligatos are the only strings I’ve tried in the $80 price bracket (all those other sets are half the price or less); I tried them after hearing a rumor that they’re the strings Liz Carroll and Kevin Burke use. :P :)

Am I stuck paying for fancy strings now that I’ve gotten hooked, or is there a magic bullet out there that’s cheaper? I’m curious about Zyex, Pro Arte, and some other sets considered “dark,” but I don’t think I can stomach buying more strings without some kind of informed advice. My fiddle is a fairly new chinese instrument made from decently aged wood, with a respectable set-up, and has a tendency to be loud and bright as goes with fiddles of such a description.

Thanks for your patience with yet another fiddle string question, and any helpful experience you might be able to share. Thanks!

Cheers,
Jeff

Re: Yet another fiddle strings thread: Question about Obligatos

The Violinos are similar to Obligatos, however they are regarded as more of a student string intended to tone down overly bright and harsh violins. I haven’t tried them, but from what I’ve heard, they’re probably not what you’re looking for.

I tried the Warchal Amber E string, and wasn’t very impressed on my violin. On another violin, I’m sure it’s a very good string, but on mine it was like it was overbearing, difficult to control. I use the Larsen Tzigane E string instead. Amazing string.

Probably the cheapest and closest you will be likely to get to Obligatos is Warchal Karneol. I use those on my violin, which is somewhat brash and bright. They darken the sound just enough to be quite pleasant, but still effective. They may not be quite as powerful as the Obligatos, but by no means are they weak or flabby. In my experience, Warchal has the best bang for the buck, if you instruments agrees with their strings.

Re: Yet another fiddle strings thread: Question about Obligatos

An assessment I’ve heard about Obligatos is that they are probably the one synthetic string that is closest to gut, given that the gut sound is one of the string maker’s holy grails that has still to be fully attained. I have played a lot on Obligatos in the past, both as a cellist and violinist, and now play my violins exclusively on plain gut (except for the E). I am therefore in a position to agree with the assessment.

A basic problem with all synthetic strings arises from the fact that they all have one or more layers between the synthetic core and the outer metal winding. With use, the layers stretch and deteriorate so that contact between the outer winding and the core reduces with time, the inevitable result being a progressive loss of projection and tone over months, or even as low as a few weeks. Many users of synthetics will have had the annoyance of an expensive string going south after a month’s use when the winding shreds or parts company from the core. You obviously don’t have this problem with plain uncovered gut and consequently there is very little deterioration in tone until the string is near to the end of its natural life, a good visual indication of which is when significant fraying sets in.

I find with my plain gut A and D that I get at least 12 months of useful playing out of them, similarly with my copper wire wound (that’s old technology, btw) gut G, and all that is with a lot of orchestral playing as well as folk music. However, the gut E doesn’t last for more than a few weeks, even with the best of care, so their main users, the specialist Baroque players, may tend to reserve them for occasions when their tonal difference over the metal E is important.

I have no complaints about the tuning stability of gut strings - in my experience no better or worse than any other type of string - but give them a chance to resettle if there are dramatic changes in temperature or humidity. A newly fitted string takes less than two hours for its tuning to stabilize, and this is mostly due to the string settling on the peg.

I find that gut strings are the easiest and most responsive of strings to play, mainly because of their low tension (~75% of synthetics?). This lower tension requires a different bowing technique - you never “dig in”, which is guaranteed to kill the tone and volume stone-dead; instead you get the volume and projection you want by the speed of bow across the string coupled with a lighter pressure and playing closer to the bridge than what you may otherwise be used to. Keep finger nails short and don’t hammer the strings with the fingers - it’s quite unnecessary for a number of important reasons, not only with gut strings but with all strings.

If anyone wants to experiment with plain gut (or covered gut for that matter) they must know that tuning is always done from the pegs because tuning adjusters don’t work with gut, so the pegs have got to be easily moved without sticking. Change a tailpiece with integrated tuners for a plain tailpiece, together with an add-on adjuster if you’re using a metal E. Apply soft pencil lead (3B) liberally to the grooves in the nut and the bridge, again to stop the strings from sticking at these places (this advice applies to all types of string, btw). Plain gut strings are retained on the tailpiece by knots or loops. Personally, I find knots tidier, but a gut E must always be looped - it’s too thin for a knot to hold reliably in the string hole in the tailpiece.

I don’t think I’m all that far out in stating that plain gut strings have got to be the best value for money, both in terms of quality and longevity, but ultimately of course string choice is always down to the individual player’s preferences.

Finally, it’s good to say a big “thank you!” next time you pass a field of sheep :-)

Re: Yet another fiddle strings thread: Question about Obligatos

Follow up from OP: Okay Trevor aka fidicen, your tireless advocacy of plain gut has had an impact! My Obligatos went dead and I replaced them with Chordas, which are plain gut strings that cost about half as much as a set of Obligatos. The Chordas ARE amazing.

I installed the Chordas last week. They took a day or so to finish stretching and I played on them in a session last night. They sound fantastic, they feel really satisfying to play on, and they’re perfectly stable. I had to tune between sets for the first 15 min as my fiddle adjusted to the temp/humidity in the room but then they were fine for several hours. I really love bowed triplets with plain gut; they are really snappy and full of character. As long as they last at least a couple months, I’m sold on gut. I am maintaining them as mentioned above. I’m using the heavy copper-wound G, medium plain D and A, and a steel E.

My only issue I need to experiment with is the D string. The Chorda plain D seems a bit raspy sounding unless you hit it *just right* with the edge of the bow at just the right speed. I’m curious to try some plain D options from Gamut, a little more expensive but not that bad.

Another update for posterity - I got a Pistoy medium D string from Dan Larson at Gamut and it is far superior to the Chorda D.
