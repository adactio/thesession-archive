Re: why are most irish tunes in D or G ?

Okay, here is a more serious answer.

19th century union pipes were usually in C, B, and sometimes Csharp. Wide bore D uilleann pipes are a 20th century invention, possibly for (a) volume and (b) to play with other “concert pitch” instruments.

To confuse things further, if you take the foot joint off a pastoral pipe chanter in C (they were around in the late 18th and early 19th centuries and are thought by some to be the predecessors of uilleann pipes), the thing plays in D.

Make of that what you will.

Re: why are most irish tunes in D or G ?

They are not “in” D or G. They are just tunes with certain intervals and ranges that are easy on certain diatonic instruments. They are in Bb and Eb on a Bb flute. In Eb and Ab on an Eb flute. etcetera. (and ethical blend is right to point out that an instrument, in itself, is not modal. That’s a fundamental missunderstanding of the word)

It’s the ranges and the intervals of the tunes that matter, not the pitch.

For example, what distiguishes the majority of tunes which you would normally describe as D tunes is that their lowest note is the root and their highest note is an octave and a sixth above that root. And what distiguishes the majority of tunes which you would normally describe as G tunes is that their lowest note is the fourth below root and range goes from this note, through the octave and up to the third above that octave.

Re: why are most irish tunes in D or G ?

You really ought to check this stuff out before you post, K of W.

The ‘modern’ church modes come from about the 8th century. Glareannas (or however he’s spelt) *described* them in the 16th century and took as a way of presenting that a basic *diatonic* scale on C. Some people think that the modes as used in Irish music are different in any case, but they’re not fundamentally different from the basic church modes. Just because Glaerannas illustrated the modes based on a diatonic scale on C doesn’t mean they have to start on those particular notes, and they most of the time don’t.

Diatonic doesn’t mean what you think it means. It’s a term originally deriving from Greek modes (very different things indeed) and refers to scales derived from a particular type of mode. Major and minor scales are derivatives (ultimately) of such modes. The major scale is *ancient*. I mean really really ancient. And the natural minor is as well, and even the harmonic minor (or close to it) is pretty old, although I can’t find a reference as to exactly how old.

Both major and minor scales - modern versions - are a huge amount older than the 18th century.

Re: why are most irish tunes in D or G ?

I doubt it is as simple as “it was adapted to the instruments of the time.” That makes a lot of assumptions which seem a bit far fetched. It implies that there was music existing in some key or other but people had access to certain instruments (unspecified ones, no less) and changed the key of this music -- lord knows what it was played on -- to fit those instruments.

Reread Llig’s post about intervals. If you’re playing on a B chanter, a tune with the lowest note of the chanter as its root will be in B, not D. When you have instruments like pipes, flutes, and whistles, which play a certain type of scale and have a limited range, it’s pretty obvious that the music written for those instruments is going to reflect their range and scale (again, think intervals, not keys!). Not all Irish music does. There are plenty of Irish fiddle tunes in keys like Gm which go beyond the range of pipes, flutes, and whistles.

This seems to me like contemplating why Highland bagpipe tunes only have one octave.

There are people about who know more about this than I do who can probably explain the development and eventual predominance of concert pitch during the twentieth century.

Re: why are most irish tunes in D or G ?

Llig got it right. The basic scale of a lot of Irish tunes is that of one octave of a flute or the uillean pipes. Which means either the authentic scale on D (with a D at each end) or a plagal scale on G (with only one tonal centre, the G in the middle).

English music does the same, for the most part (and is maybe the source for the Irish scheme). Reinforced in hardware these days, with the predominant instruments being G/D freereeds.

Scottish music shifts the whole shebang a fifth higher, under the influence of the Highland pipes (and presumably Border pipes too). It tends to use either authentic scales on A or plagal ones on D.

A fiddle can fit into either scheme, so it didn’t have any effect in determining these national tonal idioms.

↳ Posted by Jack Campin on September 1st, 2010.

Re: why are most irish tunes in D or G ?

The standard six hole simple flute or whistle plays a diatonic scale, plus has an easy fingering to play that seventh note of the scale a half tone flat. The standard flute and whistle key for this music is D, and that extra note outside the D major scale that is available is a C natural. This makes the D major scale, and G major scale, and whatever modes use the notes of those scales, as the most common modes and/or keys of Irish music.

Re: why are most irish tunes in D or G ?

llig’s got it right, with the proviso that “D” is often used to refer to the all-holes-closed note of a flute/whistle/chanter, regardless of the absolute pitch that instrument is making. This may be a modern convention, but it seems to reflect a sort of convergence on a standard, which we could expect as the habit of playing the tunes in groups (ceili bands and sessions) became entrenched in the 20th century.

How I think this happened: The shapes of the tunes (“D major tunes” don’t feel like “G major tunes” don’t feel like “A major tunes”) mostly derive, I think, from the flute/whistle/pipes side of things. The centering on D as the basic bottom note is probably due to the standard tuning of the fiddle, at least in large part. Although it wouldn’t have been hard for fiddles to tune up to E flat if that were required, tuning down any serious amount would have cost them a lot of volume, which would not be great for the dancing, so there was some incentive to converge to the fiddle if you wanted to play with them.

This is pure speculation on my part, of course. I don’t assert that it’s right, only that it seems plausible to me.
