It isn’t a question of technical difficulty, it’s an issue of usability and majority use-cases. For 99% of tunes, the tune type and the meter are related. That means the process of adding a tune can be nice and simple: select the tune type, and start inputting the ABC notation for the tune. I could re-engineer everthing for the exceptions, but that would then add an extra step (and extra complexity) for the more common use cases.

So it’s not that I regret the early design decisions. Quite the opposite. And as we’re discussing now, it still leaves the door open to expanding the number of tune types.

Applying reductio ad absurdum to the statement that the category a tune is listed in isn’t as important as reading the comments would cause us to lump all tune-types into one category and rely on reading the comment to find out whether a tune is a reel or a jig.

As far as the argument goes that a march’s timing can be altered so that the march can function as a polka, while this is true it’s also true that any tune in any idiom can have its timing altered to transform it into any other idiom. For sure if this was an STM site there would probably be separate categories for the various march idioms, which are as distinctive from each other as the various dance-tune types are from each other.

Yet, the classic Scots Guards book lumps all marches together, except for retreats and slow marches. Other GHB collections have separate categories for the various march idioms…one book even has a category for 5/4 marches, which contains only one tune!

Hi Jeremy,
Thanks so much for the site and all your work.

Three things: we play marches a fair bit in various sessions here in NSW, Victoria and the ACT. I love the feel of them, whether I can picture people marching off to battle, or accompanying a funeral etc.. as I play them. They are often very solemn and moving pieces of music, and the time signature doesn’t change that. I find it excruciating to be in a session where people play those wonderful pieces of music as, e.g., a jaunty jig (unless it’s a march that is appropriate to going off into battle!). Or, worse still, when they “swing” them.

Second, when I started playing for dances and the caller would turn to the band and say “32-bar jig or reel”, I soon realised that you could use either (although a skilled lead muso will choose a piece with the right “feel”) because in both cases the pulse, which is what the dancers want, is in “two”, not three. It seems that the same kind of thing applies to marches: what the marchers need is something that helps their feet go “left, right, left, right”. They don’t care whether that pulse is made up of groups of three or four (although the comment above about “feel” still applies).

The third thing is, (and I’m only suggesting this because you said you have time), have you ever considered having a cross-indexing system so that tunes can appear in more than one place, one according to time signature and the other according to tradition. I guess you have, and that this might open a can of worms, but perhaps that might help?

> It seems that the same kind of thing applies to marches: what the marchers need is something that helps their feet go “left, right, left, right”.

As one of the few people on this board, I imagine, to have played for marching troops, I can promise that the further away you get from 2/4 the trickier they find it. > why it’s not possible to call marches in 3/4 waltzes

I think we’re getting into the realms of philosophy a bit, but my conception is that a tune is what it is at the moment it’s being played, and one can go deeper into one style or another, if one has the musical and idiomatic fluency to do so. Many Irish musicians would struggle to play Atholl Highlanders as a march, in the way I do, without a bit of preparation, and likewise I can’t play the Jig of Slurs in the way I’ve heard Irish musicians play it.

What makes a particular idiom idiomatic is at the macro level details of structure and tempo, and at the micro level many small details of timing, melody, embellishment, etc, often things that are not ever really spoken out loud directly.
