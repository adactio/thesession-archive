Instrument role in driving the melody

Some instruments, like whistle, pipes, and fiddle, seem to have a speed advantage - they can play fast, with each note handled essentially by one finger movement. Do percussive instruments, such as mandolin and banjo, that require two coordinated finger movements to make each note, possibly have an advantage in driving_the_melody due to the more distinct and discrete representation of the beat on these instruments?

Re: Instrument role in driving the melody

When you say that the banjo etc. requires “two coordinated finger movements” to make a note, I assume you mean that both hands are involved.

Because it’s not just fingers. Yes you generally stop the string with one finger on your left hand, but a lot of the time the whole hand has to move. And at the end of your other arm (on the other hand, as it were), the string isn’t sounded with a finger but with a pick, which generally involves moving the whole hand.

As for the other instruments, both hands are involved in making many notes, and in the case of the fiddle, every note.

You could have said that, unlike the other instruments, a banjo or mandolin must produce a separate attack on every note (apart from the occasional hammer-on or pull off) by virtue of the way the sound is produced. Does this “drive” the melody? In the way that a hammer drives nails, I suppose yes. :-) But is that a good thing?

I’d prefer to think of getting lift into the melody as the goal to attain. For me, the other instruments you mention are much better at doing that than banjo or mandolin.

Re: Instrument role in driving the melody

When you talk about the “distinct and discrete representation of the beat” with plucked instruments like mandolin and banjo, you’re describing the initial attack of the note (which is often the only thing you hear in a loud session with other instruments.)

There is certainly a difference in the initial note attack with different instruments, ranging from a softer attack with flute, a bit crunchier with fiddles, and then the “ping” of the attack with a plucked instrument. But I don’t see any advantage for the sharp-attack instruments in “driving” the melody.

The rhythm pulse in a session is a collaborative effort, where everyone locks in together as a sort of group organism. If everyone is feeling and emphasizing the rhythm pulse in the same way, then it doesn’t matter what each note attack sounds like. It’s all blended together with the same rhythm pulse. It’s the pulse that matters, not the attack.

Re: Instrument role in driving the melody

Just to moderate a little between Jim & Cancrán…

It is true that certain instruments have more volume dynamics than others. But it is also true that instruments without strong dynamics are capable of articulating the notes to make it seem like they have more volume. Thus, on pipes, whistle or flute the use of cuts, rolls and crans give emphasis or attack to notes. It is also true (from physics), that a cut, rolled or cranned note actually does have more volume.

That doesn’t get into timing or syncopation which applies to any of instruments we’re talking about.

I don’t think there is general understanding on exactly what Drive and Lift mean; just another case of “ya‘ know it when ya’ see it”. At a basic level, I think of reading prose or poetry out-loud: A monotone or robotic voice is very different from an emphatic or expressive voice.
