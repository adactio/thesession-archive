Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

Having made the same transition many years ago, I have several tips for you:

1. In Irish music, most of the articulation is done with the fingers, not the tongue. Use the tongue very sparingly, and while you are learning, perhaps not at all.

2. I have Grey’s book also. Concentrate more on his descriptions of how to perform the various ornaments and less on things like his transcriptions of various flute players’ variations of tunes. I recommend using your ears to get the idea, but not to imitate what others do.

3. What I have done is mastered various ornaments that I use in specific ways. When I learn a new tune, I take a similar approach to playing each one, which means that over time, I have decided how I like to approach a phrase, so when and how I will ornament comes about very organically - I don’t much think about it.

4. While you are learning, be aware that ornaments for flute serve a functional as well as aesthetic purpose. Try to use ornaments at the beginning and/or end of phrases; also they can be used to create a space to take a breath as well as to avoid playing two consecutive notes of the same pitch (remember, you won’t be tonguing the break between them). This will give you a good base upon which to build how you will use ornaments to make tunes flow.

5. Study the following lesson closely. You will get something rich out of it every time you work with it. It is the best video of its kind I have ever seen. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5nQiDKFbYaU

6. Regarding the Irish style of playing, lean into the blow more than you would on your Boehm flute. Almost overblow to get that reedy sound Irish players like. Don’t be afraid to use glottal stops to punctuate notes or use the fingers to make certain notes pop. Listen, listen, listen.

7. Wave you fingers over the holes to get any vibrato you want and save that for sustained tones, generally at the end of the tune. Grey covers that. I am not above using throat vibrato when it serves my purposes, but start without it or you’ll be marked a “classical” player.

8. If you find your mouth gets dry, use a product like Biotene (I use the lozenges) to keep the mouth moist. I also use chapstick on the underside of my upper lip because the flow of air in Irish playing tends to dry that area out and you lose the brightness of tone.

9. When you start a session of playing, swab out the head joint to get rid of the condensation of playing a cold flute after about five minutes. After that, you can just occasionally cover all the holes and by sealing your mouth over the embouchure hole, give a few hard blows. Without the initial swab, though, I find the blow is not sufficient. Your mileage may vary.

Good luck and feel free to ask any questions you have.

Cheers.

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

When doing the cut, use the finger above and snap it so that the open hole does not sound, but rather you use your finger as you would your tongue to say “tu.” The pressure of your breath should feel like it literally blows the finger off the hole, but then it snaps right back. If you do that, you won’t trill.

Remember that you don’t sound the note before you perform the cut, you don’t sound the note when the finger comes off the hole, THEN you sound the note when the finger snaps back. So you finger three notes, but only sound one. It all happens very quickly and sounds very cool.

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

1. Look for a dark, reedy, and rich tone which is never the less pure in sound.

2. Throw in very limited amounts of vibrato you want a straight, powerful and pure sound. Have a tone so clear and straight that you “ could place a cup of coffee on it without it falling off.”

3. Practice putting in ornaments at delicate points in the music both by light fingering and breath control.

4. Have your right hand fingers placed straight over the holes.

5. Get the rhythm right.

6. First go slow then speed up.

7. Don’t be rigid when you play also pay attention to detail.

Blayne Chastain teaches Irish flute and whistle for 15 dollars a month on his website http://www.blaynechastain.com I seriously recommend it.

Look up videos on James Galway he has experience in the Irish genre and is probably the best out there on the silver flute. He might help with a transition but he certainly isn’t traditional in the Irish sense. I recommend making a playlist of his Irish music on flute (Danny boy etc), then half way in to the playlist put in music from Matt Molloy and other traditional players so it can help you transition. By including both recordings as you play you can recognize differences and move into it easily without you losing your edge.

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

Regarding your current ornamentation or lack thereof, I think it’s good to note that you can start to develop a style of playing that fits quite nicely into Irish styles without having a whole lot of ornamentation. A lot of people get entranced by ornamentation, and certainly many flute heroes including Matt Molloy, Catherine McEvoy, and Seamus Tansey make liberal use of various ornaments. But a great many great flute players, a whole lot of them unrecorded and lost to the ages, use far less and still sound quite alright in the idiom. Through practice things like ornamentation, variations, and a repertoire will get under your fingers nicely.

One thing you probably have on a lot of people is rhythm. This is a dance music, remember, so the rhythm is the most important part of the tune. Maybe yours is a bit too straight or rigid right now, but if you’ve been playing music for a while you probably have something there, which is more than many who are starting out. When you listen to other players (and listen to more than just flute players), listen to the rhythm of the tune, and how they play it (and play around with it). That, more than any any ornamentation you can put in, is going to give you a great “Irish” sound. I know I’d much rather hear someone with great rhythm play an unornamented tune than someone with a knowledge of all the twiddly bits but a fractured sense of rhythm, and I’m sure many would agree.

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

For quite a few years I taught “Irish flute” workshops at various music camps and there would always be “classical” or put more accurately “non-Irish trad” people in attendance. Some were Baroque recorder players, some were jazz/pop flute or sax players, some were actual orchestral style players.

The things they most frequently had difficulty with were

1) wanting to tongue every note

2) not knowing where and how to take breaths

3) not being able to play cuts and pats quickly enough

The first was a very difficult habit for some to break. For one thing we were fighting the music notation, because ITM is written without slurs, which indicates to a “legit” (formally trained) player that every note be separated. Grabbing their music and writing slurs over everything helps right away.

I had people who just could not keep from tonguing every note; I had to have them hold their tongue against the roof of their mouth, to experience what it’s like to not tongue the attack of notes.

For breathing-spots, they have to learn how to create breathing-spots by leaving out eighthnotes. In Baroque music you’ll take a big breath when you see a long stream of unbroken eighthnotes coming up… ITM reels and jigs are nothing but endless unbroken streams of eighthnotes! And they have to learn to leave out certain notes to carve themselves breathing-spots. (Yes it’s an oversimplification, but the principle is true.)

For finger-speed, their problem is that on the Boehm flute the fingers must be kept very close to or in contact with the keys to prevent key noise. These are called the “guide positions” of the fingers. Trouble is, you can’t get a pat to sound quickly enough moving to and from the flute surface from the low guide position. I have to train them to raise their patting finger high off the flute a split-second before the pat. The bit of extra travel allows the finger to accelerate to the right speed to get a lightning-quick pat.

They also play cuts far too slow. They want to turn Long Rolls into classical “turns” with the cut and pat given full melodic value. They have to learn to give the NOTES their FULL value and the cuts and pats no time-value at all (in effect). In other words a long roll on G must sound like GGG not GAGF#G.

Yes they need to learn to “play on the stream” (as legit players call it) or with “the flow” (as Irish fluteplayers call it).

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

Congratulations on deciding to pick up trad flute, you will get a great deal of enjoyment out of it in the long run. I play both boehm and simple system flute on a daily basis. The best advice I can give you is to treat them as two completely separate instruments, as if you had a flute and a bassoon. They are played in a totally separate manner, the style is different, the articulation is different, the type of tone that is desirable is different. It’s going to feel like starting over, so do your best not to get discouraged.

The second thing is, you need to do as much listening to traditional music as you can. My preference would be for recordings that are as far removed from classical flute playing as possible, and don’t limit yourself solely to recordings of flute players. Fiddles, pipes, boxes and banjos all have things to offer you in terms of understanding the greater diaspora of Irish traditional music.

To echo what has been said by others, find yourself a teacher via Skype. You will make significantly more progress with competent instruction. Going it alone might sound appealing, especially to an already accomplished musician, but understand that you are entering a completely new musical culture and guidance will smooth that transition.

As far as technical advice, start by playing slowly with an emphasis on achieving the correct sound and articulation for each tune you learn. Spend a week or so on each tune when you are starting. Learn it inside and out. Play it slowly, and record yourself playing so you can listen back. Ensure you sound the way you want to sound in your head. The vibrato has to go. You won’t need breath vibrato on any piece of Irish music, so let go of it altogether. Remember, its a different instrument from the silver flute, treat it accordingly. If I recall correctly, Paul Taffanel referred to vibrato as “A vulgar tool of the lesser musician,” so maybe that will provide motivation to rid yourself of it.

Best of luck.

So much good advice has been given I have only a tiny bit to add:
(1) Michael Clarkson’s ‘Irish Flute Tunes’ website (irishflute.podbean.com) is excellent for learning tunes by ear and especially for examples of where to take breaths (i.e. often, notes to leave out). (3) The OAIM flute teachers, Steph Geremia, Kirsten Allstaff, Majella Bartley, Kevin Crawford and Niall Keegan all have useful exercises for learning ornamentation and each has a very distinctive sound (tone and way of playing).
(4) The Wooden Flute Obsession CDs give a fantastic introduction to the many different styles of playing ITM on the flute.

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

I also made the switch. I had to set aside the notion that I knew how to play the flute and try to create a blank slate for learning traditional music. (And the better one plays the classical flute, the more humbling the process is!) The instruments are different (especially in terms of how to blow--as mentioned above, blow more downward, not across), and the music is totally different and must be accepted on its own terms.

Also, just to echo what’s been said, eliminate breath vibrato and tonguing from your playing. The vibrato should pretty much be permanently banished (finger vibrato is more appropriate), and you may find a few places for tonguing in the future, but you should be able to switch it off completely and at will. What worked for me was about five years with no tonguing whatsoever. Breathing is another big hurdle--instead of sneaking breaths between the notes, the goal is to drop an appropriate 8th note for a breath (and eventually, vary those breathing places). I found this very challenging, but slow and dedicated practice paid off. But it felt very odd (and difficult) for quite a long time.

I also used the Larsen book (although I think Conal O‘Grada’s accomplishes just as much in a more concise and digestible way--he really unlocks the music in a wonderful way). Here’s the trick to ornamentation: think of it as articulation, not ’ornamentation.’ This will help you know where to use it in tunes--to emphasize rhythm and phrasing. And because its function is rhythmic, learn it slowly, with a metronome (like Larsen shows). First, learn taps and cuts. (I don’t agree with Larsen’s approach to cuts, however. Having used both methods, I think it’s better to cut D, E, and F# with the left hand third finger; cut G with LH2, and cut A and B with LH1.) Once your taps and cuts are rock solid (and you’re comfortable using them in tunes), combine them to learn rolls. Don’t rush this. No rolls is vastly preferable to rhythmically imprecise rolls. It could be a year before you start learning or using rolls. No worries. Have some dinner rolls in the interim, and keep working on those taps and cuts as individual ornaments. (Also, don’t worry about Larsen’s double cut rolls and such--cuts, taps, long rolls, and short rolls are what you need to master over time.)

This is a terrific resource--between the da-bla-bla method and Larsen’s metronome advice, lovely rolls are in your future: http://www.rogermillington.com/siamsa/brosteve/

Finally, in order to internalize what this music is supposed sound like, listen at least as much as you play. 70% listening/30% playing, for instance. As they say, once you know the tune, you can play the tune.

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

Wesley’s post had good tips - as a player of both Boehm and simple system I would emphasize:
1. Treat Boehm and traditional flute as two different instruments playing different stuff. It will help you keep the differences straight in your head.
2. Listen a lot. Not just flute.
3. See if you can get some instruction, even if it is only an online lesson every so often or attending a workshop to see if you are on the right path.
4. There is no single right way of doing everything, but there are some things that everyone agrees are wrong (“inappropriate for this style of music…”). Diaphragm vibrato is one of them.
5. The tone production used for ITM is also possible on a Boehm flute and can actually be used constructively and legitimately in many other genres of music, including classical. Any expansion of your tonal palette can only make you a better musician. Work on being able to control the sound you make.
6. Don’t worry too much about how people tell you to hold the flute - what works for classical will also work for ITM. Huge waving movements of the fingers are not necessary for precise cuts and taps, nor is the pipers grip or straight fingers. Though they work for some people.
7. Sometimes people leave the top finger down for second octave D, sometimes they lift it. Try always to do one or the other because you want that particular sound at that particular place.

The Burn’s folk flutes are good - I have one as my camp-fire flute.

Since several people have gone out of their way to disparage throat vibrato, let me just say I emphatically disagree. There is no merit to the notion that you should not use throat vibrato. It is a matter of taste and approach. I would venture to say that when I use it, no one is aware of it; they just like the sound. To put it simply, if finger vibrato is good, so is throat vibrato if it accomplishes the same goal. Just do it with an Irish sensibility rather than a classical one and you’ll be fine.

Re: Advice for switching to Irish flute from classical flute

A very interesting discussion.

Two things stood out as being things I have not previously heard of
1) an aluminum Boehm flute
2) classical players using throat vibrato.

Folk singers and many traditional fluteplayers (from many different traditions) use throat vibrato. A good throat vibrato is the very opposite of the “nanny goat” thing, it’s quite subtle gives a nice shimmer to the notes. Yes it’s much faster than the typical diaphragm vibrato… perhaps faster than is possible to do with the diaphragm.

One example of a flute tradition using both styles is Andean music. On the kena and malta a rapid throat vibrato is usually heard, but as one goes down in pitch, to the sanka and especially the toyo, a slow pulsing diaphragm vibrato is used, often the pulses matching the underlying feel of the music. The same guy will switch vibrato styles as he switched instruments.
