Bar Etiquette for the Session Patrons

A few days ago, I posted this comment on “the other thread”.

https://thesession.org/discussions/50367#comment1014963

There was no response and I’m assuming that this was because the discussion had probably run its course.

However, I think this could also be an interesting topic for discussion. As I said, there’s never any excuse for bad behaviour nor should we tolerate it.
Yet there often seems to be an “us and them” attitude within many session venues these days. So, should we make more of an effort to “fit it” and understand the “ways” of the regular pub patrons/customers?

In the old days, those who took part in pub sessions were usually drinkers themselves and often regulars of the pub in question. So, they would likely know and already socialise with the patrons to an extent. This used to be the case in Sandy Bells or The Oak in Edinburgh back in the day and, maybe, still is to an extent.

However, it is now often the case that players don’t frequent the pubs on a regular basis and just turn up for “their night”. Many don’t even drink or purchase anything from the bar.
So, there is far less “mixing” or opportunity for the players and punters to bond.

Any thoughts about this? I’m not looking for another discussion about specific “behaviour” as such but, perhaps, a look at things from a wider perspective?

Re: Bar Etiquette for the Session Patrons

I come to a session to play music with the other players, not to socialize with random people at the pub.

If a patron at the pub is actually interested in the music and has questions, I’m happy to talk to them, but otherwise, no, I keep to myself.

It very much seems that way for most of the players in the sessions I attend or host. They’ll only chat with friends that might have come to listen, but other folks in the pub, not so much.

That being said, I never go to pubs to socialize with random strangers, music or not.

Heck, in some cases, I’d prefer even not to talk to some of my fellow session players. :-)

Some of them have political positions that I strongly have issues with (on the MAGA cult spectrum) and I’d prefer if we just shut up and play tunes.

Re: Bar Etiquette for the Session Patrons

I usually end up chatting with some of the patrons, because that’s sort of human nature - there’s people, they have something to say, let’s do that.

Sometimes it ends up being interesting, there was one night recently where I stepped outside between the early session and the late session, and ended up falling into a conversation with two guys who were having a beer and didn’t get back to playing at all, because I was having fun where I was.

The more usual thing is someone asks about the music, the usual questions but they don’t know that they’re cliches, and we have a chat and I encourage them to pick up a tin whistle and learn a few tunes. Or, because of where I am, it’s not uncommon for Irish travelers (not meaning “traveling folk”, just people from Ireland who are away from home) to be in and they’ll ask the usual questions about how I came to the music and the like.

I see a session as a social thing with music, so when I go out I’m usually primed to interact with people I don’t know. It works for me.

As for the rest of it, the usual bar etiquette holds. Order something, tip the staff, be kind and courteous to folks you meet, mind other people’s personal space. You’re out in public, you might as well represent yourself well.

Re: Bar Etiquette for the Session Patrons

At my main weekly local session, many of the punters are friends of the musicians. As are the pub owner and waitstaff. So there’s always some visiting and catching up before, during, and after. It’s only natural—we’re all part of the same community.

Visitors and tourists seem to pick up on this, so it’s not unusual that they come over to visit with the musicians as well. Invariably someone asks them where they’re from, and that starts a conversation about common histories, travels, sometimes even mutual acquaintances.

We also get the occasional “celebrity” musician or band passing through, or a wedding party where they clear floor space and dance the sets. Then it’s a regular hoolie.

Hobnobbing is so much a part of the session that musicians in the circle often leave their seat to go visit, often with friends, but sometime with newcomers just to welcome them.

Shoreline’s right. Pubs and sessions have a symbiotic relationship.

Players need a place to play and pubs usually like it when something’s going on which may entice people to spend money.

However the players see themselves they are often as much entertainment as anything else. Certainly most non-playing customers that wander in only think: “Ah! Live Music.” (Or, perhaps: “Arrrrgh! Live music!”) That’s why people request songs.

If you can’t deal with that, hire a hall or play round someone’s kitchen table. Of course, if you’re being paid, the game’s up and you’re performing a gig. Expectations may vary accordingly!
