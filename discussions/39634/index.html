Re: Dimensions on low D whistles

“ I really want a (Low) D whistle, but I have super small hands… I probably won’t use a pipers grip because that would just feel too odd to me…”

Sorry to say that’s not setting yourself up for success. If I may employ reductio ad absurdum it would be a bit like saying “I really want to play bagpipes but I probably won’t do the bag part of it because it would feel odd.”

Low Whistles aren’t high whistles, nor flutes, nor recorders. They’re their own thing, and it behooves the person wanting to learn to play Low Whistles to use the techniques which have been proven to be the most successful for the vast majority of players.

It’s a simple fact that flattish fingers will comfortably spread further apart than arched fingers. It’s easy to demonstrate. We use piper’s grip because it allows Low D whistles to be played with comfortable relaxed fingers, which is the goal.

Now about “ergonomic” Low D’s, be aware that no whistle maker can waive or bypass the laws of acoustics. When all 6 holes are in their acoustically correct position you get the most even voicing, the best tone, the best performance. But if the holes were in those places few could play a Low D whistle, Hole 6 would be way down the tube halfway between the open end and Hole 5.

So all Low D’s are compromises between human anatomy and the laws of acoustics.

Lay out 10 or 20 different great-playing Low D’s from various makers and you’ll see that all of them have their 6 holes in nearly the same positions. That’s because they’re all up against the same constraints, and really there’s only one best way to skin that cat.

The one huge exception is using a conical bore, like the bore of a wooden flute. Having the bore constrict towards the bell-end allows the lower-hand holes to be brought closer together without as much sacrifice in tone. Hole 6 in particular can be brought closer to the other holes.

I would get a Tony Dixon all-plastic conical-bore Low D and start learning “piper’s grip”. That Dixon is the most ergonomic Low D I’ve come across that still plays decently.

Best for your particular situation, maybe, is that Susato used to make (don’t know if they still do) a Low D whistle with keys, which allows people to use recorder-style fingering and still play a cylindrical-bore Low D with a big sound.

As far as great-playing professional-level cylindrical-bore metal Low D whistles go, I’ve owned just about all of the major makes, and of those my Colin Goldie has a hair closer finger-spacing than the others I’ve had (MK, Overton, Lofgren, Burke, Reviol, Reyburn, Chieftain, etc).

Re: Dimensions on low D whistles

Yes all Low Ds that use straight metal tubing for their bodies are going to be more or less the same regarding finger-spacing, they simply have to be. The worst is usually Hole 6, which makers move up as far as they dare. Moving a hole up the tube means making it smaller, and the smaller and further away from it’s acoustically correct position a hole gets the fuzzier the note coming out of that hole becomes. They also get Holes 5 and 6 closer by moving Hole 5 further down the tube. Moving a hole down the tube means making it bigger. It’s why you’ll usually see Hole 5 being the biggest hole and Hole 6 being the smallest; they’ve both been moved out of their correct positions to try to make the space between the two not as huge as it should be.

It’s the whole reason Theo Boehm designed covered holes and rods, linkages, and touches for the flute, so that each hole could be made in it’s acoustically correct position and yet be comfortably fingered.

Anyhow Low D’s with a metal tube body isn’t going the direction you probably want to go. With small hands you need the Low D with the closest-possible fingerholes, which would be the Dixon all-plastic conical-bore model. Its combination of light weight, narrow tube, and close-set holes make it by far the easiest Low D to play I’ve come across. (Sorry I’ve not played or even seen a Bleazy.) That Dixon also has very low air requirements.

That Dixon, the all-plastic conical-bore Low D, isn’t going to set the world on fire with power/volume/tone, but the tradeoff is its superior ease of playing.

As pointed out above the Copeland is also conical-bore. The trouble is finding a good one, and affording it if you ever find one for sale. I bought a new Copeland Low D several years ago and it was horrid. The Bottom D and Low E were very feeble. It was unacceptable and unsuited for playing music upon. There are fantastic playing old Copelands from the 1980s around, but good luck prying one from the hands of their owners.

About the keyed Sustatos, yes it’s nice to have open holes to do stylistic things, but if it’s the difference between being able to play a Low D at all, then I’d go with keys.

Personally after going through loads of different Low Ds I’ve settled on my Goldie as being the best compromise. It’s not as good as one particular Low D regarding one thing, not as good as another particular Low D regarding another thing, but every other Low D I’ve tried has some glaring Achilles’ Heel, and the Goldie does not.

It’s difficult to provide measurements between the end of the mouthpiece and Hole 1 on Low Whistles with tuning slides because it depends on where you have the slide, which depends on how you blow the whistle, what pitch you need to be at, and the room temperature you happen to play in (well unless you’re playing the whistle continuously, or have the luxury of fully warming it up every time you play it).

That’s arm-length stuff. About hand-size stuff, I just now measured my Colin Goldie Low D and my Tony Dixon Low D. The Goldie is all-alloy, the Dixon is all-plastic.

The Goldie has the closest-spaced fingerholes of any of the cylindrical metal-tubing Low Ds I’ve had, as best I can recall. The Dixon is closer yet, as the numbers show.

Total upperhand spread from the top edge of Hole 1 to the bottom edge of Hole 3:
Dixon 3.11“ Goldie 3.18”

Total lowerhand spread from the top edge of Hole 4 to the bottom edge of Hole 6:
Dixon 3.18“ Goldie 3.21”

I’ve found that that’s what matters, the total width my fingers have to seal. Measuring from hole-centre to hole-centre doesn’t take into account hole size.

Speaking of hole size, here’s the diameter of each hole, from Hole 1 to Hole 6:
Dixon: .28 .33 .26 .35 .38 .28
Goldie: .33 .43 .35 .38 .44 .33

Another thing that makes the all-plastic conical Dixon so comfortable is its narrow tube.
Dixon tube OD at Hole 1 is .93“ at Hole 6 is .88”
The Goldie is a cylinder, 1" OD the whole length.

Re: Dimensions on low D whistles

“hard to play due to the air requirements. It seems to need more than even my recorders, and it feels as if there is some resistance as I’m blowing”

Thing to keep in mind is that there are two different sorts of air requirements:

1) force of air required to sound various notes, usually called backpressure or resistance.

2) volume/quantity of air which moves through the whistle as its played, which I call efficiency.

The two are often in inverse proportion to each other, so whistles with high resistance usually have less air moving through them than freeblowing whistles.

Efficiency is very important to me. How efficient a whistle is dictates the length of musical phrases you can play on a single breath. (Being a piper I want those long phrases.) The various notes require various volumes of air, High B usually has much more air moving through the whistle than low-octave notes.

Also very important to me is ease of production of the 2nd octave notes, in particular High B. Low Whistles vary with how stiff the 2nd octave is, the stiffest note is usually High B.

What doesn’t matter to me is backpressure/resistance. I play Highland pipes and all whistles have negligible backpressure to me.

However many people who play whistle make a big deal about how much backpressure a whistle has. They’ll talk about the relative backpressure of various makes, but honestly when I play them I can’t perceive any difference.
