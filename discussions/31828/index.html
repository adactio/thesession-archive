Re: Would one ace bodhran player be OK?

I have nothing against the bodhran -- I’ve just spent a splendid hour playing along to a couple of CDs, and thoroughly enjoyed myself. The sun was shining through the window, and the skin was gradually losing its over-winter floppiness.
I have nothing against guitars -- I have four of them myself, and have played for over forty years.
I agree that when the bodhran ‘kicks in’ (an apt metaphor) the whole atmosphere can be lifted. Wonderful.

The trouble with a great many players of the bodhran, especially of the new design with the swooping, booming bass, is when they -- as evidenced by open-mouthed, closed-eyed wobble-necked head-banging -- are ‘sent’, and consequently not hearing the music, but lost in an ego-world of syncopation and percussion.
The booming bass masks -- smothers even -- any subtlety that would otherwise engage the audience; rim-shots like gunfire penetrate to the core, without sympathy for the music or the players.

The same can be said of those guitarists (I have encountered many) who know a few chords but no tunes, who ask what key you are in, who thrash all six strings all the time, who… well, you get the gist.

I am not a pure-drop fanatic. Without innovation the music would not be what it is now. But I don’t like to hear traditional music of any sort messed about. Countless musicians have gone before, done it all, tried it all, and rejected it. Honed and refined it over decades, centuries.
Very, very rarely, someone comes up with an idea that catches on. But a lot of ‘improvements’ work only by their novelty. Eventually the novelty wears off, they are seen not to be improvements at all, and are quietly abandoned.
Spoons, djembes, washboards -- they have all been discussed here. Usually the consensus is that the player is more relevant than the instrument.

To answer your question, then -- Yes.

Re: Would one ace bodhran player be OK?

“Guitar is my first language, and I can tell you that this music is hard and tricky for a 6 string birdhouse player.”

This is the key point. Thanks to the last 100 years of popular music history, the guitar is ubiquitous - and it’s an easy instrument to get started on. So there are a lot of not-particularly-accomplished guitarists around; in its most common role as an accompaniment to singing, the guitar doesn’t necessarily need to be played to a high technical standard. The problem is, when one of these adequate-for-purpose guitarists turns up at a session, without any understanding of traditional music and, perhaps emboldened by a strong cup of tea, is oblivious of their lack of understanding or how much there is to understand. Sadly, since guitarists of the said kind far outnumber the serious trad backers, so this type of occurrence is not uncommon, thus guitarists are given a bad name.

The bodhran is a slightly different case, since it has not really found an established role in any other genre of music (This, in fact, could be part of the problem, inasmuch as people perceive the bodhran as being a quintessentially ‘Irish’ instrument). What it has in common with the guitar, however, is that it is seen by many as an easy instrument; what could be easier than thumping a dead goat? So the bodhran is often taken up by people that lack the confidence or dedication to learn a ‘difficult’ instrument - and, in the end, they lack the confidence or dedication to learn to play it properly, in sympathy with the tunes. These are the ones that give the bodhran a bad name.

You can argue all night about whether these instruments have a rightful place in traditional music; there will always be those who will construct reasoned arguments for or against their inclusion. But I think that is rather missing the point. *, surely it doesn’t matter what the instrument is.

*“If a musician knows the music and knows their instrument”

If someone brings a tenor sax or warpipes to a session and drowns everyone else out, then they clearly don’t.

Re: Would one ace bodhran player be OK?

While I stand by my earlier comment: “ Without innovation the music would not be what it is now”, I have to agree with Will here (To suggest that he doesn’t know what innovation means, by the way, is a bit puerile, as is the use of ‘lackadaisically’).
What he says makes perfect sense, and I wholeheartedly agree with him: innovation does not equal good. And even if the innovation *were* good, the effect can be detrimental and corrosive further down the line.
This is what I meant by our predecessors refining and honing the music -- polishing the rough edges off, shaping and perfecting (in the literal sense of ‘making complete’) to give us what we have now. Keep modifying the shape of an airplane by all means, if it helps it to fly better. But please, no more bells and whistles.
Innovations come and go, as I said. And bear in mind that our predecessors, when they were playing, lived in the moment not in the past. They will have tried all the things that are always, naturally, being tried, over and over again, and as always -- most importantly -- rejected them.
I fear that the most recent incursions of counter-melody, syncopation and harmony seem, thanks to the ubiquitous recording industry, to be overwhelmingly gaining a foothold, despite each in its own way being, in my opinion, A Bad Thing. You can be sure, though, that they’ve been tried before and abandoned, rejected, or otherwise left by the wayside.
I’m afraid we are up against the same argument that we get from latter-day grammarians: it is a living breathing thing, and if it doesn’t change it will die. Meaning, ‘I think it will look cool if I pull its arms off and stick wings on’. Well, yes, it looks cool. But now its going to die.
Before you go messing about with body parts, you had better be pretty well versed in the anatomy of your subject. If you don’t know what the stripes are for, it’s best not to paint over them; and if you don’t know why it hasn’t got a tail, don’t nail one on. I’ve seen those things running around, and believe me, they are sterile, and they die young.

What you forget is that if you pull the arms off and stick wings on, you only kill the individual, not the whole species. And so it is with music. If someone decides that what ITM really needs is an oompah tuba accompaniment, it might well kill their band, but it won’t kill ITM.
