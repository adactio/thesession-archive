Re: An Update on Trends in Irish Traditional Music

Tirno, I’m not trying to present an argument or prove anything. I’m just venting (and chuckling) about the fad of Celtic music and it’s adherents. This is more a response to Theirlandais’ recent thread: https://thesession.org/discussions/27054

Seems to me that Irish traditional music works at large much like a festival session. There’s an inner circle of core players who know what they’re on about and who have the music from the players and mentors before who have their respect. And then there’s outlying concentric circles of wannabes and dabblers and finally clueless eejits with djembes and 12-string guitars. Naturally, the outer rings are always larger than the core.

I *do* think that popular media amplifies this trend. While there are more brilliant players today, there are also exponentially more dabblers and eejits. YouTube breeds them like ticks on a basset hound. And they in turn breed cds of themselves like Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever in ticks.

Maybe I’m just waxing wishful, hoping against all odds that a slice more of the wannabes would come to this music with genuine wonder, curiosity, and interest about the traditions and culture and *people* behind it, instead of stopping at the first cool modal chord. And it wouldn’t bother me if we took a pause and thought about relating more to the humans making this music, rather than to the recording and play-back machines and their sound files.

Surprising, to me at least, that such a perspective would startle the denizens of a forum dedicated to the music. But maybe that’s what happens when the music is filtered through pixels and megabytes….

Also, Tirno, I should explain that I spent many years in the early stages of learning this music while living in the boondocks, with no mentors and no one to play with. I’ve gone through phases of relying on radio, recordings, and even scanning the dots in hopes of learning *something.*

To be clear: I’ve lived both in hotbeds of this music, *and* isolated far from it. I’ve spent more than 30 years in Montana, USA, for pete’s sake (sorry Pete). But I still found ways to connect with great players, either on my travels or when their travels brought them near.

So I empathize with people in similar situations. But I also know from first-hand experience how limited some of these tools can be, and what a powerful difference it can make to find a face-to-face mentor. Even if just for infrequent, periodic tutoring. You can absorb a lot in a day or two or five, and then have ideas to woodshed on for years afterward.

I think it all boils down to what Kevin Burke said at my first lesson. There are players and dabblers. The difference is in how obsessed you get with the music, and where that obsession takes you--maybe to seek out authentic sources, say.

Works for me.

Re: An Update on Trends in Irish Traditional Music

Will I’ve been thinking alot about your post in light of what I’ve been doing in my lessons with Kevin Burke and the music camp I just went to.

I’m not sure it’s a question of whether or not something has been handed down in a family or not - I can point to examples of folks I know who have a “family” tradition of old-time music but their music is terrible. I’m sure there are one or two untalented musical families in Ireland as well (or maybe they are blessed with only talented musicians there and they exported all their bad musicians long ago!)

I think it has more to do with understanding where the music comes from and why it’s important….Irish tunes are deceptively “simple” in structure often and I think folks learn the notes and find they can fit a simple backbeat rhythm to it and to them is sounds peppy and Irishy (in a green-beer kind of way).

When I first started taking lessons with Kevin, I often got teased because my playing started to sound more like his (which I take as a compliment of course!) but that is certainly not his goal with my lessons. He has continually pushed me in the direction of “deeper waters” towards the folks who influenced him and understanding a deeper logic in the music than is on the surface. We’re talking a tradition that goes back at least hundreds of years, if not longer. And looking backwards and understanding how those who came before understood the music tells us lots more about what the music can hold.

Anyway, I guess what most of the folks you’ve posted videos for are missing is the fact that there is a deeper logic to this music than they can touch with just a surface treatment. And the deeper you go, the more there is to discover. Once you can HEAR that in the music, you can’t be satisfied with a surface rendition of a tune I think…..because you know there is far more in there!

And I agree with you, that in this day and age, there is NO reason (aside from perhaps money - which is where the obsession part comes in) why folks shouldn’t try to seek out “the real deal” to learn from - there are hundreds of camps around the world with all kinds of brilliant teachers out there. And with Skype, there are more folks taking on students from far away (that’s how most of my lessons happen now)….but few of us can get the “family tradition” experience - though we can join a tradition of teacher or mentor/student that has lasted for hundreds of years. Even in a workshop you can get a sense of how someone plays and thinks about the music.

I read somewhere that when you play with your mentor/teacher, you pick up all kinds of mannerisms or phrasings unconsciously….some of which they picked up from THEIR teachers/mentors. It’s kind of cool to think that the way you tap your foot or the way you hit a chord may be a move that a fiddler in a direct line back from you was doing 200 years ago….it’s what this music is about in some sense, carrying on a tradition. It’s why I never mind when someone says “that sounds like Kevin!” because even though it sounds like him, for all I know, he got it from Bobby Casey or from Paddy Canny or someone else and THEY got it from someone before them. Nice to think you’re helping to carry the tradition, even if you’re a far lesser player than they are LOL But I’m happy to be just a little more mortar in the great building of ITM :)

Anyway, just a couple of thoughts from someone who is completely obsessed but still new to the tradition…..but I’m going through this transition from hearing the surface to understanding the underneath part so it was an interesting post for me to read.

Probably late to check in, but an interesting discussion. My take on the first group of videos: There are two kinds of playing going on.

1) Those who have some chops and for whatever reasons play the music in a style that is pretty far from pure drop. But, those folks do play musically. They make musical shapes (never mind the other kind) and what they produce “speaks” musically, though not in a way those who love ITM as Will does would enjoy. I can sort of put up with them. They are using the wonderful tunes for their own ends. We don’t have to agree, but we probably can recognize they are outside the tradition, and sometimes quite far from the tradition.

2) Those who have no concept of being musical on any level. The tunes have no shape. They are tricked up (slow to fast) but without much shape. All the notes are nicely lined up but…Someone said “midi playing” and I agree. Those folks, and there are many of them in all of the areas of trad music that I know of, need to figure out what music is. If you don’t make the music speak in some manner or another you really would be best off not playing out and certainly not posting on youtube. I don’t mind the “I’ve been playing for X amount of time and this is how far I’ve gotten” folks. But I do mind the “here we are playing at the X event, aren’t we grand” folks who don’t make any sort of music at all. I play hammered dulcimer among other things. Dulcimer festivals are full of folks who just hit the notes at the right time (well mostly the right time) with no thought of dynamics or phrasing at all. Good on them when their getting satisfaction out of doing it. Bad on them when they make a CD and play out with no sense of music at all. There’s just no excuse for that. NONE. IN ANY TRADITION (including “classical” playing). Dots are not the issue. Good players make music with or without them. The unconscious ones don’t. They should be tied to a chair somewhere and force fed fine playing of all sorts so they can discover that music goes far beyond the “right” note at the “right” time.

I’ll get off the soap box now, but never mind the flame proof suit. Music is music. Notes are not.
