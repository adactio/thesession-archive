Winners & Losers: When it comes to technology, does tradition benefit?

Been reading some Neil Postman of late. Thought it interesting if you applied his ideas to that of traditional music…in this case Irish music. I’ll quote him after this intro, but basically the argument is: technology giveth -- but it also taketh away and that whenever technology and tradition clash, technology wins by *marginalizing* tradition [eg, aurality or orality perhaps in ITM’s case]. It does this by rendering tradition mute because it makes it invisible. And so we forget about it….(think of diddley in the 30s, 40s, 50s perhaps).

For eg, Why the “need” for oral transmission when you have the gramophone, or 33 1/3, or cds or iPods or mp3s? Remember the arguments for and against Coleman’s records? Now whether they did or didn’t destroy ‘regional’ styles, I’m not one to say. And how can we *really* know anyway?

technological innovations on the one hand make sharing trad music much easier and faster (the benefit), but, on the other hand what is lost?

we can use slow downer programs to ‘hear’ those mystery notes…but what do they NOT do?

Technology has some benefits (this web site for example) but there is a downside…there is always the risk of sites like this becoming a replacement for actually meeting people face to face.

So, technology has given to ITM, but what has it taken away?

I’m sure this will upset a few of the members here, but here goes anyway…

In the case of slow-down software, the technology makes it possible for people to learn tunes when they otherwise couldn’t. This could be perceived as a gain, but one could also argue that this disturbs a kind of “natural selection” of the tradition -- that the ability to learn tunes without any technological aid requires a certain degree of musical aptitude or talent. In traditions where music is learned by ear, this prerequisite helps to maintain a certain standard. Technology has compromised this standard.

(I know that people had teachers who could slow things down for them, and slow-down software is a useful tool for people who don’t have access to a teacher. But I still think one could make the argument above.)

Re: Winners & Losers: When it comes to technology, does tradition benefit?

The tradition is aural more than it is oral, so a recording can still function in helping pass on the tradition… aurally.

But there are still downsides to recordings. A recording of a piece of music is static, a snapshot in time… Listening to a recording of a tune is like looking at a picture of someone, and can’t replace the experience of actually being in the same place as that person. Irish music is alive and breathing.

Just because Matt Molloy played a tune a particular way on a recording doesn’t mean that he always plays it like that, or will ever play it like that again. There is a deeper connection between player and listener that is not present in a recording. It is mostly subconscious. But as an example, have you ever recorded a session that you thought was great, and then you go back later to listen to the recording, and it’s not the same? It could sound better or worse than your impression of it when you were there, but the point is that it sounds *different*.

And technology will never be able to replace face to face meetings to play music together, because of limitations of things like the speed of light. So while I have used video chat to learn tunes, or whatever, you simply can’t (and never will be able to) play music *together* with someone remotely.

As far as audio-slowing technology, I personally consider it a useful tool (that can become a crutch if you rely too heavily upon it). But while slowing something down to be able to get the niggly bits is useful, it is still a distortion of the original recording. The swing in the rhythm, for instance, can be heard more clearly when it is played slower, but the very fact that you don’t hear it as clearly at speed is important! If you played it at speed with enough swing so that it sounded like it had as much as it did when it was slowed down, then you’d be playing with way more swing. That’s just one example of the distortion. So use the technology to help you learn, but be aware that you’re distorting things in the process.

Re: Winners & Losers: When it comes to technology, does tradition benefit?

The “tradition” is a changing entity. It evolves. Technology has always had a hand in that change - whether it be anything from new instrument building techniques, to recording, or communication technologies… So you could argue that the process of change, in itself, is “traditional”.

But if things change enough to the point where people lose a strong connection to the past, the tradition is severed. Technology has certainly helped proliferate the tradition. I certainly wouldn’t play the music if technology didn’t have a hand in it. And proliferation is good for the tradition in terms of long term survival. But if the proliferation of the music through technology leads to changing the tradition beyond means of recognition, then maybe that’s bad.

So as I use technology to help me in my quest of breathing life into traditional Irish music, I try to stay cognizant of the fact that it is a new way of doing things, and that the new ways of doing things *may* have unintended consequences. That’s the personal choice I make, which helps me feel rooted in the tradition. YMMV ;-)

Re: Winners & Losers: When it comes to technology, does tradition benefit?

“But it seems to me that to champion blindly how great, say, digital technology is, for something like music of any kind and Irish music in particular would be naive.”

Technology should never be championed blindly, it’s a set of tools to be used to solve problems and do things that couldn’t otherwise be done. It’s not a belief system, you don’t have to choose between accepting it unconditionally or rejecting it outright.

But as far as being wary of digital technology in any kind of music, keep in mind that there are forms of music with their own traditions that were only made possible by digital technology. Underground electronic dance music began around the mid ’70s, and these days there are many different forms, each with its own history and tradition.

Electronic dance music is structurally simple but complex in execution, is largely made by amateurs, and has the main goal of creating music for people to dance to. Like it or not, it’s actually a new form of folk music, it’s just too new for people to realize that. It has its own traditional structures (like a four on the floor beat), instrumentation (such as the 303, 909, etc.), ornamentation (like filter sweeps), etc. So in this case, technology has actually helped to create new musical traditions.

So what does this mean for Irish trad? Not much. I think it’s likely that 100 years from now people will still be playing Irish trad in much the same way as today, with probably only minor differences. Why? Because people--people like you--will have chosen to keep the music that way.

That’s what tradition is all about. How large of an impact technology has on a certain type of music is largely up to the musicians themselves, the people who define and protect the tradition.

There will be unintended consequences, and technology will change trad, as it has always done. But I don’t think that technology will destroy trad or make it irrelevant, not unless the trad community allows that to happen. You can’t stop technological change from happening, but you can preserve the music in the face of change, and that’s what tradition really means.
