Cheap Thrills! ~ and ‘modern’ influences ~ love it or hate it…

Cheap Thrills! ~

Cheap thrills in the back of my car
Cheap thrills, how fine they are
Cheap thrills up and down my spine
I need it, I need it, ’cause it feels so fine ~ Frank Zappa

And then there’s the aftermath, like coming down off a fierce sugar rush… ;-)

Does one really need to follow a tale of woe, or a thoughtfully executed sean nos song or an instrumental lament ~ immediately afterwards with a quick funny quip or a pun? Maybe sometimes? Or can we, please, have a little time to contemplate, or for a tear, before we jump back in to a rush of dance melodies? This is to me one of those ‘jumping through fiery hoops’ some feel is a necessary part of putting on a show for the bums-in-seats, asses-in-chaisses, to give them a buzz, a cheap thrill…

What’s hackneyed for many of us is not so for the half-our-agers, the ‘kids’ amongst us. And then there’s the influences and inspirations. If your intravenous feed of something you choose to define as ‘tradition’ is a thick commercial ooze, then that’s what you come to expect and what you heave back up in your own machinations. Now we live in the MP3 age and the results will be folks playing as if they were compressed? Is there a laxative for that?

Amongst the many, many influences of the commercial world and studio creations and stage shows is the need it seems to take the bums-on-seats for a ride, even a rollercoaster of a ride, as to ease them in with a lovely slow air of some variety, and then goose them with a run of reels. In my sense of it, kind of using the air just to set them up for the rush, the ‘high’ adrenalin bit, throwing the air quickly aside for that cheap thrill. So the air is kind of the foil for the punchline, the reels. However, for me, I also get a rush of adrenalin and emotion with an air too, whether I’m playing it or listening to someone else giving it their all.

In my sense of things, this is a relatively new thing, but for those born to it, well, it’s their whole life experience. They really don’t know or understand anything different, or so it seems for many, who accept such cheap tricks as to be expected, the norm. While I find them tired and hackneyed.

This is the huge and overpowering influence of the likes of Seán Ó Riada, “Ceoltoiri Chualann”, “The Chieftains”, and Mícheál Ó Súilleabháin, and the drive for the highly arranged and choreographed… What’s funny about this is that many of those who are ‘obviously’ in the stream of that manufactured tradition, where “The Chieftains” are more at the source of their creations than otherwise, often bad mouth “The Chieftains” and related forms that are grand parents, parents, and brother and sister to their own exhibitions.

I’ve only mentioned the one influence, the one borrowed from the classical and performance world of ‘Suites of tunes’. There are many, many more such influences colouring this varied world of ‘traditions’ we have nurtured a passion for, connected in one way or another.

Another way we’ve experienced is to follow jigs with reels, and I remember hearing someone say, a piano player, that’s all jigs were good for. But, it was for dancers, and they did seem to get a rise out of the change, though we both prefer the differences of either, dancing to jigs or reels, rather than the cheap thrill of that kidn of a change.

I too have given such ‘changes’ a try, and I’ve heard a few I found some enjoyment from, some appreciation for, from mood swings to time signature flows and hiccups ~ but ~ personally ~ on this particular modern introduction and influence from the ‘classical realm’, I don’t find the suites particularly sweet. I mostly prefer airs on their own, and reels as reels. Though, I also have to admit enjoying the way of such things a la Cape Breton and the Maritimes, including dancing the changes from strathspey to reel. But, as recently heard, the seed to this thread ~ a lament into a run of reels, I hated it. It seemed to be just throwing the air away, as a mere foil for the reels. And, another recent listen, a set/suite of tunes that went from a waltz to a slip jig to a jig to a reel ~ UGH! Personally, for me, it sucked. I winced so hard bearing up through these two listens that it hurt. I’ll never lose those deep frown wrinkles, though they are well hidden by the greater quantity of ones caused from smiling and laughing…

So, favourites? ~ personal irritations? ~ anything in the way things are and can be handled that you find you’re fond of, or that winds you up and raises your blood pressure?

If nothing more, maybe you have a favourite track that makes such changes, in mood, tempo or time signature, something you’re particularly fond of that you’d recommend?

JNE ~ not quite, I don’t hate ‘new’ ways, and there is a long stretch of appreciation for all kinds of music, including some takes on recent forms, like rap. This is more about how tunes within the concept of a set or suite are ‘diddled’, and there’s nothing new there for ‘other’ traditions, such as the clasasical forms and Vaudeville. So, please, do not read me wrong, twice now from two different folks. I have heard and enjoyed some such treatments. However, I love melody and mood, and for me, mostly, the cute trick of jumping tempo from a lovely air into some daft run of reels does tend to wind me up. I might like how they’ve taken the air, and how the reels are treated, but the ‘hackneyed’ (do re-check the meaning) slamming together of a quick change for effect, that tends to irritate rather than amuse, for me. It’s a cheap thrill, and it quickly becomes tiring.

To have a set in an evening where dancers are treated to a change of time signatures can be great fun, once maybe. But to do it regularly becomes in my mind and experience of it, just plain silly, overkill…

As I said elsewhere, I like it tempo shifts now and then but not all the time. But I think, especially in Scotland, there is a lot of pressure on upcoming bands to try to have “interesting” and “creative” interpretations of the music. “Just the tunes, ma’am” approaches are not going to get you noticed if its big gigs you’re after. If you want to be a successful professional musician, you have be different -- which means arrangements, fusion with different genres, anything, and switching tempos is a tool in this sort of repertoire.

It’s not something I always like -- often I don’t. Some bands have really nice arrangements and others sound, well, too arranged. But people just accept this is how music is now and this is what they have to do to be professional. It’s become the norm.

It was the Chieftans who initially sparked an urge in me to play this music. Their treatment of “The Women of Ireland” is one of my favorite versions. Although, there are others that I like just as well.
It wasn’t until I heard the silence after an air played solo by Ronan Brown at the St Louis toinol that my heart skipped a beat and I was left completely breathless. The silence remaining afterward is the very thing that prompts a precious moment to take it all in.
When a reel is quickly slammed into overdrive after an air, it’s like stuffing the air in the pooper.
A solo piper moves me far more than any ensemble. I’m also moved greatly by any other solo player who has full command of their instrument. Modern ensemble playing can get pretty cheesy at times. Other times, I suppose it does have a certain charm, but rarely ,more likely never, moves me the way a good solo piper can.

My fellow bandmate and I watched the Donal Lunny ‘make a band’ pseudo-reality show on TG4. We were amused by identifying and pointing out the different little things that bands seem to have to have now.

For example, the slow into the fast, the jig into the reel, the syncopation, the harmonies, the dropping in and out of various instruments on tunes in the set, the starting of a set with whistle or flute and bodhran only, building to a crescendo, oh, we had a grand time.

We pointed them out, we laughed, we joked…and then we laughed even harder when we realized we had already done all of this for our band in a bizarre mandatory subconscious ’If you have a trad band you MUST have these things" sort of way.

I sing and we have a great female whistle player vocalist as well.

She sings this brilliant Raglan Road with no rhythm, very free and sean nosy. We have this minimalist accompaniment behind going her, not making a noise until she does, we’re all quite happy with it. The old ones cry, the young ones look stunned and drool, it’s grand.

Anyway, last night at the gig on a break she tells me that in her other band, they used to launch into a set of reels immediately after she was done singing the thing.

I laughed and said “That’s awful! No way we’ll be doing that! You sing that thing then sit there and enjoy the applause!”

…then I wake up and here’s this thread complaining about mucking up airs with cheap buzzes and the like.

I guess I’m getting old too Mr. C! ;-)

Back to the subject, but first, the crumply crumble, yes, it was good. It was a basic crumble recipe but with the fruit being half gooseberries and half blueberries, and we love to have oats in the topping, and we use varieties of raw sugars, keeping it light and tart in the fruit mix, just a couple to a few tablespoons worth. Crumbles are a regular event here, more usually combinations with rhubarb, such as with strawberries or blueberries or cherries, and then there’s the apple varieites too. It’s better when we can share them with others. Good company is the best seasoning there is. I should have baked this for our recent visitors.

Back on topic. I’ve pretty much exhausted my listens here of recordings that go from an air to something fast, and I remain more sure of my preferences, slow airs and laments on their own, fast stuff the same. However, I do still enjoy the Cape Breton changes, but they don’t start with waltzes or slow airs and then race into reels. A lovely lament I’m particularly fond of is played by Buddy MacMaster on the recording “The Judique Flyer”, “Captain O’Kane”, alias “The Wounded Hussar”, track 3. I like that way with it too, as Carolan would have had it, like a slow march, and fitting the theme. I choose to call it merely “The Wounded”, and play it with that in mind, with a wide defiinition for those that have suffered. I only wish he’d given a similar consideration for Tom Anderson’s “Da Slockit Light”, track 2 on “Judique on the Floor”. We’ve always played that air solo, with the thoughts in mind that went behind the inspiration. Buddy does a lovely job on the other tunes, but he’s once through the air and bounced into a stroppy march, and no, it doesn’t work for me as a set, though I’m sure it pleases audiences, as air, march and reel are sweetly bowed, but I find the changes clumsy, with nothing really to tie or create a particular interest between the quickly disposed melodies…
