Re: A Question for Fiddlers

@Anne, I think bluegrass fiddling has greater technical demands that Irish trad fiddling, if that’s what you meant.

There’s a real requirement to be able to play outside of 1st postion regularly, and for literal double-stops (also in positions other than 1st). There’s often some quite complex shuffle bowing needed too.

There’s also the requirement to be able to take a “break”, ie to play an improvisation around the melody and chord sequence of the tune/song. In a bluegrass jam, you’d be expected to do this, and you’d be ‘on your own’ for the duration of your break. In an Irish session, you play the melody, but if you slip up, the rest of the melody players can keep the tune going.

So, just answering the original question about bluegrass fiddling being more difficult, I’d have to say yes.

Re: A Question for Fiddlers

I’d suggest you start with some so called ‘old timey’ tunes, many of which are found in the bluegrass repertoire. In fact, much of the latter seems to me to be old timey cranked up to high speed. Many of the OT melodies are very much simpler than ITM and there’s maybe more emphasis given to rythym, which drive a simple melody along. I find playing OT music much easier than ITM. Once you get a tune together you can throw other notes in to give it more colour and interest etc.. Cuts and rolls are less common, but bluesy licks and rolling scale type stuff are plentiful, as well as using open strings as drones. “Tracy Shwartz’s Fiddler’s Companion” on Spotify will give you several standard tunes - each one played very simply at first, with more filler notes added with each repeat. These recordings provide great examples of all the above.
This link will give you plenty of tunes to start with - http://www.traditionalmusic.co.uk/ - scroll down to Old Time tunes. This link is a good search engine for tunes in ABC, PDF and other formats - http://trillian.mit.edu/~jc/cgi/abc/tunefind. Try Arkansas Traveller, Old Joe Clark, Cripple Creek.
Put ‘old time fiddle’ into youtube, find a tune you fancy, find the music and away you go - yeehaaa! :)

Re: A Question for Fiddlers

I agree with the previous post. They are different styles, and therefore difficult in different ways. That said, I do think that bluegrass requires a great deal more virtuosity, but is more straightforward in terms of execution. There are set techniques (many of them seem classical to my ear), things that can be learned in a straightforward manner from a teacher. Irish music on the other hand, while less technically demanding, is extremely difficult to make sound “Irish”. Nothing is set in stone, you just have to sort of instinctively get the hang of it. No one can teach you how to get lift and beauty in the music, making it (I think) just as difficult in its own right. At least, this has been my experience as a classical musician coming into Irish trad.

Re: A Question for Fiddlers

Irish = start with a melody and find a chord sequence that fits it.
Bluegrass = start with a chord sequence and make up a melody that fits it.
Both take equal amounts of skill, but they are differentr skills. For Irish music you need to learn a lot of tunes by rote, then learn to improvise on them.a bit. For bluegrass you need to learn a few tunes, but be able to improvise on them in all sorts of different ways.

If your’re looking for an easy starting point, go to English traditional dance music.

Re: A Question for Fiddlers

I agree somewhat with what Jim and some others have said above—that Bluegrass fiddle in general is more technically demanding than traditional Irish fiddle. The tempos tend to be faster, sometimes much faster. You are expected to play up the neck. Intonation must be spot on as there is frequent use of double stops. You need to be able to play chop rhythm and create fills, too, and these are techniques rarely encountered in Irish music, if ever.

The improvisational nature is actually pretty easy, all things considered. Well, maybe a better way to say it is that it can be as simple or as complex as you want it to be. Breaks tend to be a mix of stock phrases mixed in with the melody. Somewhat like what an Irish fiddler might do, only more complex and faster.

What bluegrass tends to lack is subtlety and soul, in my opinion. That’s not meant to be a value judgement. Rhythmically it just seems squarer, hitting downbeats like a hammer and relying on the rhythm section to create excitement through syncopation. Irish music has more push-pull. It feels more sinuous. Not better or worse, just different.

These are of course just my impressions.

Re: A Question for Fiddlers

Isn’t this more about the styles themselves rather than fiddle specific skills? First of all, I’d say that whatever bluegrass we hear is more about performance than… well, a session. There is a lot more going on in the tunes and songs (on most of the instruments!) compared to when a bunch of people play sets of tunes more or less in unison.

In that regard, bluegrass is a bit more like jazz - play a tune, then let the soloists play around the theme (and beyond it). This never happens in Irish sessions, nor on recordings. You might hear a band (e.g. Lúnasa) give a certain instrument some extra attention in a set, but you’ll never hear anything out-of-this-world, nor are the tunes played in keys way beyond the usual pitches. Mostly because that’s not what you’re “supposed to do” when playing a set of tunes. Variation on a micro-level is common, though, and in the case of Frankie Gavin, Dermot Byrne, Paddy Keenan etc. that’s enough to keep your ears busy.

(By the way, I’m a big fan of Mark O’Connor)

Re: A Question for Fiddlers

Jeff, I do think this actually has developed into a discussion about styles. Sure, there’s the performance element - but aren’t the “parking lot picking jams” the equivalent of sessions? The repertoire and level of playing is the same.

Going back to to the OP, the question was whether bluegrass fiddling is more difficult than Irish fiddling, and and I think the answer is yes (for the technical requirements mentioned earlier : complex shuffle bowing, literal double stops in the higher positions, chopping, improvisational skills etc), and I think this is a fact. None of these skills are required in Irish fiddling as standard, although of course there are some Irish fiddlers who do possess the whole technical arsenal.

As for anything else about the two genres - I think discussing them is entirely subjective. Are the Irish tunes better? Are they more melodic? Do Irish reels have more punch/nyahh/lift? Is it better to stick to simplicity, and avoid complexity? Are the ensembles more (or less) friendly? All subjective points, I think.
