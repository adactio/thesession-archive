Re: Playing for Dances vs. Sessions vs. Solo

You probably wouldn’t be able to just play any set you know and expect people to dance to it. For some dances there are specific tune types - The Ballyvourney Jig Set, for example, is danced to slides. Many sets from Cork, Kerry, and Limerick are danced to polkas. Often the polka sets will have a hornpipe.

The stucture of the dance is suited to tunes that are AA,BB. Set dances have a body and a figure. The body generally repeats several times during the dance and the figure repeats 4 times so that each couple does the figure once (in some dances, each couple does the figure twice). The music and the set are done together and the number of bars in the dance is usually divisible by 8. Sometimes, there is an extra move at the end of a set (Full House), which adds eight bars.

Ceili dances are a little different, especially if there are several long lines doing the same dance. While each line is dancing together, sometimes the lines get out of sync. So, one line may be doing the waves in the Waves of Tory, while another line has finished the waves and is doing a forward and back. The music works not matter where the dancers are in the dance and can be three part tunes or four part or two part. Won’t really matter.

I’m not an expert on dancing so someone else will more knowledge and experience can probably answer your question better. You can look for examples of set dances and ceili dances on youtube and see what they look like when they are being danced.

And don’t worry too much about joining in on a dance even if you don’t know how to dance. You will learn quickly and my guess is people will be very supportive. Have fun.

Re: Playing for Dances vs. Sessions vs. Solo

Each figure (or dance) in set dancing is defined by a specific number of bars. The band leader usually has a little book that tells him; failing that, the dance leader will know. It’s then the band leader’s job to organize a set of tunes for that figure with exactly the required number of bars. If the number of bars is an exact multiple of 32 then there’s no problem, but it’s quite likely the band leader will have to sort out tunes for a figure having a number of bars that is not a multiple of 32. In that case 48-bar tunes can show their worth, or the number can be made up by not repeating a B-part, or even by repeating the last 4 bars of a B-part. As long as the music doesn’t stop before the dancers do - that’s the biggest sin ever!
Before the dancers actually start dancing the band plays the first 8 bars of the first tune of the figure so that the dancers get in their places and get their feet tapping ready for the start. Those extra 8 bars at the beginning aren’t part of the bar count for the dance.
The band usually decides what tunes they play, but sometimes there is a particular tune associated with a dance. A common example is the last figure of the Connemara Set which is normally danced to “Maggie in the Woods” (probably played quite a bit quicker than it is played in most sessions!)

Re: Playing for Dances vs. Sessions vs. Solo

Since no one has gone into yet:

Playing for set dances (ceilis) is different than playing for step dancers, and playing for contra dances (since Jerone is in the US) is different again.

I’d recommend trying each--go dance at a ceili, learn some steps from a good step dancer, and cut the rug at a contra dance. The contra tends to feature the least complicated footwork (indeed some folks just shuffle around the moves), and the music has a different lift--more of a bounce--than the typical ceili feel.

It’s also highly instructive to play some set dance tunes for step dancers and watch the specific steps as they match the music, beat for beat.

And if you’re not quite ready to play for them, go listen and watch someone else play for them.

We had a dancer at the session this afternoon. We were doing Scottish and Irish hornpipes, but there was a group of Basque tourist in the bar. One of them did a sort of sailor’s-hornpipe-like dance to us, with his arms by his side and feet kicking out to the side, occasionally spinning round. He said they found British Isles hornpipes worked quite well a long time ago.

But, we were playing them at session speed. English hornpipe dancing speed is a lot slower, and Irish hornpipe dance speed slower still. If we’d been playing them as they were originally intended, he couldn’t have danced to us.

Morris: there are four different kinds I know about. All of them require rhythmic precision from the musicians.

Cotswold morris is the most traditional-looking sort, in the white uniforms and bells, and each “side” uses a small number of traditional tunes, usually in very local forms, precisely tailored to the dance. Not many instruments, usually.

Border morris is the wild and hairy looking stuff in strange fantasy outfits. The music for that can be almost anything. French tunes are currently popular, and the Gog Magog side from Cambridge does a brilliant dance to “The Oompa-Loompa Song” from “Charlie and the Chocolate Factory”. Anything from a solo fiddle to a near-orchestra, the wackier the better, so long as you can still deliver the requisite rhythmic oomph.

Clog morris is something like Border fused with hard-shoe stepdance.

Rapper is the sword dance form of the mining areas of the north-east of England, usually five dancers with two-handled blades weaving patterns that look impossible. It was once done to a 3/4 tune, but now it’s always done to very fast Northumbrian or Irish jigs - above normal session speed. Rapper dancers don’t like variety. You might get two tunes in a dance, but the overall repertoire of each side is about 6 tunes at most. The speed makes it difficult for more than one instrument to play, and most sides can’t find more than one sucker who can put up with the musical monotony anyway.

Re: Playing for Dances vs. Sessions vs. Solo

The most important thing about making music is Time.
Time is in your body, not in your head.
The brain is fickle, but the body has cycles, so give the body the job of Time.
Feel the Time as circles or cycles in your body (a wheel moving forward inside you).
Bend your knees (bounce) with the Time to generate and time your cycles (the count, the pulse).
When I play, I must stand, because if I don’t dance, the dancers can’t.
Time, musical Feel, is first in the body. All dancers bend their knees. Most musicians can improve their Time and Feel by bending their knees.
A lady once exclaimed to me: ‘You play with your whole body!’
I replied I didn’t think I left any of it home.
As mentioned above and above: learn to dance first, then you know how it feels to move like that.
Therefore, when you play, you feel like you want the dancers (you when you are dancing) to feel, and they do.
A fun journey!

Re: Playing for Dances vs. Sessions vs. Solo

I think it’s essential if you want to play these dance tunes to have a direct physical link with dancers and dancing. I used to play for dancers a lot when I was a young un, and dance myself. Still do occasionally (bloody hard work though). Dance and this music is like a finely balanced chemical formula.

However …

One of the most interesting things that can happen with the music is to take the direct physical link with jumping about out of the equation. I wouldn’t say that you ever really “break” the link, but if you can kind of transfer the link from actually jumping about to jumping about in your head instead. I think it’s analogous to that oft quoted bebop thing, was it Sachmo complaining that you couldn’t dance to bebop? I can’t remember.

The important thing though, if you want to this, is to get the grounding in actual physical dance first. Or you’ll end up sounding like all those millions of technically crisp but tedious bebop clones. It don’t mean a thang if it ain’t got that twang.
