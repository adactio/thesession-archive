Re: lovely lovely bowings

I’d recommend getting Peter Cooper’s Irish Fiddle Tutor, published by Mel Bay, with the accompanying CD.

Peter puts in recommended bowing patterns throughout the book, unlike Matt Cranitch or Paul McNevin where they only appear briefly in some tunes and then are missed altogether.

Once you’ve played enough tunes with the suggested bowing patterns “built in”, you’ll find them coming to you naturally, and you’ll also be using your own patterns on the tunes in the book - as Pete says, his patterns are only one possible interpretation.

Some dyed-in-the-wool traditionalists may question whether a book written by an Englishman and published in America can get to the guts of Irish fiddle bowing, but I and many others I know think this one does. I know my bowing has come on well since using this book, in all my tunes whatever their source, and I was complimented by an Irish fiddle teacher, who said my style was Kerry based, when in fact it all came from this book.

As for bowing:

Bowing is one place where sheet music can be really helpful, especially if you’re naturally predisposed to it. If you get a book that has fiddle bowings written out (try Lyth’s “Bowing Styles in Irish Music”, in particular), memorize the bowings just as you would the notes… The first few tunes will be pretty difficult, but it will get easier.

Alternately, there are a few techniques I suggested to Zina, like slurring 2-3 and 4-1 that help (try this on hornpipes first). Do this regardless of what string you are playing on. It’s good for you.

Another thing to try, is to play as much of the tune in one bow as possible, then turn around, and do it in the other direction… Make it sound as good as you can, and you’ll find you lose a number of preconceived notions, in a good way!

Aside from that, the general rule of thumb is to slur into beats, rather than out of them. Changing bow on every beat can often sound heavy-handed. If you can emphasize the beat without changing bow direction, you can get a nice swing to your playing…

Just my $.02.

Re: lovely lovely bowings

As someone who came to fiddling from classical training, I can appreciate the trouble you’re having with fiddle bowing. I struggled with that a whole bunch myself! Here are two things which really helped me:

1) An observation, by a whistle player, that fiddlers use bowings for phrasing. This helped me a lot because, as a classical player, you are taught to make invisible bow changes. Classical sheet music usually has phrasing suggestions, dynamics etc. written out for you already. The ability to do invisible bow changes comes in handy for fiddling too, but normally you want to hear the bowing - it’s one of the imporant things that makes your expression of a tune different from another fiddler’s.

2) The suggestion, by a fiddler, to choose a reel and play it every day, really slowly, until it has a good lilting sound to it. Listen to another fiddler play the tune slowly so you can to hear their phrasing (your teacher or a recording). The Wind that Shakes the Barley, for example. Try slurring as much as possible, and then try it again, playing mostly seperate bows. Eventually, you will fall into a bowing/phrasing that you like best for that tune. Once you can do it well for one tune, it gets a whole lot easier for other tunes.

Re: lovely lovely bowings

Too much of anything is bad by definition.

But for people who want to learn how to do something, it makes sense for them to do something structured--and LEARN it. Then the variety becomes easier to recognize, and to create.

Yes, you could make the argument that a student should try to learn to play as freely as possible, but in my experience as a teacher, it REALLY helps to have a bit of structure at the beginning, then work on varying it as time goes on. This is why I think learning bowings out of a book is helpful (I never said that learning bowing out of a book was the final goal, but it can be a very helpful stepping stone towards that goal).

After all, the goal is not to play everything “different” really, the goal is to play everything “well”. Granted, the two are often (if not always) complimentary, but I think it really helps to learn something well first, and THEN make it different. It’s a much more tractable task than doing it the other-way around.

Re: lovely lovely bowings

When I think of “patterns” for bowing in Irish fiddle, the patterns only cover a beat or two--a measure or less. Rarely more than that--I’m having a hard time thinking of any set bowing sequence longer than that.

And the bowing patterns are based on musical patterns--the ubiquitous |G2 BG dGBG| shape in reels, for instance. There are four or five widely used ways of bowing that sort of phrase (including all single bows, if you want to think of that as a “pattern”), and it’s useful to know all those ways and then some so you can apply any one of them whenever you run across a similar phrase (mostly, it’s the string crossing with alternating high notes): |GABG dGBG| or |A2 eA fAeA| or G2 BG cGBG| or F2 AF cFAF|, etc. Most Irish fiddlers use some version of the “figure eight” bowing shuffle for this, though not every time they play such a phrase. But knowing the figure eight will help you recreate the phrasing and timing of all those other Irish fiddlers.

Slurring across the bar lines is another bowing “pattern” that comes in handy, but it’s up to you how many notes to include in that slur, and whether to bow it up or down (likely either way).

Beyond that, it’s pretty free form, so the risk of getting stuck in patterns is nearly nil.

Re: lovely lovely bowings

Jan, I can see why you ran with my point the way you did, but it wasn’t what I intended.

I’m talking about players developing their own musical vocabulary, the tools for expressing themselves, not stardom, and not merely for the sake of technical prowess.

I’d say it would be not just sad but tragic if today’s players (1) assume that the old timers were 100% satisfied with their level of playing (which would suggest an arrogance I just can’t hear in their playing), and (2) settle for some arbitrary standard of technical expression simply because the last generation didn’t do it, or anything more is “untraditional.”

Case in point: vibrato. This particular tool doesn’t come easily to most people learning fiddle, so it’s not surprising that many trad players don’t use it much, especially thinking of folks back in the day when the average peat farmer didn’t have access to a “violin” teacher.

But vibrato *is* used in Irish trad, most notably by singers and by pipers, fluters, and whistle players (who use both finger and breath vibrato). You’ll also hear snitches of it in the playing of Coleman, Andy McGann, and Bobby Casey (listen to his version of the Drunken Gauger on Ceol an Clair for a beautiful example of how he used vibrato). For all of its initial technical difficulty, vibrato is a very natural way to sweeten or “enwild” a held note, in large part because we’re so accustomed to hearing it (and producing it) in our own voices. Far from being “outside the tradition.” And of course it naturally wants to transfer to other instruments.

So there’s this silly myth that it’s “untraditional” to use vibrato. My guess (based in part on the oral history passed from player to player to players the likes of Kevin Burke, Brendan Mulvihill, and Liz Carroll) is that more of the old timers would have used vibrato (sparingly, no doubt, in airs, on slow reels, etc.) if they hadn’t been so busy earning a living and raising a family instead of honing their technique.

Give a listen to the playing on Ceol an Clair--Junior Crehan, Bobby Casey, Joe Ryan, Patrick Kelly, and John Kelly all play powerful, wonderful music. Several of them use vibrato. Several of them also happen to have wandering intonation, scratchy tone, and repetitious, predictable bowing. I think it’s fantastic that they were able to come to terms with the conditions of their lives (political and economic hard times, a dearth of good quality instruments, physical labor, church sanctions against the music, etc.) and produce brilliant music. I also don’t doubt for a second that, given the chance, each of them would have eagerly and happily learned what they could from the expressive techniques and ideas of today’s talented players. Again, not to become “stars,” but to enjoy their own playing more.

We have the luxury of being able to hear all these wonderful musicians, over several generations of recordings now, to fill our heads with amazing sounds and ideas. I know that when I focus on improving my own playing, it’s all about reaching for those sounds in my head, not measuring myself against some other player or genre of music. Indeed, that’s an important part of this tradition that was passed directly down to me by fiddlers who received it directly from some of the old timers mentioned here.
