As far as the OP itself goes, I’m at a loss. I think it’s at the same time too big a question and too small. On the one hand, it’s a bit like asking where humans come from, or language. Actually, let’s go for the language one - I think it’s essentially the same question. Because music and language and the rest of the culture that has spawned trad, and Western Music generally, goes back as far as you want it to, and has influences from, as far as I can see, everywhere.

But what about ITM, say, as we play it today. Most of it, it seems to me, goes back to late nineteeenth century. Not much further. Of course, there are antecedents, but as soon as you try to draw a starting line, that’s when you hit the ‘it comes from everywhere’ snag.

And (one of my hobby-horses, sorry) given that the music has changed noticeably and radically in the last 40 years to my certain knowledge, having lived through that time playing the stuff, when are you going to put a starting date on what we think of as ITM as we know it?

Oh, and to answer the last question: yes, Purism is flawed, but not necessarily for the reason the OP implies. I think that Purism is too often just an attempt at exclusivity on the part of people who don’t really understand ‘the music’ or, for that matter, the nature of music itself, which tends to flow and mix and separate and coagulate, however we as individuals try to stop - or encourage - that.

And, even having said all that, sometimes you hear something and think ‘Ah that’s it!’ and know that it is. That’s pure drop. And I think it defies definition.

Re: Evolution of European Traditional to Parts Unknown

Instruments don’t necessarily carry their repertoire with them. It’s pretty certain that the Highland pipe came to Scotland from England, and equally certain that it was never used to play much music of English origin (it took up the Scottish harp repertoire instead).

Bagpipes probably originated in eastern Anatolia or the Caucasus. They spread in two broad routes, evolving into two basic types (the single-chanter type to the north and the double-chanter type to the south) which met up again in Spain a few centuries ago. The Arabic bagpipe is a double-chanter type and not ancestral to any single-chanter pipe.

There isn’t any notated Arabic music that could plausibly have been played on a bagpipe until very recent times.

Music for the launeddas and related double or triple pipes (without bag) might have gone in either direction from its heartland in the central Mediterranean. It seems quite plausible that some of it might have got into the Greek art music repertoire and from there into Ottoman and Arabic music. Which might in turn have been picked up by an Arabic folk bagpiper. And which might also have been shared somehow with the Pictish triple pipers depicted on Scottish mediaeval carvings, whose tunes might conceivably have survived the extinction of the instrument by a couple of centuries to be picked up by Highland pipers. Even Grattan Flood might have found that a might too many.

Re: Evolution of European Traditional to Parts Unknown

There are written records of English pipers in Scotland a bit before 1500. No earlier record of any kind of bagpipe, and no record at all (or any physical evidence) of Irish pipers visiting Scotland until well after the Highland pipe was well developed.

This is mainstream opinion, I’m not about to track it all down again. What seems to have happened: the old single-chanter major-scale bagpipe found all over Europe must have got to Scotland as well - trade links would have seen to that (and it’s the pipe depicted in the carvings at Melrose Abbey and Roslin Chapel). But a bit before 1500, someone in England developed it into something like the GHB we know today, it was rapidly taken up in the Scottish Highlands, and found to be a good replacement for the harp, given the changing times. It didn’t fit the English musical scene as well, since the ruling class there were more affluent and could afford to maintain larger groups of musicians (which also didn’t have to be quite as mobile). So it faded out there.

That idea of the origin of Morris dance is urban legend.

There is a very similar style of dance in Catalonia (where it seems to be documented about as far back as in England; it’s mentioned in Scotland earlier than either). But the Moors never controlled Catalonia, and given that the Catalans saw themselves before the Reconquista as the frontier state of Christendom, they were the least likely people in Europe to adopt any folkdances from the Muslims.

It probably spread right across Europe many centuries ago, and survived better in some places than others. It just disappeared in Scotland when nobody was looking.

My guess is that the “Moorish” connection with Morris dancing, and quite likely with the Catalan tradition and / or others in Western and Northern Europe, if any, is probable enough.

Ritual dancing and indeed other kinds (masked balls in Venice, e.g.) often went in for putting on disguises. This could be for all manner of reasons, including the frisson one might feel or create - and the opportunities one might take to get up to things one wouldn’t routinely do - by becoming for the time being something mildly or markedly Other. “In scottish Pipe music, I’ve researched that many tunes are of Arab Origins. So does that make it a Sub Genre of the Arab Genre? The larger question is, at what point did ITM come to be, and is it a mix of something else? Is Purism then flawed by design?” celticturntable

@ celticturntable. The answer is, IMO; no yes and yes. The second question is 2 part but I’ll tackle it in one answer. Now I’d need to qualify my statement, which I’ll do by offering the following;

“In scottish Pipe music, I’ve researched that many tunes are of Arab Origins. So does that make it a Sub Genre of the Arab Genre?”

1) Scottish pipe music doesn’t have arabic origins. That statement can be qualified by comparing both genres. It must stated here that there are many arabic regional styles where as there are only perhaps 3 or 4 scottish piping styles. A quick or, indeed an in depth analysis, will reveal that arabic and scottish musical styles and piping styles are melodically different beasts that have evolved in their respective regions in broad isolation of each other (opposed to complete isolation, no man is an island, nor are cultures). So not a sub genre of anything other than perhaps a common european piping tradition.

The “celts” of 1000 to 500bc (approx figures) traveled either in person or culturally (DNA evidence suggests a degree of this movement included people as well as culture) in two broad swathes, west through modern day arabia across north africa and by sea north to the Iberian peninsula then further north as far as grannies heelin hame and beyond . And west through mainland europe. These movement in general cultural terms had happened 500 to 1000years before “arabic” culture became established in the area we identify as arabia today……… Trade of course continued and it is likely that pipes and technical piping innovations traveled in both directions over the last few thousand years of inter culture commerce.

“The larger question is, at what point did ITM come to be, and is it a mix of something else?”

2 a&b) ITM is really a modern concept first and fore most and while ITM (in it’s broadest context) has both lent and borrowed from neighbouring musical traditions. The music of Ireland exhibits a unique regional stamp in the style in which the music is played, and therefore IMO it should be considered as a genre in it’s own right, similar to but different from close related traditions. Simply put, ITM didn’t evolve in isolation.

“Is Purism then flawed by design?”

3) Yes and no. It’s a combination of both views that both preserve the unique and distinct regional character as we now have it. And, enables that unique and distinct regional character to evolve, allowing those “accepted” evolutionary ideas and innovations to be gradually (or rapidly as it has done with the advent of modern communications, radio to internet ) absorbed into the ITM canon.
