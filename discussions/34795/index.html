Pain from fiddling

Is fiddling a labor of love, or am I alone in finding that things such as occasional neck pain, back pain, shoulder pain, arm pain, and wrist pain are more common than they ought to be?

I’m aware that there is a correct and incorrect way to hold and play the fiddle, and that the generally accepted “correct” way is probably the best ergonomically to both avoid pain and create a good tone. I *think* that I hold the fiddle correctly, and I certanily make my best attempt at replicating the good posture and techinque that my fiddle teacher taught (no longer taking lessons - havent the time/money), but recently I have run into some aches and pains. Some of the pains are nothing major, such as a sore wrist after 4+ hours of fiddling my arse off. It just makes sense that at least some part of my body would be sore from so much fiddling. But other pains, such as a sore neck, have me wondering if my future fiddling endeavors are in jeopardy or not.

I’m so worried about this that I even went to a chiropractor today. He “cracked” my neck, and strapped some electronic gadget onto one of my muscles in my shoulder to make it pulsate. I feel excellent now, and fiddled for an hour tonight without any discomfort; but should I have had the pain to begin with? Could it be that I’m fiddling too much? My play/practice time has increased significantly in the past few months from less than an hour a day to 3 or more hours a day (not counting the weekends - those are just a blur of eating, football, and fiddling all day long).

I guess the crux of my post is this simple question: Do you have pain from fiddling, or am I alone?

Re: Pain from fiddling

I take it that you haven’t been playing fiddle for all that long? It sounds as if you’re implying that you’re a relative learned in any case, but please correct me if I’m wrong.

In my experience (not for me personally, because I don’t ever remember having pain from playing fiddle) people who get neck pain are normally trying to grip the fiddle with their neck. Naturally, this leads to stiffness, especially if you’re playing as much as you are. A fiddle teacher of mine, a long time ago, advised doing relaxation exercises before starting to practice or play. I can’t remember what they are, but there must be some on the net somewhere, and it seems like a good idea. The idea would be to get relaxed before starting to play, so that you never grip the fiddle anywhere, either with your neck or left hand. It should just sort of rest there. No need to grip it at all.

Depending on who you are, I find that a shoulder rest - and the right one for you - can help. But some people manage perfectly OK without one.

Re: Pain from fiddling

It could be that your pains are not from stress or strain; but simply from remaining in the same position for hours at a stretch. Muscles are needed in order to hold still, as well as to hold objects. Try looking round the room as you play. At first you may feel you are going to drop the fiddle; but after a little practise you find a balance point. Get up and walk about; stand up, change chairs, all while you are playing. Change your head position from time to time so that you look towards the left then the right of the fiddle -- don’t maintain a fixed position because it is ‘correct’. Finally, if it starts to hurt while you are playing, stop. Don’t play through it, but shake it out, loosen up, take a few seconds to relax before continuing.

Re: Pain from fiddling

Get into a habit of checking yourself for tension. It is very easy, when concentrating on a passage or an effect in the music, to unconsciously grab tight -- in the bow hand, in your shoulder, in your neck, etc. Often this tension remains even after the passage is done, and this fatigues muscles and can eventually cause pain. Ultimately it is about managing tension -- relaxing as much as possible, and only using the right amounts of tension for the times when it is needed. If you spot tension, remind yourself over and over to release it. Check for it frequently. Until you develop the habit of playing without tension. And even then, check yourself from time to time.

Re: Pain from fiddling

Advice on chin rests: There are several different styles; fortunately, they aren’t very expensive. Buy one, try it for a month, and if you don’t like it, buy another.

I’ve played for close to six hours straight(ish) on several occasions over the last five or so years, and my wrists don’t hurt afterwards. However, that doesn’t mean I haven’t run into fiddle-related overuse injuries. For a while I had what my doctor dubbed “fiddler’s elbow” on my left side, which has elements of both tennis elbow and pitcher’s elbow. I dealt with that by wearing an elbow brace while I played, and buying a new, more adjustable shoulder rest that allowed me to tilt the fiddle to a more forward-facing position. As a result, I don’t have to supinate my wrist or adduct my elbow as much to finger the strings. Cleared right up in a couple months.

My current fiddle-induced difficulty is what I suspect to be the onset of Thoracic Outlet Syndrome, an ailment to which violinists are particularly prone, a result of turning the head to the left and down for extended periods of time without relief. So I try to remember to take breaks, move my neck through its range of motion, and stretch my chin up to the right from time to time throughout the day.

A google search for “upper body range of motion stretches” (without the quotes) yields a number of websites and videos that should give you worthwhile ideas for countering the effects of holding your body in a fixed position for an extended period of time.

Re: Pain from fiddling

Anonymous,

I taught someone with a right shoulder impairment (permanent damage), and we did all that stuff with the Wolf. It helped a lot. In the end, we made up a light harness to go round the neck, and the C bouts of the fiddle.

That was the eventual solution, because it took away the need for any pressure with the chin, and subsequent raising of the left shoulder (which often triggers unintentional movement of the right shoulder). The harness was just a grade of rubber strip used in physiotherapy for patients who need to do ‘light resistance’ recovery exercises.

Going back to the Wolf rest, it has 47 mm of height adjustment on either side. I’d be interested to know what the adjustment is on the Kun rest.
