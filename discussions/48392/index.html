Grumblers and Growlers.

Before getting into the extra-curricular adventures I mentioned in my last post, I want to explore tunes in the lower register. So far, I’m only working on three: Grusty’s Frolics, Big John McNeil’s, and Mairead Ni Mhaonaigh’s “The Red Crow”.

https://thesession.org/tunes/169
https://thesession.org/tunes/1002
https://thesession.org/tunes/184

I play so many Amaj and Bmin tunes, I never really got on with the lower side of the fiddle. I think I’m ready to make the dig now though, and the tunes above are really starting to come together for me.

It was The Millhouse that got me motivated to work on my lower range (https://thesession.org/tunes/4484 and Fergus Bogue’s version is the one that inspired me).

It doesn’t actually spend that much time on the G string but it’s at a really nice part of the tune that you want to come out strong and clear (and it’s in A so that C# is a stretch for the third finger). I started doing more left hand drills than I had been doing before.

As I gain more facility on the G string (not only with left hand but also finding the right pressure with the bow to get a decent tone) I’m really enjoying working out tunes down an octave too. Cronin’s Hornpipe is a nice one for that, you can do both parts.

Jerone, the ITM/STM tunes that go down on the fiddle’s G string aren’t in different keys in my experience. They’re just tunes in the normal repertoire that go down there in the normal G, D, and related modes because they were written (probably) by fiddlers and you have that access to the notes.

Like “The Orphan” mentioned previously. Or a tune like the “King George IV” strathspey, a great one on the low fiddle or mandolin G string. Tons of tunes like this written in the usual keys, but not by pipers, whistlers, or fluters because they don’t have that range.

Re: Grumblers and Growlers.

Seperatio, sorry I missed your last comment, I’ve been out the last few days.

I call them “grumblers and growlers” because of the grumbling and growling effect you can get out of the G and D strings. We appreciate tones across the entire spectrum within context, and rough, gritty, aggressive, and harsh tones are still appreciated when they’re used appropriately; Just as bright, clean, soft, or gentle tones aren’t necessarily appreciated when they aren’t used appropriately. Some tunes sound best when they are as sweet and smooth as you can get them, while other tunes really deliver when you can channel a fiery wildness through them. So to put it in a more endearing way, I think of it as the crackling of fire, or the rumble of thunder. Still beautiful in it’s own way, but exciting and maybe even a little dangerous.

Re: Grumblers and Growlers.

Jerone, you’ve found excellent descriptive words for fiddle tones. Nicely done!

Seperatio, FWIW, in the grand scheme of things, the low open G string on a standard-tuned fiddle really isn’t that low of a note. A tenor banjo is another octave lower. But a fiddle’s G string notes sound deep relative to its other strings. Any decent fiddler on a decent fiddle can get smooth, resonant low notes, or they can dig into them to make them thunder and growl. It’s all part of the fiddle’s expressive range.

Some fiddles are naturally more resonant on the G and D strings, some sound muffly or dull. Some are sweet on the A and E strings, others are edgy or shrill. A few well-set up fiddles are evenly balanced and, with a good bow hand, sing in any register.

Re: Grumblers and Growlers.

Jerone, maybe other flautists on other flutes find different, but on flute I find you have to make an effort to bring out expression. So the cuts and rolls, or overblowing slightly, or playing close to the edge nearly at octave jump are some ways to do that, because otherwise flutes just tend towards a purer softer sound. Even those additions are still relative gentle compared to say violin, but they stand out much more to a flautist for their contrast to steadier playing. Cylindrical flutes tend even more towards a purer steady sound, and there it is natural harmonics in the mixture of a note that can add some brilliance to it , or else very clear articulation of the notes (as in neat) helps to add texture and “pronunciation”. On Irish flutes a darker tone is often sought, that is a kind of reediness particularly in first octave, but while playing the most reedy possible the closest I find (on various flutes) is maybe halfway to clarinet sound, sometimes with a touch of oboe. Even there though it is quite a “refined” sound, in that it takes attention to keep it in the boundary and is not found by being rough in any way. To then add roughness or strong expression to it is a separate ability, and that is probably as ‘growly’ as it gets. Flautists I don’t think use that term though … I have heard “honk” used but found that maybe irreverant and it misses the mark by a long way to me. It is more a purr with some woody voice to it that makes it very special, with roughness or abruptness added separately at choice as stronger expression.

Gimpy, the footnote on flute is the one note that is possible to make really reverberate, almost like it is shuddering at a much lower frequency as well, which is possible - undertones exist here and there sometimes apparently. I wonder if something like that occurs on violin also… it sounds like it anyway, maybe there is some bounce on the bow that sets up to really create that rumbly sound ? It’s good to know that traditional players with some experience are able to play the full range of sound - maybe few question so much as I do but it makes a difference to me knowing the player chooses to play a certain way rather than being confined to playing that way for any other reason. Also to know that, or how, the instruments vary as you described helps explain a lot.
