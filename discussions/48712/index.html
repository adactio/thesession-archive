I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

Added by Philip McGarvey on October 26th, 2023

I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

(I’m only talking about Irish fiddling here, I believe American fiddling has a more strict rule on this.)

I’ve heard several fiddle teachers at workshops say that you should always do a down-bow on the down beat. I noticed in Matt Cranitch’s fiddle book all of the slur patterns on written music show this happening.

When I first encountered this, I realized that to do this means I’ll need an even number of bow strokes on each part of a tune, for example if I have two A parts that are the same which often happens, and I have an odd number of bow strokes in the A part, then the second time through the part I’ll be bowing the opposite direction. Likewise, if there are two variations of the B part, and one of them is even and the other odd, then the second time through the whole tune I’ll be reversed. Then on top of this, if I add in little variations on the fly, they may only alter the number of bow strokes in the part by an even number. I found myself trying to relearn the bowings of tunes I was playing and sticking in cuts in place of bowed notes to get the bowing parity even, and limiting my slurring variations to only replacing three bow strokes with a slur, but not two or four lest the direction be messed with!

I sometimes will come back to a tune that I had figured out a bowing for earlier but hadn’t played in a while, and I’ll play it and notice I naturally did a different bowing than I used to, and it gets reversed the second time through, and I think, !!!, now I have to pause and figure out the bowing on this one again and then practice the new bowing to make sure I don’t forget it.

One reason I stuck with it for a while was I noticed that on some tunes, because of my bow direction getting reversed, the second time through a part with the backward bowing was more awkward. And once I had relearned the bowing I could play it non-awkwardly.

But more recently I’ve noticed some fiddlers don’t seem to care about this, and play very well. I want the freedom of not having to have a fixed bowing of a tune, but being able to vary it however I want without worrying about parity. I realize this means I’ll need more practice because there are more possible maneuvers that I’ll need to be able to do smoothly. That tradeoff doesn’t bother me - it seems analogous to doing some of those violin exercises where they practice every possible permutation of something.

So, I’m inclined to try not caring which direction the bow is going. If a tune is tripping me up the second time through the B part because the bow got reversed, I could try figuring out a different bowing for the tricky bit that works both ways, or just practice the two directions enough that I can do both smoothly. I’ve kind of stopped adhering to the rule in the last couple weeks, but am a little unsure of myself on this, hence wanting to voice my thoughts and hear what others think.

I’m not trying to say this is “the right” approach or that that advice is “bad”. Clearly many people follow that rule, and it has its pros and cons, and maybe for some the pros outweigh the cons. I am curious of others’ choices and experience with this. Maybe people will say “sure you could do that, but here are other reasons why this advice may be worth following.” I want to see my blind spots; I’m always open to changing direction.

(Perhaps there ought to be a rule that a post must always end with a pun.)

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

Philip, from your profile bio, I gather you’ve been playing fiddle for little more than a year. At that stage, it’s common for people to think that bow direction is critically important. I suspect that’s partly a natural response to feeling overwhelmed by the bewildering number of possibilities, given the options for slurring various combinations of notes. Factor in the swirling variabilities of where and when to play weak beats and strong beats, and endless choices in how to phrase every part of a tune, plus variations, and the effects of cuts, rolls, trebles, etc., on bowing, and it’s all a bit too much, yes?

So a dictum such as, “Always down bow down beats” comes as a welcome guardrail, a way to pare down some of those possibilities.

Except it would be counterproductive.

I’ll give you an all-too-ripe example. Say you’re playing a jig. It’s entirely appropriate to single bow each note for a measure or two. If you do that (even just for a measure or two, and not through the rest of the tune), you will end up bowing up on a down beat. And that’s perfectly fine.

The lesson in this is that it’s enormously helpful to get comfortable playing more or less everything on both down bows and up bows. In short, I agree with your notion of practicing “every possible permutation of something.” That’s how I’ve approached fiddling, and bowing in particular, and it’s served me very well over 50 years of playing Irish trad (and some other stuff). In the long run, the goal is to give yourself *options* for how to play a phrase, not *constraints*.

I’d even go further and suggest that if something feels counterintuitive, put more time into it. Sometimes the most awkward bowings (or fingerings) end up being the most useful once you integrate them into your bag of tricks. But also recognize that each of us has our own point of diminishing return—a 20 year old may turn every bowing trick inside out and woodshed on it until it feels natural, but a 7o year old may not have that same luxury of time.

Also bear in mind that different styles of trad fiddle tend to emphasize different tools and ways of doing things. And this can vary depending on the tune form—reels vs jigs or hornpipes or polkas, etc.

Ultimately, lift and pulse and emphasis and phrasing aren’t about bow *direction* but about subtle variations in bow speed and pressure, in swelling and easing off notes, how you start and end notes (and sometimes how you create liveliness in the middle of a note), the use of silence or “breath” between notes, and artfully using single bows and slurs to articulate a melody. All of this happens regardless of which direction the bow is travelling, as long as it’s forward, with momentum and flow, like the bow of a Galway Bay hooker.

“You should always” - why? I probably use an up bow at least as often as, or more often than, a down bow on strong beats. But I agree that you need to be able to bow figures both ways and not get stuck in rigid bowing patterns. That said, you are bound to prefer one bow direction over another for certain phrases. As you get more experienced you’ll find that when you are in danger of getting “reversed” for such phrases - let’s call it wrong-footed - you just make an automatic adjustment in the notes leading up to the phrase to put you on the right bow. Without thinking about it at all, the way you would make adjustments in your run-up to a jump so that you take off on your preferred foot. The problem melts away.

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

stiamh, your “wrong-footed” metaphor is spot on.

My previous post was already going long, so I omitted talking about bowed triplets (trebles). Some people do them down-up-down, some up-down-up, and some go both ways. It’s absolutely okay to “specialize” in one approach and *not* do them wrong-footed (it worked for James Morrison and Paddy Killoran), and you’ll find the bowing works itself out so you end up where you need to be. Or if you’re so inclined, you can learn to do them both ways. It’s all good.

And I’ll second Jerone’s notion of digging deeper into tunes we already know. I do believe that new tunes can teach us new things we need to learn, but old familiar tunes can do the same, if we take the time to explore them more thoroughly. It helps to listen to a wide range of musicians playing the tune. It also helps to spend a lot of time inside the tune ourselves, and be eager to come back to it time and again over years with an open mind, not just regurgitating how we’ve played it before.

I get the sense that Philip already knows this, but I’ll say it anyway. Learning to play this music (on any instrument) is a process that unfolds over all the years that you play. Learn new things by learning new music, but also learn new things by making old familiar music new again.

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

There’s a fundamental tension here, and it’s a bit more complex than “do as you please”. Yes, good players are not putting much thought into their bowing. At the same time, though, any good player’s bowing is highly structured. Not necessarily predictable - though some are - but once they set off on a phrase there’s a very clear line of intent to it. They can do what they please because they have put a great deal of effort into bowing. Whether you call that studying bowing patterns, well, but you need some sort of basis on which to go “well I could slur these three and thus shift the accent here”.

I’d also say that a lot of people who “never play the same bowing twice” tend to have very mechanical and - gasp - predictable bowings.

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

David50, yes! As a fiddler, when I learn a tune from a flute or whistle player, I listen for how their breathing shapes the phrasing. The ornaments are often a distraction—for example, where a flute does a roll on A, that’s my open string, so I’ll do something else (not a roll). I’m most focused on the phrasing, lift, and pulse. Same with learning a tune from a box player, I mostly listen for how the bellows shapes those elements.

And when I learn a tune from another fiddler, I’m mostly listening to the bowing—dynamics, slurring, little stops that create space. It matters less (and often not at all) whether the bow is going up or going down.

Re: Calum and gilmo’s posts. Yes, good fiddlers aren’t just bowing things any which way. It may seem “intuitive,” but it’s clear that the Cathal Haydens and Matt Cranitches of the world have put lifetimes of attention into their bowing. That’s woodshedding. Then, in the flow of playing a gig or a session, you can use all those bowing ideas, and play with great freedom, but most of it happens quicker than conscious thought.

It may seem like they bow with “abandon,” but in reality it’s *freedom* built on long hours of ingraining all the many possibilities.

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

It’s true that learning another fiddler’s bowing of a tune takes time, but in my experience, it’s worth it. You can optimize the return on your time investment if you select one tune and learn the bowings of several different fiddlers on that tune.

You’ll likely find that in some places, their bowings are the same and in others they differ quite a bit. You’ll also see that bowing can change each time through a tune, partly in response to sizeable variations, but partly because varying the bowing—*even if the notes stay the same*—is a common way to give life to a tune, to vary the phrasing, pulse, and lift.

Here are the steps I used when I was first sussing out how Irish fiddlers use their bows.

- Choose a fiddler whose playing you admire.
- Choose a fairly simple tune that they play that features some challenge you’re facing—say, pedal bowing (the “rocking” string crossing bit). Try to pick a tune that’s widely played by other fiddlers.
- Sort out exactly how they’re bowing each phrase, paying particular attention to the challenge (i.e., pedal bowing).
- Follow them through each pass of the tune to see where they change their bowing.

Now find another fiddler playing the same tune and repeat the steps above.

Keep going. Do this for a jig. Then do it for a reel. Then a hornpipe, and so on.

This gets easier and less time consuming as you work through other fiddlers because in most cases you’ll find a fair amount of overlap. But it also helps to choose fiddlers whose overall styles sound distinct from one another. That way, you’ll learn how different approaches to bowing create different personal (and to some extent, regional) styles.

Also, you don’t have to do this all in one weekend. You can let one fiddler’s approach soak into your own bow hand, maybe for weeks or months. Then try on someone else’s bowing.

In the long run, understanding how your “fiddle heroes” play will give you many options for how to play all the phrasings common to this music, and from that, your own tastes will shape your sound and style. You’ll also be able to synch well with other musicians because you’ll know how to match their feel, their sense of pulse, swing, lift, and so on.

To jeff’s point - yes, you’re not likely to see a bank of fiddlers in a session simultaneously all bowing the same. We’re NOT the string section of an orchestra, following bow markings on the score. Of course, in this music, small differences in dynamics, ornamentation, even melody are acceptable and welcome as long as the overall groove is the same. Particularly in sessions.

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

I’ve commented on this before, but here goes. When you’re being taught basic “classical” fiddle-- say, the stuff in the Suzuki books-- the teacher often encourages you to think of downbows as the “strong” bowstroke and upbows as the “weak” bowstroke, and to plan out your bowing so that accents land on the downbow. It’s an approach that tends to sound good on simple baroque stuff, like the Vivaldi A Minor.

And… this approach does not work for Irish trad at all, I think. One of the distinctive things about Irish trad is that you *don’t* necessarily place accents on the downbow. The accents are wherever you want to put them in that tune, deciding where to put them is an important interpretive choice, and you’d better be able to execute them whether they happen to fall on a downbow or an upbow.

A second interesting feature of Irish trad is that you don’t have to place accents at the *start* of a bowstroke-- the accent might fall on the second note of a slurred phrase. A third interesting feature of Irish trad is that you don’t have to separate two identical notes by using separate bowstrokes (something you would do 99.9% of the time in classical)-- you can just separate them with an ornament.

The net effect of these three rules, I think, is that you end up with a lot of statistical “degrees of freedom” in Irish bowing-- because your bowing choices are not constrained by things like accent placement or the presence of repeated notes. You have a *lot* of choices. The really good players that I’ve listened to are constantly surprising you with their bowing choices (as well as their accent choices, and of course sometimes their note choices as well). It’s a hard thing for a learner like me to emulate.

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

Given how long you’ve been playing, let me give you the advice my teacher gave me about the same time of playing violin, about reels:

What’s important is what you stress, here reels should be stressed on the upbeat. The bowing is second to that, but having upbeats on a down stroke definitely helps. One easy strategy is to not slur anything unless you have a crotchet and two quavers, in which case either slur into the crotchet, or into the first quaver, so that you get an even number of bows, and each bar starts on a down bow.

Try to play reels like that for a bit, without ornementation, and then add cuts/rolls/trebles one at a time and see how they change your bowing pattern. But focusing on stress is definitely more important, and that bowing pattern is a good start for reels.

As for famous fiddle players, they “just do it”, which is not very helpful for us mortals.

Re: I feel like giving up worrying about bow direction.

Tom, you can get a lot of good music out of simple bowing, including using single bow strokes for each note. Some slurs are useful—particularly slurring onto strong beats (notes that beg to be emphasized). A tremendous amount of fiddling centers on nothing more than single bow strokes, 2-note slurs, and 3-note slurs. (Yes, sometimes we slur 4 or more notes together, but that’s not an essential part of playing most trad tunes.)

An intuitive way to think about bowing is to “sing” with your bow hand. Where you would sing louder, use a little more bow speed and pressure. Where you would distinctly pronounce each note (or syllable in a song), use single bow strokes. Where you would run notes/syllables together, slur those notes on a single bow stroke.

Enjoy making your music, and the new friends you make through it!
