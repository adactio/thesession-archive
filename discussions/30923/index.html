Adult Beginner vs. Child Beginner: Fiddle

This is a two-part post.

When I was in college, one of my professors said that if someone begins an instrument like the violin as an adult, they’ll never be able to develop the skills that children develop, since the violin pretty much grows with them. So as an adult beginner, this kind of freaks me out a little bit. I’m not trying to be a concert violinist or anything, but I still wanna sound really really good. My biggest worry is intonation. What do you guys think about my professors’ comment?

Second part. I have a little cousin that is fascinated by music, if not the instrument itself. She’s 7 years old, the same age I was when I started piano. When I went to go visit home, she always wanted to hold the fiddle. Everyday for three weeks, after we played a couple of computer games together, and she got her piggy-back ride, she wanted to hold the fiddle. But fair-play to her, she got some sound out of it.

I’m gonna teach her piano, but i’m going to have to find her a fiddle/violin teacher. I’m gonna expose her to some new music, cause right now all the poor girl knows is “Justin Beiber”. I know she’s only 7, but my goodness, if that’s all she’s exposed to, she’s going to need some guidance.

Any tips with dealing with kids that already wanna play music? On my part, i’m not sure what it’ll be like having someone else give her lessons, but I know I can’t. I have some serious posture and technical things I need to work out for myself. I don’t wanna be too pushy with her. She knows that i’m coming back home, and that i’m going to teach her music, and she’s really excited. She doesn’t know that i’m going to buy her a fiddle, and she also doesn’t know that I’m not going to teach her fiddle. Maybe i’m thinking too much about all this, I don’t know.

Re: Adult Beginner vs. Child Beginner: Fiddle

As far as intonation goes, you are aware that it is not right, and your years of piano playing have developed your ear (or more accurately your brain) to hear the difference. Only practise and concentration will add the final polish needed for accuracy, the same as any other activity. As with putting in golf, say, anyone can get the ball in the hole eventually and it gets easier the more you try, but amazing accuracy can be achieved only through constant repetition .
Your teacher is probably right to an extent, but age is not a barrier in the normal course of events, only if you want to be a classical musician of world renown while you are still physically able to stand up for an hour without a zimmer frame.
I’m happy that your cousin wants to play. The problem with formal teaching is that it divorces playing from normal life. You will probably know all about that since you started early yourself. You wouldn’t send a child to a voice coach in order to learn to speak (although some I know could have done with a few lessons :) )
Be very wary of trying to foster your desires onto others, who might not share your tastes. She has obviously picked up on your enthusiasm, and already wants to learn. The surest way to keep her interest is if she sees people thoroughly enjoying themselves. She will want to join in, and will know that there is a reward to be won after all that lessons entail.
Thousands of people all over the world have been taught by families; friends and neighbours Perhaps you could find her a teacher to show her the ‘correct’ posture, but bear in mind that that posture doesn’t apply to all music, a great deal of which is played sitting down. Good for you for buying her a suitable fiddle. I wish you (and her) luck.

Re: Adult Beginner vs. Child Beginner: Fiddle

As for your worry about intonation, why “worry?” Fix it.
It’s in tune or it’s not. You are putting your fingers in the right place, or not.
As I recall, you like to play things at a sprightly tempo. If you do so continually, without taking the care to listen carefully, and the fingers are just going down, but not quite in the right places, then you are practicing and reinforcing bad intonation. Take the time to slow it down, really hear it, and fix it.
As for being too old, well, I think it’s the case of older learners thinking too much about it, pondering it, talking about it…. just get to it. Do it.

Re: Adult Beginner vs. Child Beginner: Fiddle

The biggest problem adult musicians have is that exact narrative. “I am too old to get good at this.” It very easily becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. There is a point (60s, 70s) where you do slow down, but I see folks in their 40s and 50s saying that and it’s simply not true. You are at a disadvantage in terms of sheer years on the instrument but you’re not trying to play Ernst or Paganini.

The big advantage a child has is more patience for being a beginner. Adults get used to using their strong conceptual framework to learn and while it’s helpful in music, it can’t substitute for the kind of repetitive practice that children tend to do naturally to lay down neurological pathways. Adults tend to try to take short cuts or move forward too fast which gets them into trouble.

Hi Fiddlelearner,

I don’t think anybody, teacher or otherwise, should go around predicting future results for other individuals, no matter what they are attempting to do. I started Irish dancing at 67 and nobody’s going to stop me. (I’m now 73 and improving at the same steady rate.)

The key in your case is that you want to play the fiddle and you know what kind of sound you want to get out of it.
The key to your young cousin’s case is that she wants to learn how to play the fiddle, for whatever reason. Let her guide you. I wouldn’t teach her piano first. That’s a devious route to the fiddle, which is a melody instrument, not a harmony. Also the skills involved are not the same.

Then - whichever instrument you end up teaching her, don’t fall into the trap that there is only one way to play an instrument properly. The thing you’re aiming at is the sound you want to hear. The ways to achieve this aren’t always the same for everybody. The famous piano teacher, Peter Feuchtwanger, insisted on a serene, correct posture while playing the piano but warned for damaging techniques like making the child touch the keys with curved fingers and cupped hands. A Rumanian teacher in Amsterdam, a folower of Feuchtwanger, now in her eighties, lets the pupil find his/her own method of racing up and down the keyboard, only insisting on totally relaxed hands and shoulders. (I would add to this that learning to read music can be a serious impediment to fluent playing.)

7 years old seems to me to be an ideal age to start real learning. As someone mentioned above, the child is unencumbered by thoughts and future goals besides whatever she initially indicated: that she wants to learn the fiddle. She’s ready to immitate and learn. If you give her just as much at one time as she wants, no more no less, she’ll be neither overloaded nor bored.
In my teaching of a 7 year old to step dance, I’m continually adjusting the lesson as I go along. I have some problems to solve like the fact that the child doesn’t stick to the tempo of the music. But she continually amazes me with her accuracy and memory. These two elements seem to belong to her developmental stage and it is important to take advantage of this now before she loses it!
Have fun and good luck!
CNicolas

Re: Adult Beginner vs. Child Beginner: Fiddle

Hello! I’m new here, but wanted to jump in and share my perspective, since I have conducted the ideal real-life experiment. I took my first lessons on the violin at the same time as my son. At the time, I was 30, and he was 3.That was ten years ago. Over the years, my son has had a clear advantage in being able to pick music up by ear. He did Suzuki, and it’s truly awe-inspiring the facility with which those kids can learn a tun by ear. Many times, I’ve seen an instructor leading a workshop, teaching a whole roomful of kids a tune they’d never heard before. I kid you not, all of them are finishing the tune with him the FIRST time they hear it, and playing full tempo by the second time. This happens every time. It’s magic.

In contrast, I struggled with learning by ear, and had trouble memorizing the repertoire. In strict Suzuki pedagogy, you memorize the repertoire as you go. My teacher called it my “learning disability,” and said it was pretty typical of adults. I think some of it is just distraction, since we are often multitasking and don’t study and practice with our whole attention, like children do.

There are a couple of huge advantages to being an adult learner, though, compared to children. One, your fine muscle control is much better. Kids of 5 and 6 are still dealing with 5 and 6 year old motor skills, and an adult has already practiced many manual skills. I also was able to learn music reading and theory much more easily. The concept of “key” came to me effortlessly, whereas my son didn’t really get it until he was 9 or 10. Lastly, adults have the greatest advantage of all--discipline! The true key to getting better at music, and everything, is practice, practice, practice. And, yes, you can learn to play with good intonation, and you’ll find that the better you get, the more you hear, the more you’ll feel you need to improve. All of the same exercises for intonation work as well for adults as children, so don’t make excuses for yourself. Just do it.

Catherine

Stop memorising, get to *know* the tunes. Memory is always part of the process, but when you get to know the tunes it’s like a conversation. And you don’t memorise a conversation before you have one. You’re always in the moment. With memorisation it’s only about getting it right. Getting to know a tune is ongoing, & you can learn something new each time. Otherwise there’s no intimacy between you & the tune; just regurgitation.

Show her the path. If she loses interest in the music; you tried.

It also depends on what she wants to do with the music, if she wants to play folk music, then yes, hang loose. But, if she eventually would like to play in orchestras, then, classical training is helpful. Finding a teacher open to both is helpful. I’ve seen many kids study both and play both quite well. I’ve also known many musicians stuck in their genres, by choice, and some by limited technical skills.
I would not shy away from finding a teacher for her just because there are people that think classical instruction ruins a person’s ability to play folk music, which is what many on this site seem to think.
A child does not need to have it sorted out before they get a teacher. Their musical interests will grow and change along the way, and not having the prerequisite training for some styles will limit her possibilities.
At 7, find her a teacher and a rental instrument. Now, while she is interested. You don’t need to put her through your version of music appreciation to find out exactly what she likes. Those worlds will open to her naturally as she is learning to play. A good teacher will see to that. Good teachers don’t limit their students.
