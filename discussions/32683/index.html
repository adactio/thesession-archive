Re: 5 string banjo in irish music

“Might raise an eyebrow at first, but pretty much anyone who knows the tunes and respects the tradition would be welcome at our session.” -dinglealltheway

Bingo.

I’ve played a fair bit of clawhammer 5-string banjo in sessions. It’s actually great for any sort of song-accompanying, because you can fairly easily do melody along with the harmonies. Tune melodies are a bit tougher, because there’s only one octave between the lowest and highest (non-drone) strings. You have about an octave and half of easy playing before you start having to move up and down the neck, but that’s enough for many tunes.

Accompanying tunes is a different deal. I’ve done a fair bit of accompanying at sessions on it, and gotten really only good comments about it. Many people have told me they prefer it to guitar, and it also blends nicely with a guitarist in standard tuning for large sessions or in a band/group context. You can do counterpoint stuff with it quite easily, and so it can fit into that bouzouki niche of sometimes chords, sometimes counterpoint, sometimes melody. Reels, polkas, and hornpipes are best, because clawhammer (at least how I play it) is a mainly 2/4 or 4/4 sound. For jigs, slip jigs, and slides I can’t get clawhammer to sound right, so I’ll strum. But usually for those I’ll sit out or jump in on the whistle.

Now, it all depends on who you are and who you play with. The 4-string banjo isn’t accepted by some, and the 5-string banjo has a much tougher time. I like to think of myself as a good banjoist, with a good ear for the music and a fair bit of experience accompanying and playing, and I think that makes it easier. The same rules apply as for any instrument, only doubly if not triply so, since it’s quite “non-traditional.” Good players like some of the people mentioned in this thread, who respect the music and play it well within the idiom will be accepted most of the time. And if you do run into a group of staunch traditionalists who would never dream of playing with a banjo, then get a pint, sit back, and listen.

In fact, if you do that, I’ve found that sooner or later someone will ask you to play something (you’re sitting in a pub with an instrument, after all). And then you can demonstrate to those staunch traditionalists how good the banjo can sound in trad. And you may even get a couple converts. That’s worked for me.

Re: 5 string banjo in irish music

A few observations, as a (ahem) 5 string player:

The most suitable (IMHO) technique for Irish tunes on a 5-string is single-string, where alternating fingers (either thumb/index or thumb/index/middle) play the same string for several notes in sequence. This is somewhat equivalent to using a plectrum and enables the same types of left- and right-hand ornaments.

The use of “melodic” or Keith styles, where sequential notes are played on adjacent strings, allows a legato feel which is appropriate for slower tunes.

A number of current tenoristas, e.g., Gerry O’Connor and Enda Scahill, are incorporating rolls evidently inspired by bluegrass banjo into their playing.

Should a 5-string be welcome at a session? If it’s played in a compatible style, then why not? There were 5-strings in Ireland long before bouzoukis.

There are a number of tunes well-played by Paddy Kiernan at
http://www.banjohangout.org/myhangout/music.asp?id=6774

He was like, I don’t care what kind of banjo you use. I think that pretty much sums it up, if you are not particularly interested in banjo music, given the same players, you probably aren’t really going to notice the difference between the types of banjo, unless the player is deliberately using a technique that draws your ears to it. I’m sure there are a lot of bluegrass banjo players out there crashing sessions. The first time I heard about a session I was wandering into a bar after a bluegrass gig with my 5 string banjo in hand, and there it was, an Irish traditional session. It’s a good thing the folks there didn’t make me feel to unwelcome, as I was of course curious and sat there with my instrument in hand just listening and they even played an Americana tune so I could join in. There are obvious differences in the typical 5 string banjo repertoire to session tunes but it doesn’t have to be the case. 5 string players just learn what is accessible to them in terms of learning materials.

So anyway, I think it’s a given from my story, that I was a 5 string player that switched to 4 string to focus on and learn Irish traditional music. Looking back, I would have done the same thing, but I wouldn’t scoff at a player who managed to be just as prolific in the same genre with a 5 string, in fact my hat would be off to them. The fact is it is much harder to play trad in a melodic style on 5 string than with a plectrum on 4 strings. It’s much easier I think to do some sort of clawhammer style, but even that must be subtly adapted so as not to clash with how the tune is traditionally played. If you have the skill to convincingly play Irish trad with 5 string banjo then I think that skill should be respected by the trad community and not ostracised. However I can understand not every session wants 5 string banjo, I think it is up to them to decide that, it’s not the same as ostracism.

Re: 5 string banjo in irish music

I enjoy hearing both 5 string and tenor at a ITM session, but I am more comfortable playing tenor banjo. The range of the tenor banjo is usually a whole octave below a 5 string and gives you the ability to play a tune in a lower octave and be more supportive of the other melody players. If my band mates want more volume they’ll tell me to play the tune in a higher octave. I also favor tenor banjo rhythmically because a flat pick lets you play a fast triplet on the same string where with a 5 string you need 2 strings usually, at least I do. I find tunes like Michael Rilley’s (G), The Dawn (A), Red Crow (Am), Farewell to Ireland (Am), Tam Lin (Dm), Paddy on the Landfill (Gm) to be very dynamic on the tenor banjo because of the 2 octave range, while a 5 string arrangement might not get the full range. I will agree that a well played Scruggs style 5 string bluegrass banjo has great tone and volume for an ITM session and that the altered arrangements can be a lot of fun. In short, I always welcome a 5 string player because our choice of tunes and approach is different and adds variety.

A few points here:
1) FeaBan- the range of the tenor banjo is not an entire octave below the 5 string (tuned to openG tuning)-it goes a 5th lower to G,, where the 5 string stops at D,. Re: 5 string banjo in irish music

Irish Music is whatever Irish musicians play. They have traditions of their own which includes “borrowing” from other traditions just like we Yankees do. When you see “traditional music” advertised in Irish pubs, that usually means “in the tradition of the performer”. There are, to be sure, sessions focused on Irish heritage-type traditions but American Country & Western, Bluegrass, and Folk are in the mix as well (see http://www.amazon.co.uk/Bringing-All-Back-Home-Series/dp/B00004VIEM).

The Tenor is brash by its very nature, high string tension, short string scale. The 5-String has a wider range of voices, although some tenor players are now making their instruments sound like 5-strings.

I’ve been playing Irish music on 5-String for more than 30 years,. The band I was in, The Angel Band—not the C-W band with the same name— were featured artists three times at the two international Festivals in Ireland held in honor of Ireland’s National Composer, Turlough O’Carolan (1670 – 1738). That’s Irish Classical Music. One festival we played twice. The only other band to have done that at the time was the Chieftains. The sponsor and originator of that festival was the (late) President of Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann, Tony Finnegan. We were very well received at all performances and sessiuns while there and in “Irish” venues in the U.S. and Canada. Never ran into any “glass ceilings”.

The 5-String allows a more mellow sound than a flat-picked tenor and, consequently, sustain is easier to come by. However, there is no rule against picking the same string multiple times in a row with finger and thumb picks as a substitute for sustain. Moreover, I have played many sessiuns in the U.S. and in Ireland and the 5-String was welcomed and often marveled at. “I didn’t think you could play those tunes on a five,” I heard many times, appreciatively. The notes to a tune are the notes, play ’em and give the sessiun leader 5 Euro and, by gosh, you’re playing Irish Music for all intents and purposes. :)

On the other hand, “when in doubt, leave it out…”

Nate-I think you’re limiting ’What a 5 string banjo sounds like“ to bluegrass and especially Scruggs playing,but the instrument is much older than that and historically a wide variety of approaches have been used. The earliest published books of banjo music,in the 1850’s and 60’s,contained Irish tunes-for example A Baur’s The Banjoist’s Budget (1883) contained jigs,reels and hornpipes although jigs as we know them were called ”Irish Reels", the early Americn jig being a totally different type of tune,in 4/4 and very syncopated. The Irish tunes in some cases contained variations as well, so at least this much of the trad approach was maintained. The techniques employed included clawhammer-type approaches as well as three finger melodic and/or single string techniques similar to Keith and Don Reno styles, a mix of which is what I use to play trad.
as far as an American accent I grant you that there is probably some of that in my playing and that of other 5-stringers, but hopefully no more than any other sessioneer from this side of the pond. However much we have immersed ourselves in the music and striven for an“ authentic” Irish sound, its inevitable that your roots will show through. That’s alright,though. “A 5-string banjo is a 4-string banjo with an extra string.”

Other way around. The 5-string preceded the 4-string. A 5-string played with a flatpick, if the 5th string is omitted, is pretty sure how the “plectrum banjo” came to be. Similarly a banjo strung and tuned like a stretch mandolin using only single strings and viola! err, voilà, Tenor Banjo :).

Continuing in the spirit of Garry’s original question:

The tune “Ookpik Waltz” is played by the great Irish Band, Altan, and by Uilleann Piper, Marco Pollier, as “Valse Des Esquimaux”. On Session.org the tune was posted as “A traditional Irish waltz…” It is, in fact, a recently composed and copyrighted tune by Canadian Frankie Rodgers. “Ookpik” is, apparently, an Inuit word for “Snowy Owl”. It’s great tune, no wonder it has been appropriated as composed or under different names by Irish players, American players, and so on. You pick your Ookpik (sorry).

Similary, Roger Talroth of the Swedish band,Väsen composed “Johsefins Dop Vals” (Josephins Baptismal Waltz) played on fiddle, guitar, and nyckelharpa—a really non-trad instrument in Ireland— can be heard being played by bands of many nationalities, including Irish.

I’ve heard Earl Scruggs “Foggy Mountain Breakdown” expertly played on uilleann pipes. A lot of what we call (and the Irish call) Irish Music is played on non (Irish) traditional instruments like, bouzouki, mandolin, harmonica, and—ta da!— the 5-string banjo.

The idea that any tune should or should not be played “the traditional way” misses the point of what makes traditional music traditional. Different nationalities give different accents. The way I see it, music, especially music that attracts people strongly, has a way of making the rounds now as never before with digital technology providing near instant, global, communications media.

The Irish are masters at composing, collecting, and passing around tunes instrument to ear and are most likely to respond appreciatively to innovation in instrumentation if the spirit is in the performance. They also have the custom(?), tradition(?) of avoiding credit for composition simply referring to tunes in a manner, like “Junior Crehan’s”, or “Tommy Peoples”, “Cooley’s”, there are many more than one tune with those names. Some may be reels, some jigs, some slides. You won’t know which until someone starts it off. There are dozens of tunes titled “Gan Ainm”, Irish for “Anonymous” or, sensibly, “No Name”.

If you love the music, play it on what you can play it on. You will be respected by those who play the music because they love it.

Better yet, follow the Irish prayer,

“May those who love us love us. And those that don’t love us, may God turn their hearts. And if He doesn’t turn their hearts, may he turn their ankles, so we’ll know them by their limping.”

I love it.
