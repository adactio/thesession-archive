Stuck in a rut

I have found learning a new instrument a very analytical process, very different from how I normally enjoy playing music; I’m learning banjo.
The problem is, I’m learning analytically but my playing sounds analytical and I’m struggling to turn it into something expressive. I have to take time out to bash something out on the piano just for relief. I learn from tabs from a teacher who I record and listen to along with other recordings dropping the tab as soon as I can.
Are there ways to connect the newly learnt tune to that intuitive side that Gam mentioned before I re-enforce it in analytical concrete.. This is a tricky topic to search out on discussions so if you know of any I’d be grateful for them and any advice.

When I was first learning banjo, I would do it in two parts. First, I’d learn the tune. Then, once I had it in my fingers, I’d just try and make it “musical,” e.g. play it expressively.

I agree that you should learn totally by ear. It’s very hard to read music on paper while playing banjo or guitar. To me it feels like you’re adding a very difficult step for no reason. With Irish music, there’s really no reason to read off paper when you’ve got itunes and Amazon. Banjo and guitar players are a little different breed than fiddlers and some others who tend to come from a classical background and be strong readers.

Re: Stuck in a rut

Try ad libbing a slow air -- it doesn’t need to be anything in particular, just a series of notes. The point is to anticipate a note or phrase while you are playing. If you don’t get what you expected, you just need more practice until you can do it on the banjo the way I assume you can do it on the piano. When I say ‘practice’ I mean playing, messing about, experimenting, annoying people -- all the fun things, not boring repetition. Don’t give up.

Re: Stuck in a rut

Practice regimes, goals, set targets, and lists? Yeck. No wonder you feel bogged down in a rut.

Immerse yourself in the music. Go to sessions (even if just to listen). Talk to musicians. Try to find some friendly musicians to meet up with and just have a couple tunes. Listen to any recording you can get your hands on. And really really try to start learning by ear. You’ve surely lurked around the Mustard Board enough to have heard all the arguments for learning Irish music by ear. If the tunes are in your head, like really in your head, rather than some letters on a page, you’ll find yourself developing a far more intimate and interesting relationship with them. I don’t think this can be done with a tune who is nothing more than a vague acquaintance, a series of letters on a page. You want your playing to sound musical, rather than analytical? Start picking up tunes by ear and establish those intimate relationships with them. You want tunes to be more like best friends and/or lovers than like that guy you met a conference years ago, who’s name you forgot but you kinda remember he gave a paper on public sanitation and urban development in 1872 Edinburgh.

Learning this music can be a long uphill slog. Christ, I’ve lost count of the amount of times I have been wildly frustrated with my playing and have threatened to sell the pipes. Luckily I have friends who can talk me out of being stupid.

The easiest way -- sheet music and tabs for some -- is deceptive and may be harder. For more metaphors… I once did a hike where we first took a gondola ride up the mountain, Aonach Mor in the Nevis Range, thinking this would be the easiest scramble, ever, since we started halfway up the hill, thanks to the gondola. However, we had to slide down the ridiculously steep heathery slope for about 400 meters, thus losing most of the altitude we had gained on the gondola. Then we had to slog up this boggy glen for about four miles before reaching the ridge we were to climb. And that steep heathery slope we slid down, we had to crawl back up a similar slope to get to the ridge itself. Point being, it would have been less work if we’d avoided the gondola altogether and just started in the glen, or found a climb on a mountain which didn’t have a gondola at all.

Sheet music and tabs are like that gondola on Aonach Mor. You think it’s an easy way to get into the music but it turns out that you might be working even harder. You’ll have gotten only so far but you have to go back down again and slog uphill through an epic bog anyway to get to where you really want to be.

This got a bit long and rambly but it’s become a wee pet peeve of mine. I know several folk who could be really good players if they get their heads out of the sheet music. But they have told me, “well, I just started playing two year ago and have to catch up to all you who have been playing a lot longer.” Argh.

Re: Stuck in a rut

Good ideas, all. One that works for me: listen to your fave music, and pick a tune that you absolutely fall in love with, rather than just what the teacher suggests. Doesn’t matter if it’s played on pipes or fiddle or harp, listen and pick it apart and try to approximate the ornaments on the banjo. If you can slow down the audio, that would help, but just have fun trying to reproduce all the juicy parts by ear. You’ll come up with your own version. Then play with someone else to make sure your rhythm holds up with fancy bits. Keep your pace slow enough to get the feel of the ornamentation. Soon this will translate to other tunes you learn. Have fun!

What I was trying to say regarding the ‘slow air’ was to make something up as you go along, not play a tune you already know, and obviously if it is slow, it is easy. The point being that you hear or imagine the note in your head just before your fingers go to the place on the banjo. If the note that comes out is not the note you were expecting, you need more time with the instrument.

Tell you what might also help, Eòsaph and that’d be to get yourself a standard tin whistle. Figure out where a few notes are and then have a go at playing something you already know fairly well on the banjo - i.e. you know how it should sound.
