Re: techniques for tune-learning?

One point that hasn’t been raised on this thread, and that is, how to learn to play a tune up to speed on the fiddle, bearing in mind that the aim for most Irish tunes is a speed more or less in the range 100-120.

Kevin Burke talked about this in a workshop last weekend in Bristol. He said that if you practice a tune slowly you’ll end up being good at playing it slowly, but it won’t help you to play it up to speed. This is because, on the fiddle, the bowing action for slow playing is quite different to that for fast playing and cannot easily be transferred, or perhaps not at all in some cases.

Kevin Burke’s solution is to take the first few notes of the tune and practice playing them up to speed with the fast-playing bow action (short strokes in middle to far end of the bow). When you got that then you do it to the next group of notes and then combine the two groups. And so on. Some sections of the tune may need more work than other, especially if there are string crossings (“Sally Gardens”, for example).

Allied to this is keeping the fingers close to the strings to help speed and coordination between finger and bow.

Kevin suggested, before you start the speed practice on your new tune, to play through it a couple of times at a moderate pace just to get an idea of its layout and to identify tricky bowing or fingering bits.

Another important point that Kevin Burke talked about in the workshop - something that was not mentioned at some workshops I’ve been to over the years! - is the importance of quality of tone on the fiddle. This is down to bow control - including fundamentals such as playing straight (parallel to the bridge), and not using so much pressure that you’ll kill the tone. I’d also add that good tone and good intonation go together; one helps the other.

Re: techniques for tune-learning?

Of all the elements that go into fiddling this music, tone is the least important one. Pulse and timing top the list, followed closely by the ability to continuously change the tune each time through. Intonation matters, but occasional detours are of no consequence.

More to the point, “good tone” for Irish fiddling isn’t the same as good tone for classical or swing or…. I want to hear some dirt and grit, not clean and sweet all the time.

This isn’t “entirely a matter for the player.” Tastes in tone are set by the community of musicians who play each genre of music. Pinkas Zuckerman’s tone would no sooner fit into a session than would Junior Crehan’s tone fit into a classical string quartet. Why would anyone expect them to?

That said, tastes are changing (unfortunately), influenced by a lot more exposure to other music (and studio-cleansed recorded music) now than just a few generations ago. Paddy Canny’s tone suits this music just fine, but many modern ears won’t listen to his playing long enough to appreciate his pulse, timing, and inventiveness.

Re: techniques for tune-learning?

High frequency bow hiss and “zizz” - these don’t penetrate very far from the fiddle, no more than a yard or so at the most, and they are directional. So they are generally not picked up by an audience unless the player is close-miked.

I use plain gut strings (including the E) because I like the tone, feel, and projection (this could be worth a new thread, but that’s by the way), but a distinctive feature of such strings is the “zizz”. I listened to myself on Sennheiser headphones linked to a mic and amp about 6 feet away - no signs of the zizz. I also tried a cotton bud in my left ear. This cuts out the zizz going into that ear but interestingly it was not audible in my “budless” right ear. I believe that what I am hearing through my right ear must be fairly close to what an audience would hear. In orchestra and sessions I now often play with a bud in my left ear because it cuts out the sound of the zizz and enables me to hear better what is going on around me.

As an aside it could be argued that the sound of plain gut is the traditional sound of the fiddle/violin.

Re: techniques for tune-learning?

To be clear, a clear tone isn’t a bad thing. It just isn’t the most important thing, in this music. And it’s not a “slight” to play with gritty or dirty tone--it’s one accepted norm in traditional fiddling, just as is reedy flute tone (rather than too “flutey”) and chiff on a whistle (rather than too pure).

Some people want to impose notions of tone that come from other styles, or over-produced studio recordings. Personally, I prefer a less refined tone--it suits the music. I don’t want to hear Casey’s Pig or Clamp of Turf or Devanney’s Goat sweetened with vibrato.

I think it’s a mistake to hear this music only through the filters of professional performers and recording artists. That’s not where this music came from, or where it lived for several hundred years. The commercial aspect didn’t used to be the biggest slice of the Irish traditional pie. We’ll lose something important if we let this become just listening music instead of dance music and simply music for playing.

Re: techniques for tune-learning?

To return to the OP’s original request for advice, there is the sometimes vexed question of ornaments. Kevin Burke, in his recent workshop in Bristol, advised learning a tune initially without ornaments so as better to be able to understand the tune’s structure. He pointed out that too much ornamentation can smother a tune, and that a tune should, if required, be able to stand on its own merits without ornaments.

I was at a tune workshop a couple of years ago where the tutor persisted right from the start in ornamenting the new tune he was teaching on almost every note, claiming that the ornaments were part of the tune (which imo begs the question “what is an ornament”). It is almost superfluous to say that I, and everyone else present, failed to get much of a handle, if any, on that tune. The situation wasn’t helped by the tutor’s vagueness as to the tune’s name, so I wasn’t able even to track down a source. I wrote that one off.

At Kevin’s workshop some one asked a question about vibrato. The reply was an unequivocal “none, or as little as possible”, although he admitted to very occasional use. This goes back to the old Baroque perception of vibrato as an ornament to be used strictly as such on a par with trills, turns (rolls) and other ornaments, and never continuously, which generally speaking is an unfortunate aspect of most of the music being played today.

I believe that any music, in order to sound good, by which I mean attractive to the listener, needs to have the right mix of at least five components (in alphabetical order!) - intonation, pulse, rhythm, and tone quality, each done well according to the circumstances. The common factor in getting these components good and in the right balance is that the player must be able to listen hard to what he is doing - which is not the easiest thing in the world. Sadly, the Vince Cable clip is an example of the sort of thing that can happen when the player is concentrating on only two of the components in the mix, to the detriment of the others.

I’d be inclined to have “timing” in the list, but that is included in pulse, and the “attack” of a note is part of tone quality.

I was looking for the De Dannan clip of “Humours of Galway” to post a point elsewhere on trad fiddle technique, but I couldn’t find it. While looking for it, I came across this one. I’ve listened to it before, but I thought I’d give it another go.

It was said earlier that the point of *this* music was pulse and lift, not velvet tone or conventional intonation. Not jazzy double stops or slippery shifts up and down the neck.

After listening to this I realised this clip showed many of these elements : pulse and lift, pretty conventional intonation, jazzy (or at least slick) double stops (and lots of them), aggressive (not velvet) tone, plus a slippery shift up the neck, for the E harmonic at the end. Oh, and a fair bit of thrown bowing, too.

All in a fierce, in-your-face, Irish traditional style. The bonus for me is the very clever, but short bit of syncopation between 01‘40 and 01’43. If you blink, you miss it.

It’s played at quite a fast clip, and I think it sounds even faster with the fiddle being tuned up a semitone. The almighty Frankie letting rip!

More straw men. I didn’t say those techniques are never used. My point is that they’re peripheral to what most people who play this music do. Sure, Frankie Gavin (a professional performer) will play like that with a “show piece” tune. That’s not how he plays in the clip of him with Paddy Canny, though, eh?

Besides, his use of doublestops is hardly jazzy. Those are all simple Ionian doublestops--not uncommon in this music. Same with the high harmonic--flashy, but not a shift up to play passages out of first position at all. And since Jim mentioned bowing off the strings--well, I didn’t go there at all because that’s actually not an uncommon thing to do in some styles of Irish fiddling (notably Sligo and Donegal).

Finally, the clip of Frankie *does* show a commitment to pulse and impeccable timing. In fact, Frankie has a hard time letting go of his ingrained Irish traditional sense of pulse and timing--listen to what happens at 1:12: http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31-8MLA5kdU

Changing genres usually begs us to change our focus, our priorities. Frankie struggled with it there. Similarly, no doubt it can be challenging for a technically trained violinist coming to Irish traditional music to let go of that training and focus on something other than technique. That may be exactly what’s needed, however, to end up playing this music well.

Martin Hayes

Tradition and aspiration

“As a teenager I remember an occasion when I was alone, walking down one of the roads not so far from our house after counting cattle. I remember thinking about the music of that locality and thinking that nobody will ever want to hear it. Nobody, I thought, will ever want to hear Joe Bane play because a lot of hiss comes out of the whistle when he plays, a lot of bad tone comes out, a lot of intonation issues, a lot of getting tunes wrong.”

“The outward shell of this music, however, was just a reflection of the musical aspiration; as with any artistic expression, the message is often more significant than the artistic vehicle. They spoke their inner aspirations through the musical vernacular of their locality with a naive innocence and purity that the most accomplished of artists have a hard time achieving. This music wasn’t widely appreciated because they played in a local, almost personal, vernacular and the outward form wasn’t often very refined.”
