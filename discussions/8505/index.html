Is the music being intellectualized and/or standardized to the point of becoming a product?

Eno’s comment in the newest discussion about popular session sets set me to thinking. I quote the comment: “Basically the idea is that if you learn the sets in these books then anyone who’s learned through Comhaltas will know them.”. Now I am not criticizing Eno or the comment and I certainly am not interested in hashing out the virtues, or lack there of, of Comhaltas. My question really centers around the tradition of the playing of the music and comes admittedly from my naiveté.

In younger, more adventurous years, I traveled through areas of Appalachia and sat on porches and around open pit fires playing music with folks that in a great number of cases hadn’t been off the mountain in decades. I didn’t undertake this effort to collect tunes and songs per say, I was just looking to experience the social tradition of playing the music of Appalachia. The craic if you will, which quite honestly seemed to be more of the reason for the gathering than the tunes or songs themselves. I am assuming, perhaps incorrectly, that there are indeed strong social reasons that this brilliant traditional music of Ireland has survived especially given the adversity it has faced in its history.

My question is; if the music continues to be standardized for the purpose of universal playing compatibility is there a risk that the socially enriching aspects of its playing, assuming my assumption is correct, will be diluted or in the worse case scenario lost?

Re: Is the music being intellectualized and/or standardized to the point of becoming a product?

More than one aspect to a session:

(1) Community, we enjoy getting together to play our tunes together. For better or worse, people have learned off records and most people will know the tune that way.

(2) Performance. Some people are better than others, some like showing off more (in a nice sort of way), some feel they can teach others. This is a force tending towards variety and individuality.

(3) Sharing. New tunes get into the session, you play iot, someone will ask where you got it from, you reply the latest No Hopers CD, they go out and get it because few are attuned to picking up the music aurally straight away.

(4) Learning. See (2).

(5) Nearly forgot the drinking.

(6) You fill the rest in.

It’s almost inevitable that mass publishing and mass marketing will lead to a standardised product, but the dynamics of a good session allow for all the various aspects without you really noticing which is which. For example, you usually find you start off from cold with something easy, standard, well know, just to play the instruments in and get everyone settled. Later on, the whiz kids and old masters can stuff their strut or whatever.

↳ Posted by AlBrown on November 29th, 2005.

Re: Is the music being intellectualized and/or standardized to the point of becoming a product?

The Comhaltas books are no more of an attempt to standardise tunes than any other method of transmission (recordings by Michael Coleman, trad concert performances, the O’Neill’s 1001, etc). It’s just an attempt to make the older session tunes more easily accessible to musicians so that eventually these tunes are more widely played and musicians (particularly those who may not get to sessions as often as they’d like to) can build up their repertoire. The notation is just a snap shot of the tune at a particular point in time and it’s the prerogative of the musician to apply whatever variations he/she sees fit or adapt whatever regional style is to their liking.

Re: Is the music being intellectualized and/or standardized to the point of becoming a product?

Hmmm. To state the obvious, it depends what you mean by ‘intellectualisation’ and ‘standardisation’. As soon as we go beyond saying ‘I like that tune’ or ‘I don’t like that song’ and start to discuss WHY, we are intellectualising. Those discussions may go in different directions, of course. Sometimes it may involve thinking about how particular combinations of tunes serve to generate different images and emotions. Other times, even while considering the same set, we might think about how different modes function, so that we might look for similar patterns elsewhere. Neither of these (of course, there are plenty of other ways to ‘intellectualise’) is intrinsically right or wrong, good or bad. I suppose there are two main pitfalls here:

1. Discussing these kinds of things with someone who is not interested in what you have to say. There’s no automatic reason why anyone SHOULD be interested: some are, some aren’t. I get the impression that your Appalachian friends, Ed, would not have been too interested in a deeper discussion about structures or patterns. Fair enough. I think it’s a shame though if someone who does want to explore deeper meanings or whatever is thought of as indulging in ‘intellectualisation’ (giving it a negative or derogatory twist). I think it’s only negative when:

2. Intellectual musings, explorations etc go so deep and so far that they become abstractions, and we forget, eg, that we’re actually talking about a f*** off reel that has just blown us away! As long as we keep hold of the imprtant thing - the music - there oughtn’t be any real problem.

Standardisation is something else. Personally, I prefer to learn tunes from dots. I find it easier. What I don’t like is reading tunes that are full of ornamentation etc. I prefer the bare bones: I’ll ornament it my way thanks. For example, I’ll prefer reading something from O’Neill rather than Breathnach for this very reason. I think the more prescriptive a printed source, the more it harms the folk process. Sessions, of course, can have a similar effect, where everyone is expected to play a tune in a more or less particular way. Playing in a band is even more prescriptive (and I do). When I vary things, which I do especially in songs, where I might add a beat or two (possibly to breathe or swallow), it drives the others nuts!

Standardisation of traditional music, or ways of playing traditional music, can function to its detriment.

A recording is, I suppose, the ultimate standardisation. Well, a popular recording anyway. And therein lies a paradox. For me, the most important recordings in ITM, in terms of reinvigorating the tradition and interesting and exciting new people (including me) are those of Planxty and the Bothy Band. Without them … well, I dread to think.

From my point of view then, neither intellectualisation (which is what this discussion IS, not just what it’s about) nor standardisation is completely good or bad. As long as we hold on to the fact that we LOVE this music, ITM will continue to thrive.

(Note: these intellectualisations are purely the opinions of the author, and are not designed to be considered in any way standard ;-)

Regards

Re: Is the music being intellectualized and/or standardized to the point of becoming a product?

Also bear in mind that many players of this music--perhaps even *most* of the players with loads of experience--rarely play just one setting or version of a tune. They typically have a whole store of settings in their repertoire based on how the tune is played by various other musicians, on other instruments, and in different regions. So one player ends up holding a Clare setting of the Morning Star, and an East Galway setting, and a Donegal setting, and a Touhy setting, and a Carty setting, and a Kelly setting, and then they add their own ideas to these as well. In short. “the same tune is never the same tune twice.”

So an experienced musician may know and play a “standard” setting of a given tune in the appropriate circumstances, but also know and play all sorts of differrent and perhaps even divergent settings of that tune in other circumstances.

It’s arguable that books and recordings are actually helping to diversify this music because of how easy it’s become to create a book (or tune data base on the web) or recording--anyone can do it and capture a snapshot of their way of playing a tune. So we no longer have just O’Neill’s sense of how the tune goes, but a thousand other versions drawn from many sources. Just scan through the tunes here at thesession.org--many are littered with variations and “alternate” settings in the comments section.

Re: Is the music being intellectualized and/or standardized to the point of becoming a product?

For what it’s worth, it’s not “the” Clare setting, but “a” Clare setting, that is, the tune played in a general style recognizable as common in Clare. It doesn’t get played the same way next week, or likely not even the next go round right there on the spot, though it might still be recognizably Clare (or Donegal or whatever).

My personal understanding of different settings and versions of tunes is based on the notion, as I mentioned above, that any setting is just a snapshot of the tune. There is no such thing as a “standard” or “fixed” setting, no such thing as “the” setting.

Ed’s example of the gent spring the crooked version on him on the spur of the moment is not unusual, in my experience of Irish or Appalachian music. But in most cases, the crooked bits were worked out ahead of time--the musician has played them before and will again. As opposed to improvised on the spot. And mixed in with other variations so that even “the crooked version” is not played exactly the same way each time it’s hauled out.

Back to Irish tunes….when the jig Garrett Barry’s is played at sessions, it’s widely played with all F sharps. But I also play a version that leans heavily on F naturals, giving the tune a very different feel. I initially learned these as two distinct settings of the same tune, but when I play Garrett Barry’s I sometimes wander and choose freely between F sharps and F naturals and points in between. All the while varying other elements of the tune as well. To me, this is a defining characteristic of Irish trad music played well.

Of course, in a session, you have to listen for the collective choices and go with the flow to avoid too much dissonance. But that doesn’t mean the variety is lost--just temporarily held back in favor of blending in. This happens in any ensemble playing. In most cases, the collective choices change over time, so even the same group of session players will evolve fresh approaches to tunes.

“Of course, in a session, you have to listen for the collective choices and go with the flow to avoid too many dissonance….”

I was discussing this exact concept at our last session. A few of us were having dinner after the session and I took the opportunity to pick Tom’s brain and learn something. Tom is our most experienced player. He is Irish. He has won competitions, he has played for what - 40-50 years in many locations. Ed knows him well, by the way. Me - trying to learn the tradition and integrate into a session in a way that would somehow add to the experience musically and otherwise - about a year of playing in sessions.

So I made the observation that even the experienced, regular, players at our session do not play the *exact* same notes, and I wondered whether this was considered a problem or a natural part of the overall sound. The answer would help guide me in what I should be focusing on to various degrees.

Tom said “Of course we are not playing the exact same notes. I wouldn’t want us to play like that, even if we could. The most important point is that we should have a compatible feel and rhythm. As long as we all ”visit“ the main points that define the tune, and there is no great dissonance, the rest is of less importance”.

I found this remark immensely enlightening. I could not see how everyone could learn from different recordings, and written sources and come up with the same notes. In a session, where unison playing is The way, our similarities as well as our differences are right out there for all to hear. Some settings sound quite good when played against one another but some can cause dissonance and that’s where we need to watch out. I am slowly learning that it’s ok to listen to the tune as it goes around one time before jumping in. In addition to hearing what could be the local setting, it also adds energy and lift when more players join in at logical places in succession.
