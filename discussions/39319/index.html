Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

On the one hand I agree with others here, that leaving your right hand ring finger (R3) on the low D tone hole when playing A, B and C# will work just fine. And leaving not just one but both of your pinkies hanging in the air actually works to your benefit, good for you! Leave the pinkies hanging slightly upwards and that will help to prepare you for flutes with keys on them.

The hollow of the chin, the side of the base of the index finger and the thumb, these are the contact points of a three point grip and these are THE ONLY contact points a player needs. And so the first thing to do is to get those three points into proper alignment so that a flute is then properly balanced. Basically, the chin and the thumb should push in one direction, both of them in the same direction, and the base of the index finger should push in the opposite direction, directly opposed to the chin and the thumb. Due to the very slight weight of a flute there could be a need to make a very slight adjustment to that arrangement, but only very slight. That is a player’s first priority and then the fingers get laid out to accommodate that flute grip.

Next, the chin and first finger of the right hand play no part whatsoever in anchoring the flute. The left hand holds the upper part of the flute in a wedge between the first finger and thumb. When playing C or C#, a bit of pressure is added to the bottom lip to keep the flute in place; otherwise the entire flute is held between the wedge and the thumb of the right hand. If you can’t hold the flute away from your face in the proper anchor points with ease and comfort, you are doing something wrong and need to study some pictures or get someone to show you. Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

The right-hand pinky is not an anchor point. It simply helps to align the fingers of the right hand, which might otherwise slide up or down the flute as you play. I played for years without anchoring the pinky. It wasn’t until I started losing sensitivity in my fingers that anchoring the pinky became a must for me. As o’muirgheasain pointed out, keeping the pinky free will help you if you ever try to play an 8-key flute or Boehm. Notice how most 8-key players twist the foot joint forward to provide a place for the pinky. This renders the notes in the foot joint unplayable.

Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

I don’t think the name Rockstro has been mentioned yet. Effectively, what some of the above has recommended is the Rockstro grip. I first came across it many years ago in a discussion on the old woodenflute list. Conal O’Grada recommends it and devotes several pages of text and photos to explaining it. To quote the key passage -
“A fundamental concept to understand is that you do not hold the flute with your fingers. The fingers must be free to move. To achieve this end result the flute is suspended through the application of pressure through three points as follows:
Inward force applied through base joint of the left-hand index finger. This point acts as a pivot in the overall balance of the flute
Outward force applied through right-hand thumb (sometimes supplemented by the right-hand pinky (small finger))
Resistance force applied through the chin”
He later adds, “The left-hand thumb is used purely for stabilisation of the flute as well as helping with minor vertical support…” (But it should be possible to lift off the left-hand thumb without losing grip.)

In workshops, Conal recommends pressing the flute hard against the chin to increase stability.
Terry McGee’s flute site also offers a lengthy description:

http://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Rocksto_on_holding_the_flute.html

At the risk of disagreeing with Ailin (a man I’m trying to buy a flute from!) this is the grip I favour too.
It can feel very odd at first but will pay dividends both in stability and in reducing strain on the hands.
With regard to adhesive pads mentioned by David50, I had the pleasure to attend a lecture by Baroque flautist Lisa Beznosiuk at the Cruinniú na bhFliúit in Ballyvourney in March. She talked about these pads – I think sharkskin was mentioned but I believe modern equivalents exist!

Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

It’s not necessary to push the flute forcefully against the chin, in fact you should be able to hold the flute securely with it not touching your face, nor with any of the lower hand’s fingers touching.

With the middle of the tube laying upon the base of the upper hand’s index finger, and both thumbs supporting, I can hold it fine. This of course is an Irish flute/Baroque flute thing where the upper thumb isn’t being held above a key.

Both in Boehm playing and Irish playing it’s very common for the lowerhand little finger (L4) to rest on a key or the body of the flute to assist in balance; with the Boehm flute L4 is pushing down on the Eb key most of the time (that key being one of the exceptions to Boehm’s open-key system) which is a carryover from the 8-key flute and 1-key Baroque flute.

I know Irish fluteplayers who keep L4 on the flute all the time. This to me seems awkward, due to lifting only L3 for E. For me it’s easier to move L3 and L4 together.

So they finger, in effect
D xxx xxxx
E xxx xxox
F# xxx xoox etc.

I have L4 off for D, E, and F# but put it on for G, A, B, and C. I’m not consistent, it can be off or on for G in particular. When I have L4 on, it’s resting on the wood block that holds the footjoint keys, which I realize isn’t the way orchestral 8-key fluteplayers would do it.

Now the above pertains to pure fluteplaying. However there’s a rather different fingering approach I noticed many of “the old guys” doing when I started playing. At first it seemed strange, but as I played that way more and more it became natural and began making more sense.

“Pure fluteplaying” and for sure Boehm fluteplaying is predicated on having the fingering as open as possible, putting down as few fingers as absolutely necessary for a given note.

Many of “the old guys” I was watching play flute and whistle had a rather different approach, having a partially open fingering system, in many cases lifting as few fingers as necessary for a given note. In this way it’s more akin to bagpipe fingering.

So yes L3 would be often left down. It doesn’t seem to have any negative effect on the notes. In this system the scale would be
D xxx xxx
E xxx xxo
F# xxx xoo
G xxx oox
A xxo oox
B xoo oox
C oxx oox

The brilliance of this is that in many cases you’re moving far fewer digits when changing certain notes. For example play
D G B d g b g d B G D
while keeping U1 and L3 down.

You’re only moving four fingers to do this, and it’s actually simpler than that, because pairs of fingers are moving together. Thus only two units are moving (U1 and L3 remain on the tube and U2/U3 and L1/L2 are moving as units).

I think of it as the “G shape” because a G Major arpeggio lies readily under the fingers.

Also there’s what I call the “D shape”
xxo xoo

There are good-sized sections of tunes which can be played with U1, U2, and L1 left on the tube.
For example play
D F# A d f# a f# d A F# D
and once again only two units are moving, one being the single finger U3, the other being L2 and L3 moving as one.

Now you’ll say “but A is flat when L1 is left on”. Yes indeed it is, but only if A is in tune when played open. If you’ve played large numbers of vintage flutes you might have noted how often A is sharp; the wily old Irish fluteplayers had a solution for that, flattening the note by leaving L1 on the tube.

You can play the beginning of The Mountain Road to see that “D shape” in action:
xxx xoo
xxo xoo
xxx xoo
xoo xoo
xxx xoo
xxo xoo
xxx xoo
and you’re only moving U3 and U2, the lower hand holding an F# pedal tone in effect.

An important note is that with most Low Whistles high B won’t speak as well with L3 left on the tube. Of course Low Whistles weren’t around in the old days, when that partially open fingering system was developed and used.

I’ve had to re-learn my fingering for playing Low Whistles, putting down L4 as an anchor and leaving L3 off in the 2nd octave. (Only 2nd octave B is spoilt by having L3 down, the other notes work fine.)

Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

Oh, it is on a thread such as this that I could get myself into so much trouble because the three point grip which I mention here has NOTHING to do with what is considered a traditional Irish/Scottish flute grip. The three point grip I refer to here was perfected in the French Flute School of the later 19th century and it is the same grip as universally taught to flute students today, around the world.

1) Use a piper’s grip on pipes. It is not ideal for use on a transverse flute.

2) To keep a flute tucked into the hollow of the chin a player needs to learn an embouchure technique to accommodate that. And yes, that can be done but that embouchure technique is radically different than your typical Irish/Scottish flute embouchure. I wrote a thread about it, right here at The Session. Search for and go to, “Flute embouchure, part II”. However, I began that thread in mid-winter 2015 but then got caught up in exceptional winter weather, so I was not able to complete that thread until a few months later and so you will need to wander through a few months of the usual thread banter before getting to my concluding points. And, should you have any question(s) beyond that, I will be glad to discuss them right there on that thread.

3) Pushing against a flute with the side of the base of the upper hand index finger really needs no explanation. It is true that some players could eventually get somewhat of a reddish colored discoloration at that point but apparently of no medical consequence.

4) The lower hand thumb position does two jobs, along the length of a flute, from head to foot, and along the height of a flute, from the top to the bottom of its diameter, and here the thumb is tucked under the palm of the hand. The correct thumb position along the length of a flute balances a player’s wrist, ever so important, and the correct thumb position along the height of a flute balances the rotation of a flute. Yes, there will come a time when a player knows EXACTLY where to place the tip of their lower hand thumb.

Mark, are you telling us that you cannot play a Boehm flute? If your finger is as you say, the entire foot joint would be unplayable. You can get away with that in Irish music, but for little else.

BTW, I don’t have pictures, but my thumb placement is to place the right hand thumb facing straight out on the bottom of the flute beneath where the G and F# holes are. The left thumb is similarly placed facing the body below the B hole. The base of the left forefinger exerts just enough pressure to stabilize the flute, while the thumbs do all the work to sustain the weight and general position. The inbound edge of the embouchure hole should more or less meet the line between the skin of the face and the lower lip, only pressing hard enough to keep the embouchure hole steady, NOT to hold the flute. The thumbs do most of the work, with a small bit of help from the base of the left forefinger. It is how 99.9% of flute players hold their flutes. Use whatever variation of that works for you, but I struggle to understand why anyone would stray from that basic configuration.

Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

Good question, David. I’m not sure whether there’s a body of opinion that he was wrong or whether there are some who just don’t get it (or want to). It was recommended by Tromlitz (1791), Drouet (1830) and Rockstro (1891) among others. It’s now used by a number of Irish players, including the mighty Conal O’Grada, for whom the key thing is to keep the flute rock-steady while leaving all the digits except the right thumb free to play the instrument.
So, it’s already been said but in case it’s not clear: the base of the left index presses the flute against the chin or rather the lower lip as Ailin says. The RIGHT thumb rests on the SIDE of the flute and pushes it away from the player. (Fome’s earlier comment about moving the finger further up the side of the instrument is spot-on.) The left thumb is largely irrelevant, though it may rest lightly on the flute. The left hand does not grip at all as this would impede finger movement. The flute is suspended between the three pivot points.
I’m not saying this is the only way to hold a flute. I’m not using “should”. But it’s a great technique, which others here clearly use. I’m not interested in how I hold the flute when it’s not against my face cos I’m not playing it then.

I don’t know if my links to Grey Larsen’s book (in Google Books) are working in all locations, so I’ve copied some of his text below.

He has an extensive exercise, ‘Discovering your Normal Hold.’
https://books.google.no/books?id=HKuHKFCBD84C&pg=PA89#v=onepage&q&f=false

After the exercise he goes into more detail, including this;

An Alternate Anchor: Using the Bottom Thumb Instead of the Bottom Pinky

"There are players who prefer to anchor with the bottom thumb instead of the bottom pinky. This method was strongly advocated by Richard Shepherd Rockstro in his 1890 treatise. I do not feel that this provides me as solid and secure a hold on the ﬂute, but you may ﬁnd that it works better for you, especially if your hands or fingers are too small to permit your bottom pinky to comfortably rest on the ﬂute.

With this kind of hold the bottom thumb must provide a small amount of force that opposes the force of the base of T1. To accomplish this. you must place the thumb more on the side of the ﬂute nearest to your chest, not as much on the underside of the ﬂute. In this position the bottom thumb will be pushing the ﬂute slightly away from your body. For most players, it will work best to place the thumb on the side of the ﬂute between B1 and B2.

If you frequently use the bottom pinky to operate one or more keys, you may prefer this approach. Though the bottom pinky is no longer supplying a force opposing T1, some players who use this hold still touch the pinky to the ﬂute for further stabilization."

Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

The #1, first and foremost priority in gripping a flute is to have and to keep BOTH left and right wrists as straight as possible. Failure to do so is not inconsequential. Bent wrists are known to promote carpal tunnel syndrome which has lead to the demise of many, many flute players, ending their flute playing careers.

With straight wrists, therefore, the next thing is to position the fingers comfortably on the tone holes with the pads of the fingertips covering the tone holes. And of the three point grip, that begins with comfortably positioning the ring finger of the upper hand which for right handed players would be the L3 finger, while ALWAYS keeping that finger and all of the other fingers at least slightly bent.

Then comes the three point grip. A player has no choice about the location of the crook of their chin. The optimum positioning of the ring finger of a player’s upper hand determines the location of the side of the base of their upper hand index finger which then becomes a grip point. Then with the palm of the lower hand kept flat to allow the lower hand fingers to spread sideways and with the thumb tucked underneath the palm, the correct position of the tip of the lower hand thumb could then be determined.

Learn how to play a flute flat out horizontally before imitating Jethro Tull.

A flute player’s elbows could then each be at about the same distance from the floor. Yes, that happens!

Dory-Ann, hehehe! Oh, yes, on flute there very much are some differences between Baroque technique and Irish technique and a player trained in one of those techniques could quickly see the differences between those two techniques. And so yes, if a player wishes to be a “pure drop” Irish player then a Baroque specialist is the wrong source of information. However, Baroque and Irish musics do also have a fair amount of commonality, too, where, with the exception of much “folk” and “country” musics even of today, the Baroque was the last major music era to use Just Intonation tuning, the same system of tuning the Roman Catholic Church had insisted on hundreds of years before that. Just Intonation tuning is PERFECT for playing against a drone background and that same system of tuning is entirely native to early Irish and Scottish music, too. Both Baroque and Irish musics make extensive use of fingered ornamentation and neither of them use vibrato. And because the tuning of a flute is arguably the trickiest part of playing a flute, a Baroque specialist could be of some help to an Irish/Scottish player. Following the Baroque era just about all musics switched over to Equal Temperament tuning, Circle of Fifths, etc., and, while that could be of help to those Irish and Scottish players who play along with fixed pitch instruments such as piano, boxes of all sorts, fretted instruments and others, ET tuning is not right for playing against a drone background.

Glottal stops? Such abomination! They constrict the throat, a flute playing no-no!

Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

My thing, being an outsider, has always been to observe what “the real Irish guys” do, especially what “the old guys” did, and accept it at face value.

Watching videos of a number of Irish fluteplayers will show that there are a number of ways of holding the flute, and a number of ways of fingering the flute, which are used successfully.

This being the case I don’t think it does any good to have anyone claim that there’s only one correct way.

That being said, I do recognize that mainstream fluteplaying (called “classical” on these boards) has a far larger pool of players, a far stronger tradition of master-pupil relationships going unbroken back at least to the 18th century, and a much larger body of experience to draw from. This tradition has settled on certain ways of embouchure formation, certain ways of breathing, certain ways of holding the flute, and so on, not from arbitrary capriciousness (as one would think reading these boards) but by the very opposite of that, centuries of practical experience as to what works best for most people.

So when I was learning flute I learned from everybody, old Irish guys and Boehm flute teachers and Baroque flute teachers and all. It never hurts to learn more things.

One other thing, about L4 not reaching the flute, as was mentioned above if you seal the three lower-hand holes on the flute with the middle pads of L1, L2, and L3 you’ll find that L4 comes over nicely to the flute. This is, more or less, the so-called piper’s grip. Each finger has three fleshy pads, and the middle pad of the index, middle, and ring fingers will usually line up with the end-pad of the little finger. Pipers and fluteplayers of various traditions have been doing that for centuries. So if you feel a need to get the little finger involved and there’s no other way you can do that. Or, as mentioned above, you can arch the three longer fingers like some fluteplayers do. (As I noted on another thread Boehm fluteplayers don’t stab at the tops of the keys with the pointy tips of their fingers, but use the flats of the end-joint pads just as many Irish fluteplayers do. I posted a video of James Galway playing with the ring finger of the upper hand and the lower-hand fingers rather flat.)

Re: Flute: Using the right hand ring finger on notes B, A and C#

Fome, I have some good news for you. Yes, there are different kinds of flutes, some flutes being made of metal along with having lip plates and keys and a lot of key work mechanism, some flutes being made of wood and having an entirely different key work mechanism and some flutes having no keys at all. But believe it or not, they all have some things in common, too, and the best thing you could do is to find somebody who knows how to play a flute, any sort of a flute, and ask them to show you about playing your flute. They could show you basic things such as how to correctly hold a flute, how to correctly put your fingers on a flute and could give you some advise as to how to blow into a flute. But understand that by meeting one flute player, you also are meeting one member of an international community of flute players. Yes, flute players know other flute players and the first flute player you meet could introduce you to some of those other flute players. That would be your introduction to the flute playing community and, yes, that includes Irish flute players, too.
