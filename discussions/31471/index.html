Self taught!? ~ who’s kidding who?

“I’m self taught!”, usually dropped as a kind of badge of brag, always causes me to wince, quietly inside if not also deepening old wrinkles. But there is humour in it too, a chuckle, if at the expense of the deluded person making this claim. ‘Tradition’ is about learning from others, being inspired by them, and while as a child you may have picked up poker and tongs from the fireplace and pretended to play the fiddle, you had to see it happening and be inspired by it before you could mimic it.

It has always impressed me, and continues to, the long history behind the many wonderful musicians it has been my pleasure to know, and they never hesitated mentioning their influences and ‘sources’, including family, parents, grandparents, and beyond, visiting musicians, those they were influenced and guided by, who helped them on their road to learning. Yes, they too were necessary, their passion, their motivation, but THERE ARE ROOTS TO TRADITION - PEOPLE!!!

Sorry, I see I’m shouting, fully intentional. I love to see in the notes to recordings information about those roots, but a few of the newer ones, in our digital age, almost act as if their abilities and understanding were immaculate conception, they were the sole root and light, “ME! I did it!” Yes, alright, you put in the time, but the time stretches a lot further back than your limited allowable time with it all. It has sources, roots. That’s ‘tradition’.

So what has started this? Well, it bubbles away, but I yet again read someone saying they were ‘self-taught’, and it wasn’t the first time this very talented musician made a similar claim, giving credit to no one else except themselves. And then there was this little aside about first learning Irish music in school, and attending a local Comhaltas or other group to ‘learn’ more, taught and encouraged by many, and then, with all that, listening and copying what they heard and saw, from other musicians, including on recordings. Great, the results are good, but they come from all those sources, lucky you! And for those that teach, in my experience, we continue to learn in that process too, through the act of teaching others, from those that come to learn from us. I hope I never stop learning - from all possible sources.

Sorry, but the whole concept of being ‘self-taught’, as if it just happened, I find slightly rude and disrespectful, not crediting the sources and roots that brought us to where we are, however talented we might or might not be after all those initial inspirations, challenges, trials and initiations… Yes, credit too to those who have perceviered and come through with a skill, an understanding.

Come to think of it, I guess I have actually heard a few people that may have mostly been ‘self-taught’, evidenced in a result that was truly disconnected and dire. :-P

“How did you learn to play?” is the usual question.

You could reply: “I didn’t have any formal lessons, or any face-to-face tuition. Once I knew, from hearing some nameless old 78s, that I wanted to play this style of music, then I acquired an instrument and worked out for myself the mechanics of how I could make music on it. Along the way I listened to recordings and live appearances of various players, and went away and worked out how I could make some of the sounds they were making. I did this continuously, gradually incorporating different elements into my playing from all the sources I was listening to, to try and make an overall sound that was pleasing to me and, in my mind, respectful of the source material. I talked about music with a lot of the musicians I met. If you asked me who each of these musicians and sources were, I couldn’t possibly remember them all, and some of them I couldn’t name anyway: and it would be unfair to name just a few. All along, though, I have made my own choices about the elements I would like to try to incorporate into my playing, and I have worked out for myself, in private, how best to transfer those elements to the instrument as I play it. This process will continue for always, so I am still learning.”

Or you could say: “I’m self-taught.”

I don’t understand what has provoked this. Trained musicians may be very ready to acknowledge their teacher, especially if it’s a chance to name-drop a famous musician, but they’re no more (or less) likely to acknowledge their wider influences than those who are self-taught. Besides, they’re really two different things. A teacher may impart technique and knowledge about the music which the self-taught must work out for themselves, but in either case true understanding of the music only comes from extensive listening which you can only do for yourself.

The advantage of being self-taught (perhaps the only advantage) is that it requires you to listen more closely, to discover exactly what is going on and to work out how to replicate it. This should lead to a greater understanding than someone who does not have to listen with the same degree of attention.

Many of the comments here resonate with my own experience.

At one time, I would have acknowledged a teacher but also said that I was largely self-taught. When I had started learning Irish traditional fiddling in earnest, I was going to college in a place without a strong Irish trad scene 800-ish miles from home, where my teacher (who’s a wonderful fiddler) lives. I would take a few lessons and record a bunch of tunes when I was home on summer and winter breaks, but most of my learning took place at school, where I would spend hours slowing down and listening to recordings of my teacher and others, learning notes, rhythm, phrasing, etc. and attempting to figure out bowings and ornamentation largely on my own with some suggestions from this site.

It’s been a while since I’ve taken formal lessons from this musician, and my style has diverged significantly from his since then, but I still credit him as being my first real fiddle teacher and being a very formative influence for me (which seems to surprise people, I suppose because of stylistic differences).

I think if you were to ask me how I learned to play, I might cite earlier classical training (although I don’t really play like it, having discarded most of the technique save for my bow hand), but I would definitely mention lessons, especially those early ones which taught me the basic musical vocabulary of Irish trad, and highly emphasize spending a lot of time listening, especially sitting with, slowing down, analyzing, and learning from recordings of master musicians like Denis Murphy, Micheal Gorman, or Jackie Daly. I think extensive casual listening, this type of deep listening, and the learning that flows from them are the most important influences on my playing/ways of learning for me. Not self-taught, per se, but perhaps a somewhat (even then, only somewhat!) self-directed disciple of far better bearers of tradition.

I know and well understand, personal experiences, the hard work and dedication necessary to acquire any skill, and I have admiration and respect for those that put the time and effort in. Maybe it’s about the differences in mind set between ~

“I’m going to learn how to ~ ” & “I’m going to teach myself how to ~ ”
~ and how one defines or thinks about ‘learning’ & ‘teaching’. For me the ‘teaching’ is from external sources, whatever they are, and the ‘learning’ is the efforts I or you put into acquiring new understanding or skill.

In the past the ‘apprenticeship’ could be taken for granted, and it could be immediate family, or neighbours and friends, or travelling masters like the Dohertys of Donegal - and many, many more. And the house gatherings and dances, more immediate opportunities for it to sink in and take hold of us, inspire us, and teach us, and not just the mechanics, the heart and soul of the tradition as well, the humours and spirit. Then we have school - such as whistle classes starting in primary school, and organizations like Comhaltas, various pipers’ clubs, etc. The education involved, the teaching and learning, can so easily be taken for granted, especially when it is all around you.

More and more nowadays tradition can be less immediate, less direct, less like a natural apprenticeship. (see, for example, “The Bank of Turf”) It might be just workshops and/or classes, short or long term, if you’re lucky to have such options. It can also be that you haven’t and your only choices are various media - lesson books, and better there if it includes something for the ears as well, DVDs, YouTube. There are so many choices out there for the ears and the eyes if you’ve got access online to new media, like YouTube, or online lessons, a growing choice and offered by some great players and teachers too. However, through these less direct options it becomes more and more disconnected.

Motivations also vary, our reasons for putting in the work, less a natural part of our birth to death experiences. For some it’s competition and medal winning, for others it’s to join a session, to be part of the craic in a local pub. A great influence on all this might be a modern change of focus more toward the individual rather than family and traditions, or that larger family - community? And maybe by that there’s more of a tendancy towards saying “I’m self taught!”

To repeat, I can understand that pride in hard work and accomplishment, however connected or disconnected it might be from the wider ‘tradition’, that it might be natural that things have become less faceted, more centered on the self. But, even if much of the work is justly credited to the individual, ‘self-taught’, it doesn’t happen without outside influences and sources, the material we learn from, that helps us, that teaches us, that we learn from, those connections to ‘tradition’.

How well someone plays is dependent on several things. Who spent the hours gaining the techniques? Who observed and drew conclusions? Who gained the experience from playing out and who developed and used the memories?

Believe me, in the Highland piping world, where so much weight is put on who your teacher is, and who your teacher’s teacher was, being self-taught is nothing to brag about!

A story will illustrate:

The young Robert (Reid) and his brothers were all pipers but Robert showed the most promise. He was taught by his father until the age of twelve when be became a pupil of John MacDougall Gillies, who was teaching many of the top players at that time. Roberts association with Gillies was to last 18 years until Gillies died in 1925. Robert began competing after becoming a pupil of Gillies and in 1911 won 3rd prize in piobaireachd at the Argyllshire Gathering.

While preparing to play at the Northern Meeting one year Robert saw that an old man supporting himself with a walking stick was listening to him, and when Robert stopped playing the old man came forward and said “you’re playing that second bar of The Earl of Seaforth wrongly.” Robert replied “Oh, you think so” and the old man said “Who teaches you?” Robert told him it was John MacDougall Gillies and got the reply “Well, when you get back to Glasgow tell him he’s playing it wrongly.” So Robert said “Who are you to tell Gillies how to play?” The old man answered “I’m Sandy Cameron, I taught him.”
