Hi guys,

Your information that the first and second palce winner in each regional Fleadh qualify to compete in the All-Ireland. There are two regional qualifying fleadhanna in the US. For the Californian, yours would be the Midwest Fleadh, which will be held on May 7 in St. Louis. Here’s their website:

http://www.slia.org/Fleadh/index.htm

Online registration isn’t up yet, but probably will be before too long. Be sure to click on the links for “Competitions” and “Rules” and read all that stuff carefully.

The other one is the Mid-Atlantic Regional Fleadh, usually held in June. Last year it was in Peal River, New York. I don’t think they have the website up for this year’s competition, but you can look at last year’s to get an idea:

http://www.ccenorthamerica.org/Sites/Fleadh/MidAtlanticFleadh/index.html

It’s worth pointing out that this is the qualifying competition for the All-Ireland, so the adjudicators are supposed to judge against a standard, not just rank the competitors relative to each other. If you’re the only one in a given competition, you don’t automatically get to win first; if the judge doesn’t feel you’re ready for the All-Ireland, they can give you third. To a competitor (or parent) who’s thinking of it as just another amateur music competition, this can come as quite a shock.

As competitions go, though, it’s not a stressful or intimidating atmosphere. Some random facts:

A “big” competition might have 10-20 people, not close to 200 like the Oireachtas, and the size of the rooms and the audience that they can hold reflects this.

You don’t play into a microphone, you don’t have to announce what you’re going to play or even know the names, and the audience and adjudicators are generally quite supportive and encouraging.

The 15-18 competitions are generally more competitive and have a higher standard than the over 18s; certain solo competitions, mostly 15-18 and some 12-15, offer scholarships to Scoil Eigse to first and second place winners.

You can buy the adjudicator’s written comments for about $1.00 and have them sent to you; you can’t see them on the day of the fleadh. These are often helpful, especially if you’re lucky enough to have a judge that writes a lot.

The fleadhanna are run by CCE and adhere to a fairly conservative interpretation ITM. Technique and command of the instrument are supposed to count for about 20% of the marks, whereas traditional style is about 50%. Individual adjudicators will have a lot of different takes on this, but really flashy or new-agey stuff is to be avoided. Reproducing a performance directly off a cd is also frowned on.

Original compositions are allowed, provided they’re in a fairly strict traditional style; so are crossover (Scottish or Cape Breton) tunes, if played in an Irish style and generally accepted in the Irish tradition. If a judge feels your choice is unacceptable, they’ll simply ask if you can play something appropriate instead and if you can, will judge only that and your original selection will not count against you.

Recalls are allowed and are not uncommon. In this case, you’re most commonly asked to play one of your tunes again; you could also be asked for a different tune from a specific category, especially in the 15-18 or senior grades, but this is much more rare.

The main thing, if you want to do it and are new to it, is to read the rules and prepare thoroughly; make sure you have at least one recall tune prepared as well. But most importantly, have fun! The fleadh is a social occasion as much as a competitive one, and there are often great sessions that go on all night after the competitions have finished. There are sessions on Friday as well before the competitions, but Saturday is really the big night, and plenty of people seem to come just for that.

Re: How to compete in Fleadhs??

Unfortunately, there’s no real written out “standard,” and only very limited efforts have been made by CCE to promote a uniform approach to determining what constitutes an acceptable standard among adjudicators. I described this sort of thing in detail in another thread awhile back; rather than say it all again, if you’re interested, click here and scroll down (it’s the really long post in the middle):

https://thesession.org/discussions/5353/

As far as what you should work towards, to qualify, you should ideally be at a stage where you could hold your own at the All-Ireland in your age group. For under 12 and 12-15, this basically means that at minimum you have no problems getting around on your instrument technically, have solid rhythm, and are comfortable with basic ornamentation used tastefully. You are not expected to play slow airs in any of the under 18 grades, but it often pays off big time if you can perform them well, since few people attempt them. In 15-18 and over 18, you should have not only all the basics under control, but be very fluent with ornamentation and be able to put a distinct personal stamp on a tune. A slow air is required in the solo fiddle, flute, whistle, and pipes competitions as well; most commonly the air of a sean nos song is played, and you are expected to demonstrate that you know how to phrase it as a singer would.

I thought it might help to mention some of the biggest pitfalls that competitors tend to fall into:

1.) They (or their teacher) picks something too hard for them. It is much, much better to pick something simple that you can play very comfortably and well than something harder that is barely within your reach. I’ve seen quite a number of competitions where an intermediate-level player with a very simple arrangement played with flawless rhythm and a confident personality beat significantly more advanced players who tried to play flashy showpieces at lightning tempos. The #1 thing judges don’t want to hear is someone who doesn’t sound completely comfortable and in control, particularly of their tempo.

2.) Corollary to #1: It’s not about the speed, it’s about the rhythm and the liveliness. Trying to be impressive by playing really, really fast almost always backfires.

3.) Don’t rip off stuff from albums, stuff that you know somebody won the All-Ireland with, or tunes that are overplayed showpieces or are enjoying a current “fad” on your instrument. There are a number of players who do this, and often they get penalized for it, although it is probably considered a worse offense in the All-Ireland than in the US fleadhanna. Again, it’s up to the individual judge to decide, and some judges have a pet peeve about this.

4.) Traditional style counts for significantly more than command of the instrument. Most judges would never qualify someone who is obviously a great classical player but isn’t a great ITM player. Most would also not qualify someone who is essentially a beginning or not technically comfortable player but who clearly had a decent understanding of traditional style; however, if these two theoretical players were competing for third place, CCE would expect the judge to give it to the person with a better understanding of ITM. I’ve seen this happen a couple of times, and the classical player is invariably shocked and upset. So it’s something to be prepared for.

If you have any specific questions about your instrument or your particular situation, post back, or email me, and I’ll do my best to help you. Good luck!

Re: How to compete in Fleadhs??

Good stuff, compaqjohn!

Really good point about everything you play being up to the same standard. To win in the All-Ireland at 15-18 or senior level, you really should be at the point where you have your own repertoire and own style and are very comfortable performing. I forgot to mention before that if you’re worried about nerves and playing in front of a silent audience focused on you, you should play your competition program through in front of an audience. Not just once, but multiple times, five, six, seven times.

In the regionals in the US at least, you will basically never have anyone asking for the history or origin of a tune. Most judges will probably ask the name, but if you don’t know it they will almost never count it against you (and they are not supposed to.) In fact, they are not supposed to be allowed to count it against you at the All-Ireland either, but it is probably more likely to happen there than here, and if it does, there’s really nothing you can do. It’s really not that hard to know something about what you’re playing, though, so do it. Incidentally, they are also not supposed to count it against you if you are not a member of CCE, but it’s so inexpensive that there’s no reason not to just join. When you register for the fleadh, they will give you the option to join, if you haven’t already. It has to be renewed every year, and most people probably just do it when they register for the fleadh. The way it’s set up now, you will be listed as a member of whatever the host branch is, unless you specify otherwise.

It seems to depend on what instrument you’re competing on whether the TV and radio people are interested enough. In senior fiddle you’re definitely dealing with a mike in your face (actually, more like slightly above your head), usually on a stage in some kind of auditorium, but realistically, anyone who’s got a shot at winning it is unlikely to be inexperienced with those kind of factors. You won’t see this at all in the Midwest, at least, and I’m guessing not in the Mid-Atlantic, but I can’t say for sure about that one.

You can’t please every judge. A lot of competitors seem to get caught up in trying to figure out “how not to lose.” I think that can make the whole experience of competing counterproductive. Assuming that a competitor is more than competent at playing in a good traditional style, individuality and spontaneity are what will make you stand out. We’ve tried to point out a lot of mistakes to avoid, but assuming you have all that under control, focus on playing great music. If you’re not enjoying it, nobody else will be either!
