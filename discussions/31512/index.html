Another Rule for Backers - The Last Chord

Something of a follow-on from hnorbeck’s “Rules for Backers” thread …
https://thesession.org/discussions/31478

Assuming that a tune ends on its keynote (and I appreciate that not all tunes do):

1) In the case of major and Mixolydian mode tunes, the final chord played should always be the MAJOR chord pertaining to the tune’s keynote (tonal centre).

2) In the case of minor or Dorian mode tunes, the final chord played should always be the MINOR chord pertaining to the tune’s keynote (tonal centre).

In this particular context, chord substitutions aren’t applicable (although they may well be suitable in other parts of the tune).

I’ve encountered many guitar backers at sessions who don’t seem to understand this.

At a recent session the last tune in a set of reels was the “Maid Behind the Bar” which the guitarist finished with a B-Minor chord. Straightaway afterwards, I asked him what his final chord was. His answer? “I don’t know. How do you expect me to remember that?”

And I’ve also encountered guitarists who seem to think that a tune can only be major or minor, and (for example) always finish off an A-Mix tune with an A-Minor chord. That really jars my ears as well! :-(

Re: Another Rule for Backers - The Last Chord

Okay, maybe we could boil it down to these three more pithy rules:

1. Don’t confuse minor chords with major chords.
2. And vice versa.
3. The resolving end note is never the third of the tonal centre. For instance, when a tune ends on a D note, the corresponding resolving chord is Emaj7, of course.

Re: listen where the tunes are going w/o rules

I second these thoughts, Solidmahog and DonaldK (uhm, somehow blended together…). Let me elaborate with a somewhat extreme example, which has nothing to do with Irish trad at first glance….

When I learned classical guitar years ago, my teacher would of course never teach any plain disjointed chords, or how to “back” a classical (polyphonic) guitar piece (huh?!). But he used to ask me what parts of it were “lead” melody, and which were accompaniment (which sometimes is obvious, for example when you only have a bass line played with the thumb, sometimes not so much, when the melody is woven into arpeggios, harmonies and the like). So over time you learn what suits the melody, paying attention where the melody goes etc., without even sparing a thought about the theory behind it. Admittedly, you don’t improvise very much*, but you get a feeling for it. I found myself playing flubs which were harmonically absolutely suitable, because I just got certain progressions in my fingers (for instance, when I lost the thread in the sheet music). Well, at least when playing more simple stuff without much modulation and the like.

So I think you shouldn’t necessarily learn the rules (which doesn’t hurt, of course), as long as you learn how to play (and listen).

*Nowadays, at least… before the 20th century improvising was essential in “classical” music. Especially regarding backing, just think of this basso continuo thing.

Re: Another Rule for Backers - The Last Chord

I don’t know where tis is going, but it might be time to get a reality check (my way of saying I’m going to state my opinion).

There are no rules to being a “backer” in a session. Any “rules” in a session are made by those involved in the session. If the existing players in the session don’t want a “backer” - or don’t want more than one (or however many are actually playing in the session), then this is their prerogative. If they don’t mind a “backer” joining in the session then that’s sorted. The rest is down to etiquette and how the “backer” is perceived by the other players. That perception is governed by the skill of the “backer”, and it’s not learned from a hard and fast “rule book”.

Yes, there are various thoughts on how any particular tune can be accompanied, and there is much in the way of both consensus and disagreement. There is much in the way of “theory” that can be adhered to, or not. There are no rules saying any particular “theory” should be followed.
Even if a recognised theory is followed, it doesn’t always apply to the type of music being played. The “rules” may be “rules of thumb” rather than hard and fast.
Some “backers” are pretty adept at picking up a tune while it is actually being played, and make a pretty neat job of “backing” it, and some just can’t get the hang of it, with all points between these extremes in evidence as well. Much like there are melody players who pick up a tune on first hearing - or after the first time round in a session, and others who always struggle.

However, the notion that a “backer” doesn’t need to know the tune, whereas a melody player does, thereby lifting a “backer” to a more “privileged” position than a melody player (can walk into a session without knowing the tunes, unlike the melody players, who would be out on their airse) has to rate very highly on the tosh scale.

I like the reference to Messrs Edey, Watson and Carr (you could add several others to the list the late Peerie Willie Johnson is a fine example).
It would be interesting to hear how they feel about “knowing” the tunes that they back.
There’s a difference here between “knowing” a tune and being able to play the melody on the backing instrument. I’m sure that these players have their own “rules of thumb” that they follow to a certain extent, and also break away from frequently.

As I’ve said elsewhere, it’s all down to mutual respect between the melody players and accompanists. It’s down to using your lugs, and *understanding* the tunes (which is a lot of what “knowing” the tunes is about).
Any rules of thumb that you “acquire” along the road will be reflected in the reception you receive next time you join that session (if there is a next time!).

All these “rules” discussed here are certainly food for thought, and it is probably a good thing that they are discussed here. “Tips” that may be of use to “backers”. I think it might be wise to get to know those tunes though.

I put “backers” in quotation marks because it is not a word I would choose. Some “backers” are “crackers” and some are “hackers”, and that’s just the better ones.

I think we overthink backing way too much. For many guitar players, it’s a matter of unlearning the same cycle of 5ths thinking that serves them well in jazz and classical and composition contexts and just accepting laying down a carpet for a melody line. Once they embrace that idea, and they do quite a bit of listening, it comes pretty quickly, I think. You can play this music well if you play simply. Zouk players seem to be more successful at it than guitar players - even though so many of these same zoukers start on guitar. Maybe transferring to zouk helps with the unlearning process, or maybe those who have switched to zouk have already been successful in unlearning.

The best way, I think, for a guitar player with some skills already but who is new to Irish music is to sit next to a skilled, experienced backer, and watch and learn, and accept coaching - and maybe play very little at first. But frequently there is no skilled backer in any given community, or there’s no path in place to help a backer come to Irish music from other styles. Most of us who play melody instruments learned gradually from other melody players, and sitting near or across from experienced melody players who were themselves role models. We short-circuit that process for backers, though, and then we wonder why in cities with hundreds of perfectly capable guitarists, why it’s so hard to find someone who can back appropriately for trad!
