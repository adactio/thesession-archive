“I believe people usually use the expression ”folk music“ rather than ”traditional music“ in Scotland. ”

Slainte, you believe wrong :-) but that’s another contentious issue in Scotland too.

It really depends…… Many folk clubs and festivals feature traditional acts and musicians, therefore it is often referred to as part of “folk” music. However, most players of the music would refer to it as traditional or trad…..again often depending on who they are or the circumstances.

However, you’ll also get these very “strict” players of the music who are mostly to be found on the Fiddle and accordion , strathspey and reel, competition scenes who refer to their music…often NE style…. as traditional. It is expected to be played in a particular way and they will often “contemptuously” refer to that which doesn’t neatly fit into their world as “folk fiddling”. This would include much of which is performed by younger, more innovative players, and that which you might hear within sessions.

Of course, I’m generalising a bit….

Which is better? That’s probably one of the more ridiculous questions posted on this board. I’ll ignore it and move on.

“sounds more classical”

There was a trend, in the 18th Century, I believe, to ‘classicalise’ Highland fiddle playing, with features such as rubato and vibrato being introduced, displacing an earlier, more rhythm-centred style (said to be much like the present day Cape Breton style). But I wouldn’t say that Highland piping sounds in any way ‘classical’ (unless you’re talking about Piobhaireachd, which is the ‘classical’ music of the pipes - but a completely separate and dissimilar classical tradition, of course, from the ‘Classical’ [with a big C] music of Beethoven and Mozart) - neither does Jimmy Shand.

Ah, Northumbrian trad! Like the Reivers, English when it will, and Scottish when it chooses; and both, and neither.

I was told recently how the postwar trad revival in Northumberland, and the addition of a lot of Scottish tunes to the repertoire, owed much to Jimmy Shand and his band in the 50s/60s. When invited to Northumberland, they tailored their music to Northumbrian dances, tempos, etc (rants, rather fast waltzes, etc.), while other bands from Scotland didn’t or couldn’t do this. So Shand got invited back again and again, while the others didn’t.
