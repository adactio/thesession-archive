Fiddlers, do you aim for an Irish regional style?

A recent thread has thrown up the question of Irish regional styles and whether they are still followed, or whether today’s fiddlers follow a general ‘Irish’ style, or the Sligo style and so on.

Michael Coleman, the famous Sligo fiddler, had a huge influence through his recordings. I am quoting from the entry on Michael Coleman in the site ‘Fiddling round the World’:

So having so generously listed all the failings of these recordings, how are we to explain the reverence with which they were, and still are, treated? What shines through it all is that Coleman was an outstanding player, full of boundless energy and inventiveness. One of the key differences between a run-of the mill session player and a soloist is that the latter will decorate, embellish and vary his performance in a different way every time he goes round the tune. Coleman is at his most flamboyant on reels and hornpipes, throwing in bowed triplets ( a strong feature of the Sligo style) and cascading triplet runs. His reels are all played with a strong swing, and even his polkas are swung. It is difficult to say how much of the influence of his recordings was simply that they were among the first of their type to be heard, and how much is down to the quality of his playing. Certainly the effect in Ireland was dramatic, and in no time at all he was the one that virtually every fiddler wanted to imitate. To quote one of Coleman’s relatives, Johnny Giblin: “He immortalized tunes that weren’t worth playing. Take for instance The Boys of the Lough. I remember people playing that and it was a very ordinary, plain tune. But Coleman made it a great tune. He set a headline for tunes so that no one could ever go back to the originals”

‘It is often stated that, such was the extent to which he was copied and imitated, that Coleman destroyed the regional styles of Ireland; up until that point there had been fairly distinct geographical differences throughout Ireland in repertoire, bowing and ornamentation. As soon as everyone started copying Coleman’s Sligo style, regional variation was on an obvious downward spiral. However, you could equally well argue that the music might well have perished from lack of interest had he not galvanized fiddlers in the way he did, so that the erosion of regional differences would have been an academic point anyway. ’

From the way the second quoted paragraph was set up, I expected the last sentence to dispute the fact that Irish regional styles have been weakened by Coleman’s Sligo dominance - but in fact, it seems to take this weakening for granted.

But even if regional styles *have* been weakened or altered in the direction of Sligo style, they are still alive.
I enjoy the Donegal style because of its closeness to Scottish fiddling, but I am nowhere near good enough to adopt a ‘style’ - I’m happy for now if I can play the notes. :)

What about you? Have you got beyond learning tunes & now aim to play in a regional tradition - or what?
It would be lovely to hear about your experience.

Re: Fiddlers, do you aim for an Irish regional style?

The article you quoted, Fiddle Aunt, repeats one of the great myths of Irish music, namely that there were enough homes in Ireland equipped with gramophones that, once the 78s recorded by Coleman, Morrison, etc., sent home by relatives in the USA, arrived in the Auld Sod, that the shape of the Irish fiddle tradition was instantly reformed. This is just simply not the case.

The vast majority of Irish households could not afford the price of a gramophone at that time and, secondly, I’ve searched long and hard over the last twenty years, but I’ve never found any evidence that Irish migrants sent home large quantities of 78s.

Consider the practicalities. Shellac 78s are notoriously fragile. If migrants sent these home, how did they package them to ensure their safe arrival and, more importantly when money was tight, why would they do so in the first place?

However. what is certainly true is that the introduction of 2RN in the 1920s (the forerunner of Radio Athlone, which subsequently became Radio Éireann in 1938) did play 78s recorded by Irish musicians in Dublin, London and the USA. However, there were many areas, particularly along the western seaboard, which actually did not become connected to the Irish electricity grid until the late 1940s and, in some cases, even until the mid- to late-1950s. This meant that many outlying areas, especially the west of counties Cork and Kerry and parts of Donegal, did not have radio reception until 1956 at the earliest.

A far more likely means of transmission of ways of playing tunes from one area to another was the development of the Irish rail network which meant that someone, say, in Letterkenny (and on the look-out for work anywhere along the west coast or inland) could take her/his flute or fiddle or whatever and find fellow musicians in whichever townland, town or city they ended up.

Additionally, there are no ‘regional styles’. Said entities are only perceived by people who’ve never actually got close to the music at a local level.

It’s much more valid to think about strong local players (as Caoimhín MacAoidh does in ‘Between the Jigs and the Reels’) and how their influence might have spread or, additionally, consider how influential Irish Travellers (the Dorans, the Dohertys, etc.) may have been.

“bowing technique didn’t suddenly change when you crossed a county border” -

In the nineteenth century, James Murray (who compiled the Oxford English Dictionary) was a schoolteacher on the English/Scottish border. He did research into his pupils’ language and found that there was *no* difference between the ones that came from the English side and the ones from Scotland. All the same, there is a difference between the dialect of English and the accent spoken in, say, County Durham, and the dialect and accent used in, say, Edinburgh. So bowing style could still vary according to whether you fiddle in Galway or Cork. And probably it used to. But it may not now. :)

>>“bowing technique didn’t suddenly change when you crossed a county border” Are you sure?

I suppose it’s possible that one fiddler played completely differently to his neighbour, just because there happened to be a county border between their houses, but I think it’s unlikely.

>>“Passing fashions are a relatively recent phenomenon, and while itinerant musicians may well have aroused interest, it is hard to see how they would have altered the style of an area (if such existed).”

Fashion isn’t a new phenomenon, there have been fashions in art, music and dress for as long as there have been people.

A passing musician plays something you like (or that the local audience likes), you copy it. Simple. Musicians back then weren’t trying to preserve anything (unlike some trad players today), they were simply entertainers, playing whatever they thought their audience would like. So their music would have been constantly evolving and following fashion, just as today’s pop music does.

It is unfortunate that we only have a snapshot of what happened in the past, based on a few recordings and survivors. So we tend to build a static picture based on that snapshot. In reality there is no reason to suppose that music in the past was any less dynamic an changing than it is today. Musicians get bored playing the same old same old, and are constantly looking for new material to entertain their audience. What’s more, the snapshot we have tends to be of the best players - that ones that got recorded or noted. Their playing is probably as representative of the general music of the area as the supergroups today are of the average pub bands.

Re: Fiddlers, do you aim for an Irish regional style?

I would say that I tend to gravitate to the Clare style of concertina playing. Although this may seem a bit redundant (after all, the concertina was all but abandoned in other parts of the country) I do really mean it. I have had lessons from musicians that come from all over Ireland--and listened to their CD’s too--but I seem to be stuck in Clare.

I know that I probably don’t sound the least bit Clare, I probably sound a bit of “modern” Chicago with some spin of my own. However, I do very strongly aim for the Clare style. More specifically, I try for the East Clare style with a bit of the West Clare thrown in. This may sound crazy to those who don’t believe in regional styles, but even today if you spend a few day playing in West Clare you will feel a difference in the pulse and rhythm played there. It is this rhythm that I try to grab. I also try to throw in a bit of the West Clare legato while not having this be dominant in my playing.

Yes, these two concertina styles may come from local players, but I really do believe that it reflects the other players (of many instruments) that they learned from and played with. I think it is hard to not sound like yourself. I grew up with electricity, listening to Irish music on one hand and 90’s pop on the other. I never farmed a day in my life and didn’t dance my first ceili until I was in my twenties. How could I ever sound like east or west Clare if I am so far removed?

Nevertheless, I still actively try to sound like that regional style in my playing. In my head I call it the trans-Clare style. I love many concertina players and have enjoyed learning from them, but the rhythms, fingerings, and phrasings they use always get reinterpreted by me into what I learned in and from Clare.

Re: Fiddlers, do you aim for an Irish regional style?

Gam,

I don’t quite see the distinction - all fashions pass. It might take a hundred years, or just one season, but they all pass, that’s what makes them ‘fashions’.

But fashion works at a number of levels. If you look at clothing, you get general fashions, like hemlines or the flare of trousers, which are picked up by pretty well everyone in the country. You also get localized fashions or styles - the women of Bristol dress slightly differently to the women of Glasgow (and very differently to the women of Newcastle!). But by far the biggest difference in how people dress is completely personal. Two women living in the same street might dress completely differently, but both still within to the local and national fashions of the time. You couldn’t point to one woman and say “that is how the women of Glasgow dress”.

And that is how I see it with fiddling: yes, there are undoubtedly sweeping generalizations that can be made about how people play in one region as opposed to another, but they are far, far smaller than the differences between individual players within the same region. You can’t point to an individual and say “that is Sligo fiddling”, any more than you can point to a woman and say “that is how they dress in Glasgow”. So to me, for anyone to set out to deliberately learn to play in a particular ‘regional style’ is ludicrous. Whatever you listen to and like will influence your style, but it is the influence of individual fiddlers, not regions.

Re: Fiddlers, do you aim for an Irish regional style?

I tend to agree that ‘regional styles’ is an overblown idea. Certainly there was a time when the impracticality of travel tended to make certain types of playing more localized, but to call it a regional style is a bit of an exaggeration. I refer to no less an expert than Martin Hayes, who claims that the idea of a east Clare style was one the locals embraced, because it gave them a sense of being special. But, even in east Clare, they didn’t really believe there was such a thing as an east Clare style. There were many musicians who, within the same area, played in different styles, and it made no sense to call one of them the ‘correct’ regional style. It is more a matter of playing like one person or another person. As folks travel more and recordings have become more available, there are fewer limits on whom you can imitate or learn from — and so a regional style is a nice concept that is more clever than true.

I do think the choice of repertoire varies from locale to locale — and this is somewhat true even today
