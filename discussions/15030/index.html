To all you fiddlers out there.

Re: Fiddle comfort question.

About 7 years ago when I started the fiddle (I come from classical cello) I used a shoulder rest and a chin rest, same as everyone else. The problem was that I felt rigid and not able to relax. Then I went to the Scoil Eigse at Listowel in 2001 and noticed that several good players, including some eminent tutors, didn’t use shoulder rests. When I got back home I tried playing without it and immediately felt the freedom I had been missing, and haven’t used a shoulder rest since (God! this feels like I’m writing a dreadful tv ad for some even more dreadful kitchen or toiletry product!) The point I’m making is that the cello has no artificial support, except for the tail-pin, and the player is remarkably free to move around and to alter the angle of the instrument as he feels. The absence of a shoulder rest gives the fiddle player more of that freedom. It is worth noting that the mechanical shoulder rest is a recent innovation - it came into general use about the middle of the 20th century, and before then if players used anything at all it would be no more than a pad or piece of cloth between the fiddle and the collar bone.
Without a shoulder rest the fiddle should rest naturally on the collar bone, and you can angle it in the horizontal and vertical planes pretty well very much as you wish. Without a shoulder rest there is no problem in going up and down the fingerboard if I need to; a slight pressure on the chin rest is all that is necessary to stabilise the instrument when coming down from a higher position.
In ITM the chin rest isn’t as necessary as you might think (in the original O’Neil’s “1001” see the photos of the musucians). In fact, playing without one is comfortable and relaxing - the trick is to have the tail-piece to the left of your jaw, then the instrument can’t possibly slide down to your right. The chin barely, if at all, touches the belly of the fiddle. If you go up the fingerboard, all you need to do is to lightly grip the belly of the instrument or the tailpiece with your chin for as long as is necessary. Of course, in ITM, you rarely, if ever, need to play out of the first position.

I presume you were joking, uileanified. If the bow arm hurts at all, you’re doing it wrong. The bow arm should be relaxed and ‘easy’. There should be no pressure on the bow arm - it’s the strings that hold the bow up, not you. You just draw the bow across them.

One thing that beginners often do which causes a lot of pain and discomfort - AND means you can’t possibly play the thing - is to have their left wrist flattened against the bridge of the fiddle. The left wrist should be absolutely straight in line with the arm, with the fingers curled in a relaxed fashion above the strings.

Re: Fiddle comfort question.

Wouldn’t recommend putting the chin on the “chinrest”. Think of it more as a cheek or jaw rest. You won’t really want the chiropractor bills later.

Bowing shouldn’t hurt.

I’ve not answered this thread until this point because the tools one can use to make a fiddle comfortable are so personal. I use a really standard Kun rest on my fiddle, and a Peter Mach shoulder rest for the viola - a lot of people hate that rest on my viola, but it’s more comfortable for me to have the viola supported by the collarbone. Best thing to do is to examine your own body - if your neck is really long, you’re probably going to want something that makes extra space, either a shoulder rest or a higher chinrest. If you feel a lot of pulling on your jaw, your shoulder rest is too high. If you have pain at the base of your neck in back, or feel you need to raise your shoulders, you have too much space. If you feel like the fiddle’s slipping or you have to “pinch” it with your jaw, you need more space (extra rest, something). If your frame is smaller, it’s more likely a shoulder rest will suit you. If you have no neck, best not use anything ;-) People will use anything from nothing to a small cloth for traction to a 5" tall chinrest + shoulder rest…

Re: Fiddle comfort question.

Bowing never hurt me in the beginning. Neither did strumming guitar or mandolin. In fact, the only thing that ever hurt at all on my picking/strumming/bowing arm was wearing a thumb pick when I played 5 string banjo. I have big thumbs--even the largest picks were too tight.

But bowing should *not* hurt. It’s far too relaxed and gentle a motion. The one time I did get pain in my bow arm was when I tore rotator cuff muscles (doing something else) in that shoulder. Until it healed, it hurt to go up onto the D and G strings. In the long run, bowing fiddle was part of my physical therapy for the injury.

At worst, your bow arm might get tired during a long, loud session, if you lean on the stick to hear yourself. But that’s not a good appraoch to routinely playing anyway. Find a quieter session. Or settle with not being able to hear yourself.

If bowing is painful, you’re either tensing the arm (totally unecessary), grinding the bow into the strings (try taking the mute off the fiddle), flailing too energetically (dramatic, but inneffective for good tone production), using some motion or posture that isn’t natural (again, not necessary, but playing behind your head always wows the punters), or you have an injury or some other physiological issue that otherwise innocent bowing motions are aggravating. I’d get to a teacher or doctor straight away….
