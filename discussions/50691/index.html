I don’t think i’m interested in Bluegrass.

When I first started learning Irish folk music on the fiddle so many years ago, I initially thought it would be a solid foundation for when I would inevitably transition to Bluegrass music. Myself being from Kentucky, that was the assumption. In hindsight though, that was such a strange assumption for me to make, because I had no relationship with Bluegrass music, other than it being considered the folk music of my region. I didn’t grow up listening to Bluegrass music. My family was into popular culture, the closest thing to folk music for us would’ve been the gospel sung at church, which was hardly ever old traditional spiritual music(basically, a lot of that music was modern/popular too, it was just spiritual). My piano teachers didn’t expose me to Bluegrass music, and by the time I reached the age of reason, I chose all of my own learning material, which was New Age and Modern Classical music. Outside of piano lessons, I was learning love songs and popular standards. And of course there was no Bluegrass music in my 7-years of choir through grade school.

All in all, as romantic as it sounds, I just don’t appear to have a genuine affinity for Bluegrass music. With myself doubling down on my relationship with Irish traditional music, and being more interested in exloring my piano repertoire of popular standards on the violin, Bluegrass has become something of a non entity to me. I like Donegal music. I like reels, slip-jigs, polkas, AND HIGHLANDS. I like the pentatonic scale, and the highland pipe and harp repertoires. I like the Dorian and Mixolydian modes. And I like the earthy, misty, fiery and fragrant melodies. The lift, the stomp, the pulse, THE STEP! I misjudged. Miscalculated. And that’s not a bad thing, or a problem. But it does make me feel weird. I don’t know. But i’m grateful for the Irish tradition, and the other Celtic traditions. It’s been f* grand. And I have no regrets!

I can understand that, Jerone. Trad music and bluegrass have some of the same instruments, but bluegrass to me feels more like a flavor of jazz than anything else - it’s mostly about solos and not much about ensemble playing. And frankly, the soloing in bluegrass usually bores the tits off me, because it so often feels like showing off, playing faster than anyone else, that sort of thing. The allure of Bluegrass for accomplished melody players is improvisation. Essentially the same as Jazz or Blues, where the rest of the band lays back as a rhythm section and supports a rotating number of players who improvise solos. A musical spotlight on your improv ability.

I get the attraction. The end stage of my infatuation with the guitar was being the lead guitarist in an amateur Blues band, where I’d improvise solo breaks. Nothing fancy, just basic pentatonic extensions, nothing as good as jazz-style soloing over the changes. But every once in a while, I’d get past rote reliance on licks… the skies would open up, a ray of sunshine would come down.. and I’d play something great and I had no idea how it happened. That’s the beauty of improv, when it works.

If you’re not into that, and I left it behind to play the incredibly great melodies of Irish and Scottish trad with just a little variation here and there to satisfy the itch… then it’s understandable not to be into Bluegrass. Re: I don’t think i’m interested in Bluegrass.

Neighbour here, West Virginia.

My grandfather (b. 1896 in a log cabin) played fiddle, banjo (4 string and 5 string), guitar, mandolin, piano, and euphonium (in the local Colliery Band).

Where I’m from we call it “mountain music”, the indigenous traditional music.

Bluegrass is a modern, urban, and foreign genre which I intensely dislike.

There probably are people who blur the lines, but we regard Mountain Music and Bluegrass as quite distinct.

At Banjo Fiddle contests there are separate categories

Old Time Fiddle
Old Time Banjo
Bluegrass Fiddle
Bluegrass Banjo

I suppose with fiddle you might use the same instrument and compete in both, but not with banjo. Many old-time banjo players go full Trad and fretless. In any case the styles are utterly different, drop-thumb versus putting picks on all your digits and making the banjo sound like a jackhammer.

My grandfather’s fiddle, which I have, is distinctly set up for Mountain Music and wouldn’t work for Bluegrass, classical music, Irish music, etc. The fingerboard, bridge, and nut have been filed to a flatter, gentler curve so that three strings can be played at once and it’s nearly impossible to play either of the two middle strings individually. He died when I was very young and I never heard him play, so I don’t know what tuning or tunings he used, but most likely he played “high bass” tuning using plenty of drones.

Oh, and there’s a rattlesnake rattle inside the fiddle, tied with a thread to the soundpost.

Re: I don’t think i’m interested in Bluegrass.

Bluegrass ALWAYS requires adaptation to the singer. Songs are very important in bluegrass and the singer sings in whatever key they want. Musicians who can play in any key fit in very well. Musicians who only can play in a limited set of keys will not be able to contribute to the sound or may feel left out when the singer is in E major or B flat major for example. This is one of the reasons why stringed instruments predominate in bluegrass, and fixed key instruments are uncommon. Capos make all keys accessible for competent guitarists by the way which is why you’ll see a lot of guitars at bluegrass sessions. Violinists who can play in all keys are always in demand at bluegrass sessions. Great bands and recordings abound in bluegrass, which overlaps with Americana, Folk, Old Timey, Gospel, Blues, and yes, Country.

When Linlithgow Folk Festival was still alive, we had a strong Bluegrass element to it, largely thanks to one of our committee members having a major interest in the genre, and other members of the British Bluegrass Music Association in the area. It wasn’t of huge interest to me, but I got to know a bit more about it, thanks to those aficionados in our midst.
We always had, among other events, a Bluegrass concert on the Friday night, with a guest band: some years it was a British-based band, others we managed to tap into overseas tours of USA-based bands. There was always a great demand for tickets, which regularly sold out, and those who attended raving about how good it was. (I was usually busy stewarding some other event while it was on!)
Then, among the pub sessions over the weekend, there would be one pub nominated for Bluegrass music.
And the other very popular event was the Sunday morning Bluegrass Gospel get - together at which various groups or individuals took it in turns to lead a song: some wonderful singing! I did learn a few songs over the years to be able to take part.
So, in short, I wasn’t very interested at first, but did develop more than a passing interest in it.

One thing to note in this convo, just because it’s come up a couple times, is that bluegrass is not really a “traditional” music. It has a lot of roots in various traditional musics, but bluegrass as a separate genre that differentiates itself from “old time” and “mountain music” and other traditional musics started with professional bands and commercial concerts and recordings. There really isn’t the same distinction made in Irish or Scottish music, but the equivalent are bands like Flook and Talisk who are very much *based* on traditional music but aren’t generally considered very “traditional” as a whole.
