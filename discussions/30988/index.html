How do you decide where to place ornaments?

Assuming you’re working on a new tune, and not learning it from an already ornamented version, how do you decide where to put your rolls, trebles and cuts? Is it possible to consciously say something like “I’ll put a roll on the first beat of this bar in order to emphasise the second beat” or “I’ll put cuts on the 2nd and 4th beats in order to emphasise them”

Can you ascribe a specific function to each ornament? People talk in vague terms about the ornaments “bringing out” the melody. I suppose I’m looking for any general rules or short cuts that would allow someone to make good decisions without first putting in 20 years of subconscious osmosis by listening

Re: How do you decide where to place ornaments?

In my opinion your question comes from an incorrect premise or assumption, that Irish tunes and “ornaments” are two seperate things.

In most of the tunes I play there are no “ornaments” per se, if one uses the definition of “ornament” as it is normally used in music, that is, a device which is superfluous to the tune, which is put in for decorative effect.

Rolls and cuts aren’t like that, at least on the pipes, whistle, and flute. Rather they are necessary articulations which are integral to the melody. Taking them out would create an artificial version of the melody and force the player to tongue notes (flute or whistle) or play in a strange continuously detached style (pipes).

However it is true that I can sightread a written version of a jig or reel, which was written by someone who doesn’t understand Irish music and has created an artificial version consisting only of a skeleton of bare notes, and flesh it out by putting in traditional phrasing and articulations etc and thereby transforming it into an acceptable thing. This is done, I suppose, by following a traditional set of “rules” which probably nobody ever wrote down, and perhaps couldn’t be written down. It comes from playing hundreds of tunes in the traditional manner thousands of times over many years. I do it automatically without thinking about it.

Re: How do you decide where to place ornaments?

I’m rather scathing towards anyone specifically asking for short cuts. Basically, you are asking for an understanding of something you have no understanding of, which in itself is no bad thing, however, you are not looking for an understanding.

You need to totally rethink your learning process.

Think of how you speak. Think of how you articulate words to give your sentences meaning.Think of how you can say a sting of the same words to give different meaning.

“His trousers were so tight he couldn’t sit down in the damn things were quiet as a mouse slept in the reeds”

You hear a lot of inarticulate musicians sounding like this. It’s bloody awful

Does this make more sense:
“His trousers were so tight he couldn’t sit down in the damn things. Were quiet as a mouse. Slept in the reeds.”

Or this:
“His trousers were so tight he couldn’t sit down. In the damn, things were quiet as. A mouse slept in the reeds.”

Or this:
“His trousers were so tight he couldn’t sit. Down in the damn, things were quiet … as a mouse slept in. T? He reads his trousers were so.”

Etc. etc. The punctuation is articulation that defines phrases. It is not mere decoration.

Re: How do you decide where to place ornaments?

Alright, here’s my experience. After a little over a year of playing (flute/whistle), actively/passively listening to ITM, and going to sessions, I found a “pattern” of sorts that showed me how certain “ornaments” fit in where. This “pattern” can’t be fully described, but there are a couple of common occurrences that I can describe. For instance, in the Bucks of Oranmore, in the second part where it goes “ADFDA2” - I use a cut on the D to F, and a roll on the A2. However, it’s not a “rule”, but a matter of preference and style.

My advice would be to learn most of your tunes by ear, thoroughly dissect the tunes with “ornaments” and all, and notice how it all fits together. As you are doing this (this happened with me, it may or may not for you), certain phrases will get under your fingers, along with the cuts/crans etc. and you will start to see these so called patterns, and it will all become second nature and impulsive. Also at the same time, you specific style of playing will form. Another thing to note, which makes this possible, a friend of mine once told me, while I was early on in playing, “with Irish music, you can almost take just a couple tunes, and you will have the vast majority of the phrases that make up almost every Irish tune out there.”

Re: How do you decide where to place ornaments?

there is more than one way to approach learning where/when/how to place ornaments, and to deciding which ornament to use at a given juncture, etc.

while it’s probably fair to say that how you “really” learn this is by getting a feel for it through years of listening, there is absolutely a repository of generalized knowledge that master teachers who are good “explainers” can and do share on this issue, and it can absolutely be extremely helpful.

i personally have been in multiple workshop classes with multiple master player/teachers in ireland who quite sensibly used written music (dots in some cases, abcs in others) to demonstrate (NOTE TO THE HATERS: the IDEA, not rigid rules) of how to begin to think about where one might choose (or not choose) to put ornaments and what tool box of ornamental choices might be available in this or that spot (it can differ depending on the length of the note you’re thinking about ornamenting, whether it’s a downbeat or an off, and other issues like that).

if anyone on this thread thinks for one second that the serious young learners in ireland are not getting the rudiments of how to approach this laid out for them by their teachers both orally and in writing, said person is going to have a long wait for the tooth fairy.

having said all that, the conscious learning through words or writing won’t do it alone. you gotta have the listening. as in, years of it. the “band” recordings are pretty much useless on this. what you want are solo, duo, trio-at-most recordings by masters on your own instrument and traditional masters on instruments you don’t play. and you want to listen to the stuff constantly, doing both conscious analysing as well as osmosis-listening.

Hey friend, shortcuts + music don’t go well together, *especially in terms of learning new music. Never a good idea. You want to be thorough. If you take the back road, you may miss something very important, or… end up at a dead end, or in a ditch.

But aside from metaphors, learning musical things takes time, especially things involving understanding style and the way the “naturalists” do it. One thing that i’m beginning to realize is that this is a very stylistic music. This means that an individuals’ own style heavily influences the music, hence why there are no set rules.
(To further explain, one master may do things completely differently than another master, and even though they are both masters, they may stylistically have very little in common. So basically, if your style is made up of short-cuts and general rules, your music will sound “short cut” and generic.

↳ Posted by Steve Shaw on December 5th, 2012.

Re: How do you decide where to place ornaments?

It is not being to rigid to note that there are certain ornaments or articulations that appear in certain places more frequently than others. For example, pipers can’t play the same note twice in a row, because they can’t use their tongues or glottals or whatever, and put a cut or a tap between the notes, and the rest of us tend to copy that. A dotted quarter note calls out to be replaced with a roll. Three notes in a row also wants to be replaced by a roll on many instruments. A longer note, especially at the beginning of a phrase, gives you time to slide into it. Depending on style or tradition, some folks substitute a treble for a roll.
But there are also a lot of options available. Take the start of Out on the Ocean, which I first learned to play as BDB. Michael Gill has stated that it isn’t the same tune without a B roll there. And I have heard people substitute a B with a nice long slide up into it at that point. On my B/C accordion, there is a nice BD double stop that I can play there.
So, there are rules, but also possibilities galore.
One thing you could do is seek out a tutorial book that gives you suggestions on this front. Matt Cranich’s fiddle book, if I remember correctly, was a big help to my wife when she was starting, with hints on ornaments and even bowing.
Good luck, and enjoy those possibilities!

@tziganarama - answering your original question :

By way of example, if you were to take tune “Cup of Tea” see here : https://thesession.org/discussions/30988 … you will see that is the “bare bones”.

Often a roll or treble will be put in on a long note (eg the low E on the first bar, imagine that as a long note, without the F# that follows it). Cuts are sometimes applied to the shorter notes to give a bit of bite.

Often, depending on style, the phrases are played bare the first time, an ornamented on the second repeating phrase. That can be in the same A part, or reserved for the second time round of the tune.

Roughly speaking, rolls and triplets (or trebles) are interchangeable in that the relative time values are similar. It really depends on the context within the tune, and only individual taste can dictate that. In that sense, there are no “rules” as such. Everyone has a different way.

So, if trebles are used on the first round, rolls can be used on the second round. I some timetimes take everything out on the second round, if the melody is particulary strong or sweet.

WHAT IS ORNAMENTATION?

"When I speak of ornamentation in traditional Irish music I am referring to ways of altering or embellishing small
pieces or cells of a melody that are between one and three eighth-note beats long. These alterations and embellish-
ments are created mainly through the use of special fingered articulations and inflections, not through the addition
of extra, ornamental or grace notes.

The modern classical musician’s view of ornamentation is quite different. Ornamentation, A Question & Answer
Manual, a book written to help classical musicians understand ornamentation from the baroque era through the pres-
ent, offers this definition: “Ornamentation is the practice of adding notes to a melody to allow music to be more
expressive.”i

Classical musicians naturally tend to carry this kind of thinking with them as newcomers to traditional Irish music.
However, as long as they overlay the “added note” concept onto Irish ornamentation, they will be unable to gain flu-
ency in the language of Irish music."

↳ Posted by AlBrown on December 12th, 2012.

“Where” to place ornaments implies two things. One is that ornaments are easily divorced from the music. The other implication is ornaments exist in a location rather than happening relative to the timing of sounds (rhythm, or rhythmic articulations). The tune’s melody, ornaments (or articulation), phrasing, variation, interpretation … all these can be discussed & practiced (to an extent) in relative isolation. But each of these in themselves is not a tune. When you play a tune the line(s) between them becomes blurred.
The lines return when the tune is no longer played & practice begins.
