Just a note on harmonica tuning (don’t nod off), which I mention because of all this “let’s make sure we all turn up tuned to A440” talk. Every harmonica I can think of is tuned to at least A441+ and some are tuned to A444 or higher. I’ve heard various explanations of why this is done and none is particularly convincing. All I know is that A442-A443 works very well with guitars, for example, tuned to A440. Allegedly it’s because reeds flatten slightly when played and so the sharp tuning is a sort of compensation for that. The trouble is that there is no way that every reed will flatten to anything like the same degree when played (I won’t go into the physics right now). I’ve noticed that I am only ever challenged on tuning if I play a harp at A443 or above. Below that, the sound is bright and breezy but not discernibly sharp. I think the most important thing is that everyone gets into the ballpark for a session. The ear will decide the rest. And the ear will certainly decide that a cent or three either way will not matter. After all, we’re dealing with an lot of instruments whose intonation depends hugely on the player’s technique and not especially on whether they are theoretically tuned to be no more than a cent either side of some arbitrary standard.

Re: Might this be a better electronic tuner?

If you’re tuning a string on a fiddle by the perfect 5th system - playing adjacent strings together and listening for the difference beats that indicate how close you’re getting - the closer you get to the perfect 5th (zero beats) the more difficult it is to decide whether your string is above or below the target pitch. It is a fact that for very small differences in pitch between two notes it is very difficult for the ear to determine which note is the higher, although you do detect they are different. For example, if you’re 2 cents flat on 440 you’ll get the same number of beats per second as you would if you were 2 cents sharp, and you’re unlikely to be able to tell which is which.

The solution is to consistently tune up to the target from below (that’s why we use the phrase “tuning up”), and never downwards from above, so you can alway be sure that you’re not going to be drifting sharp while still believing you’re flat. There is also the bonus that there’s no possibility of over-tensioning the string.

Re: Might this be a better electronic tuner?

My problem with this discussion of ability to hear pitch accurately is that it doesn’t take into consideration context. Any reasonably good musician can hear the “wow” created by two sustained pitches PLAYED TOGETHER that are as little as 1 cent apart. It is much more difficult to discern pitch differences that small when the notes follow one another. The little “test” on the wiki page is interesting, but essentially valueless since you are told about what the differences are before you start.

OK that’s two pitches played without vibrato and sustained. So, clearly the group playing of slow airs can be painful if folks aren’t well in tune. But the context of a session is fist tune with many and often varied ornaments (or articulations or whatever you like to call them) played simultaneously and often in a noisy room. Not the same at all. I have great doubts that most of us would hear 5 cent differences in that circumstance.

The key thing I think everyone here can agree about is simple: Tune your instrument to the group (usually 440 but not always) and do it before you get there. Use a tuner if you wish. Once you arrive adjust as needed.

I play hammered dulcimer. Want a pain in a session? Try keeping that in tune!

Re: Might this be a better electronic tuner?

“Do fiddlers ever deliberately play not quite in beatless unison …?”
Probably not. It’s pointless. You can have two of the best players in the world playing together sans vibrato on instruments that are perfectly in tune and there will inevitably be “unison” notes that are not beat-free. The reason is that you only need one player to position a finger a minute fraction of a millimeter out of true (well within the limits of experimental error, as they say) and he’ll be producing a note that is not the same as the other guy’s, although the listener’s ear might not notice the difference in pitch. It might only be 0.5Hz or less, but that is sufficient to generate a beat not only on the fundamental but on the overtones. Bow pressure can also alter the pitch of a note, depending on where the bow is on the string and which string it is. There are too many variables at all levels to be able to ensure a beatless unison throughout a piece of music, and is it indeed desirable anyway, for purely musical reasons?

Re: Might this be a better electronic tuner?

Will, thank you for that explanation. Playing Irish traditional music on the fiddle is similar in some respects to playing baroque music, early baroque especially, in that in both genres the players use open strings (the E in particular) far more than today’s classical players. There are a number of reasons for this, among which (and here I freely admit I’m mostly guessing) are,

1. The resonance of the open strings, an important point with baroque instruments which aren’t as powerful as today’s violins.

2. The restricted range of keys (i.e. number of sharps and flats) used in the baroque and traditional music versus the classical, where players have to be able cope with extremes such as 7 flats or sharps, and even double flats and sharps beyond that (and here you wouldn’t for example play C## as an open D - the pitches are different). The classical training is therefore geared towards all this, including playing extended scale passages that don’t have any open strings in them..

3. In classical music the open E (and A) can give an obtrusive and too bright a sound in a particular context, so the player will use a fingered E or A on the string below. A lot of practicing of scales requires the player to avoid the open E and A so as to enable him or her to acquire the necessary strength and flexibility in using the 4th finger (pinky). Unfortunately, this can be taken too far and there are amateur classical players (and conductors!) around who avoid the open E at all costs. I remember some years ago in my cello days (well before I took up the fiddle) when I attended an orchestra workshop. The tutor spent a good 30 minutes teaching a group of violinists how to get a good controlled non-squeaky tone on the open E (something that an experienced folk fiddler has no problem with). It was obvious that some of the group were venturing into unknown territory!

4. It is however not true that a fingered E is unheard of in Irish folk playing, or in baroque. It depends on the musical context and the sound required. For instance, if I were playing the notes “ded” in a jig (baroque or folk) I would probably use a fingered E because the note in that context is weak and the open string would be too obtrusive and disturb the rhythm, but an E played on a strong beat would most likely require the open string.

My take on tuning. In classical music, symphonic or chamber, my experience is that there is only one degree of fine tuning (or just “tuning”), and that is perfect 5ths. I, and everyone else in my chamber orchestra, tune by ear. I have never seen an electronic tuner used other than once or twice and then only to generate an A440 for everyone else to tune to; a tuning fork is the norm. Although if we’re performing a piano concerto we’ll tune to the piano, which would have been tuned beforehand to A440 anyway.

In sessions I tune to the loudest fixed pitch instrument present (if there is one), usually some sort of squeeze box. In practice they all seem to be pretty close to A440. Otherwise I’ll use my tuning fork for the A and tune the rest by ear. Electronic tuners are common in sessions now, but there are still fiddlers and the occasional guitarist or banjo player who tune by ear.
