Re: guitars with slim necks

Not too sure about the size of your fingers and how much of a problem they pose, but regarding the thumb and grip strength:
To some extent the pressure applied by the fingers especially with bar chords should not be counterbalanced by your thumb. It is better to use the inside of your right forearm pressing on the body of the guitar. Your left hand will be more relaxed and will perform better than if you are strangling the neck of the guitar. Try practising with the thumb not even touching the neck of the guitar.
BTW if you play in dropD tuning or DADGAD there is little need for any bar chords.
Hope this is of some use to you.

I hope I draw no one’s ire, and I certainly intend no affront, but you sound like someone who should spend a small amount of money on a decent, tenor or baritone ukulele. Four strings, nicely spaced, and lots of fun. Add a “low G” string setup to extend the range below middle C.

Alternately, try a baritone ukulele, commonly tuned to DGBE (unless you’re in Hawaii, where various slack key tunings can arise). Again, four strings and even bigger than a tenor ukulele. Both relatively light and portable. You can find these things both new and used at on-line auctions. Try some Aquila nylgut strings to perk up the instrument. They’re very nice ukulele strings. A good on-line resource is “Musicguymic” on the U.S. version of E-bay (www.ebay.com). He’s Hawaiian, and quite adept at setting up the instruments before they ship, a critical factor for making them fun and playable for someone seeking to learn. He can also install Aquila strings for you, if the instrument can handle the tension.

Whatever your decision, best of luck.

Re: guitars with slim necks

I have one of those Seagull guitars. Nice instruments, especially for the price. I got to pick mine out of a line up of about 8 of the same model, so it’s a real cannon. Great grain in the soundboard.

Anyway, guitars that are made for flatpicking have narrower necks that guitars made for fingerpicking. The width of nylon string guitars is wider to accomadate the finger picking of classical players, There are also steel string accoustic guitars that are made with wider necks, though not as wide as nylon stringed guitars.

But steel stringed accoustics made for flatpicking have narrower necks. This is the string spacing used on alot of electric guitars. This is what you are after.

Other than that, you have to work with what God gave you.

Play your chord inversions up the neck as a chord scale and change chords on the quarter note as an exercise,

Playing chord scales will make your hands strong and stretchy. finger length has very little to do with it. The intrument was made for regular people.

Remember Django had his fingers fused together in a gypsy wagon fire when he was a boy and he was one of the greatest players of all time.

Re: guitars with slim necks

I have relatively small “girl” hands and prefer a standard to slightly wider neck. You need to develop your left hand and let the death grip go. then you will fly on any neck.

I cruelly force students to arch their wrist a bit and play scales from the lower to higher strings, without holding the neck at all. it hurts a litte at first so don’t overdo it. A few runs prior to practice sessions is all you need, stop short of any real pain. If you hurt the next day you did it too long. It can be two minutes, maybe just one.

What this does, and the scales don’t have to be right notes, don’t worry, this is not the point, is it builds up the wrist muscles and little ones in the fingers you never knew you had. The strength to hit the strings will gradually come out of your fingers, hand and wrist, straight on with fingers, your callouses will start to form at the top of the fingers instead of the pads. Then your death grip will go and you will be able to fly on any neck. Speed is crucial if you are to keep up with some of the players, and dance time. it works…. try it. Gradually is how it happens with just a few minutes a day, and one day you may notice you are not in the death grip…. the death grip itself will hurt your hand more than the exercises over time, it’s bad playing position and can mess up your hand for good if too tense and tight, really cramp up. These exercises will give you speed and fluidity. You can also pick tunes out for the exercise, just keep that thumb off the neck, and keep the practice short.

I rest my thumb on the back, it’s a place to put it, but it does not grip. When I play way high up the neck I really arch the wrist and the thumb goes sort of under the neck at times, not touching at all. I will do the exercises too, as a warm up.

I have been “accused” of having classical training, due to left hand position, but never had a lesson, and made the exercise up for myself, so don’t know if it is something they use. A couple of classical lessons my be great help too or maybe they have some exercises. it really is important… and no, as a new harp player, I do harp exercises, it really helps.

iris is right about the death grip going away. You get strong, but there isn’t any tension.

Basically, a death grip will just engage muscles that have nothing to do with articulating the note. this will just consume oxygen and wear you out and even get in your way of playing smoothly as the tempo goes up.

and for what its worth, every flatpicker I ever saw that had serious chops “posted”. I think that to play fast you have to anchor your hand somewhere.

and I’ve not seen many classical players play a bebop gig with tempos over 300 bpm all night, so if it is how you play, “post” away and don’t feel like it is a bad habit. Everybody does it.
