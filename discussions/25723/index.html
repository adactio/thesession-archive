Do you modify the picking pattern ahead of time in preparation for the triplet, or is there some sort of recovery pattern you pick after the triplet is done? My triplets are played down-up-down. Re: Triplets technique help required - banjo / mandolin

My method is to play the triplet D-U-D and immediately return to the pattern you were playing before the triplet and not make any adjustment before the triplet, especially consecutive down strokes as that may well cause you to lose rhythm. Also make sure that you are playing the tune at a tempo that allows you to include triplets, certainly when you are practicing, although if you’re playing with other people who have set a particular tempo this is not always possible. Bear in mind that it takes quite a lot of practice to get on top of triplets. Perseverance is the key.
Here’s a clip of Brona Graham, she has an orthodox right hand technique and note how there is a very minimal amount of pick “travel”.

Also worth looking at Eddie Whelan, same idea.

Re: Triplets technique help required - banjo / mandolin

FWIW, I start all my triplets in reels on a down stroke. I used to be able to do them in both directions, but my triplets that started on an upstroke never sounded quite right at speed.

I find triplets in reels much easier. Yes, you have consecutive down strokes, but never more than two. In a jig rhythm, if you’re sticking to the DUD DUD picking pattern, you will actually have 3 downstrokes in a row, on either front side or back side triplets. (DUD dudD DUD… or DUD Ddud DUD…) So I actually find that a lot more challenging. A lot of players will forego the DUD DUD pattern for a beat after the triplet. And a lot of other players don’t use a DUD DUD hand rhythm for jigs…

It also helps me to think of the triplet as a single “gesture”. It’s a small pattern that my hand makes, and I don’t think of it as 3 distinct movements.

There are probably as many different triplet techniques as there are players. For me, picking regular notes comes from the wrist, and picking triplets comes from a combination of finger movement, and a slight tightening of the wrist, which makes your wrist “bounce” a bit.

Good luck with it!

Re: Triplets technique help required - banjo / mandolin

Enda’s tutorial and clear description as to how he holds his pick, hand and arm were very helpful to me. After many years of playing I changed over to his recommended pick-holding position (and relaxed technique) and I no longer develop pain in my elbow when playing long sessions. And my ability to play at top speed improved. Thanks a bunch!!

As a long-time DUDU picker I agree whole-heartedly with Enda’s opinion that a solid and consistent system for right-hand technique is absolutely necessary. I don’t follow Enda’s system totally but I’ve had a system for many decades that works for me and is consistant. The biggest difference between Enda’s system and my system is that I will always double up the downstroke on the 1/8th note before the triplet (then start the triplet with an up-stroke) instead of doubling the downstroke after it to get back in sync. I consider the 1/8th note ahead of the triplet (essentially an up-beat to the triplet) and the triplet itself to be all one musical element. I heard this many years ago referred to as a ‘prepared’ triplet. Works every time. Double triplets (two beats, two consecutive triplets) I omit the up-beat, start the first triplet down then alternate pick to the third beat which falls naturally in sync as a downstroke.

Double triplets can help you get used to how triplets feel and fit in a tune. Start the first triplet with a downstroke and you’ll end up on the downstroke at the third beat, back where you want to be. It’s easy:-)

The other aspect of my system (as it pertains to triplets) has to do with the several options available when playing triplets in conjunction with crossing to and from adjacent strings, either above or below. Probably too much information…….Snore…..ZZZZZZZZ

I’m not arguing with anyone Will. I know what works for me, and I know what doesn’t. I know what I’ve managed to teach to others, and what they take to. a) gravity’s obviously got nothing to do with it, the pick is driven by muscular exertion
and
b) the thumb is a larger muscle, but both up and down strokes are driven by the rotation of the wrist, which is strong enough in either direction to put far more energy into the string than it can cope with, so the muscular effort in either direction is available if you want to use it.

So I’m just not buying that the down stroke is naturally stronger in any way that matters: both up and down strokes can put in enough energy to make the sound become a totally unmusical thwangking sound, so there’s no question that either stroke can play the full dynamic range of the instrument.

That doesn’t mean that limiting yourself to a certain pattern of up and down strokes is likely to get you better results, it just means that imitating someone who’s already learned how to make music is a quicker path to making music.

I’m not using the fiddle as an example, I’m using his musicianship as an example. For my ears, if there isn’t that degree of phrasing - if you can’t clearly hear the ideas in the music, as you can in the Tommy clip - then it’s awful. Thile playing the Bach shows how Bach’s writing requires that nearly-mechanical phrasing in order to bring out the ideas, and his playing is well suited to that. Since alternate picking and other fixed patterns of picking are suited to producing the Thile sound and not the Tommy sound, they are not, in my opinion, suitable techniques for playing Irish music. So now you’re saying there’s not really a gab or break in the sound (after saying that at least three different times), it’s that you can’t play an upstroke that sounds the same as your downstroke. Very well, there is a well-trodden path to the woodshed. Find it and follow it.

“why do yo uthink guitar players like george shuffler chose to flatpick ddu ddu du,rather than dududu, because it sounds different.it has to do with emphasis and also legato.”

In the flatpicking guitar world this type of passage is known as “cross-picking”. It basically imitates the same rhythmic pattern as the standard 5-string bluegrass banjo picking pattern, two groups of three notes followed by two notes played over the 4/4 bar. It is by no means a typical picking pattern. Regardless, there are plenty of flat-pickers who crosspick the same rhythmic pattern with DUD..UDU…DU. The rhythmic feel of cross-picked passages are dictated as much by the melodic content as they are by the accentuation (emphasis).
