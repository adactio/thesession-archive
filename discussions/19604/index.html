Do fiddlers, mandos, zouks . . . ornament tunes?

Trad players articulate. Classicos ornament. Ornamentation is embellishment of the basic melody w/more ‘notes’
Articulation, at least on flute, is different. In a nutshell ~ ornamentation always uses clear, distinct, tones, with ‘perfect pitch’ *. Articulation . . . not so much. The note being articulated is clear & distinct. The other notes barely exist.
To play a cut on flute you do not sound another note. Definitely not one which is on pitch. Maybe on taps. Consider crans (on flute). You hear plenty of articulation. What you do not hear are pitched embellishment notes.
So . . . “Do fiddlers, mandos, zouks . . . ornament tunes?” Or do you do something else. Do you articulate crans?

* A good example of articulation is a slide. The 1st note & the last note are each on pitch. In between you pass through all the possibilities yet never play a single note. It is articulation ~ not embellishment.

Re: Do fiddlers, mandos, zouks . . . ornament tunes?

Random, fiddle rolls can be played both ways--so that the pitches of cuts and taps are audible, or so that they aren’t (instead being just interruptions of the pitch that’s being rolled). Crans on fiddle work the same way. So do bowed triplets--all can be played so that pitch is audible, or so it’s not.

My two cents is that the difference between classical embellishments and Irish trad articulations isn’t pitch audibility but the essential aspect of ***timing*** in Irish trad, while timing is important but less constrained (to sound “right”) in classical embellishments. But that’s just a spur of the moment theory….

Re: Do fiddlers, mandos, zouks . . . ornament tunes?

I’m as responsible as anyone here for the term “articulation” being used on this board as much as it is.

Yes, for generations trad musicians have called the twiddly bits “ornaments.” Maybe some of them even think of them that way--as bits of decoration to be hung on the tune.

I started thinking of the twiddly bits more as articulations because so many of the old players kept telling me how integral to the tunes they are--not mere decorations, but essential elements of the tunes. And I saw evidence for that in so many tunes. Think of the B part to Humours of Tulla--all those dotted eighth note fs. The tune loses its Irish tradness if you don’t roll or triplet or somehow articulate the fs. I’ve heard the Boston Pops play such tunes without any articulations (or ornaments, for that matter), and I laughed so hard I nearly wet my pants.

So on one level, articulation vs. ornament is just semantics. And on another level, it’s an important distinction some of us like to make between decorations added to the music vs. twiddly bits that are essential to defining the timing and rhythm of this music and identifying it as Irish trad and not something else.

I have no complaints if that doesn’t make sense to anyone. :-)

Re: Do fiddlers, mandos, zouks . . . ornament tunes?

What it really comes down to for me is that Irish trad music relies (at least in part) on the twiddly bits to generate its trademark timing and rhythm. Other genres of music often use twiddly bits more as melodic embellishment (e.g., think of a measure-long trill on violin--the point of that trill isn’t to give the note a pulse, it’s simply to adorn what would otherwise be a plain long tone).

In the dance music at least, Irish trad rarely if ever does twiddly bits for melodic embellishment for its own sake. That embellishment may happen, but its a secondary effect of the articulation, whose primary purpose is to give lift and pulse to the timing.

That’s my hogwash theory and I’m sticking to it. ;-)

Re: Do fiddlers, mandos, zouks . . . ornament tunes?

Ion, I think I see what you’re getting at, but the way I think about it, it doesn’t matter how frequently a player uses articulations--they’re still articulations, not ornaments (see my distinction above). They’re not “melodic embellishments,” which is what I imagine when someone says ‘ornaments.’

Sure, this music encompasses an amazing variety in personal styles and approaches to interpreting the tunes. But the rhythmic use of cuts and triplets (in particular, but among other twiddly bits) is a significant part of what makes this sound like Irish music and not Russian music or Chinese music or American Old Timey music. I’m guessing it has been said before, no doubt on this board, and I’m just remembering it (though without attribution).

I spent a wonderful evening a few years back playing tunes with Alan Jabbour, ethnomusicologist and Appalachian fiddler. We had lots of tunes in common, just played in very different styles. When Alan started a tune (say, Over the Moor to Maggie or Pigtown Fling though he had American names for them), the tune itself was immediately recognizable to me and I would join in. In nearly every case, our playing blended well--the tune was still the tune. But our music was distinctly different.

The difference, as far as I could suss out, was in our articulations and “timing gestalt” (hah, take that, all of you who think we were over-analyzing before! :-D )--Alan sounded Appalachian, and I sounded Irish trad.
