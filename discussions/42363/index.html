Getting started (plans and advice)

Hi all!

First time posting and just getting started in earnest with Irish music. I’ve been playing guitar for about 30 years now, but my attention has been captured by the tin whistle, so I’m starting there with my daughter learning as well. My hope is that some day I can make enough progress with that that I can also pursue the flute. I wanted to run my current plan by the experts here and ask for any advice, corrections, counsel, etc. in this journey I’ve undertaken…
To make it simple, here are my goals and plan:
Goals:
* Become profficient enough with the chord progressions on traditional tunes that I can accompany my daughter as she learns the whistle parts (and perhaps contribute to a traditional session with adults as well)
* Become proficient enough on the whistle that I can eventually enjoy myself and contribute something pleasant to a session somewhere, as well as coach my daughter as she learns
* Once I feel stronger on the whistle, also start learning the flute for the same reasons as above

So here’s my plan (and this is the part where your expertise would be great to have - let me know if you have any advice)
1. We’re starting with two books that include CD’s that walk through the basics to more traditional tunes. I’ve also ordered a video that covers techniques as well as the basics of the instrument. I also plan to listen to recordings/videos of the well respected greats of traditional music and study what they’re doing.
2. Once we’ve made it through that material and we have a good base, I want to try some lessons. I know in person lessons really have no substitute, but I thought perhaps I’d try online lessons because they’re a little more cost effective ( like at https://www.blaynechastain.com or https://www.oaim.ie)
3. Then I thought if we’d made good progress on that we could go out and attend some open sessions somewhere and build some community connections, and learn in person from more experienced players

Does that sound like a reasonable plan to you?
Anything you’d do differently?

I’m very excited to dig in on this more. Something in traditional Irish music has really captivated me at this phase in my life, and I feel a strong desire to do a deep dive on this and make progress in my playing.

Welcome to the mustard board. Perhaps if you shared your whereabouts we could give specific local recommendations.

Warning: bit of a curmudgeonly rant below, but I do speak truth.

Books and CD and videos are all vastly inferior to lessons. A teacher has the ability to be objective. How would you know how to identify and stop your bad habits if you don’t know what bad habits are in the first place?

IMO, get rid of all that other stuff, bite the financial bullet, and get lessons. Just maybe you can find a teacher who will teach a father/daughter combo together at a good discount. At the very least, you should seriously consider getting lessons for at least one of you(the daughter?). Even some less frequent lessons would be better than canned material.

If you really want to take a deep dive, get involved with the community ASAP. Get to ceilis or other dance events, sit in the audience at pub sessions, etc. Introduce yourself and ask for common tunes to learn. Attend some group classes locally - usually much cheaper than individual lessons, and you are rubbing elbows with fellow musicians. Get to a concert, especially a house concert if you can.
Buying a book and sitting at home is about as anti- ‘deep dive’ as you can get.

If you want to improve fast, the above is the best way, along with listening. At least an hour or two per day if you can. Commuting, housework, shower, anywhere you can.

If your plan is to play flute, start there. I think it is much better to go from flute to whistle than the reverse. Flute is harder, but offers far more. Nothing you do on whistle will prep you for flute, save maybe the fingering, which is the easiest part. Re: Getting started (plans and advice)

I totally agree with Steve T about treating learning to play your instrument and learning the music as two separate activities.

Learning the music is mostly a case of listen, listen, listen. For learning the instrument getting a teachere is far and away the best route, but if that isn’t possible get a tutor book which will give you a structured learning sequence. Don’t be tempted to skip over boring or seemingly simple bits, study every chapter until you’ve totally nailed it. If you skimp, you may be able to play the simple tunes you are looking at at the moment just as well, but there will come a point in the future where you stop making progress because you haven’t got the right basic technique, and you’ll then have to take several steps back and re-learn stuff.

Youtube is a good resource, but only if you use it in conjunction with a structured learning plan, which you get from a book or teacher. If you simply hop about finding stuff you like and trying to play it you’ll find you avoid tunes that need techniques you struggle with, leaving gaps in your skills.

One other thing that occurred to me was regarding this part:

* Become profficient enough with the chord progressions on traditional tunes that I can accompany my daughter as she learns the whistle parts (and perhaps contribute to a traditional session with adults as well)

That’s a great goal, but I think you may find it easier to learn some tunes on whistle/flute first, and then start working on the accompaniment for those tunes once you have the melodies internalized. Accompanying Irish trad is not as formulaic as some other styles of music. The tunes tend to do a lot of strange things, like switch modes in the middle of a part, throw in lots of accidentals, etc. And we play a lot in Dorian and Mixolydian modes, which not everyone has a lot of experience with.

So even though I had played guitar for a long time prior to becoming interested in Irish trad (not unlike yourself), I found it difficult to make good decisions about chords to use until I really knew the tune. (In my case, my first teacher on guitar decided I just wasn’t getting it, and decided to teach me to play a few tunes and let me make more appropriate accompaniment decisions from there… And that just got me more interested in playing tunes, which is a slippery slope right down to the banjo… ;-)) Once you’re well steeped in the tradition, you will find that the accompaniment becomes easier.

Re: Getting started (plans and advice)

Loads of great advice.

I would say yes, in-person lessons with a good player are best.

If that’s not possible, Skype lessons with a good player is next-best.

If neither are possible, there are excellent YouTube tutorials. I feel it’s important to be able to both see and hear what’s happening, the interaction between the finger-movements with the sound that’s produced. Written music gives neither, listening to music doesn’t give the visuals. (Beware though, there are some horrid YouTube videos too.)

About your choice of whistles, I don’t think Susato high D’s are the ideal introduction to traditional Irish whistle. IMHO you two would be better off with Killarney D’s, or Jerry Freeman D’s. (Susatos force you to blow rather harder than traditional-style whistles require- once you get habituated to Susatos you’ll overblow traditional-style whistles until you recalibrate your blowing.)

There is more of a mountain to climb with flute, but here’s the thing. If one’s heart is set on playing flute at some point, that mountain won’t get any easier by spending a few years playing whistle. Re: Getting started (plans and advice)

“So a hierarchy would be, technical command of the instrument , then tunes, then eventually the ornaments, if there are any. ”

I agree with the larger principle, but I think that at least for flute, the fingered “cut” should be learned once tunes are introduced, and it should be practiced alongside the tunes. The reason is that you’ll be constantly running into two consecutive notes at the same pitch, and you need some way to break them apart. If you don’t learn the cut, you might instinctively use other techniques like tonguing or diaphragm pulsing, and may have to unlearn those methods later on.

So I don’t think it hurts to learn that one bit of ornamentation, because it’s so central to how flute players generally break apart separate notes. Then you can add other methods like glottal stops, taps, pats, rolls, and tonguing (if you like) as more varied ornamentation later on. I’m not a piper, but I suspect there are similarities in the need to break notes with finger articulation fairly early in the game.

@connical bore, yes i agree, i view these little cuts as a way to articulate a note rather than ornament. I do think that simplicity is the most important principle, so if there are other ways to break a note into 2 they should be Covered first.
The whole idea is to focus on the things that are universal, to develop basic musical skills before focusing on things that are idiosyncratic to particular instruments or genres.
The one thing to avoid is anything that will compromise rhythm.

Re: Getting started (plans and advice)

I noticed that you feel an attraction or compulsion toward flute or low whistle. That is a good sign; pay attention. It doesn’t matter which, but when the muse calls, follow her!

I went from D-whistle to flute, and the flute is definitely something that called to me and keeps calling. I’m not interested in low whistle, but that would be just as good if it works for you, and it is certainly easier than flute.

I am happy and satisfied that I learned articulations on the small D-whistle, as they are so much easier to learn there, and I can transfer or convert them to flute. The traditional D-whistle is a LOT easier than flute. And, I second the suggestion to choose Killarney or a Jerry Freeman tweaked whistle. That guarantees a pleasant beginner experience as you won’t be fighting bad intonation or a loud second register.

WRT what flute to buy, I don’t have strong opinions on which maker is best, as many are quite good. Delrin flutes are in the $300-400 price range; wood flutes from $800-1200. Aside from price considerations and whether to buy wood or delrin, the primary decision points are:
(1) Big Pratten style holes vs smaller Rudall style vs in-between.
(2) Is the embouchure forgiving for beginners (easy to play), or does it require more technical skill. Either is okay, you just need to know what to expect.

Wow. Tons of advice, all good hearted, all good, and some contradictory. Find what works for you.

Fundamentals.
Play. A lot.
Listen. A lot.
Get lessons when you can, but at least some live if possible.
Play with others when you can.
Develop your ear and the ability to learn by ear. There’s nothing inherently wrong with sheet music, but learning by ear is the tradition and the nuances of the music aren’t on the page.
Most importantly, ENJOY it and have fun. That’s what will keep you playing.
I was told that learning this music is much like learning a foreign language, and it gets truer the more I learn.. It takes time and effort, but it’s worth it.
Last, if you really want to learn flute, start now. Get a decent instrument (Copley, Burns, and Doyle all make good instruments that are beginner friendly). Try the Chiff and Fipple site. Their instruments for sale section is good, and several folks who have posted above are regulars there.

Go play, and have fun, and make sure your daughter does. It’s a great gift to share with her.
