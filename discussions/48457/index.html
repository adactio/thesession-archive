Re: Looking for low whistle recommendations

I might not have enough examples to make recommendations, but the E is usually weak on low D whistles. Some are better than others, of course, but even on whistles with a good bottom end it’s still often weaker than the D and the F#.

If you like a kind of scuzzy, airy sound, the MK Pro is good. I haven’t played another whistle that sounds quite like it. Goldies also have quite a breathy quality, but a more conventional low whistle sound. Both have metal mouthpieces that can be prone to clogging and need warming up; I’ve got used to the metal, but it still always feels nice to pick up a whistle with a delrin head.

If you look on YouTube for a contributor called CutiePie, she has done reviews and comparisons of many currently available whistles, including playing samples. It’s the most comprehensive resource I’ve come across.

A couple of things:

- The TinWhistler guy (I think) is one of the best YouTube players of the low whistle. When you get that good, I suspect he could make almost any whistle sound amazing/the whistle won’t be as important as we think (but still important)

- You have to watch out for “reverb” and “compression” in YouTube videos (added manually or artificially).

This is often a big part of the “magic” you get which we can’t seem to replicate in our playing.
It’s the only thing I don’t like about the youtuber mentioned above, her videos are brilliant but she uses quite a bit of audio processing, which sounds amazing, but makes it very difficult to compare the whistles she plays.

In the video above I think there is a small amount of reverb on the mixer (or it’s a very echoey room), which adds to the haunting feeling.

- Having said the whistle probably isn’t very important to TinWhistler, it makes a difference for mortals like me!

I have an embarrassing number of LowDs (including the V5 and my favourite by a long way is my Colin goldie medium blower. I think (reading your comments) you should get one of these. They hold their value extremely well if you don’t get on with it. It’s possibly the one used in the video/looks similar.

The only downside is it seriously clogs. This genuinely gets better in time as you learn how to blow to handle it, but was almost unplayable for me at first.
My second favourite is a MK Pro, which clogs less, but has a less airy feel.

- There are a couple of very active whistle groups (including a “Low” one) on Facebook with helpful people and lots of videos of different types it would be worth checking out.

- Just out of interest, with all whistles, as it’s such a simple instrument there’s a lot of variance in them, and hence a lot of confusing advice.
A cheap generation could be unplayable or one of the best whistles in existence, it’s pure luck.

When you pay more, you’re largely paying for the skill and attention of the maker re. Quality control, so there should be less variance.

I think this is why you get so much confusing opinions re eg “I love v5s” “v5s are awful” with most models, it’s simply that each particular one is different.
Again, with a goldie, I think that level of care and hand crafting means you get more consistency.

- Low F whistles. I’ve never played a goldie low F, but again the MKPro is great and I also like the Kerry busker (subject to my last comment!)

Re: Looking for low whistle recommendations

I think people new to Low Whistle often obsess about the tone they hear in various YouTube recordings.

Having made Youtube Low Whistle comparison videos myself I can assure you that you need to hear a whistle in person to know what it sounds like.

After 45 years playing whistle I’ve come to the point where tone is pretty far down on my list of priorities.

For me it would be something like this

1) Tuning. No matter how great an instrument’s tone, if it can’t be played in tune it’s not a legitimate musical instrument. Everything has to be in tune when playing on a more or less even breath, because in fast tunes you don’t have time to perform airstream gymnastics to force an out-of-tune whistle into tune.

2) Relative strength of the various notes. All the notes have to be very close to each other regarding their upper and lower breaking-points. This mainly concerns the two extremes, Low E (which tends to be the weakest note) and High B (which tends to be the shoutiest note).

3) Air efficiency. If a whistle is an air-hog and a note can only be sustained a couple seconds you’re huffing and puffing on the reels and incapable of playing airs.

4) Nimbleness/ease of octave shifts. I don’t like whistles where you have to force the 2nd octave. I call these “stiff” whistles.

5) Ergonomics. This ought to be #1 in truth, because if you can’t physically play a whistle all the rest is meaningless. This encompasses hole spacing, hole size, tube diameter, and weight. (Low whistles are generally aluminium. You find out why when you pick up a “Chieftain Gold” Low D Whistle which is thick heavy solid brass. It feels like trying to play a bowling ball.)

6) And in last place…tone.

“I can get a clean E note, but I have to blow very softly to do so.”

I would never never never waste one minute of my time trying to play a whistle with voicing that bad.

Get rid of it now, and get a legitimate professional quality instrument.

As I alluded to, the most feeble note on Low Whistles tends to be E in the low octave, and the loudest harshest shoutiest air-hoggiest note tends to be B in the 2nd octave.

The best test of a whistlemaker’s voicing skill is how the opposing tendencies of these two outlier notes are compromised. Your whistle, sad to say, have failed that test miserably.

I only consider Low Whistles who have this issue solved (as well as the laws of acoustics allow) serious legitimate professional instruments.

Re: Looking for low whistle recommendations

With the caveat that I don’t obsess about timbre, I’ve owned several original Bernard Overton Low Whistles over the years and now I own three Colin Goldie Low Whistles and they seem to all have that classic Overton tone.

The windway height has an impact on how the whistle plays, but as far as I can tell it doesn’t affect tone.

I have a Goldie Low D body and two Goldie heads, one a “medium blower” and one a “soft blower”. The heads seem identical other than windway height.

With the easy/soft head the Low D has a powerful Bottom D, easy-to-play sweet 2nd octave, and effortless octave transitions. The perfect Low D whistle, no?

Well, no. Because the price you pay for all the advantages of a wider-windway free-blowing Low Whistle is less air efficiency.

With the “medium” head the whistle feels tighter, the 2nd octave is a bit stiffer. Bottom D isn’t quite as powerful. But the added air-efficiency allows longer notes to be sustained, which I really like when playing airs.

I should stress that all of the differences between the “soft” and “medium” heads are subtle. Both whistles play more like each other than they probably do to any other make of whistle. And, the differences are things perceptible only to the player, not to a listener.

BTW I used to own a Lofgren Low D Whistle which was superb. It played nearly identically to the Colin Goldie “soft-blowing” Low D Whistle.
