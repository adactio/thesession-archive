Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

I reckon it makes a big difference.

By all means use the “dots” to obtain a basic framework for the melody (some phrases can be tricky to pick up by ear first time). Notation is quite rigid and you need to interpret the phrasing and lilt of the music outside of the written music.

Bear in mind that there are several different ways of playing a particular tune and the version someone has put down in notation may not necessarily be the same version that most people play.

All the tunes I know, I’ve been inspired to play by first hearing them played (live or on CD).

When learning a tune by ear, the easiest way is for someone like a tutor to play it slowly whilst you pick up the melody (as you’ve already found out).

However, It’s also possible to easily learn tunes from CD by grabbing the tracks onto your computer with e.g. Audiograbber and then using a slow-downer e.g. Transcribe! so that you can pick up the tune at a speed slightly less than break-neck pace.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

I’m fairly new to Irish music myself (going on about 2 years now), and I frequently wrestle with this issue. I’m sure if you search the archives of this site you can probably find lots of perhaps more qualified opinions on this topic, but here’s my thinking. Certainly as far as the tradition is concerned, music should be learned aurally. However, as you have indicated, this can be a slow, frustrating process, and I know the feeling. Here’s what I’ve finally concluded. I want to have fun with the music, honor the tradition, and learn some tunes. I’ve also been told that the quality of a musician is not judged by the number of tunes they know and certainly it is not, but also, what fun are sessions if you don’t know any tunes. Therefore I try to find a happy medium. I do my best to learn tunes from recordings (commercial, lessons, sessions etc.). My goal is to improve my ability to play by ear, so I know in the long run, this often tedious practicing will pay off (and it has started to). However, if I get stuck on a few measures and I can’t figure them out for the life of me, I will “cheat” and check a printed version that is comparable to the source I’m learning from. I simply do this to avoid utter frustration. However, I am more satisfied when I learn tunes entirely by ear. It’s fun if you can tell a person at a session that the source for your tune you played was a recording or an actual musician, as opposed to O’Neill’s or whatever printed source you might use. Also, I find that tunes I learn by ear stick with me better. I can do variations easier, and in general I can have fun with the tunes.

Ok, I’ve rambled long enough, but in short, do your best to play by ear, but don’t push yourself to the point of wanting to destroy your intstrument and quit playing the music. Just have fun with it.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

Mmm. Well, learning by ear has a lot of advantages. If you’re not born into a society where you here this stuff all the time and know what it’s supposed to sound like and not sound like, learning by ear has the advantage of teaching you the style at the same time. As Cara says, the dots are great for filling in blanks and such, but learning the tune won’t teach you style or feel. Learning a tune without it won’t teach you much at all.

So learn by ear as much as you can. Eventually, it really does become quicker. The only way to get it to get that way, though, is to do it. Practise learning by ear like you practise anything else. It’ll get there.

So, it’s not so much taboo as a somewhat useless thing to learn a tune by the dots unless you already have a good grip on style, feel, and such.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

Wouldn’t disagree with the comments already posted and I think your own point about experience is important. But to play Devil’s advocate for a bit, there are advantages in learning tunes from notation.

With such a tremendous heritage of music to draw from - I know from experience that the process of scanning large collections quickly, coming across tunes that sound promising, great even on first run through etc, but then searching for a recording or waiting to hear some ‘master/guru’ to deliver HIS/HER ideas from on high can be intensely frustrating not to say, sometimes pointless. How else to develop one’s OWN repertoire, playing style, signature etc, (for better or worse) - it’s an equally valid part of the learning experience as a player. Of course there’s always the old cliche about punters “knowing what they like and liking what they know”. But even some of the best tunes can have a bad night, sound tired and ‘well-travelled’.

Doesn’t do any harm to question and revisit the history of why and how so-called traditional tunes were passed on aurally - nowadays, too often it’s just regarded as ‘the done thing’, like wearing the right designer label on clothing / playing the correct brand instruments / using latest equipment etc. There were a number of reasons and they were mostly non-musical.

In any case, most modern players wouldn’t dream of playing the older tunes in an identical fashion to the way they were originally played, nor restrict themselves to the same repertoire - times do change, techniques have improved etc, audience tastes are different etc

Then again, there are some sessions where players enjoy taking say one tune new to the group and collectively developing it into a more polished article, just depends on the attitude of the players around.

Having said all that - there’s no doubt that learning by ear provides an understanding of the structure and nuances of a tune by a much faster route than learning from notation - and it seems to stick permanently.

Just don’t feel the methods are mutually exclusive. Else it would seem so restrictive.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

Franco, you don’t mention whether you get to sessions a lot. In my view, regular attendance at sessions is one of the surest routes to acquiring a repertoire. Even if you don’t do much playing at the session, just sitting and listening enables the brain to absorb the tunes over a period of time - and then one day you’ll find yourself joining in on a tune that you hadn’t consciously sat down to learn. It’s a tremendous experience when that happens the first time.
In learning by ear at sessions you almost never get a tune right the first time round - it seems to be a matter of successive approximations over several sessions before you get all the notes, and this is where a little judicious reference to the dots can help in order to fill in those little gaps.
An older person who comes into itm late after perhaps a lifetime’s experience of playing from the dots and none whatsoever of playing by ear will naturally find it difficult to learn by ear, because the pathways in the brain haven’t been developed. But they can be - it just takes longer. Such an older person, who may be quite good at sight-reading, can put his/her sight-reading ability to good use by learning tunes from the dots, but I don’t think this should be done until the style has been absorbed, and then the player will know that the printed music is no more than an outline of the real tune.

It quickly became frustrating when I would play the accompanying CD and hear all this “extra” stuff that wasn’t written -- ornamentation that just isn’t written. I also found that ornamentation wasn’t very well described in the tutorials, nor were any good examples of ornamentation given on the CD. I found a whistle tutor in my area that could help with the learning of the ornamentation and how/when to apply it. A local Irish musician told me to purchase O’Neill’s, but also said that I would have to buy CDs and “listen, listen, listen”. I have purchased several CDs . . . CDs that are ceili music, whistle music, assortments of jigs and reels, local Irish entertainers . . . you name it, I’ve purchased it! I have also purchased several different books of Irish music. When I hear a tune on a CD that I really like, I “listen, listen, listen” over and over until I can hum/sing the tune on my own. Then, I pull out the whistle and try to put it together. Sometimes successfully, sometimes not. For the not-so-successful times, I look to see if a version of that tune is in any of my music book collections, and if not, I look for downloadable sheet music here at thesession.org. I find that finding music allows me to get over the “bumps” that I encounter when trying to learn by ear. I usually find that it’s a matter of my trying to make more of a phrase than it really is, and once I see it in print, I realize it’s less complicated than I am making it. I also listen to the recording and follow along with the sheet music and make notations of different ornamentation I’m hearing . . . a cut here, a roll there, . . . etc. I find it easier to insert the ornamenation and also find that I’m (dare I say “instinctively”) beginning to add my own ornamentation, as well.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

Even this aural tradition has use for the dots, enough so that some fine players in the days before recordings were common used the dots or even invented their own transcription systems to jot tunes down so they wouldn’t lose them before they were fully learned.

The point is, no matter what your source for the tune, you’ve got to get how it *sounds* into your head, and then play it. For most of us, it’s more direct to go from how it sounds when someone else plays it, to how it sounds coming out of our own instrument. But once you get used to it, you can do the same thing with the dots or abcs. Of course, you need to know something about the style and tradition of the music to make it sound authentic, whatever that means….

And yes, get thee to a session. Start your own if you have to, but find a way to play tunes with other people so you can all expand each other’s repertoire.

And don’t be afraid to learn tunes at full speed off of cds….I still prefer this over slow-down software. Learning tunes by ear at full speed is an acquired skill, but it’ll help more when it comes to learning tunes at a session. Plus your ear will get used to hearing all the ornaments and whatever else is going on at full pace--the slowdowner stuff turns it all to mush in my ear.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

One of the biggest stylistic differences between the sounds of Irish and Scottish diddley music came obout because of the Scot’s propensity to pass tunes on with dots, and the Irish propensity to be more aural.
I’m refering to 100 to 300 years ago here.

Scottish music is much more precise than Irish music, for this reason.

Now, however, the two traditions seem to be coming closer, if not merging in some ways. I know that a lot of this has to do with the general demise in regional styles brought about by the advent of recordings and more travel, but, I can’t help feeling that Irish musicians’ discovery of the dots throughout the last century is partly to blame.

Modern Irish diddley music is without a doubt tighter than it was thirty or forty years ago. And the reason for this seems to be a greater emphasis on learning tunes “properly”.

So what have we ended up with? A good many more tunes than we need, most of which are so simmilar to each other they prompt (quite rightly) the dreaded “all sounds the same” retort. Where once, a tune might be refered to as a variation of another tune, now we have two tunes.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

Are the dots really to blame for a “superfluity” of tunes? I’m not so sure. If people had been writing the tunes down from the start, we’d probably not have had the staggering number of local and regional variations on a theme, which probably developed as tunes passed aurally from one player to the next, and over time, as memory played tricks.

So it’s not necessarily cementing the tunes in notation that gave us so many similar tunes, but the *lack* of a systematic and nit-picky way to pass them on, with original titles attached. (Not that learning aurally isn’t systematic and nit-picky--that’s how I learn tunes by ear, but not everyone does--and I mean by comparison to sheet music.)

That’s my theory, and I’m sticking to it. :o)

How is it that Michael and I always seem to be saying the same thing from opposite ends of the megaphone? :o) :o)

Of course, I also disagree that there are too many similar tunes. I *like* knowing a dozen similar but distinctive D mixolydian reels--to me, they’re like the classical pieces by a chronology of composers who piled derivatively one upon the other, showing what they could do with a predecesor’s musical ideas. That’s an important way for any musical genre to develop and evolve. If one player knows the Donegal setting of Dr. Gilbert’s (Dispute at the Crossroads) and another knows the “other” setting, who’s to say which one is “superfluous?” Seems to me it’s more a statement about a given musician’s ability to distinguish and memorize variations on a theme, and yet isn’t that precisely what most gives an Irish trad player his or her bragging rights--an ability to infinitely vary a tune?

All of which boiled down to--for me at least, in my formative years :o)--as an advantage of being able to read the dots. They helped me understand and remember similarities and differences in tunes, and in different settings of tunes, and so the dots helped me understand the structure of this music more quickly than I would have otherwise. But I was also learning by ear from the start, and it came relatively easily. I’d argue that’s the best of both worlds, not the hinderance that some folks have suggested here in the past.

So I’d say: use the dots as an aid to ear training.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

I think in a sense the ‘dots’ - or rather, as in Michaels point, the late arrival of the dots, as compared with the Scottish tradition, have contributed mightily to the “superfluity” of tunes. The point is that by the time any of the ‘old’ tunes were written down, they had already mutated into so many variations, due to the aural passing on of tunes. With the writing down of these variations, they needed an identifying tag, so acquire distinct names. It’s interesting to look at the last regional style of music to aquire ‘dots and names’, i.e. the tunes of Slaibh Luachra. There are many reels and jigs from this area which, had they been noted down half a century earlier, may well have been given distinct names, but because modern students/collectors of the music are more knowledgeable than those of the past, they are identified as variations of tunes from elsewhere. (The musicians themselves are also less likely to think their tunes are unique local tunes, as they are more familiar with a wider range of music via modern media.)

Hmmm….I’ve transcribed lots of tunes off of recordings and live players without getting names to them. In many cases it’s only years later that I run into the tune again from another source and find a name attached. And I’ve seen lots of gan ainm transcriptions (from knowledgeable players) with a note to the side about “possible setting of….” So “absolutely never” seems a tad strong to me…..

What I don’t understand about Mike and Ottery’s point here is that the variation of a tune would have to be pretty well cemented in the minds of players for it to be “set in stone” as dots. It’s not the dots themselves that keep the variation alive. Some players had to do that, just by playing the “variation,” for it to survive long enough for someone to write it down. So explain again how the dots are guilty?

Personally, I disagree that so many tunes are just variations of some prototypical theme. They are in fact different tunes. Do they share some phrases or melodic similarities? Of course. That’s how you know they’re Irish tunes and not Moravian nose flute tunes. I Buried My Wife and Danced on Her Grave and Rambling Pitchfork have strong similarities, mostly in the B parts. And Buried My Wife and Frieze Britches have some commonalities in their A parts. But I wouldn’t have any problem differentiating among these three tunes at a session, even if they were played one after the other. And I’d argue that they’re distinctive enough to keep separate even if they’re not written down.

In other words, how many Irish trad players out there either can’t or don’t bother to read the dots? And how many of them know, by heart, lots of strongly “similar” tunes?

I think the dots contribute to preserving a wealth of tunes, and tune collections have no doubt added to the superabundance of tune *names* (just look at the index in any of Brendan Breathnach’s Ceol Rince na hEireann). But having 20,000 names for 12,000 tunes is different than simply having “too many” similar tunes. Besides, I think the similarities have much more to do with folk musicians using (1) a relatively narrow range of pitches, keys, and modes; (2) a small handful of instruments on which they compose; and (3) a finite number of motifs or phrases (to avoid wandering too far beyond a “traditional” sound) to make up tunes.

Or am I all wet?

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

I’m with Zina on this one. Where Michael and I disagree is that he sees the dots as setting the tune in stone, where I see the ill-advised player doing that, only through slavish adherence to the dots.

In this music, there is no law that says “Thou shalt play only as written,” even if you are learning the tune from the page. No dot police will haul you away if you wander from the inky path. It’s not the dots’ fault if a player blinders himself to all the possibilites and plays only what the dots themselves indicate.

Part of this may be your level of experience with the dots. I’ve played enough from them to know that you don’t “read” sheet music, you *listen* to it…not much different than you’d listen to someone play the tune.

Of course, sheet music is just a snapshot of the tune, and a skeletal outline at best. It’s like hearing just the first run through of the tune from a recording. You’re not going to get all the variations that the player then layers into the next 4 times through. So I “listen” to the dots, paying attention to what I hear as the phrases and turns that give this tune its particular personality. Then I play it, finding ways to highlight those phrases and turns, and ways to add interest to the other parts. And as soon as the basic melody is in my head, I quit looking at the dots (this usually doesn’t take long). I don’t sit around staring at sheet music all day.

In other words, how I play a tune from the dots is really NO DIFFERENT from how I play a tune learned from another musician.

Far from “undermining” my memory, the dots are just another way of getting a tune into my aural memory. If anything, being able to access sheet music and abcs--and all the tunes available in these formats--has greatly enhanced my aural memory, both in scope (number of tunes) and variety (learning variations on tunes that I could find no live player or recorded source for).

So Michael, dear lad, I can only admit to thinking and learning differently than you do. And no, it doesn’t cripple my playing at all.

Re: Is learning tunes from notation meant to be taboo????

P.P.S.
Franco, in answer to your original question, I’ll offer a suggestion that may help you increase your repertoire without shelling out more money for lessons. You can improve your ear for learning tunes off recordings or live players, and make the process a little easier by using sheet music.

Sit down with a recording or two with tunes you’d like to learn. Start out fairly simple--find some easy, session-staple jigs or polkas on a cd where you can clearly hear what the main melody instrument is doing. Listen to one track 30 to 50 times over the course of a day or two. Get the tune into your head, start to hum or lilt it to yourself.

Now go find the dots for the same tune. You likely won’t find a transcription that exactly matches what you’re hearing on the recording, but that’s okay. Mostly, look for the dots in the same key.

Now listen to the tune again and read the dots as you go. You could circle any parts that seem to be really different from the recording. Now try playing from the dots, without the recording. See if you can “fix” the circled parts to more closely echo what’s going on in the recording. Then go back and forth between the recording and the dots until you have a serviceable version of the tune worked out. You might end up liking some of the ideas in the dots, or some in the recording, or a blend, or even come up with your own ideas. But you’ll have added a tune to your personal list.

If you have any difficulty finding the dots for a tune, search the archives here, and if you still can’t find it, ask. One of us will surely be able to transcribe it for you.
