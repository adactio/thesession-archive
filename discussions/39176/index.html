New to the session, percussion question!

I’m a long time American folk player who took up tin whistle about 2 years ago. I’ve fallen in love and also my Clawhammer banjo playing has fallen behind as a result!

Well this past weekend I went to my first session and had a lovely time. They were very forgiving of my inexperience and lack of tune knowledge. I sat back with my set of spoons and scallop shells for most of time.

I was wondering, how accepting are your sessions of alternate percussion instruments such as the spoons and the scallops? They certainly have a place in traditional music, but do they have a seat in the session?

Re: New to the session, percussion question!

There are different types of sessions. What tunes were the musicians playing at the session you attended?
Did they play reels, jigs, hornpipes. polkas…? Was it instrumental or did they have singing?
Single line melody or harmony parts too. Did some of the melody players also play some chords;
for instance a box player using bass buttons. Was there any percussion played?

No two sessions are the same. I would listen in to get a sense of what the musician like to play.
Introduce yourself, get to know people and maybe ask if you can record some of the tunes
for listening afterwards. All the answers for what happens in any given session are best
obtained from the horse’s mouth, if you know what I mean.

Cheers!

Re: New to the session, percussion question!

There are people who consider the spoons to be a valid traditional instrument, and there people who don’t like them. (Sometimes passionately.) Even people who do like spoons often prefer them occasionally rather than all the time. This is a subject that has been discussed on this site with some regularity, so you can probably dig up other conversations if nobody else chimes in here. Which is unlikely.

I was generally encouraged in my efforts at the spoons before I picked up the fiddle. For a real-life example, there’s a fellow named Christy Barry who plays in Doolin, Lisdoonvarna, and other towns Ireland, who is a crackerjack flute player but also pulls the spoons out once or twice a night. He’s really fun to watch as well as hear.

Re: New to the session, percussion question!

I’m not familiar with scallop shells (are they played in one hand, like bones?).
I’m not sure how far the discussion of “alternate percussion” should go, but I don’t think bones or spoons are out of place, in general--with the following STRONG caveats:
1) We have a firm “one percussionist, one backer” rule in our session. If someone wants to play bodhran, snare, or knock anything else together, other bangers have to give him/her space, until the next set. Same for guitarists.
2) Play percussion only on tunes that you know, and listen closely to phrasing and the pulse of the melody players so that you can best highlight what they are doing. Don’t just be a metronome. If you don’t know a lot of the tunes, it’s perfectly okay, even encouraged in our session, to just sit and listen.

Re: New to the session, percussion question!

BabyBearLoFi, my default response is usually to take the mickey a little (not meaning to actually cause offence).

To give you a more measured response, you will often hear on these boards the mantra “The melody is king”. Traditional Irish music as we understand it here is at its core based around melodies.

Other things that are essential to other forms of music such as harmony, chordal accompaniment and percussion are all things that are arguably not essential to this music, and some purists take that sentiment to its extreme and believe that the music is better off with nothing but the melody.

Personally I enjoy hearing good (and that opens a whole other can of worms) accompaniment on guitar, bouzouki, piano etc. I also enjoy good bodhrán playing. I must admit though, much as I admire the skill of the player, listening to the spoons or bones for more than a set or two at a session really does grate on my poor ears.

As others have hinted, if you are serious about playing this music, the whistle is a great instrument to learn, and will make you far more welcome at most Irish sessions that your spoons.

Re: New to the session, percussion question!

(full discolsure: comment from a professional percussionist, loads of different types of music, in America)

Personally, I think of Irish music like a meal. The tunes, the melodies, are the meat and starch. Gotta have em, they’re the most important thing, bar none. The basic/standard accompaniment instruments (bodhran, guitar, zouk, piano, etc) are the sauce. Good sauce makes the meal better, but does not substitute for. Bad sauce can ruin a meal.

Everything else is spice. If you like salt, you don’t dump the entire salt shaker on your meal. For more potent spices, a tiny bit goes a LOOOOOONG way. It’s a pretty crazy night if I even pull my bones out once.

I readily accept that spoons are a “legitimate” part of the traditions of American music, no problem there. I even find them interesting and musical though I have no interest in playing them myself. I don’t know how they got “into” Irish music, I simply don’t know, not enough info. That said, I CANT STAND em in Irish music. It’s nothing against the quality of the player, it’s just the timbre, it grates on my nerves so hard (it doesn’t even hurt my ears, it just….annoys me). For some reason, they don’t seem to bother me with Old Time music…I dunno, maybe I just have a weird prejudice. Like bones, you have to be SOOOOO sensitive to what’s going on, they are so very dominating of every other sound around.

Another thing to remember is that a session is NOT supposed to be a “band” situation, where you’re trying out different ideas on accompanying, arrangements/orchestrations, what weird instrument goes with this that, etc. It’s for sharing tunes and drinks and stories. Everything beyond that dilutes the intent, IMO.

A statistical example to drive the point home: I just looked on my itunes library, I have around 6000 tracks of Irish music (and remember, I’m a percussionist, which means my list of recordings is heavily skewed in that direction). I have probably the most comprehensive bones playlist you’re like to find because I’m particularly intersted in that facet of Irish percussion (it is not complete and I’m aware of that, but it’s far larger than anyone else’s I know or communicate with about bones playing in ITM). I have 127 bones tracks, the general majority of that being stuff from the Ceoltoiri Chualann/Chieftains sphere of influence or specific players like Cathy Jordan (Dervish) or Johnny McDonagh.

The number of tracks I have with spoons on them? Exactly THREE. 3 out of ~6000.

I’m not saying DONT play them, I’m saying be VERY judicious with when you do.

I think we all sense your great love of the spoons and how much you enjoy playing them in old-timey sessions and the like. In fact, for many of us, the casual drop-in player with the djembe, bongos, conga, spoons, skakey-egg, etc has not only detracted from the musical experience but has caused many of us great distress and frustration as we attempt to battle our way through their interpretations of Irish music. I would also like to point out that although there are no rules to an Irish session, there is a culture to each one that you should be aware of before attempting to play your spoons on every set. My humble suggestion would be to use your spoons like a spice in the kitchen. Nobody wants chili-peppers in every meal. Use sparingly to enhance certain meals. Best wishes and I hope you continue your journey with the melody instrument. At the end of the day I believe that will give you the most enjoyment of Irish music in the long run.

Oh dear!

I am an eyewitness (ear-witness?) to the session in question.

I can vouch for everyone so far in this discussion. Yes, the OP showed up armed with spoons, shells and a whistle. They were not a problem, since he’s apparently a pretty good listener. They weren’t played excessively loud. He has a few sets of scallop shells and chose some smaller and quieter ones to blend in. So it wasn’t really a problem. He was certainly a darned sight better than the vast majority of spooners I’ve seen show up. He was polite, enjoyable to be around, and in my book welcome back any time.

I can also vouch for the other participants on this thread. These are people who have many years of experience in many different cities playing tunes in this relatively narrow, peculiar idiom, with a particular set of conventions.
These folks aren’t speaking out of ignorance at all, but are quite well informed and quite right: Spoons, washboards and shells are generally used extremely sparingly in this music. If you listen to any of the most widely-respected classic recordings in this genre - pick any of the top 50 “desert island recordings” from trad-heads - you will not hear more than a few tracks with spoons, shells or bones on them, and when you do, it’s almost always a tone coloration for one tune. Not even a full set of three. It’s usually an accomplished bodhran player or some other experienced player pulling them out, so they know the idiom.

There are good reasons for this… they operate in a very high range that competes with the melody instruments, almost all of it in the treble clef, but they do it without reinforcing the melody.

Understand this: Irish traditional music, more than Americana, old-time, bluegrass or any other folk genre you can name, is all about the melody. Everything is subservient to the melody. Yes, it’s a dance tradition, ultimately, and rhythm is essential… but it’s ***already embedded in the melody.*** The bones or spoons can add a bit of drama, but they cannot add to the timing of a melody well played, and if played constantly, the bones and spoons lose the element of drama.

I don’t believe I’m out on a limb here… this is pretty basic to our own limited and very specific musical style and community.

Few transplants from Americana music understand jig timing or slip jigs, etc. So if they show up they tend to cause the worst kind of train wrecks: TIMING train wrecks. And this happens even when the player is a perfectly solid player in other musical contexts. (Same goes with guitar players, who are famous for it.)

Fortunately, the OP, I think, caught on pretty quickly.

The spoons are also a potential problem because a good spoon player tends to attract bad ones - and then you do have a problem. We had a spoon player at our local who damned near ruined the sesh with cluelessness before there was an intervention staged. (This individual was present as well, but thankfully, did not attempt to spoon along. If this individual did, there would likely have been a blanket spoon ban! Hey, life ain’t fair!)

He’s also a very musical whistle player, though his repertoire right now is limited because he’s new to it, as it was for all of us in the first year or so of playing a melody instrument. He’s going to be fine.

The best thing to do, LoFi, is do a tone of listening to great players (among whistle players, Mary Bergin’s Feadog Stain recordings come immediately to mind! Can’t go wrong with those, though don’t worry about matching the speed for now!) and focus on the whistle. Work on the tunes from “Dow’s List,” which you can easily find on this board in a search. No particular order… just whatever tunes catch your ear and that you think you’d enjoy playing. Start any of those, anytime, almost anywhere you go, and you will almost certainly find someone playing along with you. That list from Dr. Dow is a terrific foundation to focus the efforts of anyone learning.

You’ll get more enjoyment out of it, and you’ll be welcome at any session anywhere with solid command of even a handful of those tunes and good timing. And the timing you have already! As I said… Irish music is all about the melody. The joy, the richness, the camaraderie, everything, is in the melodies, and the timing that’s embedded in the melody. You have a melody instrument, and I can already tell that after some focus and effort, and a couple of tunes added to your repertoire every week, you will soon be wicked good at it!

I can tell this because it’s obvious to me that whatever anyone’s attitude is towards the spoons, in or out of this idiom, you’re a listener and not a banger. That’s ultimately what’s most important.

Lastly, don’t take anything personally. Nobody has said anything in bad faith. Keep coming back, and keep learning tunes. Don’t get discouraged… nobody hates you, and don’t be a stranger. Hope to see you back!

Jason

One of the great things about Irish Traditional music is that it is comfortable in it’s own skin and secure in it’s sound.

This is often reflected in a bluntness by it’s players - often unapologetic bluntness.

But that’s fine - that’s the music and that’s the tradition and the tradition is strong.

Someone might suggest to add some trumpet or synths or electric guitar because they believe (based on their musical experience) that those things if applied tastefully can add to the sonic experience of the tunes - but they’re wrong - they’re basing their opinions on their own contemporary musical experience - which has nothing to do with the traditional understanding of the music.

So bluntly speaking unless you play the music you can’t have an opinion on how it “should” sound, and even if you have an opinion because you play, that opinion will only have the weight the community of players allow it to have - that’s the beautiful but harsh reality.

So in short - play whatever instrument you want with whatever tunes you want - but if you do that and it doesn’t fit with the tradition - understand that most players will simply consider you are not part of the community. You need to work hard, play well and most importantly have respect for the music and the other players.

I think it’s largely clear from the responses here how most players judge spoons. They can be an occasional and effective novelty piece - but beyond that they are not used for the reasons outlined.

Re: New to the session, percussion question!

I think the danger that some folks fall into is projecting the musical attitudes, conventions, idioms and etiquette from one musical community onto the other. Things that are just fine at old-time gatherings are not cool at old-time jams or bluegrass jams, or Irish tune sessions, and vice versa. Not that there isn’t overlap and cross pollination, of course.

But Irish trad sessioning among experienced, dedicated Aspies… I mean, players… is its own thing… And it’s all about the tune. Not the percussion to the tune. Not a beat or a groove. Not “jamming.” Not the changes. The tune.

There are certainly bodhran and guitar players and others who don’t play a melody instrument who are extremely good. But all the ones I know who have gotten good are ones who spend a good deal of their listening time… or even almost all of it… listening to recordings of great trad players. So even if they can’t play the tune on a fiddle or accordion or flute or whistle, they recognize the melody, know where it’s going, and know the idioms so they can make informed musical decisions as to what to do with it.

For a novice melody player, it’s easy… You can play the melody, in a very straight ahead manner, in time with the other folks, and you can do fine. You can be a welcome member of almost any sesh with adequate seating with just a few tunes under your belt, and you are unlikely to be disruptive or pull in a different direction, even as a rank beginner.

The damage comes from non-melody players, who may be terrific on their instrument, but who have not done their homework, putting in the time to learn the repertoire or the conventions, who don’t know where a tune comes from or where it’s going, or how the timing works.

There’s no way to do that without putting in the time learning the TUNES, even if it’s just in your ear.
