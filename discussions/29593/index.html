Re: Eb Flute

Crackpot, you have now entered the confusing world of transposing vs. non-transposing instruments. Clarinet is in Bb, but does not actually sound Bb when you play that note. It’s very confusing, but suffice it to say that pipes are said to be in A, but are actually in Bb. If you want to play with pipes, use an Eb whistle.

Vintage instruments that are in a sharper pitch are still playing in the proper key. However, if it is sharp enough, I suppose you could fake it as an Eb flute if the tuning slide is pushed all the way in.

I’m glad David50 brought up the Matt Molloy interview. I read this a long time ago and remember that he created the “fashion” of playing with an Eb flute, which he came upon by accident. I get the impression that he favored the flute itself more so than the key. I don’t believe he uses Eb with the Chieftains and he certainly did not with the Bothy Band.

Ailin - I would have thought it obvious the difference between playing solo and in a group. I’ve never heard of a set of uilleann pipes pitched in Eb, although I’ve no doubt someone will tell me they exist. When Matt Molloy was playing in both the Bothy Band and Chieftains, he played in “concert pitch” to go along with the band. When playing solo you can more or less do what you like, as regards the pitch you play in. This does not stop bands which do not feature uilleann pipes from making their recordings in “Eb” - the most obvious examples would be “Dervish” and De Dannan“, who have both recorded albums with almost all of the instrumental tracks pitched sharp. Off the top of my head, players who have recorded tracks in ”Eb“ rather than ”concert pitch" include Frankie Gavin, Noel Hill, Paul Brock, Tommy Peoples, Mary Bergin, Gavin Whelan, and Harry Bradley.
You ask when “Eb” became popular as a pitch to play in. As far as recordings go, I will try to track that down for you, but I can absolutely guarantee that quite a few of the sessions at the Willie Clancy school in Miltown Malbay in the early 1980s were pitched up to “Eb”. I was there, was consequently musically excluded from some of them, and still have the recordings. This is only my opinion, but I think it was sometimes done by mainly professional musicians to exclude others from joining in.
I haven’t come across “Eb” sessions for a good few years, and it may be a fad which has died out. It’s not something which I’ll miss.

Re: Eb Flute

“A little explanation needed here. In none of the literature I have on ITM for flute is Eb as a key for flute ever mentioned. All of the repertoire is written primarily in D, G, and the relative minors. ”

Since an explanation hasn’t been proffered yet, I’ll take a stab at championing the obvious.

Owning an Eb flute isn’t about playing tunes in Eb. It’s about simply raising the pitch and fingering everything as though you’re still on a D flute. In other words, it doesn’t matter what keys and modes the repertoire is primarily written in--that’s all relative. I can tune my fiddle to A=466 and play all the tunes with the same fingering I use at A=440. In terms of Hz, a tune normally in D would now be in Eb, but I wouldn’t be thinking that way at all and would still be using “D fingering.”. Similarly, a guitarist could either tune up or clamp a capo on the 1st fret and play all the old familiar chords.

They date back 200 years before that. Sometimes described as Db flutes (after the seven-finger note), they were the standard pitch for military bands, and that market was so huge it must have kept leaking instruments into civilian hands until the 20th century. Db piccolos are still made in significant numbers.
