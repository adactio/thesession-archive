Too fast

I was at a session last night and a player started playing a set of reels (fairly well known ones) really fast.

Not a note out of turn nor a loss of rhythmic swing.

Just. Too. Fast.

I never expected I would think like this - does this happen to all session goers at one stage in life?

Plus I’m only around 30 which is baby stage compared to most of you old codgers!

Re: Too fast

A few thoughts on speed ….

Speed to demonstrate virtuosity has its place, but perhaps it is a small place. Sometimes a person wants to establish their credentials, and prove that they have awesome talent. And so, folks sit back and marvel -- duly impressed by the bravado performance of lightning fast techniques and flawless execution of mind-numbingly difficult passages. But then what? Once everyone knows you are awesome, do we really need you to rub it in. And, really, why did we need to hear this proof in the first place? I would say that listening to someone’s virtuosity in a session is usually not the reason most people go to sessions. For that, a concert might be more usual. Even so, it can be nice as a change in pace sometimes, but overdoing it for reasons of ego-fulfillment is rather rude.

Do not forget that this is dance music. For a variety of reasons, dancers might like speed. A common issue for dancers is the energy level of the music. Dancers take the energy from the music to drive their actions. It is not always appreciated that skillfully played music with lots of ‘lift’ can provide that energy, without a need for great speed. There is a real vitality that can be felt in the music, which drives the feet of the dancers. But, for many players, creating the energy level is difficult -- especially in a group, and so speed is used as an alternative way to generate energy. Speed is a cheap way to mimic lift.

Sessions are as much a social phenomenon as a musical one. There is a balance to be struck between ‘inclusion’ and ‘performing’. There is usually a mix -- some sets involve everyone joining in and others are solos or duets. Variety is a good thing. I like the occasional slow air, done solo. And I am fine with an occasional virtuoso piece, or crazy fast tune. But, I find sessions most enjoyable when there is plenty of playing at a pace to allow players the opportunity to be a part of it.

In my opinion, the old jazz saying “It don’t mean a thing if it ain’t got that swing” applies to playing the tunes.

On the other hand if the tempo is too slow, the swing can be lost, leaving the overall sound a trifle turgid.

As I am an Old Codger, I prefer the medium paced approach.

Re: Too fast

I’ve never come across a tune in a session which has been played too fast for me to play along with (with all the provisos that Choons mentioned in the OP). That’s not to say it wouldn’t be better at a slightly slower pace …

In my experience, what’s more common is someone starting a tune at a tempo faster than they can handle. Yes, as the esteemed GP said, they are often young too :) Often it’s because of over-excitement and under-experience - the itchy fingers shorten the rest values at the end of phrases, come in too soon with the next phrase, and that alone will speed up the already ‘too fast’ tune …

I think it’s interesting noting the actual speed (in BPM) vs perception of speed in some players. Spankie Flavin plays fast in real BPM, and sounds every bit of it. Players like Kevin Burke, for some reason, can still play at the same fast BPM, but sound totally un-hurried, and ‘not really fast’ at all.

There’s so much to do with complex mechanics going on there, I think.

Martin Hayes always sounds ‘slow’ to me, but that’s not a bad thing at all, is it?

Re: Too fast

Speed is one of the easiest dynamics of the music to vary. I personally like a variety of speeds during the course of a night. I sat in a session with a few fantastic and well-known players several years ago in Trim, and the music was fantastic. But it didn’t stray far in tempos during the course of the evening, and it got a bit monotonous after a while, for my taste. (Part of it was that it was faster than I could comfortably play at the time, I suppose).

One of the problems is that speed is something that people can sometimes use to mask problems with their rhythm. As an example, it’s a LOT harder to make a tune sound good very slow! And it’s pretty easy to tell when someone is playing faster than their ability allows them to do and make it sound good. But speed is an important aspect to a lot of this music. As has been pointed out, speed is important to people dancing (sometimes, that means playing very slow, BTW. Like a championship hornpipe, for instance). But speed can change the feel of the music too. Part of the fun of the music is how it twists and turns unexpectedly. And just like a rollercoaster, the twists and turns become more exciting (for lack of a better word) at higher speeds.

yes, above 116, 120 at the outermost, to yours truly it gets vile, full stop. but many of the turistas--oops, dancers---want the speed way upwards of 120. how it was explained to me by a teacher who would know, was that traditionally dance speeds were nowhere near the crazed, beat-the-sh88e out of it tempos you see now, but that the tempos sped up with the advent of set dancing as a hobby for people who wanted to have fun, but were not serious dancers, and did not wish to bother learning authentic traditional set dancing footwork, which unbeknownst to many people is very intricate indeed. as my teacher put it, the bands had to speed up to keep these amateur dancers “in the air,” whereas fine traditional set dancers take it somewhat slower because of the footwork. my teacher put on dvd clips from “west along the road” to give me the gist…

you can see plenty of this kind of top-quality dancing during willie clancy week in the dance recital, to players such as the lovely box musician mick mulcahy of limerick. i also saw dancers of this quality doing sets to andrew macnamara, mark donnellan, and jim corry of tulla/east clare in a tent during the feakle festival one year--amazing stuff, the foot work and the music. i wasn’t clocking it, but i’d the music they were doing for those dancers felt like 106 on the lower end to 112-116 tops…

on the other hand, one year in kinvara for the mrs. crotty concertina festival, i was watching the tulla play outdoors under a tent in the square for dancers and it was almost grotesque how hyper-speed they were playing. way, way, waaaaay faster than any tulla recording ever made. but these were turista set dancers, and there was no footwork happening a-tall, a-tall……

Re: Too fast

[So what you you guys consider a good reel tempo? I’ve had lots of problems keeping up with some of the others in my session, and frankly it’s been really discouraging lately.]

to weigh in on the original question…peer pressure to play fast, either from the outside or experienced internally due to a desire to “join in the session,” has ruined many, many potentially lovely players of traditional irish music. the idea of “ruination” in the sense of, prompting people to play faster than their skill level, is well-known and much-discussed. but there is another way apprentice or journeyman players can be ruined by speed pressure, and that is, someone who is on their way to being a good player, or already IS a lovely player, can be ruined in the sense of becoming discouraged and slinking off with their tail between their legs because they’re not banging the living sh**e out of it as fast as players they perceive as, the big boys.

in the megalopolis where i live, there is not much irish music, and barely a half-dozen or fewer people who play it really, really well (though plenty think they play it well). unfortunately, a number of these people aren’t happy unless they’re playing it to hyper-speed, and often seem to do so to let everyone know that they’re the ones getting the gigs in those faux-“Riverdance” turista holiday shows, thank you very much. (of course, these same people would slobber all over slower-playing master traditional players from ireland and put on the big “pure drop” front for them should any be passing through…)

the long and short of it is that unlike ireland, where there’s super-fast sessions led by really good people as well as traditional-tempo, relaxed-speed sessions led by really good people, my region does not have master-level players committed to playing and leading sessions at relaxed traditional speeds. the opportunity to sit in session and follow really, really good leaders at those speeds is irreplaceable for developing players, whether you then go on and tackle the hyper speeds or not. it’s not enough to get the relaxed tempos in session---the leaders have to be really, really good. the irish kids spend years doing this at comhaltas or other organized sessions, following a master teacher or leader and having lots of fun as well…

in any event--i’m not sure how long the OP has been playing or when they started. but forget being discouraged because you’re not as fast as the session. your focus should be on playing the tune a) in time to the signature. b) with a beat or pulse that is discernible in the irish style of discernibility. c) with a discernible beat or pulse that stays in the same place consistently (more or less). d) with discernible swing or syncopation in the irish style of swing or syncopation. e) with discernible irish swing or syncopation that stays in the same place consistently (more or less). f) to achieve a) through e) to the degree where it is fluid, fluent, and automatic.

if you spend three years playing your reels in the “80s” on the metronome before you get that, so be it. same with the “90s.” same with the “100 to 106” range. this is THE important thing, not the session.

Usually, if the music “sounds” too fast it’s a sign that the players have reached or are reaching the upper limits of their skill level and competence. As another poster mentioned, a good player can be playing at “quite a lick” and not necessarily sound like a speed merchant. Also, he or she will have much more clarity.
