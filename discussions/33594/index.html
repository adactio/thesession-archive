First reply answered the question. Except I would argue that F is not the relative Major of A minor, C is. I would call F just the Major VI chord (pronounced major six chord, or sixth chord). If you want the relative Major if A dorian, well it would probably be G most appropriately, which also happens to be the subtonic, since A dorian and G major are the same, diatonically. Of course in practice, true minor keys are rarely 100% diatonic but that’s another issue.

The G Major chord used in the context of A minor or dorian (arguably when you use this you aren’t in minor but either dorian or mixolydian mode) is the subtonic. D Major is the subtonic chord of E dorian, or you can say it is a diatonic chord built from the subtonic as it’s root, etc.

“the prevailing wisdom seems to be that an Fmajor chord in an A dorian tune (or the equivalent of that in other keys) is the epitome of evil.”

It’s not evil. It just takes away the “dorian” aspect.

“But, like Fliúiteadóir has pointed out, there are many recorded examples of folks doing exactly that,”

Well, an F major chord in the Bothy Band’s (recorded) version of Julia Delaney isn’t an example. They play it (as most folk do) in D dorian, where an F major chord would not be out of place. If there was a Bb major chord in there, it would be the equivalent.
Tommy Peoples’ Reel is not in the dorian mode. It is usually played in B (nat) minor (or æolian), but if it was shifted to A min, then an F major chord, again, would not be out of place.
The F natural note is the difference between the notes in A natural minor (æolian) and A dorian (where it is an F#), so, if you want to take the dorian aspect out of the tune, go ahead and play an F major. You wouldn’t be evil (IMO), you’d just be a bit of a plank.
Note that playing a chord on the sixth degree of the dorian scale (sixth degree as root) would land you in diminished territory. If you want a “major” sound in A dorian, a D major might do the job. Dropping that sixth degree note by a semitone makes your mode no longer dorian.
It’s what earns “backers” their reputation. Often misplaced, but often well earned too.

No. You’d just be playing a chord that suggests a minor mode in a tune that is in a major mode.
However, the notes within that minor chord at least occur in the tune.
Playing a chord in which all the notes do not occur within the tune is taking things a step further. It’s putting your own slant on the tune, and effectively changing the mode - not what the melody player is playing, what you are playing. You are playing in one mode, and the melody player is playing in another. Your chord does not determine the “key”, and it doesn’t necessarily determine the mode in itself. However, combined with the other notes you may be playing, it does go towards determining the mode you are playing in. You might even be switching modes. If the melody player isn’t doing the same, you are conflicting with each other.
The tune may still be in (say, as this was what started the ball rolling) A dorian, but your chords ain’t.

Oh, another idea… I don’t mind a clash, in the sense of tension.

Take the descent from the root in the famous Stairway to Heaven intro… It goes A, G# Gnat, F#, Fnat, E, and occasionally I’ll wind up on a great big fat IV chord.

Now, it’s Dorian for a bit, and it’s Aeolean for a little bit. Hell, it’s the bebop scale for a little bit. So if you’re playing a Dorian tune, you’re going to have some tension between the Fnat in the interior voice and any F#’s in the harmony.

But I think it works because there is a logic to it. The listener knows where it came from, and where it’s going. So over a repetitive dorian or aeolian melody line that more or less repeats several times, you can do it because it engages the ear and forces the mind to fill in the road map between where the line has been and where it’s going.

The notes A and E played together can be referred to as you say as a power chord. If you saw this notated in, for example, a Guns N’Roses transcription, it might be noted as A5 (A the root, E the fifth). Putting another A note on top of this doesn’t change this. You could stack up as many A and E notes as you want.

Under strict definitions the absence of a third means that this would not be viewed as a chord but it is commonly referred to as a chord for ease.

This distinction regarding whether it is a chord or not is drawn because there is no c note present to constitute an Aminor (A C E) or a c#note to make an Amajor (A C# E). Either of these chords (Am or A major) would be a triad (three notes stacked in thirds).

I like the term A5 because it tells you what you are getting in a concise way.

It could also be referred to as a dyad but I think A5 makes for better shorthand.

If you were to play through all of diatonic modes starting on A then referring to A5 as a modal chord works for all of A ionian, A dorian, A phrygian, A mixolydian, A Aeolian. I mean it works in that A5 can be seen as implying all and none of these modes. It is ambiguous in the sense of major and minor and will therefore not clash with any of these modes.

In a jazz context the term modal chord might be viewed as describing a chord that DOES imply a definite mode and is not at all ambiguous.

Staying with A, a straightforward A7 chord implies A mixolydian because of the major third and the flat 7th.

So, A7 could be looked on as denoting a certain mode and *could* be described using the term ‘a modal chord’, the mode in question being A mixolydian.

Not to kick off another round, but I suppose one of my bugbears about these discussions is the labels really and how people can get overly excited about them. They are just labels after all, sometimes useful and sometimes not.

Last point and some more labels….

The Earl’s chair, first bar B2Bd BAF#A

Adding a simple G major triad in the backing here as was suggested back up the thread implies just that overall (backing & melody combined) for the first half of the bar.

Here are some labels that might exercise people unnecessarily. The second half of the bar implies Gadd9 (GBDA) and Gmaj7th (GBDF#). Overall for the second half of the bar, you could look at it as implying Gmaj9 (GBDAF#).

If you told some people that you were going to play a Gmajor followed by Gadd9, followed by a Gmaj7 followed by a Gadd9 *all in the first bar*, they mightn’t be too happy, but that is the overall effect really with just a bog standard G major and this is just using notes present in the bar as harmony.

Now, the overall effect isn’t all that different to adding the c root note to the notes of the first half of the first bar of Drowsy Maggie to make a Cmaj9 (CEBD) there. Granted the c is from outside the key of the common setting of the tune, but in Gam’s setting with the c naturals, it wouldn’t be.

I suppose that’s all I wanted to get across from the outset. Harmony introduced by backing has an effect overall and when you pick things apart like this you start to see how welcome or unwelcome this effect might be. Knowing and understanding how to get these different effects combined with knowing when to use them is the issue really.

You can end up clouding things so that the first bar of Drowsy Maggie sounds like The Earl’s Chair, to the point that adding a note or a chord can result in both tunes sounding like a slow motion Maj9 chord from the outset.

Backing can REALLY be an unncessary intrusion in a session, and if I’m honest, I’m not a huge fan of it for the very reason that there ARE people who won’t like it no matter what you do. I do suspect though, that are a lot of people who enjoy decent backing at sessions but they don’t get heard too often enough because of the more vocal people who say they don’t.
